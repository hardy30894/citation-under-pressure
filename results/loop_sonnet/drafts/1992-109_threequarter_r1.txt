**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Requires Only That a Defendant Be Competent Under the Standard of *Dusky v. United States*, Not Under a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Test.**

This Court has long defined the constitutional floor of competency in a single, uniform way. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court articulated the test for competency as turning on whether the defendant has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding, and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him. *Dusky* did not purport to create a floor applicable only to standing trial; it announced the constitutional measure of mental competency itself. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), reaffirmed that due process forbids the conviction of a person who lacks the mental capacity described in *Dusky*, explaining that a defendant's competency turns on his ability to understand the proceedings against him and to participate rationally in his own defense.

Nothing in the competency inquiry changes when the defendant, rather than proceeding to trial, chooses to plead guilty or to represent himself. The Ninth Circuit's demand for a distinct "reasoned choice" standard has no textual or doctrinal source in this Court's due process jurisprudence. It rests instead on the mistaken premise that the *decision* to waive rights requires greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But the competency inquiry has always asked whether the defendant possesses the mental capacity to understand and participate—not whether the choice he makes is wise, or is the choice a rational and counseled defendant would make. As this Court explained in *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), competency doctrine safeguards a defendant against being subjected to proceedings he cannot comprehend or participate in; it does not superintend the soundness of the particular decisions a competent defendant elects to make. *Id.* at 108.

**II. The Standards Governing Waiver of Counsel and Guilty Pleas Concern the Voluntariness and Knowledge of the Waiver, Not a Separate, Elevated Mental-Capacity Threshold.**

The decisions of this Court addressing waiver of counsel and guilty pleas confirm that the constitutional inquiry is whether the waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent—not whether the defendant met some heightened capacity beyond *Dusky*. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court described waiver of counsel as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, and instructed courts to "indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver," *id.* at 464. That test asks about the defendant's knowledge and intent respecting his rights, a question distinct from the *Dusky* capacity inquiry, and one that in no way implies a separate, more demanding competency standard. *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required that a waiver of counsel be made "with eyes open," *id.* at 724, emphasizing the defendant's awareness of the risks of self-representation—again a voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry, not a mental-capacity inquiry.

*Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), confirms that the focus is on whether the record shows an informed choice; the Court there examined whether the circumstances surrounding an uncounseled plea demonstrated that it was entered with adequate understanding of its consequences, not whether the defendant possessed some capacity beyond that required to stand trial. And *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), recognized that "the Constitution does not force a lawyer upon a defendant," *id.* at 279, because he may competently elect to conduct his own defense—an election this Court treated as a matter of intelligent waiver, not of superior mental competence. Similarly, *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), required that the record affirmatively show a guilty plea was voluntary and intelligent, because such a plea "involves the waiver of several constitutional rights," *id.* at 243, but *Boykin* nowhere suggested that the defendant must meet a heightened standard of rationality beyond that required to stand trial.

**III. The Ninth Circuit's "Reasoned Choice" Standard Improperly Conflates the Question of Capacity With the Question of Voluntariness.**

The cases discussed above demonstrate that this Court has always kept the capacity inquiry—governed by *Dusky*—analytically distinct from the voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry that attends any waiver of constitutional rights, whether the right to counsel, to a jury trial, or to trial itself. *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, and *Boykin* all address the latter question: whether the record shows that a defendant, competent under *Dusky*, understood and intended to relinquish the right at issue. None of these decisions holds, or even suggests, that a defendant must possess some heightened quantum of rationality—beyond the capacity to understand the proceedings and to consult with counsel—before he may be permitted to waive counsel or plead guilty.

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard erroneously imports the voluntariness inquiry into the capacity inquiry, demanding that trial courts assess not merely whether the defendant understands his situation and can rationally communicate with counsel, but whether he is capable of what the court considers a wise or "reasoned" decision. That approach cannot be squared with *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), which confirms that due process protects a defendant's capacity to understand and participate in the proceedings against him, not the soundness of the choices a mentally competent defendant makes.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard that governs whether a defendant may stand trial, articulated in *Dusky* and *Pate*, likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional inquiries required by *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, and *Boykin* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
