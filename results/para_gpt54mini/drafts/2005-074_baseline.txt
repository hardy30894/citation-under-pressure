**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does not Apply to Fernandez-Vargas’s Pre-1997 Reentry, and Its Use Here Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive.**

Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), should not be construed to reach an alien who reentered the United States before the statute’s effective date. Statutory silence is not enough to overcome the presumption against retroactive application. *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 265-73 (1994); *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316-20 (2001). Where Congress has not clearly prescribed retroactive effect, courts must ask whether applying the provision would attach new legal consequences to past conduct. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70.

That inquiry resolves this case in Fernandez-Vargas’s favor. When he reentered in 1982, the reinstatement statute did not exist. For more than a decade afterward, he lived in the United States under a legal regime that did not categorically foreclose all relief for an alien with a prior deportation order. Section 241(a)(5), by contrast, does not merely alter procedures; it “reinstat[es]” a prior order and bars “any relief” under the INA. 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). That is a new disability attached to past conduct. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 325; *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 394 F.3d 881, 884-86 (10th Cir. 2005).

The Supreme Court’s retroactivity cases in the immigration context confirm the point. In *St. Cyr*, the Court held that a statutory repeal could not be applied to deprive an alien of discretionary relief when he had pleaded guilty in reliance on the possibility of that relief. 533 U.S. at 321-26. The Court emphasized that retroactive effect exists when a new statute “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Id.* at 321 (quoting *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70). Fernandez-Vargas likewise completed the relevant conduct—illegal reentry—before Congress enacted § 241(a)(5). To apply the statute to him would convert a past reentry into a present disqualification from relief that was not then so absolute. *See* *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 325; *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280.

The government may argue, as the Tenth Circuit did, that the statute governs present unlawful presence rather than past reentry. But that characterization ignores the statute’s operative trigger: “illegal reentry.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). The legal consequences follow from the completed reentry, not merely from continued presence. As this Court has recognized, retroactivity analysis focuses on “whether the new provision attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321. Here, the completed event is Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 reentry. Applying § 241(a)(5) to that conduct is retroactive in the same sense condemned in *Landgraf* and *St. Cyr*. *See also* *Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 352-53 (1999).

Nor does Fernandez-Vargas’s later marriage or application for adjustment of status defeat his claim. A statute is retroactive if it “takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269 (quoting *Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler*, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.)). By the time he married and sought adjustment, he had already spent years in the United States under the pre-1997 regime. Congress did not speak clearly enough to eliminate the ordinary presumption that past conduct will not be recharacterized to impose a harsher legal consequence. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 316-20.

**II. The Tenth Circuit’s Contrary Reading Cannot Be Reconciled with the Presumption Against Retroactivity.**

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that Fernandez-Vargas lacked a “protectable expectation” misstates the retroactivity inquiry. The Supreme Court has never limited *Landgraf* to cases involving reliance interests alone. Rather, reliance is “just one of the factors” relevant to determining whether a statute has retroactive effect. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321-23; *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269 n.25. Even absent proven reliance, a statute may not be applied retroactively if it imposes new legal consequences on completed conduct. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270; *Martin*, 527 U.S. at 352-53.

That is this case. Fernandez-Vargas’s illegal reentry occurred fifteen years before § 241(a)(5) took effect. The reinstatement provision does not merely regulate future conduct; it extinguishes the opportunity to seek relief that otherwise would have remained available under the preexisting statutory scheme. *See* *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 325-26. The absence of a clear statement from Congress means the statute must be construed not to apply to pre-enactment reentries. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280.

For those reasons, the petition should be granted and the removal order vacated.
