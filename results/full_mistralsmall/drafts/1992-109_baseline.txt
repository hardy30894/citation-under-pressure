**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Requires a Trial Court to Apply a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Standard When Assessing a Defendant’s Competency to Waive Counsel or Plead Guilty.**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause protects a defendant’s right to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of fundamental constitutional rights, including the right to counsel and the right to plead not guilty. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 396 (1993) (citing *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969)). However, this Court has long recognized that the competency standard for waiving such rights is more exacting than the standard for standing trial. *Id.* at 399-400 (citing *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975); *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966); *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960)). A defendant must possess the capacity for a "reasoned choice" among the available alternatives to validly waive counsel or plead guilty. *Id.* at 400 (citing *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981)).

This heightened standard reflects the gravity of the rights at stake. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 400. A defendant who waives counsel or pleads guilty irrevocably forfeits constitutional protections that safeguard the fairness of the proceedings. *Id.* (citing *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975)). Thus, the trial court must ensure that the defendant’s decision is the product of a rational and informed choice, not the result of mental impairment or confusion. *Id.* (citing *Indiana v. Edwards*, 554 U.S. 164, 174 (2008)).

**II. The Trial Court Failed to Apply the Correct Standard in Assessing Moran’s Competency.**

The Nevada trial court erred by conflating the competency standards for standing trial and waiving rights. *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 272 (9th Cir. 1992). The court relied solely on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran’s ability to assist counsel and understand the proceedings—criteria relevant to competency to stand trial—but not his capacity for a "reasoned choice" regarding his constitutional rights. *Id.* at 272-73 (citing *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172; *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402).

The record contained substantial evidence suggesting Moran lacked the requisite capacity. *Id.* at 269-70. He had recently attempted suicide, was heavily medicated, and responded to the court in monosyllables. *Id.* at 269. These factors, combined with his desire to prevent mitigating evidence from being presented, should have triggered a competency hearing under due process principles. *Id.* at 270-71 (citing *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987)).

**III. The Post-Conviction Hearing Did Not Cure the Error.**

The Nevada Supreme Court’s post-conviction ruling did not remedy the trial court’s failure. *Id.* at 273-75. The state court applied the wrong legal standard—competency to stand trial rather than "reasoned choice"—and its factual findings were premised on that erroneous standard. *Id.* Moreover, the psychiatric reports did not address Moran’s capacity to make a reasoned decision about waiving rights or pleading guilty. *Id.* at 274-75.

This Court has held that a post-conviction competency determination does not cure a trial court’s failure to apply the correct standard at the time of the plea or waiver. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 402-03. The Due Process Clause requires the trial court to ensure the defendant’s competency at the moment of the critical decision, not years later. *Id.*

**IV. The Ninth Circuit Correctly Applied the "Reasoned Choice" Standard.**

The Ninth Circuit properly concluded that the trial court’s failure to apply the heightened standard violated due process. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 270-71. The court’s analysis aligns with this Court’s precedents, which recognize that competency to waive rights demands more than a basic understanding of the proceedings. *Id.* at 272-73 (citing *Evans*, 800 F.2d at 887; *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The trial court’s failure to apply the "reasoned choice" standard deprived Moran of his constitutional right to a fair proceeding.
