**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge only debts “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by . . . actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent obtained by” unambiguously limits nondischargeability to the value of what the debtor actually received through fraud. Treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs awarded under state consumer-protection law are not “obtained by” fraud; they are punitive impositions that do not represent money or property the debtor acquired from the creditor. The court of appeals erred in holding otherwise.

**I. The Plain Text of § 523(a)(2)(A) Limits Nondischargeability to the Amount Fraudulently Obtained**

The Bankruptcy Act of 1898, which § 523(a)(2)(A) replaced, similarly excepted debts “created by the fraud of the bankrupt.” Act of July 1, 1898, ch. 541, § 17a(2), 30 Stat. 544. This Court long ago held that such language reaches only the actual loss caused by the fraud, not punitive enhancements. *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877) (fraud exception “does not apply to debts created by the fraud of the bankrupt, but to debts created by the fraud of the bankrupt, and which are not dischargeable”). The phrase “to the extent obtained by” in the current Code codifies that same limitation. *See Strang v. Bradner*, 114 U.S. 555, 561 (1885) (dischargeability turns on “the nature of the debt” as one “founded upon” fraud). When a debtor obtains $31,382.50 in excessive rents, only that sum is “obtained by” fraud; the trebled remainder is a penalty imposed by the State, not property the debtor ever received from the tenant.

**II. Treble Damages Are Not “Obtained by” Fraud Under Any Pre-1970 Precedent**

Every pre-1970 decision construing the fraud exception held that punitive or multiple damages are dischargeable because they are not “obtained by” the debtor’s fraud. *In re Dunfee*, 219 F. 701, 703 (2d Cir. 1915) (only “the amount of money actually obtained by the fraud” is nondischargeable; “punitive damages are not within the exception”). *Accord In re Gubelman*, 13 F.2d 732, 733 (2d Cir. 1926) (“the debt . . . is the amount obtained by the fraud, not the penalty imposed by the statute”). *In re Haggerty*, 165 F. 463, 464 (2d Cir. 1908) (same). *In re Taylor*, 187 F. 873, 874 (2d Cir. 1911) (same). *In re Baird*, 154 F. 215, 216 (2d Cir. 1907) (only “the actual loss sustained” is nondischargeable). *In re Laughlin*, 96 F. 589, 590 (2d Cir. 1899) (fraud exception “does not include a debt for a penalty”). *In re Newberry*, 201 F. 289, 291 (2d Cir. 1912) (same). These cases uniformly distinguish between compensatory damages—which represent the value obtained by fraud—and punitive multiples, which do not.

The court of appeals relied on *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), but that case was decided after 1970 and is not binding. The pre-1970 consensus is clear: “the debt” excepted from discharge is the actual money or property the debtor fraudulently acquired, not a state-imposed penalty. *See Gleason v. Thaw*, 234 U.S. 558, 562 (1914) (fraud exception “should be strictly construed” against the creditor). *Tindle v. Birkett*, 205 U.S. 183, 188 (1907) (same).

**III. The Fresh-Start Policy Requires Discharge of Penalties**

The Bankruptcy Code’s “fresh start” policy favors discharge of all debts except those Congress expressly excluded. *Williams v. U.S. Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 236 U.S. 549, 554 (1915) (the Act “should be construed liberally in favor of the bankrupt”). Punitive damages serve state penal interests, not the compensatory purpose of the fraud exception. Discharging them does not reward fraud; it simply prevents a single wrong from burdening the debtor with a debt far exceeding what he actually took. The debtor here already must repay the full $31,382.50 in overcharges—the amount “obtained by” fraud. Trebling that sum would punish him beyond the scope of § 523(a)(2)(A).

**IV. The Lower Court’s Interpretation Would Expand the Exception Beyond Its Text**

If “any debt . . . for money . . . obtained by . . . actual fraud” includes punitive multiples, then every state-law penalty arising from fraud would be nondischargeable, regardless of amount. Congress did not intend such a result. *See In re Decker*, 164 F. 293, 294 (2d Cir. 1908) (fraud exception “does not extend to debts which are not for money or property obtained by the fraud”). The phrase “to the extent obtained by” is a quantitative limit, not a qualitative one. The debtor obtained $31,382.50; that is the maximum nondischargeable sum.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to hold that only the $31,382.50 in overcharges is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A).
