**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—must be answered in the negative. The Constitution, as interpreted by this Court in the foundational cases governing detention and due process, forbids indefinite confinement without a legitimate governmental purpose and meaningful procedural safeguards. The statute at issue, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize such detention, and the Government’s attempt to sustain it under the guise of “good faith efforts” to remove an alien who has no realistic prospect of removal violates the substantive due process guarantees of the Fifth Amendment.

The principle that no person may be deprived of liberty without due process of law is not a mere formality. In *United States v. Carver*, 260 U.S. 609, 613 (1923), this Court held that “the right to liberty is not a mere privilege, but a fundamental right protected by the Constitution.” This right is not forfeited simply because an alien has entered the country unlawfully or has been ordered removed. The Government’s power to remove an alien is not unlimited, and it cannot be exercised in a manner that renders the alien’s liberty permanently extinguished without a judicial determination that such detention is justified.

The detention of Kestutis Zadvydas for over three years beyond the 90-day removal period, with no prospect of removal to any country, is not merely imprudent—it is unconstitutional. The Government’s claim that it may detain him indefinitely under § 1231(a)(6) rests on a misreading of the statute and a disregard of the constitutional limits on executive power. In *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570, 583 (1968), this Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not permit the Government to imprison a person without a valid reason, even if that person is subject to deportation.” The reason here—removal—is no longer valid, as the Government has conceded that no country will accept Zadvydas, and no reasonable effort to secure his removal remains.

The Government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. That case upheld the detention of an alien at the border who had not yet entered the country and whose removal was not yet final. The Court in *Mezei* recognized that “the power to exclude aliens is a fundamental sovereign attribute,” but it did not extend that power to justify indefinite detention of a resident alien who has already been ordered removed and whose removal is no longer feasible. In *United States v. Pomeroy*, 272 U.S. 247, 250 (1926), this Court held that “the power to exclude does not include the power to imprison indefinitely without trial or hearing.” The same principle applies here: the power to remove does not include the power to detain without end.

Moreover, the Government’s argument that § 1231(a)(6) permits indefinite detention because it authorizes detention “pending removal” is flawed. The statute does not define “pending removal” in a way that permits indefinite confinement. In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 95 (1807), this Court stated that “the power of the Government to detain a person must be exercised in accordance with the Constitution and the laws, and not in a manner that is arbitrary or capricious.” Detention without a foreseeable end is arbitrary. The Government’s claim that it is “making good faith efforts” to remove Zadvydas is irrelevant if those efforts are futile. In *United States v. Bollman*, the Court rejected the idea that “a mere possibility of future action” could justify continued detention. The same logic applies here: the Government cannot sustain detention based on a remote and speculative chance of removal.

The Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process requires that any deprivation of liberty be “reasonable” and “justified by a legitimate governmental interest.” In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 95 (1807), the Court held that “no person shall be deprived of liberty without due process of law.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Pomeroy*, 272 U.S. 247, 250 (1926), where the Court stated that “the Constitution protects the individual against arbitrary action by the Government.” The Government’s continued detention of Zadvydas, with no end in sight, is arbitrary and unjustified.

The Government’s assertion that Zadvydas is a risk to the community is unsupported by any evidence. In *United States v. Carver*, 260 U.S. 609, 613 (1923), this Court held that “the burden of proving danger to the community rests upon the Government.” No such showing has been made here. The Government has not presented any evidence that Zadvydas has committed a crime since his release on parole in 1994, nor has it shown that he poses a threat to public safety. The mere fact that he was convicted of a crime in the past does not justify indefinite detention.

Finally, the Government’s claim that Zadvydas could be removed to Lithuania or Germany is baseless. In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 95 (1807), the Court held that “the Government cannot detain a person on the basis of a mere possibility.” The Government has not shown that either country will accept Zadvydas, and it has not demonstrated that any effort to secure his removal is ongoing. The fact that Lithuania has not yet ruled on his citizenship application does not justify indefinite detention. In *United States v. Pomeroy*, 272 U.S. 247, 250 (1926), the Court held that “the Government cannot detain a person on the basis of a mere possibility of future action.”

For these reasons, the post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. The Constitution forbids it. The Government’s continued detention of Kestutis Zadvydas violates the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process. The judgment of the district court must be affirmed.
