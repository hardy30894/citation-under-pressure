**Argument**

The Patent Act’s design-patent remedy is not a license to award a patentee the infringer’s entire profits when the patented design covers only one component of a multi-component product. The statute instead limits recovery to “the extent of [the infringer’s] total profit,” 35 U.S.C. § 289, and that phrase must be read in light of the established principle that damages should compensate only for the value contributed by the patented feature. *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884); *Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 490-91 (1854). Where the asserted design patent applies only to a component, the patentee may recover only the profits attributable to that component, not the profits from the entire finished product. *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10, 17-18 (1886); *Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co.*, 225 U.S. 604, 614-15 (1912).

Section 289 provides that an infringer “shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit, but not less than $250.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. That language does not compel a winner-take-all award untethered to the article of manufacture actually embodying the patented design. The Supreme Court has long held that a patentee seeking profits bears the burden of proving what portion of the defendant’s gains is properly attributable to the infringement. *Garretson*, 111 U.S. at 121; *Westinghouse*, 225 U.S. at 614. In *Garretson*, the Court rejected a damages award based on the whole product where the patent covered only an improvement, explaining that the patentee must “separate or apportion” the infringer’s profits unless the entire value of the product is due to the patented feature. 111 U.S. at 121. That principle applies with special force here, where the design patent at issue covers only a component of a larger smartphone. *Dobson*, 118 U.S. at 17-18.

This Court’s design-patent cases are consistent with that rule. In *Egyptian Goddess, Inc. v. Swisa, Inc.*, 543 F.3d 665, 678 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (en banc), the Court recognized that the ordinary-observer test turns on the design as a whole, not on isolated elements, but that does not mean the infringer’s profits automatically attach to the entire product. *Egyptian Goddess* addressed infringement, not the proper profit base under § 289. Likewise, *Nike, Inc. v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 138 F.3d 1437, 1442-43 (Fed. Cir. 1998), held that the statutory remedy includes total profit from the “article of manufacture” embodying the design, but it did not hold that every consumer product containing the patented design is the relevant article of manufacture. To the contrary, the case law leaves room for identifying the proper article and limiting recovery accordingly. *See* *Samsung Elecs. Co. v. Apple Inc.*, 580 U.S. 53, 62-63 (2016).

The text and history of § 289 confirm that Congress sought to prevent a design pirate from retaining profits earned by use of the patented design, not to award windfalls untethered to the patented component. Congress enacted the design-patent profit remedy after *Dobson* had made it difficult to prove precise apportionment, but the remedy still presupposes that the profits recovered are those of the infringing article of manufacture. *Dobson*, 118 U.S. at 17-18; *Westinghouse*, 225 U.S. at 614-15. The statute’s “article of manufacture” language limits the profit base to the thing to which the design is applied. *See* 35 U.S.C. § 171(a); *Samsung Elecs. Co. v. Apple Inc.*, 580 U.S. at 62-63.

The contrary rule would create irrational and punitive results. A design patent on a small component—such as a portion of a smartphone face, a speaker grille, or a bezel—would automatically entitle the patentee to all profits on the finished device, even when the design contributes only marginally to consumer demand. That is precisely the sort of overcompensation the Supreme Court warned against in *Garretson* and *Westinghouse*. 111 U.S. at 121; 225 U.S. at 614-15. Nothing in § 289 authorizes such a departure from the ordinary rule that remedies should be proportional to the patented contribution.

The Federal Circuit’s en banc decision below does not compel a different result. That court recognized only that the jury verdict was supported by substantial evidence on the record before it. It did not decide the legal question presented here: whether, as a matter of law, design-patent damages must be confined to the component embodying the patented design. The answer is yes. *Garretson*, *Dobson*, and *Westinghouse* require apportionment to the relevant profit base when the patented design does not cover the whole product. Because the design patents here were directed to components of Samsung’s smartphones, any award under § 289 must be limited to the profits attributable to those components, not to Samsung’s entire profits on the finished devices.
