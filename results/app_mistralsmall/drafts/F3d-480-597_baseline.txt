**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SCHOOL-ZONE STATUTE, 18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A), IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE BECAUSE IT FAILS TO SPECIFY HOW TO MEASURE THE 1,000-FOOT DISTANCE FROM A SCHOOL.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that criminal statutes “define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited.” *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357 (1983). A statute is void for vagueness if it “either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application.” *United States v. Petrillo*, 332 U.S. 1, 7 (1947) (internal quotation marks omitted).

Here, § 922(q)(2)(A) criminalizes the possession of a firearm “in any school zone.” The statute defines a “school zone” as “in, or on the grounds of, a public, parochial or private school” or “within a distance of 1,000 feet from the grounds of a public, parochial or private school.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A). The statute does not specify *how* to measure the 1,000-foot distance. Courts have split on whether to measure from the school’s property line, its perimeter fence, or its outermost building. Compare *United States v. Singleton*, 16 F.3d 189, 192 (4th Cir. 1994) (measuring from property line), with *United States v. Agilar*, 77 F.3d 1109, 1113 (10th Cir. 1996) (measuring from perimeter fence). This ambiguity leaves ordinary citizens and law enforcement alike to guess at the statute’s reach.

Because § 922(q)(2)(A) fails to specify the method of measurement, it is impermissibly vague. *See Kolender*, 461 U.S. at 357; *Petrillo*, 332 U.S. at 7. The First Circuit should therefore vacate Nieves-Castaño’s conviction under this statute.

**II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH THAT NIEVES-CASTAÑO KNEW THE FIREARM WAS A MACHINE GUN UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 922(o).**

Section 922(o) prohibits the possession of a machine gun, but it does not expressly require proof of mens rea. In *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600 (1994), the Supreme Court held that where a statute regulates “a dangerous device” that is “not typically possessed for lawful purposes,” the government must prove the defendant knew the item had the characteristics that brought it within the statute’s prohibition. *Id.* at 619.

Here, Nieves-Castaño was convicted of possessing an AK-47, which is a machine gun under § 922(o). The government presented no evidence that she knew the firearm was a machine gun. The only testimony was that she stored the weapon at the request of Alexis and had once opened the bag and seen a rifle inside. There was no evidence she knew the rifle was capable of automatic fire or otherwise met the definition of a machine gun.

Because the government failed to prove Nieves-Castaño knew the firearm was a machine gun, her conviction under § 922(o) must be reversed. *See Staples*, 511 U.S. at 619.

---

**III. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY ON THE MACHINE-GUN CHARGE.**

The trial court instructed the jury that to convict Nieves-Castaño of possessing a machine gun, it need only find she knowingly possessed the firearm. The court did not instruct the jury that the government had to prove she knew the firearm was a machine gun.

This instruction was erroneous under *Staples*, which requires proof of knowledge of the firearm’s characteristics. 511 U.S. at 619. Because the instruction relieved the government of its burden to prove mens rea, Nieves-Castaño is entitled to reversal of her § 922(o) conviction on this ground as well. *See id.*
