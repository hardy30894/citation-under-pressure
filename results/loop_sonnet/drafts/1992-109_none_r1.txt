**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Requires Only the Competency Standard of *Dusky v. United States*, Not a Separate, Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Test.**

This Court has defined the constitutional floor of mental competency in a single, uniform formulation that applies across the criminal process. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that the test for competency is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. *Dusky* did not limit itself to the capacity to stand trial; it announced the constitutional measure of competency itself, without qualification. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), confirmed that due process forbids the conviction of a defendant who lacks that basic capacity, describing the inquiry as whether the defendant possesses "sufficient present ability" to understand and participate in the proceedings against him. *Id.* at 385.

Nothing in this framework changes when a defendant, instead of proceeding to trial, elects to plead guilty or to represent himself. The Ninth Circuit's demand for a distinct "reasoned choice" standard rests on the mistaken premise that the *decision* to waive constitutional rights requires greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But competency doctrine has never asked whether a defendant's choice is wise, or whether it is the choice a rational, counseled defendant would make; it asks only whether he has the capacity to understand and to participate. As this Court explained in *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), competency doctrine exists to protect a defendant from being subjected to proceedings he cannot comprehend—it does not superintend the wisdom of decisions a competent defendant elects to make. *Id.* at 108. Conflating the two inquiries erodes the very autonomy that the right to self-representation and the right to plead guilty are designed to protect.

**II. The Standards Governing Waiver of Counsel and Guilty Pleas Address the Voluntariness and Knowledge of the Waiver, Not a Separate, Elevated Capacity Threshold.**

This Court's waiver decisions confirm that the constitutional inquiry attending a decision to forgo counsel or plead guilty is whether the waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent—not whether the defendant satisfied some heightened capacity beyond *Dusky*. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court described waiver of counsel as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, and directed courts to "indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver," *id.* That test examines the defendant's knowledge and intent respecting his rights—a question distinct from, and independent of, the *Dusky* capacity inquiry. *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required that a waiver of counsel be made "with eyes open," *id.* at 724, so that the defendant understands the risks of self-representation. That is a voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry, not a demand for superior mental capacity.

*Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), confirms that the focus of this inquiry is whether the record shows an informed choice: absence of counsel at arraignment does not vitiate a plea where the record shows it was "entered understandingly." *Id.* at 174–75. And *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), recognized that "the Constitution does not force a lawyer upon a defendant," *id.* at 279, because a defendant may competently elect to conduct his own defense. This Court treated that election as a matter of intelligent waiver, not evidence that self-representation demands a higher order of mental functioning than standing trial. So too *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), which required that the record affirmatively show a guilty plea was voluntary and intelligent because the plea "involves the waiver of several constitutional rights," *id.* at 243—but *Boykin* nowhere suggested that a defendant must clear a threshold of rationality higher than the one that permits him to stand trial in the first place.

Taken together, these decisions demonstrate that this Court has consistently separated two distinct questions: first, whether the defendant has the mental capacity described in *Dusky*; and second, whether, possessing that capacity, he knowingly and voluntarily relinquished a particular right. The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard collapses these two inquiries into one, demanding a level of rationality that this Court's waiver jurisprudence has never required.

**III. *Westbrook v. Arizona* Does Not Establish a Substantive Competency Standard Higher Than *Dusky*.**

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard cannot be traced to *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam). *Westbrook* remanded because the record failed to show that the trial court had determined "whether petitioner had the capacity to waive his constitutional right to the assistance of counsel," *id.* at 150, but the Court articulated no standard distinct from the one already governing competency generally. Nothing in *Westbrook* suggests that waiver of counsel demands a quantum of rationality beyond the "reasonable degree of rational understanding" required by *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402. *Westbrook* stands only for the unremarkable proposition that a court must determine, on an adequate record, whether a valid waiver occurred—a voluntariness question that *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, and *Carter* already answer, not a novel mental-capacity threshold.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard that governs whether a defendant may stand trial, set forth in *Dusky* and *Pate*, likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional inquiries required by *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, *Boykin*, and *Westbrook* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
