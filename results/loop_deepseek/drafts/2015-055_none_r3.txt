**ARGUMENT**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act is unconstitutionally vague—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. A statute that is void for vagueness is void *ab initio*, and due process forbids the imposition of punishment under a law that fails to give fair warning of the conduct it proscribes.

**I. The residual clause was void from its inception, and any sentence imposed under it is invalid.**

It is a fundamental principle of constitutional law that an unconstitutional act is “in legal contemplation, as inoperative as though it had never been passed.” *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 376–377 (1879). Because the residual clause violated the Due Process Clause, it was void at the moment of Welch’s sentencing. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803) (holding that a law repugnant to the Constitution “is void” and that courts “must” disregard it). A sentence enhanced under a void provision is therefore invalid, regardless of when the constitutional defect is finally declared. The residual clause’s invalidity means that Welch’s mandatory minimum of 15 years—predicated on a prior conviction that qualified only under the residual clause—cannot stand.

**II. The void-for-vagueness doctrine is a substantive due process requirement that places conduct beyond the government’s power to punish.**

The Due Process Clause requires that laws “give the person of ordinary intelligence a reasonable opportunity to know what is prohibited.” *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). A statute so vague that it “fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that his contemplated conduct is forbidden” is unconstitutional. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939). This requirement of fair notice is “a protection of the individual against the arbitrary and capricious exercise of the police power.” *Champlin Refining Co. v. Corporation Commission*, 286 U.S. 210, 243 (1932). The residual clause, which required courts to evaluate the “hypothetical risk” of an abstract generic crime, left citizens to “guess at his peril” whether his conduct was criminal. *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 221 (1876). Such a law is void, and it cannot support a conviction or an increased sentence. *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931) (requiring “fair warning” in criminal statutes).

**III. A judicial decision holding a statute void for vagueness is substantive, and therefore applies retroactively.**

A decision that a statute is unconstitutional for vagueness does not create new law; it declares that the law never had any force. This is a substantive ruling, not a procedural one. This Court has held that a judicial interpretation that expands the scope of a criminal statute cannot be applied retroactively, because the defendant must have had “fair warning” of the law’s commands. *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 362–363 (1964). The logic of *Bouie* applies a fortiori here: if a decision cannot enlarge criminal liability retroactively, then a decision that eliminates liability altogether must be applied retroactively. A void statute is “as inoperative as though it had never been passed.” *Siebold*, 100 U.S. at 376–377. Although this Court in *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 625 (1954), declined to give retroactive effect to a vagueness ruling, it did so only because the statute was found not to be void. When a statute is genuinely void, no sentence can be predicated upon it. *Reese*, 92 U.S. at 221.

**IV. Welch’s prior conviction qualifies as a violent felony only under the residual clause, which is void.**

The Eleventh Circuit concluded that Welch’s 1996 Florida strong-arm robbery conviction was a violent felony under the residual clause because it involved force capable of causing physical pain or injury. Welch’s conviction does not qualify under the elements clause, which requires the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force, nor does it fall within any enumerated offense. Thus, Welch’s enhanced sentence rested entirely on the residual clause. Because that clause was void from the start, his sentence is invalid. The lower court’s contrary holding is constitutional error that cannot be countenanced on collateral review.

**V. The lower court erred in denying Welch relief.**

The district court denied Welch’s challenge, and the Eleventh Circuit affirmed, before this Court declared the residual clause unconstitutional. Because the residual clause was void at the time of Welch’s sentencing, the enhanced sentence he received is a nullity. The remedy is to remand for resentencing without the residual clause. This Court should reverse the judgment below and direct that Welch be resentenced consistent with the Constitution.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for resentencing consistent with *Johnson*.
