**ARGUMENT**

Respondent American Dental Association submits that a Title VII plaintiff seeking punitive damages must show conduct that is egregious or outrageous in its own right, not merely that the employer possessed a culpable state of mind. That requirement flows from the common-law pedigree of the terms Congress chose in 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1)—"malice" and "reckless indifference"—terms this Court has long understood to describe conduct of an aggravated character, not a bare mental state divorced from the nature of the act itself.

**I. The Common Law Has Long Confined Punitive Damages to Conduct of an Aggravated, Egregious Character.**

For more than a century before Congress added a punitive-damages remedy to Title VII, this Court described exemplary damages as reserved for wrongs marked by outrage, not simply for the broader class of intentional torts. In *Day v. Woodworth*, the Court explained that a jury awarding such damages looks to "the enormity of [the defendant's] offence rather than the measure of compensation to the plaintiff." 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1852). The focus on the *enormity* of the wrong, rather than solely on the actor's mental state, has been the touchstone of the doctrine ever since.

That principle recurs throughout this Court's nineteenth-century jurisprudence. In *Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms*, the Court held that exemplary damages are unavailable unless the wrongdoer's conduct rises to a level equivalent to an intentional wrong—a formulation measuring the character of the act, not merely whether the actor knew his conduct was unlawful. 91 U.S. 489, 493 (1875). In *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Humes*, the Court treated such damages as penal in nature, analogous to punishment for an offense against the public order, confirming that they are reserved for a narrower category of wrongdoing than ordinary intentional torts. 115 U.S. 512, 521–22 (1885). And in *Barry v. Edmunds*, the Court reiterated that exemplary damages punish and deter conduct the community regards as especially reprehensible, not simply any knowing violation of another's rights. 116 U.S. 550, 562–63 (1886).

This Court applied the same principle where an employer's derivative liability for punitive damages was at issue. In *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice*, the Court cautioned that punitive damages against a corporate defendant must rest on misconduct of an aggravated character fairly attributable to the corporation itself, not merely on the isolated intent of a subordinate employee. 147 U.S. 101, 108–09 (1893). The Court reaffirmed that limitation in *Washington Gas Light Co. v. Lansden*, holding that a corporation should not be punished through exemplary damages for the malice of an agent acting outside the matters entrusted to him by the corporation's own governing authority. 172 U.S. 534, 554–55 (1899). Both decisions confirm that egregiousness of the underlying conduct—and of the corporation's own connection to it—rather than the isolated state of mind of any individual employee, is what warrants the extraordinary remedy of punitive damages.

Even earlier, in *The Amiable Nancy*, Justice Story recognized that exemplary damages redress only conduct of a wanton and outrageous character, declining to impose such damages on principals who had not themselves participated in or ratified their subordinates' aggravated misconduct. 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546, 558–59 (1818). And in *Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore Railroad Co. v. Quigley*, the Court treated actual malice—understood as conduct animated by ill will or wanton disregard, not mere knowledge of wrongfulness—as the necessary predicate for departing from purely compensatory relief. 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202, 213–14 (1858).

**II. Congress Legislated Against This Backdrop, and "Malice" and "Reckless Indifference" Must Carry Their Common-Law Meaning.**

When Congress employs a common-law term without defining it, this Court presumes Congress intended the term's settled common-law meaning. Section 1981a(b)(1) does not rest punitive damages on "intentional discrimination" alone—a standard already required to establish liability—but superimposes the further requirement of "malice" or "reckless indifference." If those words added nothing beyond the intent already necessary for liability, the additional statutory language would be surplusage. Read against *Day*, *Arms*, *Humes*, *Barry*, *Prentice*, *Lansden*, *The Amiable Nancy*, and *Quigley*, "malice" and "reckless indifference" describe conduct outrageous in character—conduct exceeding the ordinary case of discriminatory intent and entering the realm the common law has always reserved for extraordinary punishment.

**III. A Contested, Good-Faith Promotion Decision Does Not Satisfy This Standard.**

The record here reflects, at most, a contested personnel decision—not the wanton or oppressive conduct this Court's precedents require before a jury may be asked to punish, rather than merely remedy, an employer. Absent proof of the egregious disregard for Kolstad's rights that *Day v. Woodworth* and its progeny demand, the courts below correctly declined to submit punitive damages to the jury.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
