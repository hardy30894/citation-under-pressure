**Argument**

The district court erred in denying habeas relief because the Bureau of Prisons exceeded its statutory authority when it treated Roussos’s drug-conspiracy conviction as a “crime of violence” based solely on a sentencing enhancement for firearm possession. Section 3621(e)(2)(B) authorizes a sentence reduction for a prisoner “convicted of a nonviolent offense” who successfully completes drug treatment. 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). Nothing in the statute permits the Bureau to convert a nonviolent drug offense into a violent one because the sentencing court applied a guideline enhancement for weapon possession. See *Reno v. Koray*, 515 U.S. 50, 61 (1995) (courts defer to reasonable Bureau interpretations of ambiguous statutory terms, but only where consistent with the statute).

This Court has already recognized that the dispositive inquiry under § 3621(e)(2)(B) is the nature of the inmate’s offense of conviction, not collateral sentencing factors imported from elsewhere. In *Roussos v. Menifee*, 122 F.3d 159 (3d Cir. 1997), the Court held that the Bureau’s Program Statement could not lawfully treat a § 846 narcotics conspiracy as a “crime of violence” merely because the sentencing judge imposed a firearm enhancement. The Court explained that the Bureau’s approach conflicted with the statutory text because § 3621(e)(2)(B) turns on whether the prisoner was “convicted of a nonviolent offense,” not whether the sentencing court later found facts supporting an enhancement. *Id.* at 163-64. That analysis controls here.

The Bureau’s regulation likewise does not support the denial. The regulation in effect provided that an inmate may be eligible for early release “unless the inmate’s current offense is determined to be a crime of violence as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).” 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 (1995). Section 924(c)(3) defines a crime of violence as a felony that either has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force, or by its nature involves a substantial risk that physical force may be used in committing the offense. 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). A conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846 does not satisfy either prong. It has no element requiring force, and this Court has rejected the Bureau’s attempt to treat it as violent simply because of a guideline enhancement. *Roussos*, 122 F.3d at 163-64; see also *United States v. Amparo*, 68 F.3d 1222, 1226-27 (9th Cir. 1995) (drug trafficking offense is not a crime of violence under the guideline definition absent a force element).

The Bureau attempted to bridge this gap through Program Statement 5162.02, but an internal agency statement cannot override the governing statute or regulation. *Roussos*, 122 F.3d at 164-65; *Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36, 44-45 (1993) (agency commentary or interpretation cannot conflict with controlling law). Program Statement 5162.02 effectively rewrote § 3621(e)(2)(B) by making a weapon enhancement dispositive of violent-offense status. But Congress conditioned eligibility on conviction of a “nonviolent offense,” and the regulation incorporated the statutory definition of “crime of violence” in § 924(c)(3). The Program Statement’s categorical exclusion of all inmates whose drug sentences were enhanced for firearm possession is therefore inconsistent with both. *Roussos*, 122 F.3d at 164-66.

Nor can the Bureau justify its action by invoking deference. Agency interpretations receive deference only when they are reasonable and consistent with the statute. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984); *Lopez v. Davis*, 531 U.S. 230, 240-44 (2001). But an interpretation that substitutes a sentencing factor for the offense of conviction is not a permissible construction of § 3621(e)(2)(B). *Roussos*, 122 F.3d at 164-65. The Bureau’s reliance on a firearm enhancement also creates an irrational distinction between similarly situated inmates: two prisoners convicted of identical drug conspiracies would be treated differently based on post-conviction guideline findings, even though Congress tied eligibility to the conviction itself. That result is incompatible with the statutory scheme. See *United States v. LaBonte*, 520 U.S. 751, 757 (1997) (agency constructions must yield to the statute’s plain meaning).

Finally, the Bureau’s approach undermines the purpose of § 3621(e)(2)(B), which was to provide an incentive for successful completion of residential drug abuse treatment. *Lopez*, 531 U.S. at 241-44. Roussos completed the required 500-hour program. Denying him the statutory incentive because of a firearm enhancement that did not alter the nonviolent character of his offense of conviction defeats the purpose Congress enacted. See *Roussos*, 122 F.3d at 165-66.

Because Roussos was convicted of a nonviolent drug offense and the Bureau’s contrary determination rests on an impermissible Program Statement, the order denying habeas relief should be reversed.
