**ARGUMENT**

The Federal Circuit’s judgment awarding Apple Samsung’s entire profits from the sale of complete smartphones rests on an erroneous construction of 35 U.S.C. § 289. That statute authorizes recovery of “the total profit made by [the infringer] from the manufacture or sale … of the article of manufacture to which [the infringed] design … has been applied.” When the patented design is applied only to a component, the “article of manufacture” is the component itself, and profits must be limited accordingly.

Section 289’s text distinguishes the “article of manufacture” from the end product sold to consumers. The phrase “to which such design … has been applied” limits the profit award to the discrete article bearing the design. This Court has long recognized that a patent covers only what it claims. See, e.g., Aro Mfg. Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co., 365 U.S. 336, 339 (1961) (“the claims made in the patent are the sole measure of the grant”). The same principle governs design patents. See Gorham Mfg. Co. v. White, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 511, 528 (1871) (infringement turns on whether the design is “substantially the same” as that “claimed”).

Precedent under the predecessor statutes confirms that, when a design is applied to a component, profits are apportioned. In Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros., 222 F. 902 (2d Cir. 1915), the Second Circuit held that the infringer’s profits on a piano case alone—not the entire piano—were recoverable because the patented design covered only the case. The court explained that “the article to which the design was applied” was the case, not the finished instrument. Id. at 903. The same rule was applied in Young v. Grand Rapids Refrigerator Co., 268 F. 966 (6th Cir. 1920), limiting recovery to the profits attributable to the infringing refrigerator lid.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule—that the entire smartphone is the only permissible “article of manufacture”—nullifies the statutory language and produces results Congress could not have intended. It would allow a design patent on a single icon or bezel to capture profits from every unpatented feature of a complex product. Such an interpretation conflicts with the foundational requirement that damages be limited to the inventive contribution. See Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 490 (1853) (rejecting “the entire profit” rule as “unreasonable” and “unjust”).

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a determination of the profits, if any, attributable to the components to which Apple’s design patents were applied.
