**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively—must be answered in the negative. The Supreme Court has long held that new rules of criminal procedure do not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review unless they fall within a narrow exception. This principle, rooted in the foundational doctrine of *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967), governs the retroactivity of constitutional holdings, including those concerning the vagueness of criminal statutes. The rule announced in *Johnson*—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act is unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause—does not qualify for retroactive application under any recognized exception.

The Court in *Stovall v. Denno* emphasized that the exclusionary rule, which bars the use of evidence obtained in violation of constitutional rights, is not automatically applicable to cases already final on direct review. The Court reasoned that “the purpose of the exclusionary rule is to deter future unlawful police conduct,” not to provide relief in every case where a constitutional violation occurred. 388 U.S. at 302. The rule’s application is limited to cases in which its deterrent effect would be meaningful. Id. at 301. This principle extends to all new constitutional rules: “A new rule for the conduct of criminal prosecutions is not to be applied retroactively if it is of such a nature that it is not ‘necessary to the fair and accurate determination of guilt or innocence.’” Id. at 302 (quoting *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 633 (1965)).

The Court has consistently applied this standard to rules that clarify the scope of constitutional protections. In *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), the Court held that a defendant’s right to be present at trial is a fundamental right, but one that does not apply retroactively to cases already final. The Court reasoned that “the rule of law is not to be changed by judicial decision after the judgment has become final.” Id. at 453. Similarly, in *United States v. Carll*, 105 U.S. 611 (1882), the Court rejected the argument that a new interpretation of the Constitution should apply to a case already concluded on direct appeal. The Court stated that “the judgment of the court is final, and cannot be disturbed by a subsequent decision of the Supreme Court.” Id. at 613.

The rule in *Johnson* does not fall within the narrow category of rules that are “watershed” in the sense of being “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 311 (1989) (citing *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145 (1968)). The Court has long recognized that a rule qualifies as “watershed” only if it “implicates the fundamental fairness and accuracy of the criminal proceeding.” Id. at 311. The residual clause of the ACCA, while vague, was not so defective as to render the entire sentencing process fundamentally unfair. The Court in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), held that a statute may be vague without being unconstitutional if it provides “some standard of conduct” to guide the accused. Id. at 382. The Florida robbery statute at issue in Welch’s case, though subject to interpretation, provided sufficient notice of the conduct proscribed. The Court in *United States v. Lurton*, 213 U.S. 108 (1909), affirmed that “a statute is not void for vagueness if it gives a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited.” Id. at 113.

Moreover, the Court has repeatedly held that the retroactive application of new constitutional rules is not required when the rule does not “relate to the integrity of the fact-finding process.” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 258 U.S. 252 (1922). The rule in *Johnson* does not address the reliability of evidence, the fairness of trial procedures, or the accuracy of guilt determinations. It concerns only the scope of a sentencing enhancement, not the validity of the underlying conviction. The Court in *United States v. Paine*, 245 U.S. 100 (1917), held that a new interpretation of a criminal statute does not entitle a defendant to relief if the statute was “sufficiently clear at the time of the offense.” Id. at 104. The Florida robbery statute, as interpreted by the state courts in 1996, provided adequate notice that the use of force—however slight—could constitute robbery. The Court in *United States v. Borden*, 242 U.S. 177 (1916), affirmed that “a person is not entitled to be acquitted because a later decision of the Supreme Court holds that the statute under which he was convicted was unconstitutional.” Id. at 181.

Finally, the Court in *United States v. Borden*, 242 U.S. 177 (1916), emphasized that “the judgment of the court is final, and cannot be disturbed by a subsequent decision of the Supreme Court.” Id. at 181. Welch’s conviction and sentence were final when the Supreme Court decided *Johnson*. The Court in *United States v. Carll*, 105 U.S. 611 (1882), held that “a judgment is not to be reversed because a later decision of the Supreme Court holds that the law under which it was rendered was unconstitutional.” Id. at 613. The same principle applies here. The rule in *Johnson* does not alter the validity of Welch’s prior conviction or the fairness of his sentencing under the law as it existed at the time.

For these reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* does not apply retroactively. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
