**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 875(c) Requires Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Statement to Be a Threat**

A criminal statute that punishes speech must be construed to require a culpable mental state. As this Court long ago explained, “[T]he contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion.” *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). The word “threat” in §875(c) denotes an act of communication: a person cannot “threaten” another without intending to communicate a threat. A speaker who transmits words that happen to be understood as threats, but who does not intend them to be so understood, has not committed the act of threatening. The rule of lenity reinforces this reading. “[P]enal laws are to be construed strictly.” *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. 76, 95 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.).

The Third Circuit’s objective standard eliminates that mens rea requirement. Under that standard, liability attaches if a “reasonable speaker” would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat—even if the defendant did not. That is a form of negligence liability for speech. This Court has never permitted such liability under the First Amendment.

**II. The First Amendment Requires a Subjective Intent To Threaten**

This Court’s speech-protective decisions have consistently required subjective intent before expression may be criminally punished. *De Jonge v. Oregon*, 299 U.S. 353, 364 (1937), reversed a conviction under a statute that did not require “a specific intent” to accomplish the prohibited result. In *Yates v. United States*, 354 U.S. 298, 324 (1957), the Court drew a “distinction between advocacy of abstract doctrine and advocacy of action” and required proof of intent to bring about action. *Scales v. United States*, 367 U.S. 203, 229 (1961), held that a criminal membership provision required “a specific intent” to bring about the organization’s unlawful ends. *Noto v. United States*, 367 U.S. 290, 297-98 (1961), explained that “the mere abstract teaching of the moral propriety or even moral necessity for a resort to force and violence is not the same as preparing a group for violent action.” And *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), held that speech may be punished only when it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” Each of these cases confirms that the speaker’s subjective intent is the touchstone for criminal liability.

**III. The Threat Statute Must Be Construed in Light of the First Amendment**

In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), this Court considered a federal statute criminalizing threats against the President. The Court agreed “that the statute initially requires a ‘true threat’” and reversed the conviction because the government had not proven that the statement was anything more than political hyperbole. *Watts* necessarily presumes that the speaker’s intent distinguishes a true threat from protected expression. A statement that is not intended as a threat—even if a reasonable listener would fear violence—is not a “true threat” and lies outside the statute.

The Third Circuit’s competing rationale—deterring fear of violence—cannot override the First Amendment. *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 153 (1959), invalidated an ordinance that punished a bookseller without proof of knowledge of the contents, reasoning that strict liability would “tend to impose a severe limitation on the public’s access to constitutionally protected matter.” If a bookseller may not be punished absent knowledge of the book’s contents, a speaker may not be punished absent knowledge that his words constitute a threat. The objective standard chills protected speech precisely as *Smith* condemned: a speaker must self-censor any expression that a reasonable person might misapprehend as a threat, including rap lyrics, satire, and political commentary.

The First Amendment demands laws regulating speech be precise. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 311 (1940). The only precise standard is subjective intent. An objective standard leaves a speaker to guess how his words will be perceived and punishes him for failing to predict that guess. Speech may be abridged only when it creates “a clear and present danger of a serious substantive evil.” *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949). A speaker who does not intend his words as threats presents no such danger.

Because the district court instructed the jury under an objective standard, and because the statute requires subjective intent, the conviction must be reversed.
