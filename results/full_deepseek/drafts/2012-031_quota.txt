## ARGUMENT

Class certification under Rule 23(b)(3) demands more than a plausible theory; it demands admissible evidence, subjected to rigorous analysis, that damages are capable of proof on a class-wide basis. The Third Circuit affirmed a certification order that rested on a damages model untethered to the sole antitrust theory remaining in the case. That was error under Rule 23, *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338 (2011), and this Court’s longstanding precedents.

### I. The District Court Failed To Ensure That Damages Could Be Proved On a Class-Wide Basis Through Common Evidence

A district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” to ensure that Rule 23’s requirements are actually satisfied. *Gen. Tel. Co. of the Sw. v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982). That “rigorous analysis” will frequently “entail some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff’s underlying claim.” *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 351. The court may not simply accept the plaintiff’s expert’s say-so; it must resolve disputes among experts where resolution is necessary to the certification question. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 323–24 (3d Cir. 2008). And any expert methodology must satisfy *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), because only admissible evidence can carry the proponent’s burden. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 323.

Here, the District Court narrowed the class’s antitrust theory to a single claim: that Comcast’s clustering deterred the entry of “overbuilders.” Yet the damages model on which the court relied—created by Dr. James McClave—measured only the difference in cable prices between the Philadelphia DMA and other geographic markets. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 186–87 (E.D. Pa. 2010). That model did not attempt to isolate any price effect attributable to deterred overbuilders from the price effects of Comcast’s other alleged conduct, such as the challenged swaps and acquisitions. The model thus purported to quantify damages caused by all of Comcast’s alleged anticompetitive conduct, not just the sole surviving theory. A damages model that cannot distinguish between lawful and unlawful causes of harm fails to provide a common method for measuring class-wide damages. As this Court long ago held, damages must be proved with a “reasonable basis” and cannot rest on speculation. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946); *see also Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100, 123–24 (1969).

The District Court’s own findings underscored the model’s disconnect. The court found that McClave’s model “did not measure the impact of overbuilder deterrence specifically” and that his regression did not “isolate the effect of the challenged conduct.” *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 186–87. Nevertheless, the court certified the class because it believed McClave’s methodology could be used to calculate damages attributable to the overbuilder theory. That was a legal error: a model that does not match the liability theory cannot supply common evidence of damages. A class action cannot proceed where the sole damages proof treats as fungible a harm the plaintiff no longer asserts and the harm actually at issue.

### II. The Third Circuit Applied an Improperly Deferential Standard and Excused the District Court’s Failure To Perform Its Gatekeeping Role

The Third Circuit affirmed under the clearly-erroneous standard, reasoning that the District Court’s findings were plausible in light of the record. That approach misapprehends the certification inquiry. Rule 23 requires the district court to make findings of fact based on admissible evidence, but the *legal* question—whether the evidence, taken as true, satisfies the predominance requirement—is reviewed *de novo*. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 312; *see also Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350–51. The Third Circuit’s rejection of Comcast’s challenge to McClave’s model was not a credibility determination; it was a failure to demand that the model correspond to the operative theory. That is an error of law.

The Third Circuit also misstated the burden. It held that the plaintiffs had shown a “common methodology” to quantify damages, but a methodology that fails to connect to the surviving theory is not a common methodology at all. Certification must be supported by “evidentiary submissions” that “prove” the rule’s requirements. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350. The burden is on the plaintiff, and it is “more than a mere pleading standard.” *Falcon*, 457 U.S. at 160–61. The District Court’s acceptance of an expert model it itself found inapposite cannot satisfy that burden.

This Court has rejected precisely this kind of public-policy-based loosening of Rule 23. In *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177–78 (1974), the Court held that a district court may not skip the rigorous inquiry required by the Rule. And in *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007), the Court stressed that antitrust plaintiffs must plead a plausible claim; class-wide damages must meet an equally rigorous standard. A district court cannot certify a class merely because an expert offers a number; the number must be tethered to the legal theory.

Finally, the Third Circuit’s deferential review allowed the District Court to delegate its gatekeeping obligation to the plaintiffs’ expert. That is incompatible with this Court’s directive that district courts must “probe behind the pleadings” before certifying. *Falcon*, 457 U.S. at 160. Because the District Court’s certification was not supported by admissible evidence that damages could be measured class-wide, the Third Circuit’s judgment should be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
