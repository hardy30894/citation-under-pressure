**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Dismissal for Lack of Jurisdiction Is a Final Decision Subject to De Novo Review in District Court.**

A decision by the Merit Systems Protection Board (Board) dismissing an appeal for lack of jurisdiction is a final decision that must be reviewed in a federal district court. The Supreme Court has long held that questions of jurisdiction are not subject to the discretion of the agency and are to be decided by the reviewing court. *United States v. Wunderlich*, 342 U.S. 98, 101-02 (1951). Here, the Board dismissed petitioner’s appeal because it found the retirement and suspension to be voluntary, concluding it lacked jurisdiction to hear the case. This jurisdictional determination is a final decision that should be reviewed in the district court, where the statute provides for review of “cases of discrimination.” 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). The Federal Circuit’s exclusive jurisdiction over Board decisions is limited to those that have been decided on the merits, not those that have been dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.

**II. The Statutory Scheme Favors District Court Review for Mixed Cases, Including Those Dismissed for Lack of Jurisdiction.**

The statute granting district court jurisdiction to “cases of discrimination” was enacted to ensure that employees alleging discrimination have access to a forum with the authority to apply the full range of civil rights remedies. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). The purpose of the statute is to provide a comprehensive judicial review of discrimination claims, not to limit such review to the specialized remedies available before the Board. When the Board dismisses an appeal for lack of jurisdiction, the employee is left with a discrimination claim that has not been adjudicated. The district court is the proper forum to address this claim, as it is the court of general jurisdiction best suited to determine whether the employee was affected by an action that may be appealed to the Board and whether that action was based on discrimination.

**III. The Distinction Between Procedural and Jurisdictional Dismissals Is Artificial and Undermines the Statute’s Purpose.**

The distinction drawn by the D.C. Circuit between procedural dismissals, which go to district court, and jurisdictional dismissals, which go to the Federal Circuit, is artificial and creates a “forum lottery.” *United States v. Morgan*, 298 U.S. 468, 475 (1936). Both types of dismissals result in the Board refusing to hear the case, leaving the employee to seek judicial review. The statute’s language is clear: “cases of discrimination” shall be filed in district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). A dismissal for lack of jurisdiction based on voluntariness is inextricably linked to the merits of a discrimination claim, as the employee must prove the agreement was involuntary to establish a violation of anti-discrimination laws. Therefore, the case should be reviewed in district court, where the court can evaluate the totality of the circumstances to determine whether the employee was subjected to discrimination.
