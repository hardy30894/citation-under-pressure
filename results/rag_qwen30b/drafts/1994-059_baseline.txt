**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether states may alter the qualifications for membership in the United States Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution—must be answered in the negative. The Constitution’s text, structure, and precedent establish that Congress is a national institution whose membership is governed by uniform, federal standards. States may not impose additional qualifications on candidates for federal office, including term limits, because such restrictions would violate the exclusive and comprehensive framework established by Article I, Sections 2 and 3 of the Constitution.

The Constitution explicitly defines the qualifications for members of the House of Representatives and the Senate. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that to be a Representative, a person must be “at least twenty-five Years of Age, and a Citizen of the United States for seven Years, and who shall have been an Inhabitant of the State he shall be chosen for at the Time of his Election.” Article I, Section 3, Clause 3 requires that a Senator be “at least thirty Years of Age, a Citizen of the United States for nine Years, and who shall have been an Inhabitant of the State he shall be chosen for at the Time of his Election.” These are the *only* qualifications for federal office set forth in the Constitution. As this Court held in *Wiley v. Sinkler*, 179 U.S. 58, 65 (1900), “the office, if it be properly called an office, is created by that Constitution and by that alone.” The Constitution does not permit states to add to or subtract from these qualifications.

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment attempts to do precisely that. By barring individuals who have served three or more terms in the House or two or more terms in the Senate from appearing on the ballot, Arkansas imposes a *new* qualification for federal office—one not found in the Constitution. This is not a mere regulation of elections or ballot access; it is a direct restriction on eligibility to hold office. The amendment’s effect is to disqualify otherwise qualified candidates based on their past service, a power the Constitution reserves exclusively to Congress. As this Court stated in *Oregon v. Mitchell*, 400 U.S. 112, 125 (1970), “the power of Congress to make or alter such regulations [of elections] is augmented by the Necessary and Proper Clause.” The power to regulate congressional elections, including the qualifications of candidates, is a federal power, not a state one.

The dissent’s argument that the amendment merely affects ballot access is legally untenable. The distinction between disqualification and ballot access is a distinction without a difference when the effect is the same: a candidate is barred from running. The amendment does not merely prevent a candidate from being listed on the ballot; it *prohibits* their name from appearing at all. This is not a procedural rule—it is a substantive bar to candidacy. The Court has long recognized that “the right to vote for a member of Congress is not dependent upon the Constitution or laws of the United States, but is governed by the law of each State respectively,” *Wiley v. Sinkler*, 179 U.S. at 65, but only insofar as the state law does not conflict with the Constitution’s *enumerated* qualifications. The state may not impose *additional* qualifications, such as term limits, because that would undermine the uniformity and national character of Congress.

The Framers rejected term limits for Congress. The debates in the Constitutional Convention show that the idea was considered and rejected. The structure of the Constitution—bicameralism, separation of powers, and the method of election—was designed to ensure stability and continuity in national governance. As this Court observed in *Hawke v. Smith (No. 1)*, 253 U.S. 221, 237 (1920), “the Constitution is a living instrument, and its provisions must be interpreted in the light of the purposes for which it was adopted.” The purpose of the Framers was to create a national legislature with enduring legitimacy, not one subject to the whims of state-level popular referenda. To allow states to impose term limits would create a patchwork of qualifications across the states, undermining the uniformity required by Article I.

Moreover, the amendment’s severability is irrelevant. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the amendment’s provisions were inextricably linked. The Preamble refers to “elected officials” in the singular, and the ballot language presented the entire amendment as a single package. The voters were not given a choice between state and federal term limits. As the court noted, “the two groups were not only inextricably linked—they were systemically fused in such a manner that each ceased to have a separate existence for voting purposes.” This is not a case where a single provision can be severed without altering the nature of the measure. The amendment was designed as a unified package, and its validity cannot be salvaged by a severability clause.

Finally, the argument that the amendment is a valid exercise of the people’s initiative power fails. The initiative power is not unlimited. The people of a state may not use their constitutional authority to alter the qualifications for federal office. The Constitution is supreme, and no state may by popular vote override its provisions. As this Court held in *Newberry v. United States*, 256 U.S. 232, 257 (1921), “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land, and no state can by its own action impair its provisions.” Arkansas cannot, through a constitutional amendment, impose a term limit on members of Congress. That power belongs to Congress alone, and only through the amendment process set forth in Article V.

For these reasons, the Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment is unconstitutional. It violates the exclusive qualifications for federal office established by the Constitution. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court must be affirmed.
