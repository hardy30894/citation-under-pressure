**ARGUMENT**

I. ARIZONA’S LIMITATION ON THE USE OF EXPERT EVIDENCE DID NOT VIOLATE CLARK’S DUE PROCESS RIGHTS

A. Due Process Does Not Confer a Right to Present Expert Evidence of Mental State to Negate Mens Rea Separate from an Insanity Defense

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause does not guarantee a defendant an unfettered right to present expert testimony about his mental state to rebut the prosecution’s evidence of criminal intent. *See Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952) (holding that due process does not require a state to adopt any particular rule of evidence or burden of proof in criminal trials). Arizona’s rule restricting such evidence to the insanity defense is a permissible exercise of its authority to define defenses and evidentiary rules. *See Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201-02 (1977) (upholding a state’s allocation of burdens of proof in criminal cases as a matter of due process).

Clark’s reliance on *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44 (1987), is misplaced. In *Rock*, the Supreme Court held that a state’s per se rule excluding all hypnotically refreshed testimony violated due process. *Id.* at 56-57. Unlike the absolute exclusion in *Rock*, Arizona’s rule does not categorically bar all evidence of mental state; it merely confines expert testimony about mental disease or defect to the insanity defense. *See Clark v. Arizona*, 206 Ariz. 567, 573 (2004) (explaining that the rule permits expert testimony on mental state only for the insanity defense). This distinction is critical: *Rock* addressed a blanket evidentiary exclusion, whereas Arizona’s rule is a targeted limitation tied to a specific defense. *See Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 43 (1996) (distinguishing rules that broadly exclude evidence from those that regulate its admissibility in a particular context).

B. Arizona’s Rule Comports with the Principle That States May Define the Scope of Criminal Defenses

Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert evidence is consistent with the principle that states have broad discretion to define the elements of crimes and the defenses thereto. *See United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 510-11 (1995) (recognizing that states define the elements of offenses). The Supreme Court has repeatedly deferred to state legislatures in structuring criminal defenses and evidentiary rules. *See, e.g., Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 201-02; *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798. Arizona’s rule does not offend due process because it does not eliminate an essential element of the offense or arbitrarily exclude evidence material to guilt or innocence. *See Estelle v. McGuire*, 502 U.S. 62, 69 (1991) (holding that due process is violated only where evidence is “fundamental to the accused’s defense”).

Clark’s argument that the rule violates due process by preventing him from fully presenting his mental state to the jury misapprehends the nature of the limitation. *See Clark*, 206 Ariz. at 573 (noting that the rule does not bar all evidence of mental state, only its use to negate mens rea). The Supreme Court has rejected the notion that due process requires states to permit defendants to introduce evidence of mental state to negate mens rea where the state has chosen to define insanity as the exclusive vehicle for such evidence. *See Shannon v. United States*, 512 U.S. 573, 583 (1994) (upholding a federal statute that confined evidence of mental disease or defect to the insanity defense).

II. ARIZONA’S RULE DOES NOT IMPERMISSIBLY INFRINGE ON CLARK’S RIGHT TO PRESENT A DEFENSE

A. The Right to Present a Defense Is Not Absolute

While the Due Process Clause protects a defendant’s right to present a defense, *see Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967), this right is not without limits. *See Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 43. Arizona’s rule does not prevent Clark from presenting any defense; it merely channels expert evidence of mental disease or defect into the insanity defense. *See Clark*, 206 Ariz. at 573. This is a permissible regulation of the scope and presentation of a defense, not an outright denial. *See, e.g., United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998) (upholding a military rule excluding polygraph evidence despite its potential relevance).

B. The Rule Is Rationally Related to Arizona’s Interest in Defining the Insanity Defense

Arizona has a legitimate interest in defining the scope of the insanity defense and ensuring that expert testimony about mental disease or defect is used consistently with that defense. *See Clark*, 206 Ariz. at 573. The state’s rule advances this interest by preventing jurors from being misled by expert testimony that could improperly conflate the concepts of insanity and mens rea. *See Shannon*, 512 U.S. at 583. The Supreme Court has recognized that states may tailor evidentiary rules to prevent confusion in the minds of jurors. *See, e.g., Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554, 560-61 (1967) (upholding a rule permitting introduction of prior convictions to impeach credibility despite potential prejudice).

III. THE ARIZONA SUPREME COURT’S DECISION DOES NOT CONFLICT WITH FEDERAL PRECEDENTS

The Arizona Supreme Court’s decision is consistent with federal due process jurisprudence. *See Clark*, 206 Ariz. at 573. The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld state evidentiary rules that restrict the use of expert testimony to specific defenses or contexts. *See, e.g., Scheffer*, 523 U.S. at 308; *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 43. Arizona’s rule is no different: it is a targeted limitation designed to ensure that expert evidence of mental disease or defect is used only in the context of the insanity defense, as defined by state law. *See Clark*, 206 Ariz. at 573.

**CONCLUSION**

Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert evidence of mental state does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause. The rule is a permissible exercise of the state’s authority to define defenses and evidentiary rules, and it does not impermissibly infringe on Clark’s right to present a defense. The Arizona Supreme Court’s decision should be affirmed.
