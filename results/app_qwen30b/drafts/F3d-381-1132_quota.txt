**ARGUMENT**

The United States Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government and denying Tuthill Ranch, Inc.’s motion for summary judgment on its claim of a per se physical taking under the Fifth Amendment. The undisputed facts establish that the government’s installation of fiber optic cables—far exceeding its current operational needs and leased to for-profit telecommunications companies—constitutes a permanent physical occupation of Tuthill’s land, which is a per se taking under established constitutional law. The Court of Federal Claims erroneously concluded that the scope of the easement governs the takings analysis and that mere physical presence of cables, even when leased to third parties, cannot amount to a taking. This misapplication of precedent violates binding Supreme Court authority and renders the government’s actions immune from constitutional scrutiny despite their profound and permanent impact on Tuthill’s property rights.

**I. A Permanent Physical Occupation of Land Constitutes a Per Se Taking, Regardless of the Easement’s Terms**

The Fifth Amendment’s Takings Clause provides that private property shall not be taken for public use without just compensation. *Kelo v. City of New London*, 545 U.S. 469, 485 (2005). The Supreme Court has long recognized that a “permanent physical occupation” of private property by the government is a per se taking, requiring compensation regardless of the government’s motive or the property owner’s prior consent. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 435 (1982). In *Loretto*, the Court held that the installation of a cable television antenna on a landlord’s building constituted a taking, even though the government had authorized the installation under a regulatory scheme. The Court emphasized that “the physical invasion must be permanent,” and that “the presence of the cable is not temporary or incidental.” Id. at 436–37. The Court further stated that “the fact that the government has authorized the invasion does not alter the constitutional analysis.” Id. at 437.

Here, the BPA permanently installed fiber optic cables across Tuthill’s property—cables that are not only physically present but are now leased to third parties for commercial telecommunications. The cables remain in place, and their presence is not temporary or incidental. The government’s assertion that the easement permits the installation is irrelevant under *Loretto*. As the Court explained, “the Constitution does not permit the government to take property for public use, even with the owner’s consent, without just compensation.” Id. at 435. The mere existence of an easement does not immunize the government from the constitutional requirement of compensation when the occupation exceeds the scope of what is reasonably necessary for the easement’s purpose.

**II. The Government’s Overbuilding and Third-Party Leasing Constitutes a Physical Occupation Beyond the Easement’s Scope**

Tuthill’s claim is not based on a breach of contract or tortious misuse of an easement, but on a constitutional taking. The Court of Federal Claims erred by conflating property law remedies with constitutional takings. *See Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council*, 505 U.S. 1003, 1015 (1992) (“The Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of just compensation applies when the government takes property, regardless of whether the taking is accomplished through regulation, eminent domain, or otherwise.”). The government’s decision to install 36-fiber cables—far exceeding its current needs—was not a mere “upgrade” but a permanent overbuilding that transformed the nature of the occupation.

The Supreme Court has recognized that a taking may occur when the government’s use of property “goes beyond what is reasonably necessary” for the purpose of the easement. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 264 (1946) (holding that the government’s use of airspace for low-flying military aircraft constituted a taking). In *Causby*, the Court emphasized that “the right to use the air above the land is not absolute,” but that “the government may not use the air above the land in such a way as to interfere with the use and enjoyment of the land.” Id. at 265. Here, the government’s installation of fiber optic cables—now leased to for-profit entities—constitutes a permanent, non-essential use of Tuthill’s land that interferes with Tuthill’s exclusive control over its property. The cables are not merely “appurtenant” to the power lines; they are now a commercial asset generating revenue for the government and third parties.

**III. The Government’s Leasing of Excess Capacity to Third Parties Constitutes a Physical Occupation**

The Court of Federal Claims dismissed Tuthill’s claim on the ground that “no additional physical intrusion” occurred when the BPA leased unused fibers. This reasoning is legally unsound. The presence of the cables themselves is the physical intrusion. *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 436. The fact that the government leases the unused capacity to third parties does not negate the permanence or the physical nature of the occupation. *See United States v. 50.1 Acres of Land*, 530 U.S. 466, 475 (2000) (holding that the government’s use of land for a military installation, even if partially leased to private entities, constituted a taking). The Supreme Court has consistently held that the government cannot avoid constitutional liability by delegating the use of its physical occupation to private actors. *See *Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff*, 467 U.S. 229, 242 (1984) (noting that “the Constitution does not permit the government to circumvent its obligations by using private parties to effect a taking”).
