**ARGUMENT**

The text of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) is unambiguous: after coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings conclude, the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall remand" each transferred action to its originating district. The district court’s invocation of § 1404(a) to assign this case to itself for trial not only defied that clear statutory command but also usurped a power reserved exclusively to the Panel. This Court must reverse.

**I. Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand After Pretrial Proceedings; Section 1404(a) Cannot Override That Command.**

The Multidistrict Litigation statute was enacted to centralize only *pretrial* proceedings. Congress limited the transferee court’s authority to those proceedings, and required mandatory remand before trial. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The statute’s plain language admits no exception. "Remand" is not optional; the Panel *shall* remand. *Cf. Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 58 (1949) (interpreting "shall" as mandatory in venue statute). The district court’s use of § 1404(a) to reassign the case to itself for trial directly contravenes that mandate.

Long before Congress enacted § 1407, this Court recognized that venue statutes must be strictly construed to respect plaintiffs’ choice of forum. *Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert*, 330 U.S. 501, 508 (1947). Section 1404(a) is a codification of the *forum non conveniens* doctrine, *Norwood v. Kirkpatrick*, 349 U.S. 29, 32 (1955), and it permits transfer only "to any other district or division where it might have been brought." But § 1404(a) operates within the ordinary venue framework; it does not authorize a transferee court to *retain* a case that must be remanded under § 1407. The lower court’s reasoning that § 1404(a) can be used during pretrial proceedings wrongly conflates two distinct statutory schemes.

**II. The Transferee Court Lacked Subject-Matter Jurisdiction to Entertain a § 1404(a) Motion That Would Circumvent the Panel’s Exclusive Remand Authority.**

The Judicial Panel’s role is not merely administrative. Section 1407(a) vests the Panel with exclusive authority to remand cases. *United States v. Stone & Downer Co.*, 274 U.S. 225, 239 (1927) (statutory scheme that vests exclusive authority in one body precludes others from exercising that power). When the district court transferred this case to itself under § 1404(a), it effectively rewrote the Panel’s remand order—which never existed—and nullified the Panel’s statutory responsibility.

The Multidistrict Panel’s own rules cannot authorize what the statute forbids. *Cf. Sibbach v. Wilson & Co.*, 312 U.S. 1, 10 (1941) (procedural rules must be consistent with Acts of Congress). Rule 14(b) of the Panel’s Rules, which purports to allow a transferee court to transfer an action to itself for trial under § 1404(a), is ultra vires. Congress alone defines the scope of judicial authority; a procedural rule cannot expand it. *Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938).

**III. The Lower Courts’ Interpretation Conflicts with the Statutory Purpose and Historical Practice.**

Section 1407 was designed to avoid the inefficiency of duplicative discovery across multiple districts, not to rewrite venue rules for trial. *H.R. Rep. No. 90-1130*, at 2 (1968). Nothing in the legislative history suggests that transferee courts should retain cases for trial. Indeed, Congress explicitly rejected proposals that would have granted the Panel permanent transfer authority. *See* 114 Cong. Rec. 14,688 (1968). The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), is misplaced; both decisions postdate § 1407’s enactment and ignore the statute’s plain language. This Court has consistently held that when statutory text is clear, courts must follow it. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

Moreover, the practice of retaining cases undermines a plaintiff’s fundamental right to choose the forum, a right this Court has long protected. *Gulf Oil Corp.*, 330 U.S. at 508. Lexecon chose the Northern District of Illinois. Forcing it to trial in Arizona—where it was sent only for pretrial proceedings—violates that choice.

**IV. A Direct Appeal Is an Inadequate Remedy; Mandamus Should Issue.**

The Ninth Circuit denied mandamus because Lexecon could appeal after trial. But forcing a party to endure the expense and inconvenience of an unauthorized trial is precisely the type of injury mandamus was designed to prevent. *Ex parte Fahey*, 332 U.S. 258, 260 (1947) (mandamus appropriate to confine lower court to lawful exercise of its jurisdiction). The error here is "clear and indisputable," *Bankers Life & Cas. Co. v. Holland*, 346 U.S. 379, 384 (1953), and will recur unless this Court intervenes.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed. The district court exceeded its authority, and the case must be remanded to the Northern District of Illinois.
