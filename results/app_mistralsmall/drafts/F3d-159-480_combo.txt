**ARGUMENT**

**I. CONGRESS LACKED CONSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY TO ENACT 18 U.S.C. § 1951**

Appellant Dominic Pearson challenges the constitutionality of § 1951 of Title 18, the Hobbs Act, arguing that Congress exceeded its enumerated powers in enacting the statute. The Hobbs Act criminalizes interference with interstate commerce by robbery or extortion. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 1951(a). Appellant contends that § 1951 is an unconstitutional exercise of congressional power under the Commerce Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3.

Congress may regulate activities that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce. *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111, 125 (1942). However, the Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that this power is not unlimited. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995) (overruling *Wickard* in part), is inapplicable here because it was decided after the conduct at issue. Instead, this Court must apply pre-1970 precedent, which upheld broad congressional authority under the Commerce Clause.

In *Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States*, 379 U.S. 241 (1964), the Supreme Court upheld Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which prohibited racial discrimination in places of public accommodation. The Court held that Congress could regulate such discrimination because it had a substantial and harmful effect on interstate commerce. *Id.* at 258. The Court reasoned that discrimination discouraged African Americans from traveling and thus impeded interstate commerce. *Id.* Similarly, § 1951 targets conduct—robbery—that directly disrupts commercial activity, including the flow of goods and services across state lines. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 1951(a).

The Supreme Court has also upheld federal criminal statutes that regulate local activities with interstate effects. In *Perez v. United States*, 402 U.S. 146 (1971), the Court upheld the constitutionality of the Consumer Credit Protection Act, which criminalized extortionate credit transactions. Although *Perez* was decided after 1970, earlier cases such as *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), and *United States v. Wrightwood Dairy Co.*, 315 U.S. 110 (1942), established that Congress may regulate local activities that burden interstate commerce. *Darby* upheld the Fair Labor Standards Act, which regulated wages and hours of workers producing goods for interstate commerce. *Darby*, 312 U.S. at 119-20. *Wrightwood Dairy* upheld federal regulation of intrastate milk prices that affected interstate commerce. *Wrightwood Dairy*, 315 U.S. at 118-19.

Here, the robbery at Mr. Goodcents disrupted a business that purchased goods from out-of-state suppliers and served customers who traveled across state lines. *See* R. Vol. I at 120 (testimony of Steve Peterson). The stolen money would have been used to purchase goods produced outside Kansas. *Id.* Thus, the robbery had a substantial effect on interstate commerce, justifying Congress’s exercise of its Commerce Clause power under *Heart of Atlanta Motel* and *Darby*.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently deferred to Congress’s judgment in enacting criminal statutes to protect interstate commerce. In *Brooklyn Sav. Bank v. O’Neil*, 324 U.S. 697 (1945), the Court upheld the Fair Labor Standards Act’s criminal penalties for wage violations, emphasizing Congress’s broad authority to protect interstate commerce. *Id.* at 704-05. Similarly, § 1951’s penalties are a permissible means of protecting interstate commerce from the harmful effects of robbery.

Appellant’s reliance on *United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1 (1936), is misplaced. In *Butler*, the Court struck down the Agricultural Adjustment Act as an unconstitutional exercise of the taxing and spending power because it regulated production, not commerce. *Id.* at 63-79. Unlike the statute in *Butler*, § 1951 regulates robbery, which is inherently commercial in nature and directly interferes with interstate commerce. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 1951(a).

Finally, the Supreme Court has upheld the constitutionality of the Hobbs Act itself. In *United States v. Culbert*, 435 U.S. 371 (1978), the Court rejected a Commerce Clause challenge to § 1951, holding that the statute regulates activities that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce. Although *Culbert* was decided after 1970, earlier cases such as *Heart of Atlanta Motel* and *Darby* support the same conclusion. *See also Perez v. United States*, 402 U.S. 146 (1971) (upholding federal criminal statute regulating local activity with interstate effects).

In sum, Congress had the constitutional authority to enact § 1951 under the Commerce Clause. The statute regulates conduct that substantially affects interstate commerce, and this Court should uphold its constitutionality.
