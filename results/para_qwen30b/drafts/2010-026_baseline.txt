**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard, and its application of Exemption 2 to non-law enforcement, non-regulatory safety data, constitutes a fundamental misreading of the statute, a departure from binding precedent, and a distortion of the narrow scope of FOIA exemptions. This Court should reverse.

**I. The Ninth Circuit’s “Predominantly Internal” Standard Expands Exemption 2 Beyond Its Statutory Bounds**

Exemption 2 protects only those materials “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has made clear that FOIA exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and “must be narrowly construed.” *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151, 109 S.Ct. 2841, 106 L.Ed.2d 112 (1989); *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361, 96 S.Ct. 1592. The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the “predominantly internal” standard—borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981)—transforms this narrow exemption into a broad, open-ended exception that threatens to swallow the rule of public access.

The D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test, which the Ninth Circuit now endorses, permits agencies to withhold any information that is “predominantly” used internally, regardless of whether it relates to actual personnel practices or regulatory compliance. But the statute does not speak in terms of “predominance” or “internal use.” It speaks of materials “related solely” to “internal personnel rules and practices.” The phrase “solely” is critical: it excludes information that serves any external purpose, even if it is also used internally. *See* *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 363, 96 S.Ct. 1592 (noting that Exemption 2 applies only to “matters that are not of genuine and significant public interest” and are “related solely” to internal operations).

The ESQD arc maps at issue here are not internal personnel rules or practices. They are technical safety calculations used to determine safe storage distances for explosives. As Commander Whitbred himself stated, the maps are used “to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” This is not personnel management; it is engineering and operational planning. The maps are not internal guidelines for Navy employees on how to perform their jobs—they are technical tools for physical safety planning. The Navy does not use them to train personnel, discipline employees, or manage internal workflows. They are not “rules” or “practices” in any meaningful sense of the term.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision effectively redefines “internal personnel rules and practices” to include any technical data used in agency operations, so long as it is not publicly disclosed. This expansion is not only inconsistent with the statutory language but also with the Supreme Court’s repeated insistence that exemptions must be narrowly construed. *See* *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151, 109 S.Ct. 2841 (“The exemptions are not to be expanded by judicial interpretation.”).

**II. The ESQD Maps Are Not “Law Enforcement” Materials, and Exemption 2 Does Not Apply to Non-Law Enforcement Safety Data**

The Ninth Circuit’s opinion repeatedly conflates Exemption 2 with law enforcement materials, suggesting that any information used in safety planning qualifies as “law enforcement” under the exemption. This is incorrect. *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), the Ninth Circuit’s foundational case on Exemption 2, held that law enforcement materials—such as raid manuals—may be exempt because their disclosure risks circumvention of the law. But *Hardy* did not hold that all safety or operational data is exempt. It held that *law enforcement* materials are exempt *if* they are “related solely” to internal personnel rules and practices and pose a risk of circumvention.

The ESQD maps are not law enforcement materials. They are not compiled for the purpose of investigating, prosecuting, or adjudicating violations of law. They are not used in enforcement actions. As the dissent correctly notes, the maps are used for “risk mitigation and enhanced safety,” not for “adjudicative or enforcement purposes.” *Church of Scientology v. IRS*, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (9th Cir. 1979). The Navy’s own affidavit confirms this: the maps are used to “design, array, and construct” storage facilities. This is engineering, not law enforcement.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision to apply Exemption 2 to non-law enforcement safety data undermines the carefully balanced structure of FOIA. If safety data used in operational planning can be withheld under Exemption 2, then any technical document used internally—regardless of its purpose—can be shielded from public view. This would allow agencies to withhold virtually any technical or operational information under the guise of “internal use,” effectively nullifying the public’s right to know.

**III. The Risk of Circumvention Is Speculative and Not Supported by the Record**

Even assuming the maps are “internal,” the Navy has failed to demonstrate a *real* risk of circumvention. The agency’s claim rests on the speculative possibility that a malicious actor could “reverse engineer” the maps to determine the types and quantities of explosives stored at NMII. But this is not a risk of circumvention of regulation—it is a hypothetical threat of intelligence gathering.
