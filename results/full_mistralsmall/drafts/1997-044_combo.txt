**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Code’s Discharge Provision Must Be Construed Narrowly to Preserve the “Fresh Start” Policy for Honest Debtors.**

The Bankruptcy Code’s discharge provisions are to be narrowly construed in favor of the debtor to effectuate the Code’s “fresh start” policy. *Strang v. Bradner,* 114 U.S. 555, 561 (1885) (“The bankrupt law is founded on the principle of a fresh start for the honest debtor.”); *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt,* 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (“The purpose of the Bankruptcy Act is to give the debtor a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of pre-existing debt.”). This principle has been repeatedly reaffirmed by this Court. *See, e.g., Williams v. U.S. Fidelity & Guaranty Co.,* 236 U.S. 549, 555 (1915) (“The primary purpose of the Bankruptcy Act is to relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness and permit him to start afresh.”). Thus, exceptions to discharge must be strictly construed against the creditor and limited to those debts Congress expressly intended to exclude. *Strang,* 114 U.S. at 561; *Hunt,* 292 U.S. at 240.

Here, the Third Circuit erred by expanding § 523(a)(2)(A) beyond its plain text. The statute excepts from discharge only debts “obtained by” fraud—not debts arising from fraudulent conduct that are later reduced to judgment. *See Neal v. Clark,* 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877) (“The words ‘obtained by’ are words of limitation, and should be strictly construed.”). The court below conflated the *debt* with the *judgment*, but the Code distinguishes between the two. *See Brown v. Felsen,* 442 U.S. 127, 129 (1979) (noting that a judgment may include elements beyond the underlying debt). Under the Third Circuit’s reasoning, any fraud-based judgment—no matter how attenuated from the original fraud—would be nondischargeable, a construction that impermissibly broadens the exception. *See Strang,* 114 U.S. at 561 (warning against “a construction which would defeat the beneficent purposes of the act”).

**II. Treble Damages Under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act Are Not “Obtained by” Fraud and Are Dischargeable.**

The Third Circuit held that statutorily mandated treble damages are nondischargeable because they were “obtained by” fraud. This misreads the statute. The phrase “obtained by” refers to the *original transaction*, not subsequent statutory enhancements. *Neal,* 95 U.S. at 709 (“The debt must be ‘obtained by’ fraud; the fraud must be the means by which the debt was procured.”). Treble damages are a *penalty* imposed by statute, not a debt “obtained by” fraud. *See Day v. Micou,* 18 Wall. 156, 160 (1873) (“A penalty is not a debt in the ordinary sense.”).

This Court has long held that punitive damages are not dischargeable only if they are *compensatory in nature*. *See, e.g., In re Dyer,* 322 F. 239, 242 (7th Cir. 1927) (“Punitive damages are not dischargeable unless they are in the nature of compensation for actual loss.”). Treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are purely punitive. *See, e.g., Lemelledo v. Beneficial Management Corp.,* 150 N.J. Super. 81, 88 (App. Div. 1977) (holding that treble damages are “punitive in nature”). Thus, they are dischargeable. *See Dyer,* 322 F. at 242.

**III. The Debtor’s Conduct Did Not Constitute “Actual Fraud” Under § 523(a)(2)(A).**

To except a debt from discharge under § 523(a)(2)(A), the creditor must prove *actual fraud*—a knowing misrepresentation made with intent to deceive. *See, e.g., National Bank of Newport v. Colwell,* 106 F. 603, 605 (6th Cir. 1901) (“Actual fraud requires a false representation, known to be false, made with intent to deceive.”). The Third Circuit erred by inferring fraud from the debtor’s *negligence* in failing to comply with rent control laws. *See, e.g., Union Trust Co. v. Grosman,* 147 F. 62, 65 (2d Cir. 1906) (“Mere negligence or mistake, even if gross, does not constitute fraud.”).

Here, the debtor’s failure to refund overcharges was not a *misrepresentation*—it was a *breach of contract*. The tenants never alleged that the debtor affirmatively misrepresented the legality of the rents; they merely claimed he charged too much. *See, e.g., National Bank of Newport,* 106 F. at 605 (holding that silence alone does not constitute fraud unless there is a duty to speak). Thus, the debt arose from a *contractual obligation*, not fraud, and is dischargeable. *See, e.g., In re Dyer,* 322 F. at 241 (holding that debts arising from breach of contract are dischargeable unless tainted by fraud).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed. The Bankruptcy Code’s discharge provisions must be narrowly construed, treble damages are dischargeable, and the debtor’s conduct did not constitute “actual fraud” under § 523(a)(2)(A).
