The judgment below should be reversed because the government, not the accused, bears the burden of proving that a conspiracy charge falls within the limitations period. A conspiracy is a continuing offense, but it continues only so long as the defendant remains a member. See Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912); United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607-08 (1910). Once the defendant produces evidence of withdrawal before the limitations period, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was still a conspirator during the relevant time. That allocation of proof follows from the basic rule that the prosecution must prove every fact necessary to constitute the offense charged. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Although Winship is later than the date range reflected by the authorities below, it restates the constitutional command grounded in earlier cases. See Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 274-75 (1952); Tot v. United States, 319 U.S. 463, 469 (1943).

This Court has long treated conspiracy limitations as depending on continued membership, not on an assumption of guilt that persists indefinitely. In Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 216-17 (1946), the Court explained that conspiracy is a continuing offense and that the statute of limitations runs from the last overt act only so long as the defendant remains part of the combination. The same principle appears in Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396-97 (1957), which recognized that limitations in conspiracy cases is designed to cabin criminal exposure to a fixed period of time. A defendant who introduces evidence of withdrawal therefore negates the government’s claim that the offense continued into the actionable period as to him. See Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 334-35 (1957); United States v. Bayer, 331 U.S. 532, 542-43 (1947).

The government cannot shift that burden by calling withdrawal an affirmative defense in the abstract. It is true that a defendant may come forward with proof of withdrawal, just as he may present evidence negating identity, intent, or participation. But once that evidence is in the case, the Due Process Clause requires the government to prove the contrary beyond a reasonable doubt. Winship, 397 U.S. at 364; Morissette, 342 U.S. at 274-75. That rule is especially important where, as here, the statute of limitations is the very line between a live prosecution and one that is time-barred. See United States v. Cook, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 168, 179-80 (1872); United States v. Scharton, 285 U.S. 518, 520-21 (1932). Criminal limitation periods are not technicalities; they protect against stale accusations and require the sovereign to proceed within the time Congress prescribed. Scharton, 285 U.S. at 520-21.

The rule urged by the government would invert that structure. It would allow conviction unless the accused disproved continued participation, even after he has produced evidence of withdrawal before the limitations period. That is inconsistent with the Court’s repeated insistence that guilt must rest on proof, not presumption. Tot, 319 U.S. at 469; Morissette, 342 U.S. at 274-75. It is also inconsistent with Hyde and Kissel, which make clear that conspiracy liability turns on actual membership in the unlawful agreement, not on a legal fiction that persists after departure. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70; Kissel, 218 U.S. at 607-08.

Nor does Fiswick suggest otherwise. To the contrary, it confirms that when a defendant’s membership ends, so does his exposure for the continuing offense. 329 U.S. at 216-17. The government must therefore show, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the defendant remained in the conspiracy during the limitations period. If the evidence raises a real question of withdrawal before that period, the prosecution has failed to prove timeliness as to that defendant.

Because the court below placed the ultimate burden on the accused to prove that he was no longer a member when the limitations period began, it applied the wrong rule. The Constitution and this Court’s conspiracy cases require the opposite result. The judgment should be reversed.
