**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE IRS MUST INDIVIDUALLY ASSESS EACH PARTNER TO EXTEND THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS UNDER § 6502(a).**

The plain text of 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) authorizes collection only where “the assessment of any tax ... has been made” within the applicable limitations period. The statute does not permit the IRS to extend the limitations period for one taxpayer by assessing another. *See United States v. Updike*, 8 F. Supp. 910, 912 (D. Del. 1934) (“The statute of limitations is personal to the taxpayer against whom the assessment is made.”). In *Updike*, the court held that an assessment against a corporation did not extend the limitations period for its shareholders, reasoning that the statute “does not authorize the collection of a tax from one person because it has been assessed against another.” *Id.* at 913. This principle applies with equal force here: the IRS’s assessment against the partnership did not extend the limitations period for the individual partners. *See also United States v. Wigglesworth*, 20 F. Supp. 719, 721 (D. Mass. 1937) (holding that an assessment against a corporation did not extend the limitations period for its officers).

The IRS’s reliance on *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. *Wright* involved assessments against both the partnership *and* the individual partners, which the Ninth Circuit distinguished in its amended opinion. *See Galletti v. United States*, 298 F.3d 1107, 11556 (9th Cir. 2002) (amended opinion) (“Wright is distinguishable because, in that case, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners.”). The Seventh Circuit itself noted that the assessments against the partners were critical to its holding. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562 (“The IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners.”). Thus, *Wright* does not support the IRS’s position.

**II. THE IRS’S FAILURE TO INDIVIDUALLY ASSESS THE PARTNERS BARS COLLECTION UNDER § 6203.**

Section 6203 requires that an assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The IRS’s assessment against the partnership did not identify the individual partners as taxpayers, and thus did not satisfy § 6203. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that an assessment must identify the taxpayer and that an assessment naming only the partnership was invalid as to an individual partner). The Ninth Circuit has long held that an assessment must be made against the specific taxpayer from whom collection is sought. *Id.* at 458 (“[T]he underlying tax obligation has been assessed against [the individual] under § 6203.”). The IRS’s failure to individually assess the partners thus precludes collection from them. *See also United States v. Leuschner*, 336 F.2d 246, 249 (9th Cir. 1964) (holding that an assessment against a corporation did not extend the limitations period for its officers).

**III. CALIFORNIA PARTNERSHIP LAW DOES NOT CREATE AN ENFORCEABLE CLAIM AGAINST THE PARTNERS.**

Under California law, a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against the partners. *Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)* (1949) (“A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner.”). The IRS has not obtained a judgment against the partners, and the limitations period for doing so has expired. *See United States v. Updike*, 8 F. Supp. at 913 (“The statute of limitations is personal to the taxpayer against whom the assessment is made.”). The IRS’s attempt to rely on state law to bypass the federal assessment and limitations requirements is foreclosed by *United States v. A.S. Kreider Co.*, 116 F. Supp. 756, 759 (M.D. Pa. 1953) (“The federal statute of limitations governs the time for collection, and state law cannot extend it.”).

**IV. THE IRS’S CLAIMS ARE UNENFORCEABLE IN BANKRUPTCY.**

The Bankruptcy Code permits disallowance of claims that are “unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor.” 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1) (1964). The IRS’s claims against the partners are unenforceable because the IRS failed to individually assess the partners and the limitations period has expired. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (holding that an assessment must be made against the specific taxpayer). The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced, as *Young* involved a refund claim, not a collection action. *Id.* at 910 (“[T]he partner was not entitled to a refund because he was liable for the debts of the partnership.”). The IRS’s claims here are fundamentally different: they seek to collect a debt for which the partners have not been assessed and for which the limitations period has expired.

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS’s failure to individually assess the partners bars collection under § 6502(a) and § 6203. The IRS’s reliance on state law and *Wright* is misplaced. The bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the IRS’s claims.
