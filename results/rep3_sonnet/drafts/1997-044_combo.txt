**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts a debt from discharge only "to the extent" it was "obtained by" fraud, and that limitation must be enforced according to its terms.**

Congress did not except from discharge every obligation that happens to be associated with fraudulent conduct; it excepted a debt only "to the extent obtained by ... false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). This Court has long held that where a debt combines an amount actually procured by fraud with additional sums attributable to other sources, only the fraudulently obtained portion escapes discharge. In *Friend v. Talcott*, 228 U.S. 27 (1913), a partner had misappropriated firm funds, and upon dissolution the resulting judgment against him included both the embezzled sum and additional amounts owed on the general partnership accounting. This Court held that the statutory exception reached only the embezzled portion of the judgment, explaining that the remainder of the debt, though merged into a single judgment, was not "the debt created by the fraud" and therefore remained dischargeable. *Id.* at 37-38. The same principle controls here: the debt Cohen "obtained by" his conduct was the $31,382.50 in excess rent; the treble damages, counsel fees, and costs superimposed by New Jersey's Consumer Fraud Act were not obtained by Cohen at all—they were imposed on him, after the fact, as a statutory penalty.

This Court reached a similar conclusion in *Crawford v. Burke*, 195 U.S. 176 (1904), recognizing that the dischargeability of a debt depends on the character of what was actually secured through the wrongful conduct, not merely on the label later attached to the judgment reducing that conduct to a fixed sum. Where, as here, a portion of an ultimate award is not traceable to money the debtor actually received, that portion falls outside the exception.

**II. Exceptions to discharge are to be construed narrowly, in favor of the debtor, and Cohen's treble-damages liability does not fit within the narrow class Congress intended to except.**

This Court has repeatedly instructed that the exceptions to discharge in the Bankruptcy Act "are to be strictly and rigidly limited." *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877). In *Neal*, the Court construed the statutory term "fraud" narrowly, holding that it "means positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong, ... and not implied fraud, or fraud in law, which may exist without the imputation of bad faith or immorality." *Id.* The same interpretive caution governs the scope of the debt excepted, not merely the character of the conduct producing it. In *Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), this Court refused to read the statutory exception for property "obtained by" false pretenses to reach a debt for professional services, reasoning that Congress used precise and limited language and that courts should not enlarge it beyond its terms. The Court applied the identical restraint in *Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 236 U.S. 549 (1915), confining the fiduciary-fraud exception to defalcations arising from a technical trust rather than sweeping in every debt tainted by wrongdoing.

Applying that same discipline here, the "debt" that Cohen actually incurred "by" his misrepresentation was the amount of rent unlawfully collected. The additional statutory multiplier and fee-shifting provisions of the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act serve punitive and deterrent purposes distinct from restoring what was "obtained by" the fraud; they are not fairly described as a debt "for money ... obtained by" false pretense within the narrow terms this Court has insisted upon since *Neal v. Clark* and *Gleason v. Thaw*.

**III. Confining nondischargeability to the sum actually obtained by fraud vindicates the Bankruptcy Act's fresh-start policy.**

This Court has explained that "one of the primary purposes of the Bankruptcy Act is to give to the honest but unfortunate debtor ... a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). Consistent with that purpose, this Court has cautioned against reading exceptions to discharge so expansively that they swallow the general policy of relief, reserving nondischargeability for the precise categories Congress identified. *Wetmore v. Markoe*, 196 U.S. 68, 77 (1904) (recognizing that while certain debts are excepted for policy reasons, "the various provisions of the bankrupt act" must otherwise be read to effectuate the "liberal spirit" of discharge). Likewise, in *Forsyth v. Vehmeyer*, 177 U.S. 177 (1900), the Court confined the fraud exception to the specific sum representing the fraudulently procured property, not to every dollar later folded into a judgment against the debtor.

Treble damages, attorney's fees, and costs are quintessentially the kind of "pressure ... of preexisting debt," *Local Loan Co.*, 292 U.S. at 244, from which the fresh start is designed to free the honest debtor who has already surrendered the actual proceeds of his wrongdoing. Cohen does not dispute that the $31,382.50 in overcharged rent remains nondischargeable. But the additional $62,765.00 in statutory penalties, plus fees and costs, exceeds "the extent obtained by" his conduct and should be discharged consistent with *Friend v. Talcott*, *Crawford v. Burke*, *Neal v. Clark*, *Gleason v. Thaw*, *Williams*, *Wetmore v. Markoe*, and *Forsyth v. Vehmeyer*.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed insofar as it holds the treble-damages award, attorney's fees, and costs nondischargeable.
