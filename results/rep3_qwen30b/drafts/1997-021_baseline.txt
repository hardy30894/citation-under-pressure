**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS BOARD’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS IRRATIONAL AND CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.

The National Labor Relations Board’s rigid requirement that an employer must possess “objective indications raising a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status before conducting a poll renders the poll an ineffective and impractical tool—precisely the opposite of what Congress intended. The Board’s standard, which equates the evidentiary threshold for polling with that for withdrawing recognition or seeking an RM election, creates a paradox: an employer may not poll unless it already has sufficient grounds to act without polling. This circular logic undermines the very purpose of polling—to obtain more precise, reliable information about employee sentiment before making a binding decision.

The Board’s justification for this standard—that polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling”—is not a valid basis for judicial deference. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989). The Act does not prohibit polling; it merely regulates its use. Congress entrusted the Board with administering the Act, but not with eliminating lawful employer tools that serve a legitimate purpose. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37 (1987). The Board’s policy preference for RM elections over polls does not justify rendering polls functionally useless. Indeed, the Board itself acknowledges that polling “can serve an important role” in allowing an employer to “act with confidence and certainty” after a reasonable doubt has been formed. *Id.* at 1063. If the Board believes polling is inherently problematic, it should regulate the process—not prohibit its use altogether.

The courts of appeals that rejected the Board’s standard did so not out of judicial overreach, but out of recognition that the Board’s rule is irrational. The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits unanimously held that an employer may conduct a poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence falls short of justifying withdrawal of recognition. *A.W. Thompson, Inc.*, 651 F.2d 1139, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). These decisions reflect a sound understanding of the employer’s need to test union support without risking a violation of § 8(a)(2) by bargaining with a minority union. The Board’s refusal to adopt this more practical standard is not merely inconsistent with precedent—it is contrary to the Act’s goal of promoting stable, voluntary collective bargaining.

Moreover, the Board’s standard is not administratively neutral. It forces employers to choose between risking an unfair labor practice by polling without sufficient grounds or foregoing a tool that could prevent such a violation. This is not a rational regulatory scheme. As the Sixth Circuit observed, “an employer who would commit an unfair labor practice if it refused to bargain with a union having majority support or did bargain with a union which did not enjoy that support” is placed in a “dilemma.” *Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 867. The Board’s standard exacerbates this dilemma by making polling unavailable when it is most needed.

The Board’s insistence on a “reasonable doubt” standard that mirrors the standard for withdrawal of recognition is particularly irrational in the successor employer context. Here, Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. inherited a bargaining unit of 32 employees from Mack Trucks, Inc., a company that had operated under a union contract for decades. The new employer had no prior relationship with the employees and no history of labor disputes. Yet the Board demands that it prove a “reasonable doubt” about the union’s majority status—despite the fact that the union had not been decertified, no election had been held, and no evidence of a decline in support had been presented by the union itself.

The Board’s approach ignores the reality of successorship. *Fall River Dyeing*, 482 U.S. at 36–41. A successor employer inherits the predecessor’s bargaining obligations, including the presumption of continuing majority support. But the Board’s standard effectively treats that presumption as unshakable, even when the composition of the unit has changed significantly. In this case, the new employer reduced the workforce from 45 to 32 employees, and the new unit included only those employees who had worked for the predecessor. The Board’s refusal to count statements from employees who were not in the unit on January 25, 1991—when Allentown Mack sent its letter refusing recognition—undermines the employer’s ability to assess current sentiment. The Board’s rigid adherence to a static date, without regard to the evolving nature of the unit, is arbitrary and capricious.

II. THE BOARD’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED A GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The ALJ and the Board discounted the statements of seven employees who expressed opposition to the union, relying on technicalities and speculative inferences. But the record contains more than enough objective evidence to justify a reasonable doubt.

First, seven employees—Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, Randy Zoltack, Mike Ridgick, Dennis Marsh, Kermit Bloch, and Ron Mohr—made statements indicating that they or others in the unit opposed the union. While the Board excluded McArthur, Wehr, and Zoltack because they were not in the unit on January 25, the record shows they were hired shortly after the sale and were part of the new workforce. Their statements, made during interviews, were not irrelevant; they reflected a clear anti-union sentiment among a significant portion of the new employees. The Board’s refusal to consider them is arbitrary.
