# ARGUMENT

## Arizona's Channeling of Mental-Disease Evidence to the Insanity Defense Does Not Violate the Due Process Clause

### I. States Possess Broad Authority to Define the Insanity Defense and to Structure the Presentation of Mental-Disease Evidence.

This Court has long recognized that "the definition of the insanity defense is substantially open to the State." *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 536 (1968). In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld a state rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, holding that "the science of psychiatry has not reached [a] point where its practitioners are able to prove … clear-cut definition and understanding" sufficient to constitutionalize any single formulation of the defense. *Id.* at 800–01. The Court reaffirmed that principle in *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197 (1977), explaining that a State may allocate the burden of proving an affirmative defense to the defendant without offending due process, because "the Due Process Clause requires the prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt all of the elements included in the definition of the offense," but "nothing more." *Id.* at 210.

*Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437 (1992), controls the analytical framework here. There, the Court held that state procedural rules governing the litigation of mental-state defenses are subject only to the historic due-process test of *Patterson* and *Leland*—whether the rule "offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Id.* at 445 (quoting *Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 202). Applying that test, *Medina* upheld California's allocation to the defendant of the burden of proving incompetency to stand trial, reasoning that "the State's power to regulate procedural burdens was subject to proscription under the Due Process Clause only if it offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Id.* at 446. Arizona's rule confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry is precisely the kind of procedural channeling *Medina* permits.

### II. *Montana v. Egelhoff* Forecloses Petitioner's Claim.

The most directly controlling precedent is *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996). There, a plurality of this Court upheld a Montana statute barring consideration of voluntary intoxication evidence on the question of mens rea, even though such evidence was unquestionably relevant to whether the defendant actually formed the required intent. The plurality explained that "the introduction of evidence of voluntary intoxication to negate the requisite mental state" is not "so deeply rooted in our legal tradition" as to be a fundamental element of due process, and that States retain "the authority to make substantive changes in the law of criminal responsibility by excluding evidence of voluntary intoxication." *Id.* at 56 (plurality opinion). Justice Ginsburg's controlling concurrence characterized the statute as a substantive redefinition of the mental-state element rather than an evidentiary exclusion, concluding that Montana "has not violated due process" because "the definition of mens rea … is a matter for the State to determine." *Id.* at 58 (Ginsburg, J., concurring in the judgment).

Arizona's rule is functionally identical. By directing that evidence of mental disease and capacity bears only on the insanity defense—not on the mens rea element itself—Arizona has made a substantive judgment, consistent with *Leland* and *Patterson*, about how the concepts of mental illness and criminal responsibility interact. Under *Egelhoff*, that legislative choice does not violate due process merely because it forecloses one particular use of relevant psychiatric testimony.

### III. Petitioner Was Not Denied Meaningful Access to Psychiatric Evidence.

Petitioner's reliance on the general due-process right to present a defense, as recognized in *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284 (1973), and the psychiatric-assistance right recognized in *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68 (1985), is misplaced. *Chambers* condemned the mechanistic exclusion of reliable, exculpatory hearsay evidence where no valid state interest justified the exclusion. 410 U.S. at 302–03. Here, by contrast, Arizona permitted full presentation of Clark's psychiatric evidence—it simply directed the factfinder to consider that evidence within the insanity framework rather than as freestanding rebuttal of intent. *Ake* guaranteed indigent defendants access to psychiatric expertise to prepare an insanity defense, 470 U.S. at 83; it did not hold that a defendant is entitled to deploy that expertise however he wishes once obtained. Arizona satisfied *Ake* in full: Clark received expert assistance and used it extensively—just channeled to the insanity determination, as *Jones v. United States*, 463 U.S. 354, 365 (1983), confirms is a legitimate structural choice, since "the fact that a person has been found, beyond a reasonable doubt, to have committed a criminal act and to have been insane at the time … is an adequate foundation" for the State's distinct treatment of insanity acquittees.

### CONCLUSION

Under *Leland*, *Powell*, *Patterson*, *Medina*, and *Egelhoff*, Arizona's channeling rule reflects a permissible substantive judgment about mens rea and mental illness, not a fundamentally unfair evidentiary exclusion. The judgment below should be affirmed.
