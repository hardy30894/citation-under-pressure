**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings.**

The Bill of Rights restricts only the federal and state governments, not Indian tribes. In *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896), this Court held that the Fifth Amendment’s grand jury clause does not apply to tribal governments because tribes are “separate sovereigns” predating the Constitution. That principle extends to the Sixth Amendment. *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556, 571 (1883), recognized that tribes retain “original natural rights” of self-government, including the power to punish tribal offenders. *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 381-82 (1886), affirmed Congress’s plenary power over tribes but did not impose constitutional constraints on tribal courts. The Tenth Circuit has squarely held that “the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.” *Native American Church v. Navajo Tribal Council*, 272 F.2d 131, 134 (10th Cir. 1959). Accordingly, Bryant’s tribal court convictions are not subject to the Sixth Amendment’s counsel requirement. They are valid under the Indian Civil Rights Act, which provides due process but not a right to appointed counsel coextensive with the Constitution.

**II. Valid Prior Convictions May Serve as Predicate Offenses in Recidivist Statutes.**

This Court has long upheld recidivist statutes that enhance punishment based on prior convictions, even when those convictions were obtained without counsel, so long as the prior convictions were valid under the law of the rendering sovereign. *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616, 623 (1912), sustained a habitual criminal law against due process challenge. *McDonald v. Massachusetts*, 180 U.S. 311, 312 (1901), upheld a similar statute, reasoning that the prior convictions “are not put in issue” but merely establish the defendant’s status. *Moore v. Missouri*, 159 U.S. 673, 678 (1895), confirmed that a state may punish a subsequent offense more severely based on prior convictions that were “lawfully had.” Here, Bryant’s tribal court convictions were lawfully had under tribal law and ICRA. Their use as predicates under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment because the Amendment does not govern the proceedings that produced them.

**III. *Burgett v. Texas* Is Inapposite.**

In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 114 (1967), this Court held that a prior uncounseled felony conviction could not be used to enhance punishment in a *state* court proceeding where the Sixth Amendment applied. But *Burgett* expressly relied on the fact that the prior conviction was “obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment.” *Id.* at 115. Because the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, *Burgett* is distinguishable. The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on a supposed “reliability” concern conflates the distinct sovereigns. Tribal court convictions are entitled to comity and respect. *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217, 223 (1959), recognized that tribal courts are “competent” to adjudicate matters arising on reservations. There is no basis to presume that tribal court convictions are unreliable simply because they do not mirror federal procedures.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with This Court’s Precedent and Undermines Tribal Sovereignty.**

By requiring tribal courts to provide a Sixth Amendment right to counsel before their convictions can be used as predicates, the Ninth Circuit imposed “judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts,” contrary to *Williams*, 358 U.S. at 223. This Court has warned against “unnecessary judicial paternalism” toward tribes. *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) (citing *Talton*). The decision also frustrates Congress’s intent in enacting § 117(a) to combat domestic violence in Indian country. The statute explicitly includes tribal court convictions as predicates, reflecting Congress’s judgment that such convictions are reliable. This Court should reverse.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
