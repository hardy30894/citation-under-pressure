**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Statutory Terms "Malice" and "Reckless Indifference" Incorporate the Common-Law Requirement That Punitive Damages Be Reserved for Egregious Misconduct.**

Section 1981a authorizes punitive damages only where a plaintiff "demonstrates that the [employer] engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Congress did not write on a blank slate. It borrowed this standard from the common law of punitive damages, which has long confined such awards to conduct that is qualitatively worse than the conduct sufficient to establish liability itself. As the Restatement explains, "[p]unitive damages may be awarded for conduct that is outrageous, because of the defendant's evil motive or his reckless indifference to the rights of others." Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) (1979). This Court has likewise recognized that punitive damages serve the distinct purposes of retribution and deterrence, and are not simply an additional increment tacked onto every finding of intentional wrongdoing. *Pacific Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Haslip*, 499 U.S. 1, 19 (1991).

Title VII liability, by contrast, may rest on nothing more than a jury's rejection of an employer's proffered nondiscriminatory explanation. *St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993) (once a plaintiff's prima facie case is coupled with proof that the employer's stated reasons are pretextual, "no additional proof of discrimination is required"); *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). If "malice" and "reckless indifference" require nothing more than the intent already necessary to prove pretext, then every Title VII plaintiff who prevails on liability would automatically be entitled to place punitive damages before the jury. That result collapses the statute's careful two-tiered structure — a structure Congress adopted deliberately when it created a separate damages remedy in the Civil Rights Act of 1991 while leaving Title VII's underlying liability standard untouched. *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 252–55 (1994) (describing the 1991 Act's expansion of remedies as a significant departure from prior law). Congress's use of the traditional punitive-damages vocabulary — malice, reckless indifference — should be read to carry with it the traditional requirement that the conduct itself be egregious, not merely intentional.

**II. This Court's Precedent Confirms That "Reckless Indifference" Denotes Outrageous Conduct, Not Simply Knowing Conduct.**

This Court has already construed the phrase "reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others" in the closely related context of civil-rights damages actions. In *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), the Court held that "a jury may be permitted to assess punitive damages in an action under § 1983 when the defendant's conduct is shown to be motivated by evil motive or intent, or when it involves reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others." *Id.* at 56. The Court's formulation treats "reckless or callous indifference" as the functional equivalent of evil motive — a standard of blameworthiness well above ordinary intentional conduct, and one historically reserved for defendants whose conduct approaches the outrageous. Section 1981a's identical language should be given the same meaning. Congress is presumed to legislate against the backdrop of this Court's settled interpretations of closely analogous statutory text, and nothing in the 1991 Act's text suggests that Congress meant to strip the phrase of the very content this Court had already assigned it.

**III. The Legislative History Confirms That Congress Intended Punitive Damages to Remain Extraordinary Relief.**

Even setting aside the statutory text and this Court's precedent, the legislative record confirms that Congress did not intend punitive damages to follow automatically from a finding of intentional discrimination. The House Report accompanying the 1991 Act stated that recovery of punitive damages demands "an even higher standard" than proof of intentional discrimination. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. I, at 12 (1991). And the bipartisan interpretive memorandum submitted by Senator Dole and his co-sponsors — offered specifically to explain the operative Senate compromise that became law — stated that punitive damages would be available only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Interp. Mem. of Sen. Dole et al.). Nothing in the statute's final text disturbs that shared understanding. To adopt Petitioner's reading — that any employer found liable for intentional discrimination is automatically exposed to punitive damages — would render Congress's repeated invocation of "egregious" conduct meaningless.

**IV. The Court of Appeals Correctly Required Proof of Egregious Conduct Beyond Mere Intentional Discrimination.**

Applying these principles, the en banc court below correctly recognized that Title VII punitive damages require more than the bare showing of intentional discrimination that supports liability. *See Kolstad v. American Dental Ass'n*, 139 F.3d 958 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (en banc). Petitioner proved, at most, that ADA preselected a candidate and offered shifting explanations for its decision — precisely the kind of ordinary pretext evidence that satisfies *McDonnell Douglas* but falls far short of the malice, evil motive, or outrageous indifference this Court described in *Smith v. Wade*. Because Petitioner offered no evidence that ADA's conduct rose to that level, the judgment below should be affirmed.
