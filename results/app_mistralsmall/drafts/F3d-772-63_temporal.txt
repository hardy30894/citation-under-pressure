**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING FOLEY’S STATE-LAW CLAIMS FOR FAILURE TO STATE A CLAIM.**

A. **Foley’s Chapter 244, § 35B Claim Survives Dismissal.**

Wells Fargo contends that Foley’s claim under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 35B—a statute requiring lenders to consider borrowers for loan modifications before foreclosing—fails because the statute does not create a private right of action. This argument misreads the statute and controlling precedent.

In *Salmon v. F.W. Woolworth Co.*, 332 Mass. 439, 442 (1955), the Supreme Judicial Court held that a statute’s silence on a private remedy does not bar a claim where the statute was enacted to protect a class of individuals (here, distressed borrowers) and the harm alleged falls within the statute’s protective ambit. *See also* *Commissioner of Ins. v. Massachusetts Accident Co.*, 318 Mass. 238, 246 (1945) (statutes enacted for the protection of a class may be enforced by private right of action absent express authorization). Section 35B was plainly designed to shield borrowers from foreclosure without consideration of modification, and Foley’s allegations—that Wells Fargo failed to evaluate him for HAMP or MAP2R despite the settlement agreement’s mandate—state a cognizable claim. *Cf. Sullivan v. Liberty Mut. Ins. Co.*, 344 Mass. 565, 568 (1962) (holding that statutory duties may give rise to private enforcement where the plaintiff is within the class the statute protects).

B. **Foley’s Chapter 93A Claim Is Sufficiently Pled.**

Wells Fargo argues Foley’s Chapter 93A claim fails because he did not allege an unfair or deceptive act in trade or commerce. This misapprehends the statute’s scope.

In *Springfield v. Guzik*, 322 Mass. 207, 210 (1948), the court held that Chapter 93A applies to “any unfair or deceptive act or practice in the conduct of any trade or commerce,” including conduct by financial institutions. Foley’s complaint alleges that Wells Fargo misled him about his modification eligibility, failed to process his application, and provided contradictory foreclosure alternatives—all of which, if proven, could constitute deceptive practices under *Guzik*. *See also* *Porter v. Clerk-Magistrate of the W. Roxbury Div. of the Dist. Court Dep’t*, 325 Mass. 746, 749 (1950) (holding that misrepresentations in commercial dealings may support a Chapter 93A claim).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT FOLEY’S CLAIMS ARE PREEMPTED.**

Wells Fargo’s preemption argument rests on the erroneous premise that federal law displaces state-law duties in mortgage servicing. This is incorrect.

In *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941), the Supreme Court held that federal law preempts state law only where Congress has “occupied the field” or where state law conflicts with federal law. Neither condition is met here. The Home Affordable Modification Program (HAMP) does not purport to preempt state servicing requirements; it merely establishes an optional federal framework. *See* *Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R.R.*, 272 U.S. 605, 613 (1926) (federal law does not preempt state law unless it is “inconsistent” or “repugnant”). Moreover, the settlement agreement’s explicit requirement that Wells Fargo consider Foley for HAMP or MAP2R demonstrates that state and federal regimes can coexist. *Cf. Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. 1, 210 (1824) (federal law does not preempt state law where both can be harmonized).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in dismissing Foley’s state-law claims and in finding federal preemption. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
