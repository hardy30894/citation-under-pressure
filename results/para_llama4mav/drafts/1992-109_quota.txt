The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court erred in failing to apply the "reasoned choice" standard to Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights.

The competency standard for waiving constitutional rights is distinct from the standard for competency to stand trial. As this Court has held, a defendant is competent to stand trial if they have a "rational and factual understanding of the proceedings" and are "capable of assisting their counsel." Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975). In contrast, a defendant is competent to waive counsel or plead guilty only if they have the capacity for "reasoned choice" among the available alternatives. Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); Chavez v. United States, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981).

The state court's failure to apply the correct standard of competency to Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty plea was error. The state court's post-conviction ruling relied solely on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran's competency to stand trial, not his competency to waive constitutional rights. As the Ninth Circuit noted, these evaluations did not address the effects of the medications Moran was taking at the time of his plea hearing, which is a critical factor in determining his competency to waive his rights. Moran v. Godinez, 972 F.2d 263, 271 (9th Cir. 1992).

The Ninth Circuit's decision is consistent with this Court's precedent. In Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), this Court held that a trial court must hold a competency hearing if there is a "bona fide doubt" about a defendant's competency to stand trial. Similarly, in Godinez v. Moran, this Court will consider whether the Due Process Clause requires a heightened standard for competency to waive constitutional rights.

The state's argument that the post-conviction hearing cured any error is unpersuasive. As the Ninth Circuit held, the state court's application of the wrong legal standard renders its findings unreliable. Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963). Furthermore, the psychiatric evaluations on which the state court relied were inadequate to support a finding of competency to waive constitutional rights. See Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211, 214-15 (9th Cir. 1973).

The heightened "reasoned choice" standard is necessary to ensure that a defendant's waiver of constitutional rights is voluntary, knowing, and intelligent. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969). As this Court has held, a waiver of constitutional rights must be made with "utmost solicitude" and courts must indulge in "every reasonable presumption against waiver." Evans v. Raines, 800 F.2d 884, 885 (9th Cir. 1986).

In conclusion, the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court erred in failing to apply this standard to Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
