**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve substantial government interests, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The petitioners’ challenge fails under settled precedent, including *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480, 147 L.Ed.2d 597 (2000), which affirmed a similar regulation. The Court should not revisit or overrule *Hill*, which remains sound and consistent with the First Amendment’s time, place, and manner framework.

**I. THE MASSACHUSETTS BUFFER ZONE LAW IS A VALID TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION**

The First Amendment does not prohibit reasonable, content-neutral regulations that protect public safety and the free flow of traffic. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that sidewalks and public ways adjacent to healthcare facilities are “quintessential” public forums, yet subject to reasonable regulation to protect the rights of patients and staff. Id. at 715. Massachusetts’ law, which establishes a fixed 35-foot buffer zone around entrances, exits, and driveways of reproductive health care facilities (RHCFs), is precisely such a regulation.

The law is content-neutral: it applies equally to all speech, regardless of message. *Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 576 U.S. 155, 163, 135 S.Ct. 2218, 192 L.Ed.2d 236 (2015). The statute does not target pro-life or anti-abortion speech; it restricts all speech within the buffer zone, including pro-choice advocacy. The exemptions—such as for patients, employees, and emergency personnel—further confirm its neutrality. *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 377, 88 S.Ct. 1673, 20 L.Ed.2d 672 (1968). The law’s purpose is not to suppress ideas but to ensure safe and unimpeded access to medical care.

The regulation is narrowly tailored to serve substantial government interests: protecting the safety and privacy of patients and staff, preventing intimidation, and ensuring the orderly operation of clinics. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The First Circuit correctly found that the buffer zone is “narrowly tailored” to these ends. The law does not ban all speech; it merely restricts proximity to entrances and driveways. The petitioners retain full ability to communicate through oral speech, literature distribution, signage, prayer, and symbolic expression—activities that occur outside the buffer zone. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791, 109 S.Ct. 2746, 105 L.Ed.2d 661 (1989). The existence of alternative channels is sufficient to satisfy the First Amendment. *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293, 104 S.Ct. 3065, 82 L.Ed.2d 221 (1984).

**II. THE FIRST CIRCUIT CORRECTLY APPLIED HILL V. COLORADO**

The petitioners argue that *Hill* should be limited or overruled. But *Hill* remains binding precedent. In *Hill*, the Supreme Court upheld a Colorado law that prohibited “any person from knowingly approaching within 8 feet of any individual entering or leaving a health care facility” if the purpose was to communicate. 530 U.S. at 707, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The Court found the law content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and consistent with the First Amendment. Id. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The Massachusetts law is even more precise: it creates a fixed 35-foot buffer zone, clearly marked, with specific exemptions. It does not prohibit all communication, only proximity-based interaction near entrances.

The petitioners’ claim that *Hill* is “out of step” with modern First Amendment doctrine is unfounded. *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 570 U.S. 315, 133 S.Ct. 2671, 186 L.Ed.2d 769 (2011), and *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310, 130 S.Ct. 876, 175 L.Ed.2d 753 (2010), do not undermine *Hill*. Those cases addressed content-based restrictions on commercial speech and campaign finance, not public sidewalk regulations. *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 131 S.Ct. 1207, 179 L.Ed.2d 172 (2011), involved offensive speech at a military funeral, not access to medical facilities. The Court has never overruled *Hill*, and no compelling reason exists to do so now.

**III. THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE BARS RELITIGATION OF FACIAL CHALLENGES**

The petitioners’ attempt to relitigate the facial constitutionality of the law is barred by the law of the case doctrine. *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir.2011). This doctrine prevents relitigation of issues already decided in the same case. *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir.2009), upheld the law’s facial validity.
