**ARGUMENT**

The decision below—that a single plaintiff may state an equal protection claim based solely on a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him”—finds no support in the text or history of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Equal Protection Clause, as consistently interpreted by this Court before 1970, protects individuals only when they are members of a discrete class that has been subjected to arbitrary discrimination. A “class of one” theory, absent any allegation of group-based animus, is incompatible with the Clause’s settled meaning.

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Does Not Prohibit Single-Instance, Animus-Driven Disparate Treatment Outside a Class or Group.**

From its earliest applications, this Court has understood the Equal Protection Clause to guard against discriminatory classifications, not isolated acts of hostility toward a lone individual. In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373–74 (1886), the Court struck down a law that was administered “with an evil eye and an unequal hand,” but only because the discrimination targeted an entire class of Chinese laundry owners. Similarly, in *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27, 31 (1885), the Court emphasized that the Clause “does not interfere with the police power” so long as the law “applies equally to all persons” within a rationally defined class.

The requirement that a plaintiff identify a “class” is not a mere pleading formality; it is the very essence of the equal protection guarantee. As this Court explained in *Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141, 147 (1940), “the equal protection clause does not require that all persons be dealt with identically, but it does require that a distinction made have some relevance to the purpose for which the classification is made.” A plaintiff who does not allege membership in any class cannot invoke that standard.

**II. The “Class of One” Theory Has No Foundation in This Court’s Pre-1970 Precedent.**

The Seventh Circuit’s “spiteful effort” test is a novel invention. Every pre-1970 case in which this Court found an equal protection violation involved either a law that on its face created a suspect classification, or a pattern of enforcement that systematically harmed a particular group. See, e.g., *Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada*, 305 U.S. 337, 349–50 (1938) (state must provide equal legal education to black students); *Skinner v. Oklahoma*, 316 U.S. 535, 541 (1942) (sterilization law that discriminated between classes of criminals). None of these decisions suggests that a single act of personal spite, unconnected to any group animus, can violate the Clause.

In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944), the Court held that even “the unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination.” Critically, the Court added that the discrimination must be “directed against a person or class of persons.” *Id.* at 11. Here, respondent alleged only that the Village singled her out because of “spite.” She did not claim that she was a member of any identifiable class. *Snowden* thus forecloses her claim.

**III. The Rational Basis Standard Does Not Authorize Judicial Review of Isolated, Non-Classification-Based Decisions.**

When a plaintiff challenges a law or policy as facially discriminatory, this Court applies deferential rational basis review. See *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78–79 (1911) (classification valid if “any state of facts reasonably can be conceived” to justify it). That standard permits a wide range of legislative classifications. But it does not empower courts to second-guess a single administrative decision in the absence of a class-based challenge. As the Court held in *F.S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia*, 253 U.S. 412, 415 (1920), a classification must “rest upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation.” That inquiry is meaningless when applied to a single individual.

Moreover, the Court has consistently rejected attempts to transform equal protection into a general “fairness” requirement for state action. See *Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. Matthews*, 174 U.S. 96, 106 (1899) (classification need not be “mathematically precise”); *Gulf, Colorado & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. Ellis*, 165 U.S. 150, 165 (1897) (classification must be “based on some real difference”). The Seventh Circuit’s approach would invite courts to police every allegedly spiteful government decision, even those that affect no group. That is not the role of the Equal Protection Clause.

**IV. The Seventh Circuit’s Test Conflicts with the Principle That Equal Protection Claims Require a Showing of Class-Based Discrimination.**

This Court has repeatedly stated that the Equal Protection Clause “is directed against discrimination on account of race or class.” *Hayes v. Missouri*, 120 U.S. 68, 72 (1887). And in *Connolly v. Union Sewer Pipe Co.*, 184 U.S. 540, 564 (1902), the Court struck down a law that exempted certain businesses from antitrust liability, but only because the exemption created an arbitrary classification. The violation arose from the classification itself, not from the legislature’s subjective motive. Here, respondent does not challenge any classification; she challenges a single, discretionary decision. The Clause does not reach that far.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
