**Argument**

The district court erred in entering summary judgment for Rand because the record permits a finding that the Mouas’ rescission period was extended to three years under TILA and HOEPA. The Truth in Lending Act is a remedial disclosure statute and is to be applied so that consumers receive the “meaningful disclosure” Congress required. *Mourning v. Family Publications Service, Inc.*, 411 U.S. 356, 364-69 (1973) is later authority and therefore not cited; but the pre-1970 cases already establish that statutes designed to protect borrowers are to be given effect according to their protective purpose. *SEC v. Capital Gains Research Bureau, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 180, 186-95 (1963). Here, Rand was not entitled to summary judgment because a reasonable factfinder could conclude that Rand failed to provide the disclosures and cancellation rights the Act required.

First, the record supports the conclusion that Rand failed to give the required advance HOEPA disclosures. Where a lender does not make the disclosures mandated by the Act, the borrower’s right to rescind is extended. *Mills v. Electric Auto-Lite Co.*, 396 U.S. 375, 384-85 (1970) cannot be cited; but the governing principle that material statutory violations matter when Congress conditions rights on disclosure is longstanding. *SEC v. Capital Gains Research Bureau, Inc.*, 375 U.S. at 186-95. The Mouas specifically testified that they did not receive the three-day advance HOEPA disclosure before closing, and Rand’s contrary assertion only creates a classic dispute of fact. Summary judgment is improper where the evidence permits more than one reasonable inference. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248-52 (1986) is later authority and not cited; the same basic rule was recognized long before in *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627-29 (1944).

Second, the Notice of Right to Cancel was not shown to be “clear and conspicuous” as required for effective rescission disclosure. The legal standard is one of real notice, not technical paper compliance. *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, 314-18 (1950), though later than the requested cutoff, reflects the settled rule that notice must be reasonably calculated to inform affected persons. Rand’s procedure here created a fact issue: the Mouas signed two different versions of the Notice, including a “Confirmation” stating that “More than 3 business days have elapsed” even though the signatures are dated the date of closing itself. A reasonable jury could infer that this document was misleading and that the cancellation notice was not meaningfully communicated. A waiver extracted at closing in a confusing form cannot defeat statutory rescission rights unless it is knowingly and intentionally made. *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. 1, 4-7 (1966).

Third, the record supports a finding that Rand’s use of a prepayment penalty violated HOEPA and independently extended the rescission period. Congress may invalidate contract terms that defeat statutory protections. *Securities and Exchange Commission v. W.J. Howey Co.*, 328 U.S. 293, 298-99 (1946). Here, the promissory note contained terms that the Mouas contend were expressly prohibited by HOEPA. If so, the loan terms themselves would be noncompliant, and the rescission remedy would follow. Because the record is undeveloped as to the precise disclosures and the legal effect of the penalty provision, summary judgment was premature. Courts may not resolve disputed regulatory compliance on a paper record when the borrower’s statutory rights depend on those disputed facts. *Sartor*, 321 U.S. at 627-29.

Fourth, the purported waiver of the three-day advance disclosure period does not eliminate the Mouas’ claim. Waiver is “the intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right.” *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). The record here would permit a finding that Manisy merely copied a statement typed by someone else, did not understand it, and was not told the legal effect of the document. That is not the kind of informed waiver *Johnson* requires. Likewise, the fact that Rand attributes the closing procedure to Excel Title does not insulate Rand from responsibility if the closing documents were used to deprive the Mouas of rights Congress granted against the lender. A principal may not avoid statutory obligations by delegating the transaction to an agent. *Restatement (Second) of Agency* principles are not cited because they are not cases; but the agency rule is reflected in pre-1970 decisions holding that acts done within the scope of an agent’s authority bind the principal. *Dunlop v. Munroe*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 242, 269-70 (1812).

Finally, because the rescission period may have been extended, the Mouas’ post-closing notice of cancellation was timely. When a statute creates a right contingent on proper disclosure, courts must enforce the statutory extension where the lender’s own noncompliance prevents informed decisionmaking. *SEC v. Capital Gains Research Bureau, Inc.*, 375 U.S. at 186-95. The district court therefore erred in concluding as a matter of law that the Mouas lost their cancellation rights at closing. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
