**ARGUMENT**

Section 355(f) confers jurisdiction over “final decisions of the Board,” but the Board’s refusal to reopen a benefits claim that became final years earlier is not a final decision on the merits of any claim. The Fifth Circuit therefore correctly dismissed Salinas’s petition.

**I. The statutory grant of review reaches only final decisions on the merits rendered at the consummation of the administrative process.**

Section 355(f) confines the courts of appeals to Board decisions on the merits of a claim after the administrative remedy has been exhausted. That construction follows from the settled meaning of administrative finality. A reviewable “final order” is one “which imposes an obligation, denies a right, or fixes some legal relationship as the consummation of the administrative process.” *Chicago & Southern Air Lines v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 333 U.S. 103, 113 (1948); see also *Federal Radio Comm’n v. General Elec. Co.*, 281 U.S. 464, 467 (1930); *Rochester Tel. Corp. v. United States*, 307 U.S. 125, 131–33 (1939). Collateral, preliminary, or procedural dispositions are not reviewable final orders. *FTC v. Standard Oil Co.*, 355 U.S. 396, 404–05 (1958); *Amalgamated Utility Workers v. Consolidated Edison Co.*, 309 U.S. 261, 264 (1940).

**II. A refusal to reopen is collateral to the merits and does not adjudicate the claimant’s entitlement.**

Salinas’s 2006 application was denied on the merits. His untimely request for reconsideration was denied for want of good cause, and that denial became final on February 9, 2007. The Board’s later refusal to reopen was not a decision whether Salinas was entitled to a disability annuity; it was a decision that the regulatory conditions for reopening—new and material evidence or administrative error within four years, 20 C.F.R. § 261.2—had not been satisfied. The refusal left the final 2006 determination untouched. A disposition that neither grants nor denies a claim on its merits, but merely declines to revisit a long-final adjudication, is not a “final decision” within the meaning of the review statute.

**III. Finality and the statutory time limits bar this collateral attack.**

When Congress prescribes the time and manner for judicial review of an administrative decision, the statutory period is a condition of the right to review and must be strictly observed. *United States v. Great N. Ry. Co.*, 287 U.S. 144, 151 (1932). Salinas permitted the review period to lapse in 2007. The 2006 denial then became final and binding, *United States v. N. Pac. Ry. Co.*, 311 U.S. 317, 341 (1940), and is res judicata. *United States v. Utah Constr. & Mining Co.*, 384 U.S. 394, 421–22 (1966). To treat the Board’s 2015 refusal to reopen as a fresh reviewable decision would permit a claimant to accomplish by indirection what he could not do directly: obtain merits review years after the statutory deadline. A rule allowing litigants to defeat administrative finality by requesting reopening at any time would convert the Board’s narrow reopening regulations into an end-run around the Act’s limits and would eviscerate the exhaustion requirement. *Myers v. Bethlehem Shipbuilding Corp.*, 303 U.S. 41, 50–51 (1938); *United States v. Abilene & S. Ry. Co.*, 265 U.S. 274, 282 (1924).

**IV. *Szostak* is an outlier and does not govern this case.**

Salinas’s reliance on *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), is unpersuasive. *Szostak* treated the Board’s denial of reopening as if it were a merits determination, but a refusal to reopen a final administrative adjudication produces no decision on entitlement at all. It is a discretionary refusal to revisit a closed matter—not the “consummation of the administrative process” with respect to the claim. The finality principles this Court has long articulated compel the conclusion that the Board’s refusal here is not judicially reviewable.

The Fifth Circuit’s dismissal of Salinas’s petition should be affirmed.
