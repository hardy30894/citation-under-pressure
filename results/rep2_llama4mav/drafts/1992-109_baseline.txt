The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that a defendant is competent to waive counsel or plead guilty only if he has the capacity for "reasoned choice" among the alternatives available to him (Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); Evans v. Raines, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986)). This standard is more stringent than the standard for competency to stand trial, which only requires a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and the ability to assist counsel (Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975)).

The trial court's failure to apply the "reasoned choice" standard to Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty plea was a clear error. The state court's post-conviction ruling, which relied on psychiatric evaluations that only assessed Moran's competency to stand trial, did not cure this error (Moran v. Godinez, 972 F.2d 263, 272 (9th Cir. 1992)). The psychiatric evaluations, conducted by Dr. O'Gorman and Dr. Jurasky, focused on Moran's ability to understand the charges and assist his attorney, but did not assess his capacity for "reasoned choice" (Id. at 272-273).

The record raises significant doubts about Moran's competency to waive counsel and plead guilty. Moran had attempted suicide, was taking multiple medications, and gave monosyllabic responses to the court's questions (Id. at 268-269). Under these circumstances, the trial court should have held a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights (Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973)).

The Ninth Circuit's decision should be affirmed because the trial court's failure to apply the "reasoned choice" standard and hold a competency hearing violated Moran's due process rights. The state court's post-conviction ruling, which applied the wrong legal standard, does not constitute a valid finding that Moran was competent to waive his constitutional rights (Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963)). Therefore, Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty plea were not knowing, voluntary, and intelligent, and his conviction and sentence should be reversed.
