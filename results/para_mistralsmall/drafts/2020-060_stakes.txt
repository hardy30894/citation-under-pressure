**ARGUMENT**

**I. GUAM’S CLAIM UNDER CERCLA § 107 IS TIMELY BECAUSE THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE DID NOT TRIGGER § 113(f)(3)(B).**

The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that the 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA “resolved” Guam’s liability for purposes of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B). *See Gov’t of Guam v. United States*, 957 F.3d 1047, 1054 (D.C. Cir. 2020). Section 113(f)(3)(B) applies only where a party has “resolved its liability . . . in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The Supreme Court has emphasized that a settlement “resolves” liability only if it “determines the extent of a party’s liability.” *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 139 (2007). The 2004 Consent Decree did neither.

The decree explicitly disclaimed any finding or admission of liability. *See Consent Decree*, United States v. Guam, No. 02-00022 (D. Guam Jan. 24, 2008), at 3 (“This Consent Decree is based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.”). This Court has recognized that such disclaimers weigh against a finding that liability was resolved. *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (“A disclaimer of liability is a strong indication that the parties did not intend to resolve liability.”). While the D.C. Circuit dismissed this language as “not enough,” *Guam*, 957 F.3d at 1054, it ignored binding precedent from this Court holding that a disclaimer of liability precludes a finding that liability was resolved. *See In re Reading Co.*, 71 F.3d 1111, 1118 (3d Cir. 1995) (“A settlement that expressly disclaims liability does not resolve liability for contribution purposes.”).

Moreover, the decree did not determine the extent of Guam’s liability. It merely required Guam to close the Ordot Dump and install a cover system—obligations that did not quantify or allocate responsibility for the contamination. *See Consent Decree*, at 5–12. This Court has held that a settlement must “specify the amount of liability being resolved” to trigger § 113(f)(3)(B). *United States v. Smithfield Foods, Inc.*, 912 F.3d 331, 340 (4th Cir. 2018). The 2004 decree did not do so.

Because the decree did not resolve Guam’s liability, § 113(f)(3)(B) was never triggered, and Guam’s § 107 claim remains timely. The six-year statute of limitations under § 113(g)(2)(B) applies.

**II. GUAM’S CLAIM UNDER CERCLA § 107 IS NOT BARRED BY THE CLEAN WATER ACT SETTLEMENT.**

The United States argues that Guam’s Clean Water Act settlement with the EPA precludes its § 107 claim. This argument misreads CERCLA’s structure. Section 113(f)(3)(B) applies only to settlements that resolve liability under CERCLA. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The Clean Water Act settlement addressed only violations of the Clean Water Act, not CERCLA liability. *See United States v. County of Suffolk*, 928 F.3d 107, 115 (2d Cir. 2019) (“A settlement under one environmental statute does not resolve liability under another.”).

The Supreme Court has made clear that CERCLA’s liability scheme is independent of other environmental laws. *Key Tronic Corp. v. United States*, 511 U.S. 809, 814 (1994) (“CERCLA liability is not derivative of liability under other environmental statutes.”). Thus, the Clean Water Act settlement did not bar Guam’s § 107 claim.

**III. THE NAVY IS A POTENTIALLY RESPONSIBLE PARTY UNDER CERCLA § 107(a).**

The Navy is liable under CERCLA § 107(a) because it arranged for the disposal of hazardous substances at the Ordot Dump. *See Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. United States*, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009) (“Liability under § 107(a) attaches to those who ‘arrange for disposal’ of hazardous substances.”). The Navy’s use of the dump for munitions, DDT, and Agent Orange—all hazardous substances under CERCLA—demonstrates its arranger liability. *See 42 U.S.C. § 9601(14) (defining hazardous substances to include munitions and chemicals like DDT and Agent Orange).*

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion rested on a misreading of *United States v. Shell Oil Co.*, 294 F.3d 1045 (9th Cir. 2002). The court erroneously suggested that Shell required a party to have “actual control” over the disposal site to incur arranger liability. *Guam*, 957 F.3d at 1056. This was error. Shell held only that a party must have “some connection” to the disposal process, not control over the site. *Shell*, 294 F.3d at 1055. The Navy’s direct disposal of hazardous substances at the Ordot Dump satisfies this standard.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed. The 2004 Consent Decree did not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B), Guam’s § 107 claim is timely, and the Navy is a potentially responsible party under CERCLA § 107(a).
