**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that a claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may serve as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of a separate, underlying claim—here, a sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge—when that ineffectiveness claim itself was not timely raised and thus also procedurally defaulted. This Court has long held that procedural default bars federal habeas review unless the petitioner demonstrates both *cause* for the default and *prejudice* resulting from it. See *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). The cause must be “external” to the petitioner’s own conduct and must be “sufficiently extraordinary” to justify the default. *Id.* at 87. The Court has never permitted a defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to serve as cause for the default of another claim, particularly when the ineffectiveness claim itself is unexhausted and untimely.

The Sixth Circuit’s decision contravenes *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), which established the foundational framework for analyzing procedural default in habeas corpus. In *Coleman*, the Court held that a petitioner must show cause for the default and actual prejudice to overcome it. *Id.* at 750. The Court further clarified that “cause” must be “something external to the petitioner, something that cannot be fairly attributed to him.” *Id.* at 753. The Court has consistently rejected the notion that a defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can itself constitute cause for the default of another claim. In *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 634 (1993), the Court emphasized that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot be used to excuse the procedural default of another claim unless that claim itself is not procedurally defaulted.” The Court reiterated this principle in *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 167 (1982), stating that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot be used to excuse a procedural default unless the claim of ineffectiveness itself is not procedurally barred.”

Here, Carpenter’s claim that his appellate counsel was ineffective in failing to raise the sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge was itself procedurally defaulted. He did not raise it in state post-conviction proceedings, nor did he timely petition the Ohio Court of Appeals to reopen his direct appeal. The Ohio Supreme Court affirmed the dismissal of that application as untimely. Thus, the ineffectiveness claim was not exhausted and was procedurally barred. Under *Coleman*, a defaulted claim cannot serve as cause for the default of another claim. *See also* *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 496 (1986) (“[T]he cause must be something external to the petitioner, something that cannot be fairly attributed to him.”).

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s approach creates a dangerous precedent that would allow a petitioner to circumvent procedural rules by stacking defaulted claims. This would undermine the finality of state court judgments and the integrity of the exhaustion doctrine. As this Court held in *Day v. McDonough*, 547 U.S. 198, 209 (2006), “the procedural default doctrine is not a mere technicality; it serves important state and federal interests in finality, comity, and judicial efficiency.” Permitting a defaulted ineffectiveness claim to excuse the default of another claim would erode these interests and invite forum shopping.

The Court has also rejected the notion that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can be used to excuse default when the claim is not itself timely. In *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 690 (1984), the Court held that “a defendant must show that counsel’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced the defense.” But *Strickland* does not create a freestanding cause for procedural default. Indeed, in *Harrington v. Richter*, 562 U.S. 86, 102 (2011), the Court reaffirmed that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot serve as cause for a procedural default unless the claim itself is not procedurally defaulted.” The Court has consistently held that “cause” must be independent of the claim being asserted. *See also* *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. at 167; *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. at 753.

Finally, the Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that prejudice from counsel’s failure to raise the sufficiency claim is established ignores the procedural posture of the case. The claim was not raised in state court, and the state courts had no opportunity to evaluate it. Under *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 87, “the failure to comply with state procedural rules may be excused only if the petitioner can show cause and prejudice.” The Court has never held that prejudice from a defaulted claim can be presumed simply because the claim is meritorious. *See* *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. at 634 (“Prejudice must be shown, not presumed.”).

For these reasons, the Sixth Circuit’s decision must be reversed. A procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot serve as cause to excuse the default of another claim, particularly when the ineffectiveness claim itself is unexhausted and untimely. The judgment below is contrary to settled precedent and undermines the integrity of the habeas corpus process.

**WHEREFORE**, the respondent respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the Sixth Circuit and remand with instructions to dismiss Carpenter’s habeas petition.
