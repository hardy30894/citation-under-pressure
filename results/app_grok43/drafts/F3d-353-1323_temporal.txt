**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that the expropriation and subsequent offer of substitute land fell outside any exception to foreign sovereign immunity. The Government of Punjab’s dispatch of a representative to the United States to negotiate an exchange of parcels was a commercial transaction that produced a direct effect here.

Long before the modern statute, the Supreme Court recognized that a foreign sovereign’s entry into ordinary commercial dealings within the United States subjects it to judicial process. In *Republic of Mexico v. Hoffman*, 324 U.S. 30, 35 (1945), the Court observed that immunity had been “restricted” when the sovereign engaged in “private” or “commercial” activity. The same principle appears in *Ex parte Republic of Peru*, 318 U.S. 578, 588 (1943), where the Court stated that immunity does not extend to acts “of a private nature” performed within the territory of the forum. The offer of alternative land, conveyed by an official emissary on American soil, was precisely such an act.

That contact also satisfied the requirement of a direct effect in the United States. In *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945), the Court held that “minimum contacts” giving rise to “obligations” within the forum justify the exercise of jurisdiction. The Lahore High Court’s acceptance of the exchange, later rejected by the Supreme Court of Pakistan, created precisely such an obligation; the negotiations occurred here, and any remedy for their failure must be sought here.

Because the conduct at issue was commercial in character and produced a direct effect within the United States, the district court possessed subject-matter jurisdiction. The judgment of dismissal should be reversed.
