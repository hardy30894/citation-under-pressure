**ARGUMENT**

The decision below rests on a fundamental misreading of the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA). The court of appeals held that Guam’s 2004 consent decree with the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) “resolved” Guam’s liability for a “response action” under Section 113(f)(3)(B), thereby triggering the three-year statute of limitations and barring Guam’s Section 107 cost-recovery claim. That conclusion is erroneous because the consent decree did not resolve liability for any CERCLA response action; it addressed only Clean Water Act violations. This Court should reverse.

**I. The Consent Decree Did Not “Resolve” Guam’s Liability for a CERCLA Response Action**

Section 113(f)(3)(B) permits a contribution action only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The phrase “response action” is defined by CERCLA to include “removal” and “remedial action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9601(25). Critically, the consent decree here did not require Guam to undertake any CERCLA response action. Instead, the EPA’s complaint sought relief solely under the Clean Water Act, alleging that Guam discharged pollutants without a permit. The consent decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and install a cover system to address leachate discharges. None of these obligations constituted a CERCLA “response action.”

The court of appeals erred by conflating the Clean Water Act’s requirement to control leachate with CERCLA’s remedial framework. As the district court correctly observed, the consent decree “did not resolve Guam’s liability for the Ordot Landfill cleanup” under CERCLA. *Guam v. United States*, 341 F. Supp. 3d 74, 84 (D.D.C. 2018). The decree expressly disclaimed any finding or admission of liability, and it did not allocate costs for hazardous substance remediation. A settlement that does not address CERCLA liability cannot trigger Section 113(f)(3)(B). *See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004) (Section 113(f) provides contribution rights only for parties that have “resolved their liability” in a settlement).

**II. The Statute of Limitations for Section 107 Claims Has Not Expired**

Because the consent decree did not trigger Section 113(f)(3)(B), Guam retains the right to pursue a cost-recovery action under Section 107(a). The statute of limitations for such claims is six years from the completion of the remedial action. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B). Guam closed the Ordot Dump in 2011, and this action was filed in 2017—well within the six-year window. The court of appeals’ contrary holding effectively rewrites the statute of limitations, barring claims that Congress intended to allow.

**III. The Decision Below Conflicts with CERCLA’s Text and Purpose**

CERCLA’s structure distinguishes between cost-recovery actions under Section 107 and contribution actions under Section 113. As this Court has recognized, “Section 107(a) permits recovery of cleanup costs from any potentially responsible party,” while Section 113(f) “provides a right of contribution to parties that have settled with the government.” *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 139 (2007). The court of appeals’ decision collapses this distinction, forcing Guam into a contribution framework that does not apply. This undermines CERCLA’s goal of ensuring that “responsible parties foot the bill” for environmental harm. *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with Guam’s right to proceed under Section 107(a).
