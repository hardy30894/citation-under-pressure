**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE MASSACHUSETTS BUFFER ZONE IS A VALID TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION**

The First Circuit correctly held that the Massachusetts 35-foot buffer zone is a valid time-place-manner regulation under *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781 (1989). Under *Ward*, a regulation is constitutional if it is content-neutral, serves a significant government interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication.

First, the law is content-neutral. *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288 (1984), requires that a regulation be justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech. The Massachusetts statute applies equally to all persons within the buffer zone, regardless of their message. It does not target the petitioners’ speech based on its viewpoint.

Second, the law serves a significant government interest. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), recognized that the state has a compelling interest in protecting the safety and privacy of patients and clinic staff. The First Circuit properly found that the buffer zone is a reasonable means to ensure the safe passage of persons wishing to enter healthcare facilities.

Third, the law is narrowly tailored to serve that interest. *Madsen v. Women’s Health Center*, 512 U.S. 753 (1994), requires that restrictions be no broader than necessary to achieve the government interest. The 35-foot radius is a reasonable restriction that prevents obstruction without unduly burdening speech.

Finally, the law leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. *Madsen* requires that a regulation not effectively silence a substantial portion of the speaker’s message. The petitioners retain the right to speak from outside the buffer zone, distribute literature, and engage in silent prayer.

**II. THE LAW IS CONSISTENT WITH *HILL V. COLORADO***

The First Circuit properly applied the framework established in *Hill v. Colorado*. *Hill* upheld a statute prohibiting certain communicative activities within 100 feet of healthcare facility entrances. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715. The Massachusetts statute is less restrictive than the Colorado statute upheld in *Hill*, creating a 35-foot buffer zone rather than a 100-foot zone.

*Hill* explicitly allows for exemptions for persons entering or leaving the facility, employees, and law enforcement. The Massachusetts statute contains identical exemptions. *Hill* also recognized that sidewalks and areas outside healthcare clinics are "quintessential" public forums. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715. The First Circuit correctly concluded that the Massachusetts law is a permissible regulation of conduct in a public forum.

**III. THE LAW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIRST AMENDMENT**

The petitioners’ arguments regarding prior restraint and vagueness fail. *Meyer v. Grant*, 486 U.S. 414 (1988), held that a permit requirement for a public demonstration is a prior restraint, but a time-place-manner regulation is not. The Massachusetts law is a time-place-manner regulation, not a prior restraint.

Furthermore, the law is not unconstitutionally vague. *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104 (1972), requires that a law give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited. The Massachusetts statute clearly defines the buffer zone and the persons exempted from it.

For these reasons, the First Circuit’s decision should be affirmed.
