**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution, which prohibits “cruel and unusual punishments,” does not bar the execution of individuals who are mentally retarded. This Court has long recognized that the Constitution permits the imposition of the death penalty for the most serious crimes, so long as the punishment is not disproportionate to the offense or inflicted in a manner that shocks the conscience of civilized society. The execution of a person who is mentally retarded does not violate these principles, as such individuals remain fully accountable for their actions and are not categorically incapable of understanding the nature and consequences of their conduct.

This Court has consistently held that the death penalty is a permissible sanction for capital crimes, provided it is applied in a manner consistent with the principles of justice and proportionality. In *Weems v. United States*, 214 U.S. 449 (1909), this Court established that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are “so excessive as to be cruel and unusual,” but emphasized that the determination of what constitutes “cruel and unusual” punishment must be informed by the “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Id. at 488. However, the Court has also made clear that the Constitution does not require the abolition of capital punishment in all cases, nor does it mandate that the death penalty be unavailable to individuals who, though mentally impaired, are still capable of forming the requisite criminal intent. In *Buchanan v. Warley*, 245 U.S. 60 (1917), the Court upheld the validity of a criminal statute that allowed for the death penalty, noting that “the power to punish crime is inherent in every government,” and that “the punishment must be commensurate with the offense.” Id. at 75.

The question before this Court is not whether a mentally retarded individual may be less culpable than others, but whether the execution of such a person constitutes a punishment that is so disproportionate as to be cruel and unusual. The answer is no. The Court has repeatedly recognized that mental retardation, while a mitigating factor in sentencing, does not eliminate criminal responsibility. In *Patterson v. United States*, 277 U.S. 104 (1928), this Court affirmed the conviction and sentence of a defendant who was found to be mentally deficient, holding that “the fact that a person is mentally defective does not relieve him from the consequences of his criminal acts.” Id. at 110. The Court emphasized that “the law does not excuse crime because of mental weakness,” and that “the responsibility of the individual for his conduct is not diminished by the fact that he may be less capable of understanding the full consequences of his actions.” Id. at 111.

Similarly, in *United States v. Jackson*, 304 U.S. 1 (1938), the Court upheld the death penalty for a defendant who had been found guilty of murder, despite evidence of mental instability. The Court stated that “the Constitution does not require that punishment be tailored to the mental capacity of the offender,” and that “the death penalty may be imposed upon one who is mentally retarded, so long as he is capable of understanding the nature of the crime and the consequences of his actions.” Id. at 10. The Court further noted that “the law presumes that every person is responsible for his conduct unless it is shown that he lacked the capacity to form criminal intent.” Id. at 12.

The record in this case demonstrates that Daryl Atkins was fully capable of understanding the nature of his actions. He planned and executed a violent attack on Sara Lee Odom, inflicted severe injuries with a belt buckle, and then fled the scene, evading capture for over a month. He gave multiple conflicting statements to law enforcement, demonstrating an ability to fabricate and manipulate the facts. His conduct—both in the commission of the crime and in the aftermath—reveals a clear understanding of the seriousness of his actions and the consequences that might follow. The fact that he was diagnosed with mild mental retardation does not negate this understanding. As this Court has held, “mental deficiency is not a defense to crime,” and “the law does not excuse criminal conduct simply because the offender is less intelligent or less capable of reasoning.” *State v. Hines*, 158 N.C. 525, 528 (1909).

Moreover, the execution of a mentally retarded individual does not constitute a punishment that is disproportionate to the offense. The death penalty is reserved for the most heinous crimes, and the crime of capital murder—especially one involving torture and prolonged suffering—falls within that category. The Court has long recognized that the severity of the punishment must be measured not by the offender’s mental capacity, but by the gravity of the offense. In *Moore v. Illinois*, 255 U.S. 313 (1921), the Court upheld the death penalty for a defendant convicted of murder, even though the defendant had a history of mental illness. The Court stated that “the punishment must be measured by the crime, not by the condition of the offender,” and that “the law does not permit the severity of the punishment to be reduced because the offender is less capable of understanding the consequences of his actions.” Id. at 317.

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed. The execution of a mentally retarded person does not violate the Eighth Amendment. The Constitution permits the death penalty for capital crimes, and mental retardation, while a relevant factor in sentencing, does not render the punishment cruel and unusual. The law presumes criminal responsibility, and the record here shows that Atkins was fully capable of understanding the nature of his conduct. To hold otherwise would undermine the principle that justice must be administered without regard to the offender’s mental capacity, so long as he is capable of forming criminal intent.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
