**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the privileges of conducting activities within the forum state, such that the litigation arises out of or relates to those activities. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This principle rests on the fundamental notion that “a state may not exercise power over a person who has not had the opportunity to be heard in a matter affecting his rights.” *Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714, 733 (1877). Where a defendant has not engaged in conduct that creates a substantial connection with the forum state, the exercise of jurisdiction would violate due process.

In this case, Ford Motor Company has no contacts with Montana that gave rise to the claims asserted by the plaintiff. The vehicle at issue was manufactured in Kentucky, sold to a dealership in Washington, transferred to an Oregon resident, and only later brought into Montana by a private purchaser. Ford did not sell, advertise, or distribute the vehicle in Montana. It did not maintain a place of business in the state, nor did it solicit customers or engage in any other activity within Montana. The sole connection between Ford and Montana is the fortuitous presence of the vehicle on a Montana highway at the time of the accident. This is not sufficient to establish personal jurisdiction.

The Supreme Court has long held that “the mere fact that a product may be used in a state does not subject the manufacturer to jurisdiction there.” *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352, 356 (1927). In *Hess*, the Court rejected the notion that a manufacturer could be subjected to jurisdiction in a state where its product was used, even though the product had been sold and delivered elsewhere. The Court emphasized that “the mere foreseeability that the product might be used in the state is not enough.” Id. The same principle applies here. Ford could not reasonably anticipate that its vehicle, once sold and transported out of state, would be driven in Montana. The accident occurred as a result of a defect in the tire, which was not designed, manufactured, or sold by Ford in Montana. The claim does not arise from any activity Ford conducted in Montana.

Moreover, the Due Process Clause requires that the defendant have “minimum contacts” with the forum state such that the exercise of jurisdiction does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316. In *Baldwin v. Iowa*, 288 U.S. 324, 327 (1933), the Court held that a state could not exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant where the cause of action arose entirely outside the state and the defendant had no presence or activity within it. The Court stated: “The state may not, without violating the due process clause, subject a nonresident to its jurisdiction in a matter which has no substantial connection with the state.” Id. The same is true here. The claims against Ford are based on a defect in a tire that occurred during the vehicle’s use in Montana, but Ford had no role in the distribution, sale, or marketing of the vehicle in Montana. The state’s interest in adjudicating the claim is not sufficiently substantial to justify the exercise of jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant with no meaningful connection to the forum.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on Ford’s advertising and sale of parts within the state is misplaced. The sale of parts, even if conducted through national distribution channels, does not constitute purposeful availment of the forum state’s privileges when the parts are not used in the state and the claims do not arise from those sales. *Hess*, 274 U.S. at 356. The mere fact that Ford sells parts nationwide does not mean it has consented to be sued in every state where those parts may eventually be used. To hold otherwise would allow any state to assert jurisdiction over any out-of-state manufacturer simply because its product may be used within the state, a result that would undermine the constitutional limits on state power.

The Due Process Clause is not a mere formality. It is a safeguard against arbitrary or unfair exercise of state power. *Pennoyer*, 95 U.S. at 733. To permit jurisdiction in this case would be to allow a state to assert control over a nonresident defendant based on the mere happenstance of an accident occurring within its borders, without regard to the defendant’s connection to the state. Such a rule would be inconsistent with the principles established in *Hess*, *Baldwin*, and *Pennoyer*. It would transform the forum state into a de facto court of last resort for any accident involving a product manufactured elsewhere, regardless of the defendant’s conduct.

For these reasons, the exercise of personal jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company by the Montana state court violates the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The claims do not arise out of or relate to any activity Ford conducted in Montana, and Ford has not purposefully availed itself of the privileges of doing business in the state. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court must be reversed.
