**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the unconstitutional policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the revision of Georgia Gwinnett College’s (GGC) “Freedom of Expression Policy” and the removal of the challenged provision in the Student Code of Conduct rendered Appellants’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot. Moreover, the claim for nominal damages alone cannot revive a case that has become moot. This Court has long held that “a case is moot when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). Here, the challenged policies have been permanently revised, and Appellants have not demonstrated a reasonable expectation that the prior policies will be reinstated. The case is therefore moot, and the judgment below must be affirmed.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “a plaintiff’s interest in a particular remedy is not sufficient to preserve standing once the underlying injury has been eliminated.” *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), aff’d, *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 587 U.S. ___ (2019). In *Flanigan’s*, this Court held that a claim for nominal damages alone cannot save a case from mootness where the challenged policy has been rescinded and the plaintiff has no ongoing interest in the relief sought. The Court explained that “the mere possibility of future harm, without more, is insufficient to maintain standing.” *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256. Here, GGC has unambiguously terminated the prior policies and replaced them with a regime that permits expressive activity anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances. The district court properly concluded that there is “no reasonable basis to expect that [GGC] will return to them.” App. at 6. This is not a case where the policy was merely suspended or where the plaintiff faced a credible threat of future enforcement. The change is permanent and comprehensive.

Appellants argue that their claim remains live because they seek nominal damages. But this argument is foreclosed by *Flanigan’s*, which explicitly rejected the notion that nominal damages can “save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.” 868 F.3d at 1255. The Court in *Flanigan’s* emphasized that “nominal damages are not a substitute for injunctive or declaratory relief” and that “a plaintiff cannot maintain standing simply by seeking a token award.” Id. at 1257. The Court further noted that “the availability of nominal damages does not transform a moot case into a live controversy.” Id. Appellants’ attempt to distinguish *Flanigan’s* on the ground that their challenge was “as-applied” rather than facial is unavailing. The Court in *Flanigan’s* made clear that the distinction between facial and as-applied challenges does not alter the mootness analysis when the challenged policy has been rescinded. Id. at 1258. The fact that Appellants were personally subjected to enforcement of the prior policy does not create a live controversy once the policy is gone and the plaintiff has no intention of returning to the same conduct.

Appellants also contend that they suffered “actual injury” in the form of time, travel, and reputational harm. But the district court correctly observed that “compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit must be based on actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the ‘abstract value’ of the constitutional rights that may have been violated.” *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000). Appellants’ complaint contains no allegations of monetary loss, physical pain, emotional distress, or reputational damage. They merely assert that they “suffered, and continue to suffer, irreparable harm,” without specifying what that harm is. This is insufficient to support a claim for compensatory damages. *Id.* at 1231. The request for “nominal damages” in the prayer for relief, coupled with the absence of any factual allegations of actual injury, confirms that Appellants’ claim is based solely on the violation of their constitutional rights—precisely the type of abstract injury that *Flanigan’s* held insufficient to preserve standing.

Finally, Appellants argue that the district court abused its discretion by denying them leave to amend their complaint to seek compensatory damages. But the district court did not err. Appellants sought leave to amend through a response to a motion to dismiss, a procedurally improper method. *Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018). Moreover, they failed to specify the substance of the proposed amendment or attach a proposed pleading. *Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC*, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018). Even if the amendment were simple, the procedural defect remains. The district court did not prevent Appellants from seeking leave to amend after judgment; it merely declined to entertain a motion filed in the wrong form. *Czeremcha v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers, AFL-CIO*, 724 F.2d 1552, 1556 (11th Cir. 1984). There was no abuse of discretion.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit must be affirmed. The revision of GGC’s policies has rendered Appellants’ claims moot, and nominal damages alone cannot revive a case that has become moot. The judgment below is correct and should be upheld.
