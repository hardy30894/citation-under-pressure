**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether debts arising from fraud, including statutory treble damages and attorney’s fees awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, are dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code—requires this Court to interpret the scope of § 523(a)(2)(A), which excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud.” The Third Circuit correctly held that such debts are nondischargeable, and its decision is consistent with this Court’s precedent that *“the appropriate starting point in every case involving construction of a statute” is the language of the statute itself* (Kelly v. Robinson, 479 U.S. 36, 41 (1986)).

The Bankruptcy Court’s finding that Cohen committed *actual fraud* under § 523(a)(2)(A) is supported by the record and must be upheld. Cohen, a landlord aware of the Hoboken Rent Control ordinance, charged rents in excess of the statutory limit to tenants who were largely non-English-speaking and lacked legal sophistication. He made no inquiry of the Rent Leveling Board, consulted no attorney, and never read the ordinance. Yet he charged rents that were demonstrably unlawful. This conduct constitutes an *implicit misrepresentation* that the rents he charged were lawful. As this Court has recognized, “a false representation may be made by conduct or omission as well as by words” (Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. 59, 70 (1995)). Cohen’s silence, coupled with the imposition of unlawful rent, amounted to a material misrepresentation that the rent was lawful. The Bankruptcy Court’s conclusion that this constituted *actual fraud* under § 523(a)(2)(A) is not clearly erroneous and is entitled to deference.

Moreover, the requisite *scienter*—knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth—is established by Cohen’s conduct. He failed to seek any guidance from the Rent Leveling Board, despite being on notice of overcharges. He made no effort to verify the legality of his rent increases, even though he had previously sought to pass on city-imposed tax and water surcharges. This pattern of conduct—ignoring regulatory requirements while profiting from them—demonstrates a *reckless indifference to the truth*, which this Court has held sufficient to satisfy the *intent to deceive* and *knowledge of falsity* elements under § 523(a)(2)(A) (In re Cohn, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118–19 (3d Cir. 1995); In re Woolley, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr. E.D. Va. 1991)). The Third Circuit correctly applied this standard, concluding that Cohen’s conduct rose above mere negligence and amounted to reckless disregard.

The more difficult question—whether the *statutory treble damages* and *attorney’s fees* awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are dischargeable—must be resolved in favor of nondischargeability. The Third Circuit correctly rejected the argument that such damages are “punitive” and thus dischargeable under *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 195 (9th Cir. 1991). That case held that punitive damages are not excepted from discharge because they are not “obtained by fraud.” But this reasoning is inapposite here. The treble damages in this case were not discretionary; they were *mandated by statute* for violations of the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act. Unlike a jury’s punitive award, which is a judicial exercise of discretion, the treble damages here were *foreseeable and codified*. As the Third Circuit observed, “the non-compensatory aspect of the award in this case was codified and therefore entirely foreseeable by the debtor at the time he made the false representations.” This is not punishment imposed after the fact, but a *consequence of the fraudulent conduct itself*. The debt was *obtained by fraud*—the fraud was the overcharging, and the treble damages were the statutory consequence of that fraud.

This interpretation is consistent with the policy underlying § 523(a)(2)(A): to prevent debtors from using bankruptcy to escape the consequences of their fraudulent conduct. As this Court has stated, “the ‘fresh start’ policy of the Bankruptcy Code may only be availed by the ‘honest but unfortunate debtor’” (Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286 (1991)). Cohen was not honest. He knowingly charged unlawful rents to vulnerable tenants, failed to seek legal advice, and refused to reimburse them after being notified of the overcharges. To allow him to discharge the statutory penalties for his fraud would undermine the integrity of the bankruptcy system and reward recklessness.

Finally, the Third Circuit correctly rejected the argument that the treble damages are not “debt” within the meaning of the Bankruptcy Code. Section 101(11) defines “debt” as “liability on a claim,” and a judgment imposing statutory damages is a liability on a claim. The Court of Appeals properly applied *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), which held that a judgment for both compensatory and statutory damages is not dischargeable because it is a *debt incurred through fraud*. The Third Circuit’s conclusion—that the statutory treble damages are not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A)—is sound and should be affirmed.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
