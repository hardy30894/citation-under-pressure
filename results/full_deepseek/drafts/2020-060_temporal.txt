## Argument

The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that the 2004 Consent Decree triggered Guam’s right to contribution under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B). That Decree explicitly disclaimed any finding or admission of liability and was entered under the Clean Water Act, not CERCLA. Consequently, Guam retains its right to seek cost recovery under § 107, and its claim is timely.

### I. The Consent Decree Did Not Resolve Guam’s Liability Under CERCLA

A consent decree is a contract, and its terms control the parties’ obligations. *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 115 (1932). The Decree expressly states that it is “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” J.A. 92. Such a disclaimer of liability means that no liability was resolved. *Callen v. Pennsylvania R. Co.*, 332 U.S. 625, 630 (1948) (a release that expressly denies liability does not constitute an admission). The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on the Decree’s substantive provisions—requiring Guam to close the dump and install a cover system—ignores this fundamental principle. A party may assume obligations without conceding liability, and the mere existence of such obligations does not transform a compliance order into a settlement of CERCLA liability. *See United States v. Atlantic Refining Co.*, 360 U.S. 19, 23 (1959) (consent decrees are not admissions).

Moreover, the Decree was entered under the Clean Water Act, which addresses “pollutants,” not “hazardous substances” as defined by CERCLA. *United States v. Republic Steel Corp.*, 362 U.S. 482, 490 (1960) (Clean Water Act targets discharges of pollutants). The EPA’s complaint sought to enjoin discharges of untreated leachate—a pollutant—not to remediate hazardous substances. A settlement of Clean Water Act violations cannot trigger a CERCLA contribution right, because the two statutes address distinct harms. *See United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939) (statutes in pari materia must be read together, but not conflated). The D.C. Circuit erred by treating the Decree’s requirement to install a cover system as a “response action” under CERCLA. That requirement was designed to stop ongoing discharges, not to clean up past contamination. *Cf. United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 384 U.S. 224, 226 (1966) (abatement of pollution is distinct from remediation).

### II. The Navy, as the Original Operator, Bears Primary Liability

The Navy constructed and operated the Ordot Dump for decades, depositing munitions and chemicals. Guam assumed ownership only after the Navy relinquished sovereignty. Under traditional principles, the party that created the hazard bears primary liability. *United States v. Yellow Cab Co.*, 340 U.S. 543, 553 (1951) (primary tortfeasor is liable for contribution). Guam’s subsequent compliance with EPA orders does not shift that primary liability to Guam. The Decree was a response to EPA’s enforcement action, not an admission that Guam was the responsible party. *See United States v. Sherwood*, 312 U.S. 584, 590 (1941) (sovereign immunity is waived only to the extent consented). The Navy cannot escape its responsibility by pointing to Guam’s compliance with regulatory demands.

### III. The Statute of Limitations Did Not Begin to Run

Even if the Decree could be construed as a settlement, the statute of limitations for contribution under § 113(g)(3) runs from the date liability is “resolved.” But liability is not resolved until the settling party has actually incurred costs or performed the required actions. *United States v. Wunderlich*, 342 U.S. 98, 100 (1951) (cause of action accrues when the right to sue arises). Guam’s costs were incurred after the Decree’s entry, and the Decree itself did not fix Guam’s liability for CERCLA response costs. The D.C. Circuit’s holding that the limitations period expired in 2007—three years after the Decree—ignores that Guam’s liability was contingent on future performance. *Guaranty Trust Co. v. United States*, 304 U.S. 126, 136 (1938) (statutes of limitations are strictly construed against the government). Congress could not have intended to bar contribution claims before the settling party has even incurred the costs for which it seeks contribution.

### IV. The District Court’s Interpretation Is Correct

The district court properly held that the Decree did not resolve Guam’s liability because it lacked the hallmarks of a CERCLA settlement: it did not allocate responsibility for hazardous substances, did not identify Guam as a PRP, and expressly disclaimed liability. *See Londoner v. Denver*, 210 U.S. 373, 385 (1908) (due process requires clear notice of obligations). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion rewrites the Decree and expands § 113(f)(3)(B) beyond its plain text. This Court should reverse and reinstate the district court’s order.
