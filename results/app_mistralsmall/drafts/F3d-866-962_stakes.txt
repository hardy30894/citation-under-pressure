**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Appellants’ FRSA Retaliation Claims Because Appellants Presented Sufficient Evidence to Raise a Genuine Issue of Material Fact as to Whether BNSF’s Disciplinary Actions Were Motivated by Retaliatory Animus.**

To prevail on a retaliation claim under the Federal Railroad Safety Act (FRSA), 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C), a plaintiff must show that (1) they engaged in protected activity; (2) the railroad knew of the protected activity; (3) the railroad subjected the plaintiff to an adverse employment action; and (4) a causal connection exists between the protected activity and the adverse action. *See, e.g., Tinsley v. Soo Line R.R. Co., 834 F.3d 730, 735 (8th Cir. 2016)*; *Kaniff v. CSX Transp., Inc., 652 F.3d 779, 785 (7th Cir. 2011)*. The burden then shifts to the railroad to articulate a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason for the adverse action. *See Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White, 548 U.S. 53, 60-61 (2006)*. If the railroad meets this burden, the plaintiff may still prevail by showing that the railroad’s proffered reason is pretextual. *Id.* at 61.

Here, Appellants engaged in protected activity by reporting unsafe working conditions at the Creston bridge—specifically, the lack of handrails, lighting, and a designated walkway—both in their written statements to Trainmaster Dixon and in their recorded statements to Claims Representative Murphy. *See 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C) (prohibiting discrimination against employees who "provide[] information relating to any Federal Railroad Safety Act violation or any Federal railroad safety law, rule, regulation, order, or standard")*. BNSF undisputedly knew of this protected activity, as Dixon and Murphy were directly involved in the investigation. Appellants then suffered adverse employment actions when BNSF disciplined them for alleged rule infractions arising from the April 2, 2012 accident. Foster was terminated; Kline received a thirty-day "record suspension"; and Snyder received a formal reprimand and a one-year review period. These actions constitute adverse employment actions under the FRSA. *See Tinsley, 834 F.3d at 735* (holding that a suspension and reprimand were adverse actions).

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because Appellants presented sufficient evidence to create a genuine issue of material fact as to whether BNSF’s proffered reasons for the discipline were pretextual. BNSF claimed that Appellants violated safety rules by failing to blow the train’s whistle, improperly turning off the headlight, and fouling the tracks. However, Appellants testified that they were orally instructed to change crews west of the bridge—a location they had previously reported as unsafe—contradicting BNSF’s assertion that the crew-change location was clearly designated east of the bridge. *See, e.g., Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Thurston, 469 U.S. 111, 122 (1985)* (holding that an employer’s inconsistent explanations for an employment decision may support an inference of pretext). Moreover, Appellants presented evidence that BNSF subjected them to increased operations testing and reassigned them to less lucrative work following their protected activity, which could support an inference of retaliatory animus. *See, e.g., Allen v. Stewart Enters., Inc., 247 F.3d 741, 748 (8th Cir. 2001)* (holding that temporal proximity between protected activity and adverse action, combined with other evidence, may support an inference of retaliation).

**II. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Appellants Failed to Establish a Causal Connection Between Their Protected Activity and BNSF’s Adverse Actions.**

The district court held that Appellants failed to establish a causal connection between their protected activity and BNSF’s disciplinary actions. However, Appellants presented evidence that BNSF initiated its investigation of rule infractions *immediately* after Appellants reported the unsafe conditions at the bridge. *See, e.g., Tinsley, 834 F.3d at 736* (holding that temporal proximity between protected activity and adverse action may support an inference of causation). Additionally, Appellants testified that they felt compelled to avoid inexperienced coworkers and sign up for less lucrative work due to BNSF’s increased scrutiny following their protected activity. This evidence, if credited, would support a finding of causation. *See, e.g., Allen, 247 F.3d at 748*.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, Appellants respectfully request that this Court reverse the district court’s grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with the FRSA.
