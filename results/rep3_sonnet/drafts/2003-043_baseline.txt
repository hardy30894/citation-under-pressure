# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEXT OF THE INTERNAL REVENUE CODE REQUIRES A SEPARATE ASSESSMENT AGAINST EACH TAXPAYER, AND PARTNERS ARE TAXPAYERS DISTINCT FROM THE PARTNERSHIP

The statute of limitations for collecting a tax debt is governed by a straightforward statutory scheme. Section 6501(a) requires the IRS to assess a tax "against the taxpayer" within three years of the filing of the taxpayer's return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). If the IRS timely assesses the tax, Section 6502(a) extends the government's time to collect "such tax" by "levy or by a proceeding in court" to ten years "after the assessment of the tax." 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). Throughout this scheme, Congress used the term "taxpayer" deliberately, and that term has a precise statutory meaning.

Section 7701(a)(14) defines "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and Section 7701(a)(1) defines "person" to include "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (a)(14). Critically, this definition lists "individual" and "partnership" as separate and distinct categories of "person." An individual partner is not subsumed within the definition of "partnership"; each is a separately identified taxpayer with its own taxpayer identification number. As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, "[t]he Partnership is a 'taxpayer' within the meaning of the statute, but so is each individual Debtor a separate 'taxpayer.'" Pet. App. 12.

This reading is reinforced by the assessment regulations themselves. Treasury Regulation § 301.6203-1 provides that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer." 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1 (emphasis added). An assessment against the Partnership identifies the Partnership as the taxpayer; it does not identify Debtors individually. Because Section 6502(a)'s ten-year extension applies only to the specific "tax" that was validly "assessed" against a particular "taxpayer," an assessment against the Partnership can extend the limitations period only with respect to the Partnership—not with respect to Debtors, who were never assessed.

## II. THIS COURT'S PRIOR DECISIONS DO NOT SUPPORT THE GOVERNMENT'S POSITION

The government's reliance on Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In Young, a partner who had voluntarily paid his share of a partnership's assessed tax liability sought a refund on the ground that the assessment had not named him individually. The court held he was not entitled to a refund, reasoning that his substantive liability for the debt arose under state partnership law regardless of whether he was named in the assessment. See Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959), aff'd, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960).

But Young addressed only whether a partner who had already paid could recover a refund; it did not hold that the IRS could compel payment from an unassessed partner who refused to pay. Indeed, the district court in Young expressly recognized that collection would require either "resort[ing] to court action" or an individual assessment: "It is the government's contention that where an assessment names an entity such as in the instant case, that it is unnecessary to name the individual members of the entity in order to establish individual liability and that the only reason for naming such individual or adding such individuals' names as here is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action. With this contention I agree." Id. at 76,050 (emphasis added). This case is the mirror image of Young: here, Debtors never paid, and the IRS seeks to compel payment through an assessment that never named them.

United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), likewise supports Respondents. There, the district court held that a lien against an individual partner's property could not exist without an assessment against that individual, because Section 6203 requires that "the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him." Coson v. United States, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). This Court affirmed on other grounds but did not disturb that reasoning. Coson, 286 F.2d at 458, 462-63.

## III. THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT'S DECISION IN WRIGHT IS INAPPOSITE

The government's principal case, United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), does not address the question presented here. In Wright, the IRS had assessed *both* the partnership and the individual partners. United States v. Wright, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only question was whether the limitations period applicable to the partners—already validly commenced by individual assessment—was tolled during the partnership's bankruptcy. Wright, 57 F.3d at 562-63. The Seventh Circuit "had no opportunity to address" whether an assessment against a partnership alone suffices to extend the limitations period against unassessed partners, because that assessment had already occurred. Pet. App. 3-4. Wright is simply inapplicable.

## IV. STATE PARTNERSHIP LAW DOES NOT SUPPLY WHAT THE ASSESSMENT LACKS

The government's fallback theory—that joint and several liability under state partnership law independently authorizes collection—fails for a separate, dispositive reason: California law forecloses direct collection from a partner absent a judgment against that partner individually. California Corporations Code § 16307(c) states plainly: "A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner's assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner." Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c).

Thus, even accepting that Debtors are substantively liable as partners for the Partnership's debts, the IRS was required to obtain an individual judgment against each Debtor to collect that debt from Debtor's personal assets. It obtained none. And because the ordinary three-year assessment period of Section 6501(a) governs Debtors as separate taxpayers, and no assessment or judicial action was taken against them within that period, the claims are now time-barred. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a); 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1) (bankruptcy court must disallow claims "unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor").

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
