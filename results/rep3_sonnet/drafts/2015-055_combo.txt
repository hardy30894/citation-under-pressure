# ARGUMENT

## I. The Rule Announced in *Johnson* Is Substantive Because It Narrows the Class of Conduct the ACCA May Punish, and Substantive Rules Must Apply Retroactively on Collateral Review

A conviction or sentence resting on a statute that is unconstitutionally void is not merely erroneous—it is a nullity. As this Court held long ago in *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 376-377 (1880), "[a]n unconstitutional law is void, and is as no law. An offence created by it is not a crime. A conviction under it is not merely erroneous, but is illegal and void, and cannot be a legal cause of imprisonment." The residual clause invalidated in *Johnson v. United States* operated exactly this way with respect to Welch's sentence: it purported to authorize a fifteen-year mandatory minimum for conduct that the Due Process Clause forbids treating as criminal-sentencing conduct at all, because the clause failed to give "fair warning" of what it punished. A rule that removes an entire category of conduct from a sentencing enhancement's reach is substantive, not procedural—it does not merely regulate *how* guilt or eligibility is determined, but *whether* the underlying enhancement may be imposed at all. Under *Siebold*, a judgment resting on such a void enhancement cannot stand merely because it became final before the constitutional defect was recognized.

## II. The Traditional Retroactivity Factors Confirm That *Johnson* Must Apply to Cases on Collateral Review

This Court's retroactivity jurisprudence has long asked three questions: "the purpose to be served by the new standards, the extent of the reliance by law enforcement authorities on the old standards, and the effect on the administration of justice of a retroactive application of the new standards." *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293, 297 (1967); *accord Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott*, 382 U.S. 406, 417 (1966); *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 636 (1965). This Court has recognized that "the Constitution neither prohibits nor requires retrospective effect," *Linkletter*, 381 U.S. at 629, but where the purpose of the new rule "went to the fairness of the ... process" rather than merely deterring future misconduct, retroactive application has been required. *Tehan*, 382 U.S. at 416; *see also Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244, 249 (1969) (applying the *Linkletter* factors).

Each factor favors Welch. First, purpose: the vagueness holding in *Johnson* does not deter police or prosecutorial misconduct—it corrects a fundamental defect in the statute's capacity to give fair notice of what the law forbids, a defect that goes to the very legitimacy of the sentence imposed, not merely to the procedure by which it was imposed. Second, reliance: no law-enforcement interest is disturbed by recognizing that a defendant's sentence rested on an unconstitutionally vague clause; unlike the exclusionary-rule contexts in *Linkletter* and *Tehan*, retroactive application here imposes no new investigatory burden on police conduct already completed. Third, the effect on the administration of justice favors retroactivity because the alternative is to leave individuals serving enhanced sentences under a clause this Court has already declared void for every future defendant—an anomaly the *Stovall* factors were designed to avoid when the right at issue bears on the basic fairness of the proceeding rather than a technical trial procedure.

## III. The Fair-Notice Guarantee Underlying *Johnson* Has Always Been Treated as Fundamental to Due Process, Requiring Full Retroactive Effect

The vagueness doctrine applied in *Johnson* traces directly to this Court's due process precedents holding that "a statute which either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application, violates the first essential of due process of law." *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). As the Court explained in *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939), "[n]o one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes." These due process guarantees are not confined to trial procedure; they define the outer limits of the State's power to punish at all. This Court has likewise held that the same due-process fair-notice principle bars retroactive application of an unforeseeable judicial narrowing or expansion of criminal liability, because doing so would deprive a defendant of the very notice due process requires. *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 350-352 (1964). The logic runs equally in Welch's favor here: because the residual clause never gave adequate notice of what conduct triggered the fifteen-year mandatory minimum, sentences imposed under it lack the constitutional predicate that due process demands, regardless of when the conviction became final. Cf. *United States ex rel. Angelet v. Fay*, 381 U.S. 654, 660-61 (1965) (distinguishing between rules aimed at trial-type reliability and rules implicating the fundamental fairness of the underlying proceeding).

## Conclusion

Because the *Johnson* rule is substantive, corrects a fundamental due process defect rather than a mere trial-procedure error, and satisfies every traditional retroactivity factor this Court has applied, it must apply to defendants such as Welch whose sentences rest on the invalidated residual clause. The judgment below should be reversed.
