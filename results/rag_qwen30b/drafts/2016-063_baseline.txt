**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to resolve a conflict between two lines of precedent: the D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), and the Supreme Court’s holding in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012). The D.C. Circuit, in this case, concluded that *Kloeckner* did not “eviscerate” *Powell*, and thus transferred the case to the Federal Circuit. That conclusion is erroneous. *Kloeckner* not only overruled *Powell* in substance but also reaffirmed the principle that judicial review of mixed cases—those alleging discrimination in connection with an action appealable to the MSPB—must lie in district court when the Board dismisses on procedural grounds. The D.C. Circuit’s refusal to recognize that *Kloeckner* applies equally to jurisdictional dismissals, and its insistence on a rigid distinction between “procedural” and “jurisdictional” grounds, is contrary to the statutory text, the Court’s reasoning in *Kloeckner*, and the fundamental purpose of the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA).

The CSRA establishes a dual system of judicial review: for most MSPB decisions, review lies exclusively in the Federal Circuit, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A); but for “mixed cases”—those in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and [alleges] that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” federal law—review lies in district court, id. § 7702(a)(1). The critical inquiry under this provision is whether the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” *Kloeckner* made clear that this inquiry is not contingent on the Board’s ultimate disposition. The Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on a procedural ground—such as untimeliness—the employee may seek review in district court. *Kloeckner*, 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Court emphasized that the statutory language “does not depend on the Board’s ultimate decision about jurisdiction,” but rather on whether the underlying action was appealable. Id. at 604. The Court rejected the argument that “the only discrimination cases that could go to district court” were those decided on the merits, stating that “two sections of the CSRA, read naturally, direct employees like Kloeckner to district court.” Id. at 604.

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* on the ground that it involved a “procedural” dismissal while this case involved a “jurisdictional” one is legally untenable. The Court in *Kloeckner* explicitly acknowledged the existence of such a distinction, noting that “the question of whether something is a mixed case” is different from “whether something is a mixed case.” Id. at 604. Yet the Court did not limit its holding to procedural grounds. It held that the statutory text—“affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB”—governs the forum for review, regardless of the reason for dismissal. The Court’s reasoning applies with equal force to jurisdictional dismissals: if the action was appealable to the MSPB, and the employee alleged discrimination, then the case is a mixed case, and review lies in district court. The D.C. Circuit’s insistence that jurisdictional dismissals are “never appealable” to the MSPB, while procedural dismissals are, distorts the statutory framework. As the Federal Circuit observed in *Conforto v. Department of the Treasury*, 713 F.3d 1113, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013), “the Board has the authority to entertain appeals that are procedurally defective under its own regulations, but it may not hear a case over which it lacks jurisdiction.” This distinction does not alter the fact that the underlying action—here, a mandatory retirement and suspension—was appealable to the MSPB. The Board’s jurisdictional determination does not negate the fact that the action was appealable.

Moreover, the D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is misplaced. *Powell* predates *Kloeckner* and was decided before the Supreme Court clarified the scope of § 7702(a)(1). The Court in *Kloeckner* did not merely “clarify” the law—it resolved a circuit split and established a new rule: when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on any ground that does not go to the merits, review lies in district court. The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to preserve *Powell* as a “jurisdictional” exception is inconsistent with *Kloeckner*’s plain language and purpose. The Court in *Kloeckner* rejected the idea that the forum for review should depend on the Board’s reasoning. It held that “the only discrimination cases that could go to district court” were those the Board had decided on the merits—only to immediately reject that premise. *Kloeckner*, 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Court concluded that “the statute’s text and structure” required district court review in all mixed cases, regardless of the basis for dismissal. Id. at 604. The D.C. Circuit’s effort to carve out an exception for jurisdictional dismissals undermines this principle and creates a perverse incentive for agencies to dismiss cases on jurisdictional grounds to avoid district court review.
