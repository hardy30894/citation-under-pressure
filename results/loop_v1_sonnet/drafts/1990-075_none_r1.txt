# ARGUMENT SECTION — BRIEF FOR RESPONDENT

## I. Section 811(h) Satisfies the Intelligible-Principle Standard That Governs Congressional Delegations.

Article I vests "all legislative powers" in Congress, but that provision has never been read to forbid Congress from enlisting executive assistance to administer a complex regulatory scheme. This Court settled the controlling test more than sixty years ago: delegation is proper "if Congress shall lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). Section 811(h) satisfies that test many times over.

**A. Congress supplied concrete, judicially administrable standards.** Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. It specified the factors he must weigh — the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)) — required a finding that scheduling is necessary "to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety," § 811(h)(1), mandated notice to and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, § 811(h)(4), and capped the duration of any temporary schedule at one year, extendable by only six months, § 811(h)(2). That is precisely the kind of guiding "criterion" whose *absence* doomed the statute in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), where the Court found the statute "did not qualify the President's authority" or establish "any criterion to govern the President's course." The same defect proved fatal in *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 541-42 (1935), where Congress had left the President free to approve virtually any industry-drafted code without meaningful confining principles. Petitioners' reliance on those decisions is misplaced: both turned on the *total absence* of standards, not on the fact that criminal consequences might follow from the delegatee's action. Section 811(h) supplies exactly the standards those statutes lacked.

**B. This Court has consistently sustained broader delegations in fast-moving regulatory fields.** In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 423-27 (1944), the Court upheld the Price Administrator's authority to fix "fair and equitable" wartime prices, recognizing that the "rapidity of changes" in the regulated subject justified broad executive discretion. The same logic governs here: Congress found that clandestine chemists could synthesize new "designer drugs" faster than the ordinary scheduling process could respond — which is precisely why it authorized *temporary* action pending completion of full notice-and-comment rulemaking. Likewise, in *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 225-26 (1943), the Court approved the open-ended "public interest, convenience, or necessity" standard for broadcast licensing, and in *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946), it sustained the similarly general "fair and equitable" standard governing utility reorganizations. Section 811(h)'s multi-factor, imminent-hazard standard is considerably more specific than either.

**C. The absence of exhaustive technical detail is not fatal where the field possesses established standards.** In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court upheld a bank-conservatorship statute containing scant express criteria because the regulated field possessed "well-known and generally acceptable standards" on which the delegatee could draw — just as Congress here relied on established pharmacological and law-enforcement criteria codified in § 811(c). And in *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-86 (1948), the Court sustained the Renegotiation Act's authorization to recapture "excessive profits," an inherently flexible standard, because Congress reasonably relied on the delegatee's expertise to fill in details Congress itself could not practically anticipate. The same practical necessity justifies Congress's choice here: the specialized, rapidly evolving nature of clandestine drug chemistry is not a subject Congress could regulate substance-by-substance through ordinary legislation.

**D. Judicial review of the delegation's constitutionality remains available.** The concern this Court identified in *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952) — that meaningful review must remain open — is fully satisfied here. Section 811(h)(6) bars review of the substantive correctness of a particular scheduling order, but it does not foreclose a defendant's right to challenge the delegation itself, consistent with the presumption of reviewability underlying *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), where the Court explained that the non-delegation principle protects "the integrity and maintenance of the system of government ordained by the Constitution." That structural safeguard remains fully intact in this prosecution.

## II. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Redelegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

Petitioners' subdelegation argument fails for a simpler reason: Congress expressly authorized it. Section 871(a) empowers the Attorney General to "delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice." 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). That express statutory authorization distinguishes this case from *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 363-64 (1942), where the Court invalidated a subdelegation precisely because the enabling statute contained no comparable authorization permitting the Administrator to delegate his personal duties to regional officers. Where Congress has supplied that authorization, subdelegation to an agency built to administer the very statute at issue is unremarkable. *Cf. United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932) (upholding delegated executive authority to set enforceable standards carrying criminal consequences). The Attorney General's 1987 regulation assigning temporary-scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), falls squarely within the authority Congress granted in § 871(a), and the subdelegation was therefore proper.
