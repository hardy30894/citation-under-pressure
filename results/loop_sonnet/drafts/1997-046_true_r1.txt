**ARGUMENT**

**I. Woodard Has No Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings Protected by the Due Process Clause.**

Executive clemency has never been treated as an extension of the judicial process to which due process attaches. From the founding of the Republic, this Court has described clemency as an act of sovereign grace, wholly distinct from the adjudication of guilt. In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833), Chief Justice Marshall explained that "[a] pardon is an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws, which exempts the individual on whom it is bestowed from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed." That characterization has never wavered. In *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866), this Court confirmed that "[t]he power thus conferred is unlimited, with the exception stated," and explained that it "may be exercised at any time after its commission, either before legal proceedings are taken, or during their pendency, or after conviction and judgment"—that is, entirely outside the ordinary sequence of trial and appeal that due process governs.

The discretionary, non-adjudicatory character of clemency was reaffirmed in *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927), where Justice Holmes wrote that "[a] pardon in our days is not a private act of grace from an individual happening to possess power. It is a part of the Constitutional scheme," representing "the determination of the ultimate authority that the public welfare will be better served" by leniency than by the judgment as entered. Nothing in that description suggests that clemency functions as a further stage of adjudication subject to the procedural protections that attach to trial or appeal.

Due process protections attach only where the government threatens to deprive an individual of an interest to which he is already entitled by law. See *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961) (the process due depends on "the precise nature of the government function involved as well as . . . the private interest that has been affected by governmental action"). A capital prisoner's expectation of clemency is not such an entitlement; it is, as *Wilson* and *Garland* make clear, wholly dependent on executive grace. Because clemency does not adjudicate guilt or redetermine a sentence already lawfully imposed, but merely asks whether mercy should mitigate a judgment that due process has already validated, no protected liberty interest arises from the clemency process itself. The Sixth Circuit therefore erred in extending due process scrutiny—however "minimal" or "barely perceptible"—to a proceeding this Court has consistently placed outside the adjudicatory system.

**II. Because Participation in the Clemency Interview Is Voluntary, It Does Not Compel Self-Incrimination in Violation of the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment forbids only *compelled* self-incrimination. This Court has long recognized that the privilege protects an individual's freedom to decide for himself whether to speak, and that no penalty may attach to a decision to remain silent. See *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 7–8 (1964) (the privilege against self-incrimination secures the right of an individual to decide for himself whether to answer questions that might incriminate him). Ohio's procedure honors precisely that choice. A death-sentenced inmate may request a personal interview or may decline one; nothing in the regulation compels him to answer any question he does not wish to answer, and no penalty attaches to silence.

Where disclosure is voluntary, no Fifth Amendment violation can occur. See *Rogers v. United States*, 340 U.S. 367, 371 (1951) (privilege protects against compulsion, not voluntary disclosure). The privilege belongs to the individual and must ordinarily be invoked as to particular questions rather than treated as a bar to participation altogether. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 594–97 (1896). Consistent with that principle, this Court in *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951), explained that the privilege "extends not only to answers that would in themselves support a conviction . . . but likewise embraces those which would furnish a link in the chain of evidence needed to prosecute the claimant"—a protection that presupposes the witness's freedom to decline to answer specific questions, exactly the freedom Ohio's interview procedure preserves. See also *United States v. Sullivan*, 274 U.S. 259, 263–64 (1927) (a person confronted with a request for information must claim the privilege as to particular questions rather than refuse to participate altogether).

Nor can Woodard manufacture a constitutional violation from speculation about hypothetical future prosecutions. This Court has cautioned against extending the privilege to remote and contingent risks. *Ullmann v. United States*, 350 U.S. 422, 430–31 (1956) (privilege does not protect against speculative future harm where no realistic threat of prosecution exists). Woodard's own complaint disclaims any effect on his existing convictions or sentence, and Ohio's regulation places no penalty on his silence at the interview. Any risk of self-incrimination is thus wholly theoretical, precisely the sort of contingency this Court has declined to remedy in advance.

Because the clemency interview is optional, and no adverse consequence follows from declining it or from limiting one's answers, Ohio's regulation does not compel Woodard to be a witness against himself. The Sixth Circuit's contrary holding should be reversed.
