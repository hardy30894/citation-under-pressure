## Argument

### I. This case is not moot because a claim for nominal damages preserves a live controversy.

The Constitution limits federal jurisdiction to live “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. art. III, § 2. But a case is not rendered moot merely because prospective relief becomes unavailable. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a completed constitutional violation can still support judicial relief in the form of nominal damages. Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247, 266–67 (1978); Farrar v. Hobby, 506 U.S. 103, 112–13 (1992). And the Court has explained that nominal damages are “the appropriate means of ‘vindicating’ rights whose deprivation has not caused actual, provable injury.” Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski, 141 S. Ct. 792, 802 (2021) (quoting Carey, 435 U.S. at 266).

That principle controls here. Uzuegbunam and Bradford allege that Georgia Gwinnett College enforced unconstitutional speech restrictions against them, and they sought nominal damages for those completed past violations. A request for nominal damages is a request for relief that a court may grant if it finds a constitutional wrong. See Carey, 435 U.S. at 266–67; Farrar, 506 U.S. at 112–13. Because that relief “satisfies the redressability element of standing,” Uzuegbunam, 141 S. Ct. at 802, the case remained live even after GGC revised its policy.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary rule cannot stand. The Court in Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs, 868 F.3d 1248, 1264–65 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), held that nominal damages alone would not save a moot challenge unless the plaintiff had also plausibly pleaded compensatory damages. But the Supreme Court rejected that approach in Uzuegbunam, holding that nominal damages themselves suffice to keep a case alive where the plaintiff alleges a completed constitutional violation. 141 S. Ct. at 802–04. The Court reasoned that “nominal damages are unavailable where a defendant has caused no actual injury,” but where a plaintiff alleges a past violation of a legal right, nominal damages can redress that injury in law. Id. at 800–02.

Nor does GGC’s policy revision moot the case. Voluntary cessation moots a case only when it is “absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000); City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 & n.11 (1982). Even apart from nominal damages, that doctrine is demanding because a defendant should not be able to evade review by temporarily changing course. Friends of the Earth, 528 U.S. at 189. Here, however, the dispositive point is simpler: the damages claim seeks redress for the completed past injuries, so the policy change does not eliminate the controversy. Uzuegbunam, 141 S. Ct. at 801–04.

### II. The complaint plausibly alleged a constitutional injury and sought damages for it.

A complaint need only give fair notice of the claim and the relief sought. See Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007); Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). The complaint here did so. It alleged that campus police stopped Uzuegbunam from distributing literature and later stopped him again while he was speaking in a designated zone. It also alleged that Bradford desired to speak publicly but had been chilled from doing so by the challenged policy. Those allegations identify concrete past injuries stemming from the enforcement of an unconstitutional speech regime. See Healy v. James, 408 U.S. 169, 180–81 (1972).

The complaint expressly requested nominal damages, and it also sought “damages in an amount to be determined by the evidence and this Court.” The district court and the Eleventh Circuit erred in treating the nominal-damages request as though it were the only damages demand capable of supporting jurisdiction. A plaintiff may plead alternative forms of relief. Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(d)(2); see also Johnson v. City of Shelby, 574 U.S. 10, 11–12 (2014) (per curiam). At minimum, the complaint alleged enough to permit the court to adjudicate the constitutionality of the prior policy and award nominal damages if warranted.

### III. The district court also erred in refusing leave to amend, but that issue is unnecessary to resolve the mootness question.

If the Court were to conclude the pleadings were insufficiently clear, dismissal still should not have been the end of the case. Leave to amend should be freely given when justice so requires. Foman v. Davis, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962). The Court of Appeals recognized that plaintiffs may seek amendment after judgment, including through Rules 59(e) and 60(b). Czeremcha v. International Association of Machinists & Aerospace Workers, AFL-CIO, 724 F.2d 1552, 1556 (11th Cir. 1984). But because the nominal-damages claim itself preserves Article III jurisdiction, the Court need not rely on amendment to reverse the judgment.

### Conclusion

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Under Carey, Farrar, Friends of the Earth, and especially Uzuegbunam, a live claim for nominal damages prevents mootness even after a challenged policy is revised during litigation.
