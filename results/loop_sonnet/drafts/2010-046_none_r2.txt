# ARGUMENT

The three-judge court's order reducing California's prison population to 137.5 percent of design capacity rests on a firm foundation: the settled equitable authority of federal courts to remedy systemic constitutional violations, this Court's repeated approval of specific, quantifiable structural relief where lesser measures have failed, and a record establishing a severe and prolonged violation of the Eighth Amendment. The order satisfies the Prison Litigation Reform Act's requirement that relief be narrowly drawn and represent the least intrusive means necessary, because that requirement codifies principles this Court has applied to structural remedies for over a century.

## I. Federal Courts Possess Broad Equitable Authority to Remedy Systemic Constitutional Violations.

A court sitting in equity has traditionally possessed expansive authority to fashion whatever relief a violation demands. As this Court explained in *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944), "the essence of equity jurisdiction has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case." *Id.* at 329. That authority extends to remedies reaching beyond individual relief when the violation is systemic in origin. *See In re Debs*, 158 U.S. 564, 593 (1895) (recognizing the broad power of a federal court to enter relief adequate to secure compliance with federal law). The population order applies this principle directly: having found, after years of litigation and extensive evidentiary hearings, that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of continuing Eighth Amendment violations, the three-judge court was entitled to order the one remedy the record showed would actually work.

Nor does the PLRA's narrow-tailoring requirement displace this framework. Courts have always been obliged to calibrate relief to the violation, but calibration has never meant powerlessness. In *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106 (1932), this Court held that "a continuing decree of injunction directed to events to come is subject to adaptation as events may shape the need." *Id.* at 114. That is precisely what occurred below: the court considered less intrusive alternatives, made detailed findings explaining why those alternatives had failed over years of stipulated remedial efforts, and retained jurisdiction to modify the order as conditions changed.

## II. This Court's Structural-Remedy Precedents Confirm That District Courts May Order Specific, Quantifiable Relief to Eliminate Entrenched Constitutional Violations.

The State's contention that a numerical population cap is inherently too intrusive cannot be reconciled with this Court's repeated approval of specific, quantifiable orders where officials failed for years to cure an established violation. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294 (1955) ("*Brown II*"), this Court directed the district courts to enter "such orders and decrees consistent with this opinion as are necessary and proper to admit to public schools on a racially nondiscriminatory basis," *id.* at 301, entrusting district courts with discretion to design remedies commensurate with the violation notwithstanding the substantial reordering of state institutions such remedies required.

That discretion is not nominal. In *Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, 377 U.S. 218 (1964), this Court sustained an order compelling a local government to levy taxes and reopen public schools, explaining that "the time for mere 'deliberate speed' has run out." *Id.* at 234. In *Green v. County School Board of New Kent County*, 391 U.S. 430 (1968), this Court reaffirmed that once a violation is established, officials bear an "affirmative duty to take whatever steps might be necessary" to eliminate it "root and branch." *Id.* at 437-38. And in *Louisiana v. United States*, 380 U.S. 145 (1965), this Court approved a remedy restructuring an entire state voter-registration system, holding that a court confronting a violation of that magnitude "has not merely the power but the duty to render a decree which will so far as possible eliminate the discriminatory effects of the past." *Id.* at 154.

These decisions establish a consistent rule: when a coordinate branch of state government has, over years, failed to remedy an established constitutional violation despite repeated opportunities to do so, a federal court does not exceed its authority by ordering specific, quantifiable relief—whether framed as pupil assignments, tax levies, or, as here, a population ceiling tied to design capacity. The order below is not meaningfully distinguishable from the remedial decrees this Court has consistently sustained.

## III. The Order Is a Proportionate Response to an Ongoing Violation of the Eighth Amendment.

The severity of the underlying violation confirms the propriety of the remedy chosen. The Eighth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962), draws its meaning from "evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). This Court has long held that punishment inflicting needless suffering offends the Constitution regardless of its formal legality. *See Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 366-67 (1910). A prison system so overcrowded that inmates are systematically denied adequate medical and mental health care—as the district court found after extensive hearings—inflicts exactly the suffering the Eighth Amendment forbids. And where, as here, state officials repeatedly consented to remedial orders yet failed to comply with them over the course of years, the obligation to ensure compliance falls squarely on the courts. *See Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 4 (1958) (state officials cannot forestall enforcement of established constitutional rights through delay). The State cannot now claim that a remedy carefully tailored to the violation it allowed to persist exceeds the PLRA's bounds.

## CONCLUSION

The population order is narrowly drawn, represents the least intrusive means of correcting a violation the State itself permitted to fester for years, and is fully consistent with this Court's equitable and Eighth Amendment precedents. The judgment below should be affirmed.
