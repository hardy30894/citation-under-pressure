**ARGUMENT**

**I.  The Statutory Text Imposes a "Situs of Injury" Requirement**

The text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) unambiguously requires that the injury itself occur on the outer continental shelf. The statute provides that benefits are payable for the "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This language imposes a geographic limitation on the location of the injury, not merely on the location of the operations. The Fifth Circuit correctly interpreted this as a "situs of injury" requirement. In *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), the court held that a claimant must demonstrate that the injury occurred on an outer continental shelf platform or the waters above it. The court explained that the statute "draws a clear geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA." *Id.* at 422 (quoting *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985)). This construction is consistent with the Supreme Court’s observation that the "inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation" of the statute. *Herb’s Welding*, 470 U.S. at 422. Accordingly, an injury occurring onshore, even if related to offshore operations, does not satisfy the statutory requirement.

**II.  The Ninth Circuit’s "Substantial Nexus" Test Is an Improper Expansion of the Statute**

The Ninth Circuit’s holding that OCSLA coverage extends to injuries occurring onshore so long as there is a "substantial nexus" between the injury and the operations is a judicial invention that ignores the text of the statute. The court held that an injury is "the result of" operations if there is a "substantial nexus between the injury and the operations." *Valladolid v. Pacific Operations Offshore*, 6933, 6958 (9th Cir. 2010). This reasoning conflates the location of the injury with the location of the operations. The statute requires the injury to occur *on* the shelf; it does not require the injury to be causally related to the operations in a way that stretches the definition of "occurring on" to include onshore locations. *Mills* explicitly rejected a "but for" test, holding that a welder injured during the onshore construction of a platform was not eligible. 877 F.2d at 362. The "substantial nexus" test is functionally identical to the "but for" test rejected by the Fifth Circuit and renders the geographic limitation of the statute meaningless.

**III.  The Third Circuit’s Approach Is Inconsistent with the Text**

While the Third Circuit in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), rejected the situs test, that decision is inconsistent with the text and the Supreme Court’s general statements regarding OCSLA’s geographic focus. *Curtis* allowed benefits for an employee injured in a car accident on his way to a platform, finding the injury "as the result of" operations. 849 F.2d at 811. This reading permits benefits for injuries that have no geographic connection to the shelf whatsoever. The Respondent respectfully disagrees with *Curtis* and urges this Court to follow the Fifth Circuit’s clear construction of the statute, which limits OCSLA coverage to injuries occurring on the outer continental shelf itself.
