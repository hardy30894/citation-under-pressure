**ARGUMENT**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, as applied to Timothy Hurst, is unconstitutional under the Sixth Amendment. *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), held that “capital defendants, no less than noncapital defendants,” are entitled to “a jury determination of any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in their maximum punishment.” Id. at 589, 609. Florida’s statute conditions death eligibility on findings that aggravating circumstances exist and that they are sufficient to justify a death sentence. Yet, under the scheme upheld below, the trial judge—not the jury—made the critical findings necessary to impose death. That violates *Ring*.

Florida’s old capital sentencing system suffered from the same defect the Court rejected in *Ring*. In *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), the Court had approved judicial factfinding in capital sentencing. *Ring* overruled that aspect of *Walton*, reasoning that Apprendi’s rule applies with full force in capital cases. 536 U.S. at 589, 609. The question is not whether the State labels aggravation a “sentencing factor” or a “consideration,” but whether the finding increases the punishment beyond that authorized by the jury’s verdict alone. Id. at 602-03. Florida’s system plainly did so. As the Florida Supreme Court recognized, the jury’s recommendation was advisory only and did not require specific factual findings. That is not enough under *Ring*.

The State may argue that the jury convicted Hurst of first-degree murder and robbery, and thus necessarily found one aggravator. But *Ring* requires that the jury, not the judge, make the findings that make the defendant death-eligible. 536 U.S. at 609. The Florida scheme allowed the judge to independently find aggravation, weigh aggravating against mitigating circumstances, and impose death. That is precisely the sort of judicial factfinding *Ring* forbids. See also *Hitchcock v. Dugger*, 481 U.S. 393, 398-99 (1987) (describing Florida’s statute as requiring the sentencer to make the aggravation-mitigation determination before death may be imposed).

The Constitution also permits no response that Florida’s advisory jury recommendation cures the defect. In *Hurst’s* case, the jury recommended death by only seven votes to five. A nonunanimous advisory vote is not a jury finding of the facts necessary to expose Hurst to death. The Sixth Amendment demands more than a recommendation. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 597-98, 609. And because the aggravating findings were not made by a jury, Hurst was sentenced in violation of the Sixth Amendment.

The Eighth Amendment independently reinforces the same conclusion. The Court has long recognized that “death is different” and requires “heightened reliability” in capital sentencing. *Woodson v. North Carolina*, 428 U.S. 280, 305 (1976); *Gardner v. Florida*, 430 U.S. 349, 357-58 (1977); *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). Florida’s scheme falls short of that standard because it permits a death sentence without a unanimous jury determination of the facts authorizing death. Justice Ginsburg’s opinion in *Ring* stressed that the jury trial right applies to the facts that make a defendant eligible for death; the Eighth Amendment’s demand for reliability makes it especially important that those facts be found by the community’s conscience, not a single judge. 536 U.S. at 607-09.

The same Eighth Amendment principles also foreclose any scheme that deprives a capital defendant of a meaningful jury determination on mental retardation. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment bars execution of the mentally retarded and left the States to develop procedures to enforce that constitutional restriction. Id. at 317. But a State may not make the Atkins protection illusory by denying the jury any role in the factual determination that determines whether the defendant may be executed. In *Schriro v. Smith*, 546 U.S. 6 (2005), the Court made clear that *Atkins* challenges are to be resolved through appropriate state procedures; they cannot be displaced by federal courts inventing alternate procedures. Id. at 7-8. Florida’s refusal to permit the jury to decide mental retardation as an absolute bar to death, while allowing that evidence only as mitigation, undermines the constitutional command of *Atkins*.

Nor can Florida’s system be saved by arguing that mental retardation is a mere “mitigator” rather than a bar to execution. *Atkins* held the opposite: mentally retarded offenders are categorically ineligible for death. 536 U.S. at 321. Once Hurst produced evidence of mental retardation, the Constitution required a reliable adjudication of that issue before the State could execute him. See *Bobby v. Bies*, 556 U.S. 825, 829-30 (2009). Florida’s procedure did not provide that safeguard.

Finally, the State cannot rely on earlier precedent approving Florida’s scheme. *Ring* changed the constitutional landscape by applying Apprendi to capital sentencing. 536 U.S. at 589, 609. After *Ring*, Florida may not continue to treat the jury as advisory on the very facts that make death available. The judgment below should be reversed.
