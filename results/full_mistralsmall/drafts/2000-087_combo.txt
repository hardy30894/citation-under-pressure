**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION BEYOND THE REMOVAL PERIOD

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), must be construed to avoid grave constitutional doubts. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 690 (2001) (“Based on our conclusion that indefinite detention of aliens in the former category would raise serious constitutional concerns, we construe the statute to contain an implicit ‘reasonable time’ limitation”). This Court has long recognized that statutes should be interpreted to avoid constitutional infirmities where possible. *United States v. Rumely*, 345 U.S. 41, 47 (1953) (“[W]here the constitutionality of an Act of Congress is challenged, and the Court can see that it might be construed in a way that would avoid the constitutional question, it will adopt that construction”).

The Fifth Circuit’s construction of § 1231(a)(6) as permitting indefinite detention unless the alien proves deportation is impossible is untenable. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690. Such a construction would authorize perpetual detention, a result that offends due process. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896) (“[A]ll persons within the territory of the United States are entitled to the protection named in the [Fifth] Amendment, and the provisions of the Constitution are not suspended or abrogated by an Act of Congress”).

II. THE CONSTITUTION FORBIDS PROLONGED DETENTION WITHOUT A REASONABLE LIKELIHOOD OF REMOVAL

The Due Process Clause prohibits arbitrary and indefinite detention. *United States ex rel. Potash v. District Court*, 169 F.2d 747, 750 (7th Cir. 1948) (“[D]etention without trial is a violation of due process of law”). This principle applies with equal force to aliens ordered removed. *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 238 (“[A]n alien who has entered the country, even if illegally, is entitled to due process of law”).

The government’s detention authority is limited to the period reasonably necessary to effectuate removal. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 699 (“[O]nce removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute”). This Court has repeatedly held that detention must be tied to a legitimate governmental purpose. *United States ex rel. Zdolli v. Commanding Officer*, 46 F.2d 1011, 1013 (2d Cir. 1931) (“[D]etention for an indefinite period without charge or trial is arbitrary and oppressive”).

III. THE SIX-MONTH PRESUMPTION ESTABLISHED IN *ZADVYDAS* GOVERNS THIS CASE

The Court in *Zadvydas* established a presumptively reasonable period of six months for post-removal detention. 533 U.S. at 701. After this period, the government must demonstrate a significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. *Id.* Here, petitioner has been detained for years beyond the removal period with no realistic prospect of removal. The government has failed to rebut the presumption of unreasonableness.

This six-month rule comports with due process principles. *United States ex rel. McCandless v. Adams*, 16 F.2d 987, 990 (3d Cir. 1927) (“[D]etention for a period far exceeding that necessary to effectuate a legitimate purpose is unconstitutional”). The Court has long recognized that prolonged detention without justification violates due process. *United States ex rel. Potash*, 169 F.2d at 750.

IV. THE GOVERNMENT’S EFFORTS TO SECURE REMOVAL DO NOT JUSTIFY INDEFINITE DETENTION

The government’s good-faith efforts to secure removal do not authorize indefinite detention. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 699 (“[T]he Constitution does not permit indefinite detention based solely on the Executive’s perception of the difficulties of removing an alien”). This Court has rejected the notion that administrative inconvenience justifies prolonged detention. *United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy*, 347 U.S. 260, 268 (1954) (plurality opinion) (“[A]n administrative agency may not, in the name of efficiency, ignore the fundamental rights of individuals”).

The government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 700 (distinguishing *Mezei* as involving an excludable alien at the border). The Court has long held that resident aliens are entitled to greater constitutional protections. *Truax v. Raich*, 239 U.S. 33, 41 (1915) (“[T]he right to work for a living in the common occupations of the community is of the very essence of the personal freedom and opportunity that it was the purpose of the Amendment to secure”).

V. CONCLUSION

The statute does not authorize indefinite detention, and the Constitution forbids it. The six-month presumption established in *Zadvydas* governs this case, and the government has failed to rebut it. The judgment below should be affirmed.
