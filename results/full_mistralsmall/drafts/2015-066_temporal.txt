**ARGUMENT**

I. The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies only to federal prosecutions, and tribal courts are not federal courts.

The Sixth Amendment provides that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right to ... have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right attaches only in federal prosecutions. *Barron ex rel. Tiernan v. Mayor of Baltimore*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 243, 250 (1833) (“The Constitution was ordained and established by the people of the United States for themselves, for their own government, and not for the government of the individual States.”); *Fox v. Ohio*, 46 U.S. (5 How.) 410, 434 (1847) (“The sixth amendment ... relates only to trials in the courts of the United States.”). Tribal courts are not federal courts. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 322 (1978) (“Indian tribes are unique aggregations possessing attributes of sovereignty over both their members and their territory.”); *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 382 (1896) (“The powers of local self-government enjoyed by the Cherokee Nation existed prior to the Constitution, were not granted by it, and are not controlled by it.”). Thus, the Sixth Amendment does not apply in tribal court proceedings.

II. The Sixth Amendment does not invalidate uncounseled convictions used as predicate offenses in recidivist statutes.

This Court has long held that recidivist statutes do not punish prior convictions but instead enhance punishment for the current offense. *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616, 628 (1912) (“The punishment for the second offense is greater than for the first, but the prior conviction is not used to punish the defendant for the earlier offense.”); *Moore v. Missouri*, 159 U.S. 673, 677 (1895) (“The second offense is punished more severely, not because of the first, but because of the repetition.”). The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel attaches to the current prosecution, not to prior uncounseled convictions. *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 323 (1937) (“The right to counsel is a fundamental right, but it is a right that attaches at trial.”). Thus, prior uncounseled convictions may be used as predicate offenses in recidivist statutes without violating the Sixth Amendment.

III. The use of tribal court convictions as predicate offenses respects tribal sovereignty and comity.

Federal courts have long recognized the comity owed to tribal court judgments. *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217, 220-21 (1959) (“[T]he States have no power to regulate the affairs of Indians on their reservations.”); *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Comm’n*, 411 U.S. 164, 172 (1973) (“[T]ribal courts are courts of competent jurisdiction whose judgments are entitled to full faith and credit.”). To disregard tribal court convictions would undermine tribal sovereignty and federal comity principles. *Williams*, 358 U.S. at 223 (“[T]he policy of leaving Indians free from state jurisdiction and control is deeply rooted in the Nation’s history.”).

**CONCLUSION**

The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, and prior uncounseled tribal court convictions may be used as predicate offenses in recidivist statutes without violating the Sixth Amendment. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to the contrary should be reversed.
