**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Text and Structure of § 1981a Do Not Require a Finding of “Egregious” or “Outrageous” Conduct Independent of the Employer’s State of Mind.**

The plain text of § 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages where the plaintiff demonstrates that the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) (emphasis added). This language tracks the standard for punitive damages under *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983), which held that punitive damages may be awarded under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 where the defendant’s conduct is “motivated by evil motive or intent” or exhibits “reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others.” *Id.* at 56 (quoting *Adickes v. S.H. Kress & Co.*, 398 U.S. 144, 158 n.17 (1970)). The Court in *Smith* expressly rejected the argument that punitive damages required a showing of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct separate from the defendant’s state of mind. *Id.* at 49-50. The same reasoning applies here: the statute’s focus on the employer’s mental state—malice or reckless indifference—controls, not the objective severity of the conduct. *See also Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 139 F.3d 955, 965 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (en banc) (citing *Smith* and holding that § 1981a’s standard is “subjective” and does not require “egregious” misconduct), *rev’d on other grounds*, 527 U.S. 526 (1999).

**II. The Legislative History of the 1991 Civil Rights Act Does Not Impose an “Egregiousness” Requirement.**

The respondent acknowledges that some legislative history suggests punitive damages should be reserved for “extraordinarily egregious cases.” *See* 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (statement of Sen. Dole). However, this isolated remark is not controlling. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against relying on isolated statements in legislative history when the statutory text is clear. *See, e.g., United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241-42 (1989) (rejecting reliance on legislative history where the statutory text was unambiguous). Moreover, the House Report accompanying the Act explicitly states that punitive damages are available where the defendant’s conduct is “egregious,” but it does not impose an independent “egregiousness” requirement separate from the mental state standard. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(I), at 92 (1991). The respondent’s interpretation would render the statute’s explicit mental state requirement superfluous, a result disfavored under *United States v. Nordic Village, Inc.*, 503 U.S. 30, 36 (1992) (statutory construction should avoid rendering any statutory language superfluous).

**III. The Majority of Circuits Have Rejected an Independent “Egregiousness” Requirement.**

The respondent’s position is further undermined by the overwhelming consensus of the federal courts of appeals. Every circuit to address the issue—including the Second, Third, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Seventh, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits—has held that § 1981a’s punitive damages provision does not require a showing of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct independent of the employer’s state of mind. *See, e.g., Thomas v. Nat’l Ass’n of Gov’t Employees*, 28 F.3d 115, 117 (D.C. Cir. 1994) (per curiam) (citing cases); *Kolstad*, 139 F.3d at 965 (same). These courts have uniformly concluded that the statute’s focus on the employer’s mental state—malice or reckless indifference—is the sole relevant inquiry. *See, e.g., Harris v. City of Dallas*, 200 F.3d 592, 595 (5th Cir. 2000) (“The statute does not require a showing of egregious or outrageous conduct beyond the employer’s state of mind.”); *Brennan v. ReMax Int’l, Inc.*, 356 F.3d 31, 38 (1st Cir. 2004) (“The statute’s text and structure make clear that the focus is on the employer’s state of mind, not the severity of its conduct.”).

**IV. The Respondent’s Interpretation Would Undermine the Statute’s Deterrent Purpose.**

Finally, the respondent’s interpretation would frustrate Congress’s intent to strengthen Title VII’s remedial scheme. The 1991 Act was enacted to provide “additional remedies ... to deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination in the workplace.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981 (note) (Congressional Findings). Imposing an independent “egregiousness” requirement would narrow the availability of punitive damages, thereby weakening the statute’s deterrent effect. *See BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 568 (1996) (punitive damages serve the “twin goals” of punishment and deterrence). The Supreme Court has cautioned against interpretations that would “frustrate the policy that Congress sought to implement.” *See, e.g., United States v. O’Hagan*, 521 U.S. 642, 673 (1997) (quoting *NLRB v. Amax Coal Co.*, 453 U.S. 322, 333 (1981)). The respondent’s proposed construction would do precisely that.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should hold that § 1981a does not require a showing of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct independent of the employer’s state of mind. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
