**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Categorically Prohibit the Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders.**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” must be interpreted by reference to “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). In assessing whether a punishment violates the Eighth Amendment, this Court examines “objective indicia of society’s standards, as expressed in legislative enactments and state practice,” as well as “the Court’s own understanding and interpretation of the Eighth Amendment’s text, history, meaning, and purpose.” *Roper v. Simmons*, 543 U.S. 551, 563 (2005) (internal quotation marks omitted).

In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), this Court declined to recognize a categorical bar on executing mentally retarded offenders, concluding that “there is insufficient evidence of a national consensus against executing mentally retarded people convicted of capital crimes.” *Id.* at 334. The Court emphasized that only two States had enacted statutes prohibiting such executions at the time, and that Congress had not done so. *Id.* Moreover, the Court noted that the “medical community’s own inquiry into the matter” had not yielded a consensus that execution of the mentally retarded was cruel and unusual. *Id.* at 333.

Since *Penry*, the legal landscape has not changed in a manner sufficient to justify a different result. As this Court recognized in *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the number of States prohibiting the execution of the mentally retarded had grown to 18 by the time of oral argument. *Id.* at 314. However, this Court cautioned that “[i]t is not so much the number of these States that is significant, but the consistency of the direction of change.” *Id.* (internal quotation marks omitted). The Court further noted that “[t]he practice, therefore, has been rejected by a substantial number of States,” but it did not find that this shift reflected a “national consensus” sufficient to justify a categorical Eighth Amendment bar. *Id.*

**II. The Virginia Supreme Court Correctly Applied *Penry* and Did Not Err in Upholding Atkins’ Sentence.**

The Virginia Supreme Court’s decision in this case is consistent with *Penry* and does not warrant this Court’s intervention. The Virginia court relied on *Penry* in rejecting Atkins’ claim that his mental retardation rendered his execution unconstitutional. *See Atkins v. Commonwealth*, 260 Va. 375, 382-83, 536 S.E.2d 44, 48-49 (2000). This was a reasonable application of *Penry*, which had explicitly declined to recognize a categorical bar on executing mentally retarded offenders.

Moreover, the Virginia court’s decision was supported by the evidence presented at trial. The forensic psychologist who testified on Atkins’ behalf in the penalty phase of his trial described Atkins as “mildly mentally retarded,” with an IQ of 59. *See id.* at 380, 536 S.E.2d at 47. However, the State presented rebuttal testimony challenging the reliability of Atkins’ IQ score and the methodology used to assess his mental retardation. *See id.* at 381, 536 S.E.2d at 47-48. The jury was thus presented with conflicting evidence regarding Atkins’ mental capacity, and it was entitled to credit the State’s evidence over the defense’s. *See Penry*, 492 U.S. at 327 (noting that juries are capable of considering mitigating evidence in assessing a defendant’s moral culpability).

**III. This Court Should Defer to the Virginia Supreme Court’s Application of *Penry* and Uphold Atkins’ Sentence.**

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that “state courts are the principal expositors of state law,” and that federal habeas courts must defer to their interpretations unless they are “contrary to, or involved an unreasonable application of, clearly established Federal law.” *Woodford v. Visciotti*, 537 U.S. 19, 24 (2002) (per curiam) (internal quotation marks omitted). The Virginia Supreme Court’s decision in this case was a reasonable application of *Penry*, and it does not warrant this Court’s review.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
