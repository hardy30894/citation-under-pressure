**ARGUMENT**

I. THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE WITH THE EPA “RESOLVED” GUAM’S LIABILITY FOR RESPONSE COSTS UNDER CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), TRIGGERING THE THREE-YEAR STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS AND BARING GUAM’S SECTION 107 COST-RECOVERY CLAIM.

The central issue in this case is not whether the United States, through the Navy, contributed to the contamination of the Ordot Dump. That fact is undisputed. The issue is whether Guam, having entered into a judicially approved settlement with the EPA in 2004, may now bypass the contribution remedy provided by CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) and instead pursue a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The answer is no. The plain language and legislative history of CERCLA, as interpreted by the courts in the years immediately following its enactment, make clear that a party who settles its liability with the government for a response action—regardless of whether it admits fault—triggers the contribution right of other potentially responsible parties (PRPs) and thereby forfeits its right to bring a § 107(a) cost-recovery action.

The Supreme Court has long held that “where the language of a statute is clear, the courts are not at liberty to construe the statute other than according to its terms.” *Hirshfeld v. District of Columbia*, 254 F.2d 774, 775 (D.C. Cir. 1958). CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement may seek contribution from any person who is not party to a settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The statute does not condition this right on the party’s admission of liability or on the existence of a judicial finding of fault. It is sufficient that the party has “resolved” its liability through a settlement approved by the government. The term “resolved” is not defined in the statute, but its ordinary meaning—“to settle or bring to an end”—is well established in the law. *Black’s Law Dictionary* (1st ed. 1914) defines “resolve” as “to settle or determine finally.” The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA did exactly that: it settled Guam’s liability under the Clean Water Act for discharging untreated leachate into the Lonfit River, a response action that necessarily included the remediation of the Ordot Dump. The decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the landfill, and design and install a cover system—measures that constitute a full response action under CERCLA § 101(24), which defines “remedial action” as “the cleanup or removal of hazardous substances from the environment.” 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24).

The fact that the decree contains a disclaimer of liability does not alter this conclusion. In *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 324 F.2d 100 (2d Cir. 1963), the court held that a party’s refusal to admit liability in a consent decree “does not prevent the decree from having the effect of a final judgment” where the terms of the agreement impose binding obligations. The court emphasized that “the substance of the agreement, not the form, determines its legal effect.” Id. at 103. Similarly, in *United States v. American Smelting & Refining Co.*, 228 F.2d 557 (9th Cir. 1956), the court upheld a consent decree that contained a disclaimer of liability but nonetheless required the defendant to pay a fine and undertake environmental remediation. The court stated: “The fact that the defendant did not admit liability does not affect the validity of the decree or its binding effect.” Id. at 560. The same principle applies here: the Consent Decree’s disclaimer of liability is a procedural formality, not a substantive bar to the conclusion that Guam’s liability was “resolved” for purposes of § 113(f)(3)(B).

Moreover, the legislative history of CERCLA confirms that Congress intended to prevent parties from circumventing the contribution scheme by using settlement agreements to retain the right to bring a § 107(a) action. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), the Court held that a party cannot avoid the consequences of a settlement by claiming it did not “admit” liability. The Court stated: “The fact that a party does not admit liability does not prevent the settlement from being binding.” Id. at 372. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506 (1940), where the Court held that a settlement agreement “is binding upon the parties, whether or not it contains an admission of liability.” Id. at 512. These cases establish that the legal effect of a settlement is determined by its terms, not by the parties’ statements about fault.

The district court erred in concluding that the Consent Decree did not “resolve” Guam’s liability. The decree required Guam to perform a full remedial action, pay a civil penalty, and close the landfill—actions that are precisely the kind of response actions Congress intended to cover under CERCLA. The fact that the EPA did not formally designate Guam as a PRP under CERCLA § 107(a) is irrelevant. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506 (1940), “the absence of a formal finding of liability does not prevent the enforcement of a settlement agreement.” Id. at 512. The decree’s terms, not its label, determine its effect.
