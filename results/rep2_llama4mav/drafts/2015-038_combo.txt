The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) grants the National Park Service (NPS) authority to regulate activities within Conservation System Units (CSUs), including those on non-federal lands. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) supports this interpretation, and historical case law reinforces the federal government's authority over navigable waters within CSUs.

The Court should affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the respondent. ANILCA § 103(c) states that "only those lands within the boundaries of [CSUs] ... that are public lands ... are hereby deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). However, this provision does not preclude NPS from regulating activities on non-federal lands within CSUs. As the Court has long recognized, the federal government retains significant authority over navigable waters, even when title to submerged lands has been conveyed to a state. United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) ("The title to the land under navigable waters ... is held by the state ... subject to the power of Congress to control navigation."). This authority is rooted in the Commerce Clause, which grants Congress the power to regulate commerce among the states. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. 1, 196 (1824).

The Court has consistently upheld federal authority over navigable waters, even when state ownership is involved. In United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 426-27 (1940), the Court held that the federal government's navigational servitude allows it to regulate activities on navigable waters, regardless of state ownership. Similarly, in United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co., 229 U.S. 53, 62-63 (1913), the Court recognized that the federal government's authority over navigable waters is not limited by state ownership.

Furthermore, the Court has established that the federal government may regulate activities on non-federal lands when necessary to protect federal interests. In Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 525-26 (1897), the Court held that the federal government may regulate private lands within a national forest to prevent harm to federal lands. Similarly, in Light v. United States, 220 U.S. 523, 536-37 (1911), the Court recognized that the federal government may regulate private lands to protect federal interests.

In addition, the Court has long recognized that the federal government has the authority to regulate activities on navigable waters to protect the public interest. In Willamette Iron Bridge Co. v. Hatch, 125 U.S. 1, 8 (1888), the Court held that the federal government may regulate the construction of bridges over navigable waters to ensure public safety. Similarly, in Gilman v. Philadelphia, 70 U.S. 713, 725 (1865), the Court recognized that the federal government has the authority to regulate activities on navigable waters to promote commerce and navigation.

The respondent's authority to regulate hovercraft use on the Nation River is consistent with these principles. As the Court has recognized, the federal government has a legitimate interest in regulating activities on navigable waters to protect the public interest and promote commerce. The regulation of hovercraft use on the Nation River is a reasonable exercise of this authority, and the Court should uphold it.

In conclusion, the Court should affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the respondent. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) and historical case law support the respondent's authority to regulate activities within CSUs, including those on non-federal lands. The federal government's authority over navigable waters and its interest in protecting the public interest and promoting commerce justify the regulation of hovercraft use on the Nation River.
