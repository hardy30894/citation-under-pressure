The Due Process Clause does not require a separate, heightened “reasoned choice” standard for competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. This Court has long held that the constitutional touchstone is whether the defendant has sufficient present ability to understand the proceedings and to consult with counsel with a reasonable degree of rational understanding. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam); Bishop v. United States, 350 U.S. 961 (1956) (per curiam). That standard governs the question whether a defendant may be tried at all; due process does not add a second, more demanding constitutional threshold for a defendant’s decision to plead guilty or proceed without counsel. Cf. Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298 (1957); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966).

The Supreme Court’s decisions in Walker v. Johnston, 312 U.S. 275 (1941), and Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), require that waivers of constitutional rights be knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. But those cases address the validity of the waiver itself, not a different mental-competency test. Johnson held that waiver of the right to counsel is an “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” 304 U.S. at 464. Walker likewise focused on whether a waiver was made with understanding. 312 U.S. at 286-87. Neither decision suggests that the Constitution imposes a special “reasoned choice” competency standard distinct from the familiar inquiry into whether the accused understands the nature of the proceedings and can assist in his defense.

This Court’s competency cases confirm the point. In Dusky, the Court rejected formulations that turned on whether the defendant could make wise decisions, and instead established a practical, functional standard: whether he has “sufficient present ability” to consult with counsel and a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings. 362 U.S. at 402. That standard was reiterated in Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), and Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), which emphasize the court’s duty to inquire when facts raise doubt about present competency. But neither case recognized a constitutionally required “reasoned choice” test for the narrower acts of pleading guilty or waiving counsel.

The Ninth Circuit’s heightened standard cannot be squared with the line of cases governing guilty pleas and waiver. In Smith v. O’Grady, 312 U.S. 329 (1941), the Court invalidated a guilty plea obtained without fair notice of the charge, because a plea must be a deliberate and informed choice. Likewise, in Chambers v. Maroney, 399 U.S. 42 (1970), the Court stressed the importance of a voluntary and understanding choice among alternatives, but that voluntariness inquiry is distinct from a newly invented competency threshold. The Constitution requires a valid waiver, not a separate judicial finding that the defendant made the best or most rational choice available.

Nor does the pre-1970 precedent support equating competency with optimal decisionmaking. In Moore v. Michigan, 355 U.S. 155 (1957), the Court set aside a plea entered without understanding, but it did so because the record failed to show a knowing waiver. In Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), the Court required searching inquiry before accepting a waiver of counsel, again because the waiver must be intelligent and informed. And in Carnley v. Cochran, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), the Court held that waiver of counsel may not be presumed from a silent record. These cases insist on procedural safeguards to ensure an informed waiver; they do not constitutionalize a special mental-capacity test beyond the established competency standard.

The lower court’s “reasoned choice” formulation also conflicts with the practical administration of due process. Competency doctrine has always focused on present ability, not on whether a defendant can make a wise, prudent, or strategically sound decision. Bishop, 350 U.S. 961; Dusky, 362 U.S. 402. A defendant who understands the nature of the proceedings and can consult with counsel may still choose to plead guilty for reasons the court may consider irrational, but due process does not authorize substituting judicial judgment for the defendant’s own. Johnson, 304 U.S. 458; Walker, 312 U.S. 275.

Because the Constitution already supplies a settled competency rule, the judgment below should be reversed. The trial court was required to determine whether Moran possessed the capacity recognized in Dusky and its predecessors, and whether his waiver was knowing, intelligent, and voluntary under Johnson, Walker, Von Moltke, and Carnley. It was not required to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard that appears nowhere in this Court’s pre-1970 precedent.
