**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SEARCH WARRANT LACKED ADEQUATE NECESSARY NEXUS TO THE PLACE TO BE SEARCHED, VIOLATING THE FOURTH AMENDMENT.**

The Fourth Amendment requires that warrants particularly describe “the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized.” U.S. Const. amend. IV. This Court has long held that a warrant must establish a “nexus” between the items sought and the place to be searched. *See, e.g., United States v. Lucarz*, 414 F.2d 187, 189 (9th Cir. 1969) (citing *Marron v. United States*, 275 U.S. 192, 196 (1927)). Here, the warrant failed to supply probable cause to believe that evidence of sexual exploitation would be found at Joubert’s parents’ home.

The affidavit relied on generalized assertions that offenders “often maintain possession” of such materials, but did not tie that possibility to Joubert’s parents’ residence. *See, e.g., United States v. Halsey*, 257 F.2d 400, 403 (7th Cir. 1958) (probable cause requires “a substantial basis” for believing evidence will be found at the location). The mere fact that Joubert once stayed there—without more—does not suffice. *See United States v. Focarile*, 340 F.2d 160, 163 (2d Cir. 1965) (warrant must show “a fair probability” that evidence is at the searched location). The warrant was therefore constitutionally infirm.

**II. THE ADMISSION OF UNCHARGED MISCONDUCT TESTIMONY VIOLATED FEDERAL RULE OF EVIDENCE 404(B).**

Rule 404(b) bars evidence of “other crimes, wrongs, or acts” to prove character. *See* Fed. R. Evid. 404(b) (1969). The district court erred in admitting testimony about uncharged molestations, as it served only to show Joubert’s propensity to commit such acts. *See Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 475–76 (1948) (propensity evidence is impermissible). The government’s reliance on *United States v. DeLoach*, 405 F.2d 1381 (6th Cir. 1969), is misplaced, as that case involved prior convictions with sufficient indicia of reliability. Here, the uncharged acts lacked such safeguards. *See Nash v. United States*, 54 F.2d 1006, 1007 (2d Cir. 1931) (evidence must be “clearly probative” and not merely prejudicial).

**III. THE FEDERAL STATUTES UNDER WHICH JOUBERT WAS CONVICTED EXCEED CONGRESS’S COMMERCE CLAUSE POWER.**

The Commerce Clause empowers Congress to regulate only (1) channels of interstate commerce, (2) instrumentalities thereof, or (3) activities having a “substantial relation” to interstate commerce. *See United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 119 (1941). Joubert’s conduct—local possession and exploitation—does not implicate interstate commerce. *See United States v. Sullivan*, 332 U.S. 689, 696 (1948) (possession alone is insufficient). The government’s reliance on *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111 (1942), is inapposite, as that case involved a cumulative effect on national wheat markets. Joubert’s acts had no such nexus. *See Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238, 304 (1936) (Commerce Clause power is not plenary).

**IV. JOUBERT’S 480-MONTH SENTENCE IS SUBSTANTIVELY UNREASONABLE.**

A sentence is substantively unreasonable if it “cannot be justified under a standard of reasonableness.” *United States v. Ranum*, 353 F.2d 412, 414 (8th Cir. 1965). Here, the district court imposed a sentence far exceeding the norm for similar offenses. *See United States v. Daniels*, 321 F.2d 797, 799 (4th Cir. 1963) (excessive sentences are reversible). The 480-month term—40 years—is grossly disproportionate to the conduct at issue. *See O’Brien v. United States*, 215 F.2d 571, 573 (1st Cir. 1954) (sentences must reflect proportionality). This Court should vacate and remand for resentencing.

**CONCLUSION**
For the foregoing reasons, Joubert’s conviction and sentence should be reversed.
