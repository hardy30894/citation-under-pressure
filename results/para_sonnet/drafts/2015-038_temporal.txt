# ARGUMENT

## I. The Plain Text of ANILCA Confines § 103(c) to a Definition of "Public Lands"; It Does Not Strip the National Park Service of Authority Over Activities Occurring Within Park Boundaries.

"It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 103(c) of ANILCA defines which lands qualify as "public lands" for purposes of the statute's land-classification scheme; it says nothing about the Secretary's independent regulatory authority over activities—such as the operation of vehicles—occurring within the boundaries of a conservation system unit. Sturgeon's reading requires this Court to treat a definitional provision as an implicit repeal of the Secretary's pre-existing regulatory authority. But "repeals by implication are not favored," *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), and "the intention of the legislature to repeal must be clear and manifest," *Posadas v. National City Bank*, 296 U.S. 497, 503 (1936). Nothing in the text of § 103(c) manifests an intent to dismantle the uniform system of park administration that Congress otherwise preserved.

## II. Congress's Power Under the Property Clause and the Federal Navigational Servitude Independently Support Regulation of Surface Activity on the Nation River, Regardless of the Ownership of the Submerged Lands.

Even if the submerged lands of the Nation River belong to the State, the Park Service's regulation of surface activity on the river's surface waters within Yukon-Charley rests on two independent and long-settled sources of federal power.

*First*, Congress possesses plenary authority to regulate the use of federal property, including the conduct of those who come upon it. In *Light v. United States*, 220 U.S. 523, 537 (1911), the Court explained that "the government has, with respect to its own lands, the rights of an ordinary proprietor, to maintain its possession and to prosecute trespassers," and may "prescribe the conditions upon which others may use" that property. The Court likewise held in *Camfield v. United States*, 167 U.S. 518, 525 (1897), that the government may adopt "reasonable and necessary" measures to protect the use and enjoyment of federal land, even where those measures incidentally touch adjoining or interspersed private holdings. A hovercraft ban applicable throughout a national preserve, adopted to protect wildlife and visitor experience within federally administered land, falls squarely within this recognized authority—authority that does not evaporate merely because the streambed beneath the water is state-owned.

*Second*, the federal government's power over navigable waters is not diminished by state ownership of the submerged lands. In *United States v. Twin City Power Co.*, 350 U.S. 222 (1956), the Court held that the federal interest in navigable waters is a dominant one, arising from the Commerce Clause, that is not subordinate to competing property claims in the riverbed. The Court reaffirmed in *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 123 (1967), that this authority "is the outgrowth of both its power to regulate commerce and its power to control its own property," and extends to the water itself independent of who holds title to the bed beneath it. Because the Nation River is unquestionably a navigable water of the United States, see *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1871), the Park Service's authority to regulate the manner of navigation upon it—here, the use of hovercraft—does not depend on, and is not defeated by, the State's ownership of the riverbed.

## III. Sturgeon's Construction Produces Results Plainly at Variance with Congress's Evident Purpose and Must Be Rejected.

Even where statutory language appears susceptible of a party's proposed reading, courts must decline that reading where "the literal words" would produce consequences "so opposed to the manifest purpose" that Congress could not have intended them. *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). ANILCA's conservation system units were designed to be managed as integrated wholes—single preserves administered under uniform rules for hunting, wildlife protection, and visitor safety. Sturgeon's interpretation would fracture that scheme into a checkerboard of enforceable and unenforceable zones within the same unit, turning on the vagaries of subsurface title that neither rangers nor visitors can readily discern in the field. Such a patchwork cannot be squared with Congress's evident design, and this Court should not adopt a construction that would produce it.

## IV. The State of Alaska, in Any Event, Lacks Standing to Press This Challenge.

Insofar as Alaska's parallel claim remains at issue, it fails for want of standing. A State may not appear in federal court "as parens patriae" to shield its citizens "from the operation of" a federal statute; that function belongs to the citizens themselves or to Congress. *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 485–86 (1923). Nor may a court entertain claims resting on abstract disagreement with federal policy rather than a genuine case or controversy. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346, 361 (1911). Alaska's asserted sovereign interest in unencumbered management of its submerged lands, untethered to any concrete and continuing injury, does not satisfy this settled requirement.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
