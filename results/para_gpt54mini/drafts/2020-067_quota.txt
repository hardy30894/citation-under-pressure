Respondent’s position is straightforward: on plain-error review, an appellate court may consider the entire record—not just the evidence actually presented to the jury—to decide whether the error affected substantial rights and whether correction is necessary to protect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.

Plain-error review is intentionally broader than record-bound sufficiency review. Under United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), a defendant must show not only error that is plain, but also prejudice, and then satisfy the Court that the error “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Id. at 732, 736 (alteration in original). That inquiry necessarily asks what actually happened in the case, not merely what the jury heard. The Court in Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461 (1997), applied plain-error review by examining the case as a whole and declined to reverse where the evidence of guilt was “overwhelming.” Id. at 470. Likewise, in United States v. Cotton, 535 U.S. 625 (2002), the Court considered the full record in concluding that an omitted indictment allegation did not warrant correction because the evidence was “overwhelming and essentially uncontroverted.” Id. at 633.

This Court’s precedents likewise permit consideration of the whole record when evaluating whether an unpreserved error affected substantial rights. In United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), the Court held that a defendant challenging a Rule 11 error on plain-error review could not confine the inquiry to the plea colloquy; rather, the reviewing court could consider “the whole record.” Id. at 59, 74-75. The same principle controlled in United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74 (2004), which required a defendant to show a “reasonable probability” of a different outcome in light of the record as a whole. Id. at 83. And in Puckett v. United States, 556 U.S. 129 (2009), the Court emphasized that plain-error review is a “circumscribed” doctrine that depends on a contextual assessment of prejudice and discretion. Id. at 135-36.

Rehaif v. United States, 588 U.S. 176, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), does not alter that framework. Rehaif announced the substantive mens rea requirement for § 922(g), but it did not change the ordinary operation of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b). See Greer v. United States, 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2096-97 (2021) (holding that a Rehaif error is reviewed under ordinary plain-error principles). Under Greer, a defendant who failed to preserve a Rehaif claim must “make[] a sufficient argument or representation on appeal” that he would have presented evidence of lack of knowledge if the issue had been litigated below. Id. at 2097. Greer confirms that appellate courts may look beyond the trial evidence and consider undisputed record facts, including prior felony convictions, in deciding whether the error mattered. Id. at 2097-98.

That approach is consistent with the Court’s broader harmless-error and plain-error jurisprudence. Neder v. United States, 527 U.S. 1 (1999), held that an omitted element can be harmless if the record establishes that the verdict was surely unattributable to the error. Id. at 15. United States v. Marcus, 560 U.S. 258 (2010), explained that substantial-rights review asks whether the error affected the outcome in the actual case, not in the abstract. Id. at 262. And Henderson v. United States, 568 U.S. 266 (2013), reaffirmed that plain-error review is meant to correct only errors that matter in concrete cases, not every legal defect. Id. at 272-73.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would force courts to ignore the most relevant source of prejudice: the defendant’s own record. That would invert Olano, Cotton, Vonn, Dominguez Benitez, Puckett, and Greer. It would also create an unwarranted asymmetry, allowing defendants to obtain reversal for an unpreserved error even when the record shows they plainly knew the relevant status, a result the Court rejected in Greer, 141 S. Ct. at 2097-98. Plain-error review does not ask whether the jury was formally presented with every fact; it asks whether, in light of the whole case, there is a reasonable probability of a different result and whether correction is necessary to preserve confidence in the judiciary. Olano, 507 U.S. at 734-37; Cotton, 535 U.S. at 631-34; Puckett, 556 U.S. at 135-36.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
