**Argument**

I. The district court abused its discretion in denying Winbush a fingerprint expert and a continuance.

An indigent defendant is entitled to expert assistance when it is necessary for an adequate defense. Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68, 77 (1985). Although Ake addressed psychiatric assistance, the Court’s reasoning applies more broadly: the State must provide the basic tools of an effective defense when a particularized need is shown. Id. at 77, 83. This Court has likewise held that due process requires expert assistance when the defense makes a preliminary showing that the expert is reasonably necessary. Moore v. Kemp, 809 F.2d 702, 712–13 (11th Cir. 1987) (en banc) (cited approvingly in Ake, 470 U.S. at 83 n.14). In federal prosecutions, the Criminal Justice Act authorizes funding for “investigative, expert, or other services necessary for adequate representation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3006A(e)(1).

Winbush made that showing. The government’s case depended in substantial part on the physical evidence recovered after the arrest: the handgun, the jacket, the cocaine, and the inference that those items were tied to him. But the government itself acknowledged that no latent fingerprints of value were found. The need for a defense expert was therefore obvious. A fingerprint expert could have explained to the jury that the absence of prints on a handgun, jacket, or baggies does not meaningfully corroborate the government’s theory of possession, and could have aided cross-examination of the government’s witnesses. See United States v. Sloan, 776 F.2d 926, 929–30 (10th Cir. 1985) (recognizing that expert assistance may be required where it would assist counsel in preparing and presenting a defense). Denying such assistance deprived Winbush of the “basic tools” necessary to challenge the government’s possession theory. Ake, 470 U.S. at 77.

The district court’s principal reason—timing—does not justify the denial. A continuance and expert request may be refused when the movant lacks diligence, but the court must still weigh the asserted need against the prejudice from denial. Morris v. Slappy, 461 U.S. 1, 11–12 (1983); Ungar v. Sarafite, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964). This Court reviews a denial of a continuance for abuse of discretion, considering the circumstances of the request and the resulting prejudice. United States v. Sellers, 645 F.3d 830, 834–35 (7th Cir. 2011); United States v. Miller, 843 F.2d 552, 557 (7th Cir. 1988). Here, the government disclosed its expert-witness plans shortly before trial, and Winbush sought relief promptly thereafter. The case had been pending for years, but delay alone cannot eclipse a defendant’s right to a meaningful opportunity to defend. Ungar, 376 U.S. at 589.

Prejudice is clear. The government was allowed to present expert testimony about narcotics-trafficking practices and the supposed significance of the recovered evidence, while Winbush was left without a comparable expert to explain the forensic silence. That asymmetry mattered. The government’s evidence of possession was circumstantial, and the missing-fingerprint issue went directly to whether Winbush knowingly possessed the gun and drugs. Where a denied continuance “substantially impair[s] the defendant’s opportunity to secure counsel of his own choice or otherwise to prepare his defense,” reversal is warranted. United States v. Sellers, 645 F.3d at 835; see also Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 59 (1932). The denial here did exactly that.

II. The evidence was insufficient to sustain the convictions for possessing the Tennessee crack, the Kentucky crack, the firearm, and the firearm offense under § 924(c).

A conviction may stand only if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt. Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). But speculation cannot substitute for proof. United States v. Jones, 713 F.2d 1477, 1480 (7th Cir. 1983).

The government’s proof that Winbush possessed the Tennessee crack and the Kentucky crack was thin. The Tennessee crack was found one block away, in an area through which police pursued Winbush after he fled. The Kentucky crack was found in his jacket, but the chain of inferences linking that jacket to exclusive possession of the narcotics was not airtight, particularly because the jacket had been discarded during a chaotic chase. Possession under § 841 may be actual or constructive, but constructive possession requires proof of the power and intent to control the contraband. United States v. Kitchen, 57 F.3d 516, 521 (7th Cir. 1995). The record here showed proximity and suspicion, not proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

The same is true of the firearm charge. A felon-in-possession conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) requires proof that the defendant knowingly possessed the firearm. Rehaif v. United States, 588 U.S. 170, 173–74 (2019); United States v. Morris, 576 F.3d 661, 664 (7th Cir. 2009). The gun was found later inside the discarded jacket; no witness testified to Winbush’s continued possession after the chase ended. On this record, the jury was asked to infer possession from the jacket alone.

Finally, the § 924(c) conviction fails because the government had to prove that Winbush possessed the firearm “in furtherance of” a drug trafficking crime, not merely that he possessed a gun and drugs at the same time. United States v. Castillo, 406 F.3d 806, 815–16 (7th Cir. 2005). While the jury heard evidence that Winbush was armed during the chase, the government’s theory depended on an inference that the gun advanced the drug offense. That inference was not compelled beyond a reasonable doubt.

For these reasons, Winbush’s convictions should be vacated, or at minimum the case remanded for a new trial.
