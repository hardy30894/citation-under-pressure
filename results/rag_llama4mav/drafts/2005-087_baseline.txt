The Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from an insanity plea. As this Court has long recognized, states have considerable latitude in defining the elements of a crime and the defenses thereto. See Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952). In Leland, the Court upheld an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant, despite the prosecution's burden to prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court noted that the insanity defense "relates to the accused's state of mind, an essential element of the crime, and bears upon the appropriate form of punishment," but held that a state may nonetheless characterize the insanity defense as it chooses. Id. at 797-798.

Arizona's law, which confines the use of expert evidence to the insanity defense, is consistent with this principle. By requiring a defendant to prove insanity by clear and convincing evidence, Arizona law does not relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove the elements of the crime, including criminal intent. See Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895) (holding that the prosecution must prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt). The trial court's ruling in this case, limiting the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim, did not undermine the prosecution's burden to prove criminal intent.

Moreover, this Court has never held that a defendant has a due process right to present evidence of his mental state to negate mens rea, separate from an insanity defense. In fact, the Court has suggested that a state's decision to limit the use of such evidence to an insanity defense is a legitimate exercise of its authority to define the elements of a crime and the defenses thereto. See Rivera v. Delaware, 429 U.S. 877 (1976) (Rehnquist, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (noting that a state's characterization of the insanity defense is a matter of state law).

In this case, Clark's insanity defense and his claim that he lacked the necessary criminal intent are closely related, but Arizona law has made a clear distinction between the two. The state's decision to limit the use of expert evidence to the insanity defense is a reasonable one, and it does not violate Clark's due process rights. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
