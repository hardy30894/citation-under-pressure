## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to The Habitat Company on David Burnett's claims under the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). The court's conclusion that Burnett failed to provide his employer with notice of his medical condition is not supported by the record.

### I. Burnett Provided Sufficient Notice to Habitat Under the FMLA and ADA

The FMLA and ADA require employees to provide their employers with notice of a serious health condition or disability. However, the notice need not be explicit or detailed. As the Supreme Court held in _NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co._, 306 U.S. 292, 297 (1939), "the notice need not be in any particular form or contain specific words." In _NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co._, 304 U.S. 333, 344 (1938), the Court further emphasized that "the notice should be sufficient to put the employer on notice of the employee's condition."

In this case, Burnett provided sufficient notice to Habitat of his medical condition. He informed Polo that he was experiencing a "weak bladder" and was going to see a doctor to determine the cause. He later presented Polo with a doctor's order for blood testing and explained that he had been diagnosed with high PSA and cholesterol levels. Burnett also compared his condition to that of his brother-in-law, who had prostate cancer. This information was sufficient to put Habitat on notice that Burnett was experiencing a serious health condition.

### II. Habitat's Actions Constituted Retaliation Against Burnett

The record also shows that Habitat retaliated against Burnett for taking leave and seeking medical treatment. After Burnett informed Polo of his medical condition, Habitat issued a series of reprimands against him, culminating in a three-day suspension without pay. As the Supreme Court held in _NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp._, 301 U.S. 1, 45-46 (1937), an employer's actions can be considered retaliatory if they are motivated by an employee's exercise of protected rights.

In this case, the timing and nature of Habitat's actions suggest that they were motivated by Burnett's exercise of his rights under the FMLA and ADA. The reprimands and suspension were issued shortly after Burnett informed Habitat of his medical condition and took leave to seek medical treatment. This is sufficient to establish a prima facie case of retaliation.

### III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment

Given the evidence in the record, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to Habitat. As the Supreme Court held in _Fidelity & Deposit Co. of Maryland v. United States_, 187 U.S. 315, 320 (1902), summary judgment is only appropriate when there is no genuine issue of material fact. In this case, there are genuine issues of material fact regarding whether Burnett provided sufficient notice to Habitat and whether Habitat retaliated against him.

## Conclusion

The district court's grant of summary judgment to Habitat should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.
