**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Due Process Clause requires a heightened “reasoned choice” standard for determining a defendant’s competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. This Court has consistently defined competency to stand trial as the ability to understand the proceedings and assist counsel. The same standard applies when a defendant chooses to waive constitutional rights.

**I. The Due Process Clause Prescribes a Unitary Competency Standard**

In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam), this Court held that the test for competency to stand trial is “whether [the defendant] has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Drofe v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), reaffirmed that a defendant is incompetent if he lacks “the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense.” *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966), requires a competency hearing whenever there is “sufficient doubt” of the defendant’s mental capacity. This unitary standard governs all stages of a criminal proceeding.

This Court has never adopted a heightened competency standard for waiving counsel or pleading guilty. In *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam), the Court merely required that the trial court conduct a separate inquiry into the defendant’s competence to waive counsel—it did not impose a higher evidentiary threshold. The “reasoned choice” standard applied below conflates competency with the validity of the waiver itself.

**II. Competency Is Distinct from the Knowing and Intelligent Nature of a Waiver**

The Constitution separately mandates that any waiver of rights must be “knowing and intelligent.” *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938); *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975). But competency and the validity of a waiver are distinct inquiries. *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157, 170 (1986) (holding that a waiver of Miranda rights may be voluntary even if the defendant is mentally ill). A defendant may be competent yet still make an unknowing waiver; conversely, a defendant may lack capacity to make a reasoned choice but still meet the *Dusky* standard. The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that a defendant have “capacity for reasoned choice” improperly imports the waiver analysis into the competency determination.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Heightened Standard Lacks Support in Precedent**

No decision of this Court before *Godinez* required a higher level of mental functioning for waiving counsel or pleading guilty. In *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105, 108 (1954), the Court stated that a defendant may be competent to stand trial yet incompetent to waive counsel, but *Massey* did not articulate a separate legal standard—it merely recognized that the same *Dusky* test must be applied at the waiver stage. The Court in *Rees v. Peyton*, 384 U.S. 312, 314 (1966) (per curiam), applied the *Dusky* standard to a capital defendant’s decision to abandon appeals. Consistent with these authorities, the unitary standard remains the constitutional floor.

**IV. The “Reasoned Choice” Standard Is Unworkable and Unnecessary**

Requiring a heightened standard would force trial courts to assess a defendant’s decision-making ability beyond the rational understanding required by *Dusky*. Such a standard invites subjective judgments and creates an unwarranted distinction between defendants who plead guilty and those who demand trial. A defendant competent under *Dusky* can rationally choose to plead guilty even if the choice appears unwise. *Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742, 758 (1970) (a guilty plea is not invalid simply because it is entered to avoid a possible death penalty). The proper safeguard is the requirement that the waiver itself be knowing and intelligent, not that the defendant possess a higher level of mental capacity.

**V. The State Court Applied the Correct Standard**

The Nevada trial court conducted the requisite competency hearing and, based on psychiatric evaluations, found Moran competent under *Dusky*. The postconviction court similarly reviewed the evidence and concluded that Moran was competent. Because the *Dusky* standard was satisfied, the state courts correctly declined to apply a heightened test. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary conclusion usurps the state court’s role and imposes a novel constitutional requirement that has no basis in this Court’s precedents.

**Conclusion**

The Due Process Clause requires only the *Dusky* standard for competency, regardless of whether a defendant pleads guilty or waives counsel. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
