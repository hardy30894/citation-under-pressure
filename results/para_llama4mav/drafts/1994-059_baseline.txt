## ARGUMENT

### I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE U.S. CONSTITUTION PRECLUDE STATES FROM IMPOSING TERM LIMITS ON MEMBERS OF CONGRESS

The Qualifications Clauses of the U.S. Constitution, found in Article I, Sections 2 and 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress. The Constitution sets forth specific requirements for age, citizenship, and residency that candidates must meet to be eligible for congressional office. The framers of the Constitution deliberately chose to limit the qualifications for congressional service to these enumerated criteria, thereby precluding states from imposing additional qualifications, including term limits.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are a constitutional ceiling, beyond which states may not add further requirements. In Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that Congress may not add to the qualifications specified in the Constitution, emphasizing the exclusivity of the constitutional criteria. Similarly, in U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill, the Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals observed that the Qualifications Clauses "establish the exclusive, rather than the minimum, qualifications for service in Congress." (citing Powell v. McCormack).

The historical context and the constitutional text itself support the conclusion that the Qualifications Clauses are exhaustive. The framers rejected proposals to allow states to set additional qualifications for congressional candidates, opting instead for a uniform national standard. As the Supreme Court noted in The Federalist No. 52, the framers sought to ensure that the qualifications for congressional service would be consistent across the states, thereby promoting national unity and coherence.

### II. AMENDMENT 73'S TERM LIMITS PROVISION FOR CONGRESSIONAL CANDIDATES IS UNCONSTITUTIONAL

Amendment 73's provision limiting the terms of congressional candidates from Arkansas is unconstitutional because it imposes an additional qualification not enumerated in the Constitution. By preventing candidates who have served a certain number of terms from appearing on the ballot, Amendment 73 effectively adds a term limit qualification that is not authorized by the Qualifications Clauses.

The circuit court correctly determined that Section 3 of Amendment 73, which pertains to congressional term limits, violates the Qualifications Clauses of the U.S. Constitution. The court's conclusion is supported by the Supreme Court's decision in Powell v. McCormack, which held that Congress may not impose additional qualifications beyond those specified in the Constitution.

### III. THE SEVERABILITY CLAUSE DOES NOT SAVE SECTIONS 1 AND 2 OF AMENDMENT 73

The severability clause in Amendment 73 does not permit the survival of Sections 1 and 2, which impose term limits on state officials, in the face of the unconstitutionality of Section 3. The ballot title and the text of Amendment 73 presented the term limits for state and federal officials as an indivisible package, making it impossible to discern whether voters would have approved term limits for state officials independently of the congressional term limits.

The practice of bundling a constitutional provision with one of dubious legality, as occurred here, undermines the integrity of the electoral process and is unfair to voters. The sponsors of Amendment 73 chose to present the term limits for state and federal officials together, without providing voters with a clear opportunity to approve or reject the provisions separately. As a result, the unconstitutionality of Section 3 taints the entire amendment, rendering Sections 1 and 2 unenforceable as well.

In Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964), this Court established that the test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion. Given the inseparable presentation of the term limits provisions in Amendment 73, it is impossible to determine whether the voters would have approved Sections 1 and 2 in the absence of Section 3. Therefore, the severability clause cannot save the remaining provisions of Amendment 73.
