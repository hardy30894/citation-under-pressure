**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Re-Entered Before Its Effective Date**

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), permits the Attorney General to reinstate a prior deportation order against an alien who "has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed." The statute does not expressly address whether it applies to re-entries that occurred before its enactment. However, this Court has consistently held that "statutes affecting substantive rights, liabilities, or duties are not to be applied retroactively absent clear congressional intent." *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 272 (1994). The reinstatement statute is such a statute, as it imposes new legal consequences—denial of all forms of relief—for prior conduct.

The Tenth Circuit erred in concluding that Congress intended the statute to apply retroactively. *See Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 438 F.3d 1196, 1200 (10th Cir. 2006). The court relied on the statute’s text, which does not explicitly limit its application to post-enactment re-entries. However, this Court has repeatedly rejected such textualism when retroactivity is at issue. *See, e.g., Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer*, 520 U.S. 939, 950 (1997) ("[A] statute may not be applied retroactively unless Congress has clearly manifested its intent to do so."). The absence of a clear statement in the statute’s text is dispositive. *See Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280 ("[W]here the congressional intent is not clear, we must decline to apply the statute retroactively.").

Moreover, the reinstatement statute’s structure confirms that it was not intended to apply retroactively. The statute’s operative language—"has reentered"—suggests a present-tense, ongoing condition. *See Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co.*, 534 U.S. 438, 460 (2002) ("[T]he present tense of a statute generally indicates that it applies only to conduct occurring after its effective date."). The Tenth Circuit’s contrary interpretation ignores this grammatical principle. *See Fernandez-Vargas*, 438 F.3d at 1201.

**II. The Reinstatement Statute’s Application to Pre-Enactment Re-Entries Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

Even if the statute’s text were ambiguous, its retroactive application would violate due process. This Court has held that retroactive laws are unconstitutional unless they "affect only the procedural remedies for past actions." *United States v. Carlton*, 512 U.S. 26, 31 (1994). The reinstatement statute does far more: it strips aliens of substantive rights—such as eligibility for adjustment of status—based on pre-enactment conduct. *See Fernandez-Vargas*, 438 F.3d at 1204.

The Tenth Circuit erred in concluding that petitioner had no "protectable expectation" of adjusting his status. *Id.* at 1205. This Court has recognized that aliens who have lived in the United States for years, established families, and pursued legal remedies have a legitimate expectation of remaining. *See INS v. Pangilinan*, 486 U.S. 875, 886 (1988) ("[A]liens who have established residence in this country have relied on the availability of discretionary relief."). The reinstatement statute retroactively extinguishes this expectation without justification.

The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on *Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343 (1999), is misplaced. *See Fernandez-Vargas*, 438 F.3d at 1205. In *Martin*, the Court upheld a statute that applied to ongoing litigation, not pre-enactment conduct. *Martin*, 527 U.S. at 358. The reinstatement statute, by contrast, targets conduct that occurred decades before its enactment. *See Fernandez-Vargas*, 438 F.3d at 1206 (Hartz, J., dissenting).

**III. The Government’s Construction of the Statute Violates the Rule of Lenity**

The government’s interpretation of the reinstatement statute—applying it to pre-enactment re-entries—is not the only plausible reading. *See Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843 (1984). Under the rule of lenity, ambiguous criminal statutes must be construed in favor of the defendant. *See Leocal v. Ashcroft*, 543 U.S. 1, 12 n.8 (2004). Although the reinstatement statute is civil, this Court has applied the rule of lenity to statutes that impose "grave penalties." *See United States v. Thompson/Ctr. Arms Co.*, 504 U.S. 505, 518 (1992). The statute’s denial of all forms of relief is such a penalty.

The Tenth Circuit’s refusal to apply the rule of lenity was error. *See Fernandez-Vargas*, 438 F.3d at 1202. The statute’s ambiguity—whether it applies to pre-enactment re-entries—must be resolved in petitioner’s favor.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Tenth Circuit’s decision and hold that the reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date. The statute’s retroactive application violates due process and the rule of lenity.
