## ARGUMENT

### I. The Constitution fixes the exclusive qualifications for Members of Congress.

The Qualifications Clauses provide that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the State from which he is chosen. U.S. Const. art. I, §2, cl. 2. A parallel clause governs Senators. U.S. Const. art. I, §3, cl. 3. This Court has held that the Constitution prescribes the *sole* qualifications for congressional service. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court reviewed whether Congress could refuse to seat a Member who met the constitutional qualifications. The Court interpreted the Qualifications Clauses as exclusive and stated that “Congress has no power to expand or contract the qualifications” contained in the Constitution. *Id.* at 521–22. If Congress cannot add qualifications, a fortiori a State cannot.

The exclusivity of the federal Qualifications Clauses was also recognized in *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651 (1884). There, the Court explained that the right to vote for Members of Congress is “derived from the Constitution” and that the qualifications of electors and candidates are fixed by federal law. *Id.* at 663. The Framers deliberately chose uniform, national standards, rejecting proposals for property qualifications and other tests. A State cannot rewrite that balance.

### II. Amendment 73 imposes an additional qualification, not a permissible election regulation.

Arkansas attempts to characterize Section 3 as a mere “ballot access” regulation. The text says otherwise. Section 3 provides that any person who has served three or more House terms “shall not be certified as a candidate and shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot.” That is a categorical disqualification. No incumbent who meets every federal constitutional qualification may run. It does not set the time, place, or manner of elections under Article I, Section 4; it removes a class of wholly qualified individuals from the electorate’s choice.

The substance, not the label, determines whether a State has imposed a qualification. In *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724 (1974), the Court upheld certain ballot-access restrictions only because they were “closely tied” to the integrity of the electoral process. *Id.* at 730. But a State cannot use its election power “to impose substantial burdens on the right to vote or to associate for political purposes” without a compelling justification. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 789 (1983). Amendment 73 burdens voters who wish to support incumbents and burdens candidates who have already served ably. The asserted justification—limiting terms of federal officials—is not a state interest the Constitution permits States to pursue. It is an interest committed to the Article V amendment process.

### III. The only lawful route to congressional term limits is a constitutional amendment.

Appellants invoke popular dissatisfaction with congressional incumbency. But the remedy is not state legislation. This Court has never permitted a State to add a qualification for Congress, whether styled as an eligibility requirement or a ballot restriction. As the Court emphasized in *Powell*, the “exclusive” qualifications were “intended to be permanent” and cannot be “supplemented by either Congress or the states.” 395 U.S. at 522, 548.

If Arkansas could limit its congressional delegation to two terms, 49 other States could impose different limits, or no limits. The result would be a “hodge-podge” of qualifications that defeats the Framers’ design for a national legislature. Members of Congress are officers of the United States; they represent the Nation as much as their districts. State-imposed tests would permit each State to dictate who may sit in the national councils, contrary to the Supremacy Clause and to *Yarbrough*’s recognition of uniform national electoral standards.

The principle that a State cannot do indirectly what the Constitution forbids directly is fundamental. In *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. 316 (1819), the Court struck down a state tax on a federal instrumentality because “the power to tax involves the power to destroy.” Here, the power to disqualify is the power to control the composition of Congress. No State may wield that power.

### IV. State-imposed qualifications would undermine the right to vote.

The right to vote is “preservative of all rights.” *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 370 (1886). Amendment 73 deprives Arkansas voters of the right to vote for candidates who are unquestionably qualified under Article I. It forces the removal from the ballot of officials who have served their State and Nation, based solely on a limitation the Constitution does not contain. The amendment’s ballot-access label cannot obscure its effect: it adds a third, fourth, and fifth qualification to those enumerated in the Constitution.

### Conclusion

States may not alter the qualifications for the United States Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution. Section 3 of Amendment 73 is unconstitutional, and the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
