# ARGUMENT

## A GRANT OF TEMPORARY PROTECTED STATUS CONSTITUTES AN "ADMISSION" UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1255

### I. The Plain Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Satisfies § 1255's Admission Requirement

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) (emphasis added). Congress did not write this provision in a vacuum—it directed courts to import lawful nonimmigrant status directly into the § 1255 framework.

Critically, "nonimmigrant status" is itself a term of art that presupposes admission. The INA's structure confirms this: a person cannot possess "nonimmigrant status" without having been admitted as a nonimmigrant. Every category of nonimmigrant enumerated in § 1101(a)(15) describes an alien admitted for a specific purpose and duration. When Congress deemed TPS recipients to be "in… lawful status as a nonimmigrant," it necessarily deemed them admitted in that status, because there is no such thing as unadmitted nonimmigrant status under the statutory scheme.

This Court has long recognized that statutory fictions must be given their intended legal effect. In *Astoria Federal Savings & Loan Ass'n v. Solimino*, 501 U.S. 104 (1991), the Court explained that when Congress creates a legal fiction, courts must apply it according to its evident purpose. Here, Congress's purpose was unmistakable: to place TPS recipients on equal footing with other nonimmigrants for adjustment purposes, notwithstanding the manner of their entry.

### II. The Statutory Structure Confirms That TPS Status Encompasses Admission

The Sixth and Ninth Circuits correctly recognized this structural point. In *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548 (6th Cir. 2013), the court held that "the plain language of the TPS statute" compels the conclusion that "a TPS recipient is eligible to apply for adjustment of status" because "the language of the TPS statute unambiguously grants nonimmigrant status, which necessarily means admission." *Id.* at 553-54. The Ninth Circuit reached the same conclusion in *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954 (9th Cir. 2017), explaining that "an individual can only be in lawful status as a nonimmigrant if he or she has been admitted." *Id.* at 960 (internal quotation marks omitted). As the Ninth Circuit observed, "[b]y the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted." *Id.*

The Third Circuit's contrary holding rests on an artificial distinction between "status" and "admission" that Congress did not draw in § 1254a(f)(4). While this Court has recognized that "admission" and "status" are analytically distinct concepts in other contexts, *see Hanif v. Att'y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012), Congress expressly bridged that gap here by legislating that TPS recipients possess "lawful status as a nonimmigrant"—a status that, by definition, requires admission.

### III. The Third Circuit's Reliance on Inapposite Analogies Misconstrues the Statute

The court below erroneously compared TPS to asylum and Family Unity benefits, reasoning that because those programs confer lawful status without admission, TPS must operate the same way. But neither the asylum statute nor the Family Unity Program contains language analogous to § 1254a(f)4)'s explicit deeming provision. Congress chose specific statutory language for TPS recipients that it did not employ for other status-conferring programs. Under the interpretive canon articulated in *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16 (1983), "where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another," that choice is generally "intentional and purposeful." *Id.* at 23. Congress's decision to describe TPS recipients as being in "lawful status as a nonimmigrant"—rather than merely conferring a benefit or protection from removal—reflects a deliberate choice to align TPS recipients with admitted nonimmigrants for adjustment purposes.

### IV. This Interpretation Furthers Congress's Remedial Purpose

TPS is fundamentally a humanitarian program designed to protect individuals from countries suffering armed conflict, environmental disaster, or extraordinary conditions. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Reading § 1254a(f)(4) to permit adjustment of status for TPS recipients—regardless of manner of entry—serves this remedial purpose by allowing individuals who have lawfully resided in the United States for years, often married to citizens or employed lawfully, to normalize their status without the absurdity of requiring departure and reentry into potentially dangerous conditions from which TPS was designed to shield them.

The Third Circuit's suggestion that TPS recipients may simply "remain in the United States with lawful nonimmigrant status" indefinitely, without adjusting status, ignores the precarious and explicitly temporary nature of TPS protection, which depends entirely on continued executive branch redesignation and can be terminated at any time.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that a grant of Temporary Protected Status constitutes an admission under 8 U.S.C. § 1255, and reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit.
