Riddle is entitled to habeas relief because the prosecutor’s conduct violated due process and the adversarial structure guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment. A prosecutor may not “win a conviction” by injecting the prestige of his office into the case as a witness and then resuming his role as advocate. Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935). This Court has long recognized that a conviction obtained through fundamentally unfair prosecutorial conduct cannot stand. Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935); Pyle v. Kansas, 317 U.S. 213, 216 (1942).

Here, the elected district attorney, Danny Hill, testified for the State to rebut Riddle’s explanation that Hill had threatened him with the death penalty after arraignment. Hill then remained in the case and his office used his testimony as substantive evidence of Riddle’s dishonesty in closing argument. That sequence violated the basic rule that “the functions of witness and advocate are inconsistent.” Berger, 295 U.S. at 88. The vice is not cured because the prosecutor’s testimony addressed a collateral matter. Once the prosecutor became a witness against the accused, his testimony carried the imprimatur of official authority and impermissibly bolstered the State’s case. See id.; see also Young v. United States, 315 U.S. 257, 258-60 (1942) (reversing where the prosecutor’s conduct undermined the fairness and dignity of the proceeding).

The danger is especially acute in a capital case, where the Supreme Court has emphasized that the sentencing process must be free from arbitrary and unreliable influences. See Lisenba v. California, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941). Due process is violated when the State’s conduct “so infused the trial with unfairness” that the resulting conviction is unreliable. Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952). Hill’s dual role did exactly that. He was not merely a background fact witness; he was the chief prosecutor, the public face of the State’s accusation, and then an evidentiary weapon used to paint Riddle as a liar. That combination “cannot be tolerated in a system that aims at justice.” Berger, 295 U.S. at 88.

The State may argue that any error was harmless. But where the prosecutor’s personal testimony becomes part of the evidentiary case and is later argued to the jury as proof of the defendant’s untruthfulness, the influence on the verdict is obvious. See Napue v. Illinois, 360 U.S. 264, 269 (1959) (due process offended when false or misleading evidence may have affected the judgment of the jury); Alcorta v. Texas, 355 U.S. 28, 31-32 (1957) (reversal required where the State allowed the jury to be misled on a matter affecting credibility). The prosecution’s case turned on Riddle’s intent, consent defense, and credibility. Hill’s testimony went directly to those issues. In that setting, the State cannot show the error harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. See Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967).

Riddle also was entitled to an evidentiary hearing in federal court. Where the material facts were not adequately developed in the state proceedings, a habeas petitioner is entitled to a hearing if he alleges facts which, if proven, would entitle him to relief. Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 312-13 (1963). That standard is satisfied here because the critical facts are undisputed: Hill testified, remained as prosecutor, and his testimony was used affirmatively against Riddle. To the extent the State relied on the paper record alone to defeat relief, it ignored that habeas courts must ensure a full and fair opportunity to prove constitutional error. Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 463-65 (1953).

This Court should therefore reverse the judgment below, grant the writ, or at minimum remand for an evidentiary hearing consistent with Townsend. A capital conviction secured through the prosecutor’s testimonial participation and continued advocacy is incompatible with due process under Berger, Mooney, Pyle, Napue, and Chapman.
