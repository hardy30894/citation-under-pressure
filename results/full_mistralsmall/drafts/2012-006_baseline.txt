**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE GOVERNMENT BEARS THE BURDEN OF PERSUASION TO PROVE CONSPIRACY MEMBERSHIP BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT**

The government’s burden of proof in conspiracy prosecutions is well settled. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, the Supreme Court held that conspiracy convictions require proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a knowing and voluntary participant in the conspiracy. 438 U.S. 422, 465 (1978). This burden does not shift to the defendant, even where the defendant presents evidence of withdrawal. *Griffin v. United States*, 502 U.S. 46, 59 (1991) (citing *Holland v. United States*, 348 U.S. 121, 138 (1954)).

The D.C. Circuit has consistently applied this rule. In *United States v. Childress*, 58 F.3d 693, 704 (D.C. Cir. 1995), the court reiterated that the government must prove each element of the conspiracy, including the defendant’s knowing participation, beyond a reasonable doubt. The court rejected the argument that withdrawal alone shifts the burden, explaining that “[t]he government’s burden of persuasion does not evaporate simply because a defendant claims to have withdrawn.” *Id.* at 705 (citing *United States v. Shively*, 927 F.2d 804, 812 (5th Cir. 1991)).

Here, the government failed to meet its burden. The record shows that the alleged leaders of the conspiracy, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, split prior to the limitations period. Without evidence that the conspiracy continued into the relevant period, the government’s case failed as a matter of law. *United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10, 16 (1994) (requiring proof of an agreement and overt acts in furtherance during the limitations period).

II. **WITHDRAWAL DOES NOT SHIFT THE BURDEN OF PERSUASION**

Petitioners do not dispute that withdrawal is a defense to conspiracy liability. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1233 (7th Cir. 1981). However, withdrawal does not alter the government’s burden of proof. The Supreme Court has made clear that “[t]he burden of persuasion never shifts to the defendant.” *Holland*, 348 U.S. at 138. The D.C. Circuit has followed this rule strictly. *Childress*, 58 F.3d at 705.

Petitioners’ proposed rule—that withdrawal shifts the burden to the government to disprove membership—finds no support in Supreme Court or D.C. Circuit precedent. To the contrary, courts have held that withdrawal is an affirmative defense that the defendant must prove by a preponderance of the evidence. *United States v. Jimenez Recio*, 537 U.S. 270, 275 (2002) (citing *Griffin*, 502 U.S. at 59). Even if Petitioners had presented evidence of withdrawal, the government retained the burden to prove membership beyond a reasonable doubt.

III. **THE JURY WAS NOT PROPERLY INSTRUCTED ON THE GOVERNMENT’S BURDEN**

The jury was not instructed that the government bore the burden of proving Petitioners’ membership in the conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt. This error was structural. *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275, 281-82 (1993) (holding that a constitutionally deficient reasonable-doubt instruction is structural error requiring automatic reversal). The D.C. Circuit’s failure to recognize this error warrants this Court’s review.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed. The government bore the burden of proving Petitioners’ membership in the conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, and that burden did not shift upon evidence of withdrawal. The jury was not properly instructed on this burden, and the error was structural. The petition for certiorari should be granted.
