ARGUMENT

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention of a removable alien. Detention must remain tied to a lawful purpose and may not extend once removal ceases to be reasonably foreseeable.

The Fifth Amendment forbids deprivation of liberty without due process of law. Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886). This protection extends to all persons within the United States, including resident aliens. Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896). Detention beyond the removal period that has no realistic prospect of termination therefore raises grave constitutional concerns that the statute must be construed to avoid. See Yamataya v. Fisher, 189 U.S. 86, 100-01 (1903).

Congress has long been understood to authorize detention only as an incident to removal. When removal becomes impossible, the justification for continued custody evaporates. Ex parte Mitsuye Endo, 323 U.S. 283, 297 (1944). The statute’s text supplies no affirmative grant of power to detain once that purpose can no longer be served. See Ng Fung Ho v. White, 259 U.S. 276, 281 (1922).

Resident aliens who have effected entry stand on different footing from excludable aliens stopped at the border. Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953). For the former, indefinite detention without criminal trial or other procedural safeguards cannot be reconciled with due process. Bridges v. Wixon, 326 U.S. 135, 154 (1945). Even when an alien poses a danger to the community, detention remains subject to judicial inquiry into its duration and necessity. Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 537-38 (1952).

The writ of habeas corpus supplies the mechanism for testing whether custody has exceeded statutory and constitutional bounds. Chin Yow v. United States, 208 U.S. 8, 13 (1908). A court must therefore determine whether removal remains reasonably foreseeable; if it does not, continued detention is no longer authorized.

Because the statute can and must be read to impose a reasonable time limitation, it does not empower the Attorney General to detain removable aliens indefinitely.
