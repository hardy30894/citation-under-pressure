**ARGUMENT**

Bond may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to overturn her conviction. The Supreme Court has already held that private parties, “absent the states or their officers,” lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment objections to federal action. *Tennessee Elec. Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Auth.*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939). That rule controls here.

A. **Bond lacks standing to assert a Tenth Amendment challenge to § 229**

In *Tennessee Electric*, private utility companies contended that a federally created and operated power program invaded the field of local regulation protected by the Tenth Amendment. 306 U.S. at 143-44. The Court rejected the claim, stating that “the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [A]mendment.” *Id.* at 144. That holding is jurisdictional in character and therefore precedes any merits inquiry. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95, 101-02 (1998).

Bond argues that *Tennessee Electric* is dicta. It is not. The Supreme Court “dismissed the Tenth Amendment claim after analyzing both the standing issue and the merits,” and the standing ruling was therefore an alternative ground, not obiter dictum. *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005); see also *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978). The Third Circuit correctly recognized that *Tennessee Electric* forecloses Bond’s position. See *United States v. Bond*, 581 F.3d 128, 134-36 (3d Cir. 2009).

Bond is also wrong that private-party Tenth Amendment standing follows from the nature of the claim itself. The courts of appeals have divided on the issue, but the better-reasoned decisions follow *Tennessee Electric* and deny standing. See *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 33-36; *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004). The rationale is straightforward: the Tenth Amendment protects the sovereignty of the States, and where the States themselves do not object, a private criminal defendant may not convert that structural limitation into an individual defense. *Tennessee Elec. Power*, 306 U.S. at 144; *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35.

B. **Bond’s conviction under § 229 is not beyond federal power**

Even if Bond had standing, her Tenth Amendment argument would fail. Congress enacted 18 U.S.C. § 229 to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention, a valid treaty ratified by the United States. The Supreme Court has long held that when a treaty is valid, Congress may enact legislation “necessary and proper” to carry it into effect. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432-33 (1920). That principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004). The Third Circuit correctly noted that treaty-implementing legislation need not rest on an independent enumerated power such as the Commerce Clause. *Bond*, 581 F.3d at 136-39; see also *United States v. Lue*, 134 F.3d 79, 84-85 (2d Cir. 1998); *United States v. Ferreira*, 275 F.3d 1020, 1027-28 (11th Cir. 2001).

Bond’s premise—that § 229 must be invalid because her conduct was local and would ordinarily be prosecuted under state law—misunderstands the federal law at issue. Congress criminalized the possession and use of “chemical weapon[s]” as defined by statute. 18 U.S.C. § 229(a)(1), § 229F(1). Bond’s repeated application of toxic chemicals to a victim’s home, car, and mailbox falls squarely within that prohibition. See *United States v. Powell*, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975) (a vagueness challenge fails if the statute is clear in its application to the defendant’s conduct). Her claim that state law would have provided a more appropriate forum does not create a constitutional defense to a valid federal prosecution. *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945).

C. **The Court should affirm the judgment**

Because Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge, this Court need not reach the merits. *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 94-95. But if it does, the judgment should still be affirmed because § 229 is valid treaty-implementing legislation under *Holland* and *Lara*. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432-33; *Lara*, 541 U.S. at 201. Bond’s conviction therefore stands.
