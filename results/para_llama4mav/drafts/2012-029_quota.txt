The Supreme Court of Texas erred in holding that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-law legal malpractice claim. The court's decision was based on an overly broad interpretation of the "arising under" language in 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), which was influenced by the Federal Circuit's decisions in Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed.Cir.2007), and Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed.Cir.2007).

However, the Supreme Court has established a more nuanced test for determining whether a state-law claim arises under federal law. In Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308 (2005), the Court held that a state-law claim arises under federal law if it "necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." Id. at 314. Applying this test, it is clear that Minton's malpractice claim does not arise under exclusive federal patent law jurisdiction.

First, while resolving a federal patent issue is necessary to Minton's state-law malpractice claim, the federal issue is not actually disputed. The experimental use exception to the on-sale bar is a well-established concept in patent law, and the parties do not dispute its applicability. See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co., 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed.Cir.2005).

Second, even if the federal issue were disputed, it is not substantial within the context of the malpractice claim. The malpractice claim is primarily a state-law issue, and the patent law issue is merely incidental to it. See New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner, 270 Neb. 264, 702 N.W.2d 336 (2005); E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP, 189 Cal.App.4th 1140, 117 Cal.Rptr.3d 516 (Cal.Ct.App.2010).

Third, exercising jurisdiction over Minton's malpractice claim in state court will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. The state court is capable of applying federal patent law principles to the extent necessary to resolve the malpractice claim. See Grable, 545 U.S. at 313-14; Singh v. Duane Morris LLP, 538 F.3d 334, 338 (5th Cir.2008).

The Federal Circuit's decisions in Air Measurement and Immunocept are not controlling, as they fail to properly apply the Grable test. See Davis v. Brouse McDowell, L.P.A., 596 F.3d 1355 (Fed.Cir.2010); Warrior Sports, Inc. v. Dickinson Wright, P.L.L.C., 631 F.3d 1367 (Fed.Cir.2011). Other courts have also declined to follow the Federal Circuit's approach, instead applying the Grable test to determine whether a state-law claim arises under federal patent law. See, e.g., Roof Technical Servs., Inc. v. Hill, 679 F.Supp.2d 749 (N.D.Tex.2010); Genelink Biosciences, Inc. v. Colby, 722 F.Supp.2d 592 (D.N.J.2010).

In Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800 (1988), the Supreme Court established that a case arises under federal patent law if "federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law." Id. at 808-09. Minton's malpractice claim does not meet this standard, as it is a state-law claim that only incidentally involves patent law.

The decision of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed, and the case should be remanded to the state court for further proceedings. The state court has jurisdiction to hear Minton's malpractice claim, and the Federal Circuit's decisions do not control the outcome. By applying the Grable test and considering the relevant case law, it is clear that Minton's claim does not arise under exclusive federal patent law jurisdiction.
