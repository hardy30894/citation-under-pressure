# ARGUMENT

## I. THIS COURT'S PRECEDENT PERMITS LODESTAR ENHANCEMENTS IN RARE AND EXCEPTIONAL CASES SUPPORTED BY SPECIFIC EVIDENCE

This Court has never held that quality of performance and results obtained are categorically foreclosed as grounds for enhancing a lodestar fee. To the contrary, in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), this Court expressly left open the possibility of enhancement, holding only that the plaintiffs in that case had failed to justify one. The Court stated that upward adjustment may be warranted in the "rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged." *Id.* at 899. Critically, *Blum* did not announce a categorical rule; it announced an evidentiary standard.

This Court reaffirmed that standard in *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986) ("*Delaware Valley I*"). Far from overruling *Blum*, the Court there "further refined" its views and cited *Blum* with approval, confirming that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are ... permissible ... in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." *Id.* at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898–901). While the Court cautioned that "the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar," it used the qualifier "ordinarily" precisely because it recognized that extraordinary cases exist. *Id.* at 566 (emphasis added). Had the Court intended a categorical bar, it would have said so; instead, it preserved the *Blum* framework and simply demanded rigorous evidentiary support.

This Court's earlier decision in *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), likewise recognized that "where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee," and that "in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified." *Id.* at 435. *Blum* quoted this very language with approval. 465 U.S. at 901. This unbroken line of authority—from *Hensley* through *Blum* to *Delaware Valley I*—establishes that enhancement is available, not foreclosed, when the fee applicant carries the demanding burden of proof.

## II. EVERY CIRCUIT TO CONSIDER THE QUESTION HAS CONFIRMED THAT ENHANCEMENTS REMAIN AVAILABLE IN EXCEPTIONAL CASES

The uniform view of the courts of appeals confirms this reading. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits have all held that enhancement remains available upon the requisite showing. *See Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999) (lodestar "may be adjusted based on several factors, including in particular the results obtained"); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999) (affirming enhancement "on account of the exceptional results obtained"); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993) (enhancements proper where supported by specific evidence and detailed findings); *Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794–95 (6th Cir. 2004) (*Delaware Valley I* permits enhancements in rare and exceptional cases); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999) (applicant must show quality and results "superior to what one reasonably should expect"); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000) (enhancement justified "only in the rare case where there is specific evidence" of superior quality and exceptional success); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997) (lodestar may be adjusted "especially where the degree of success achieved is exceptional"). This unanimity among the circuits—with no split for this Court to resolve—confirms that petitioners' categorical rule finds no support anywhere in the federal judiciary.

## III. THE DISTRICT COURT'S DETAILED FINDINGS SATISFY THE *BLUM*/*DELAWARE VALLEY I* STANDARD

The district court did precisely what *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* require: it made detailed, record-based findings explaining why the lodestar failed to capture the true value of counsel's performance. The court explained that "the superb quality of counsel's representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee." This finding rested on the court's direct observation of counsel's performance "throughout this long and difficult case," informed by the court's own "58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge." *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006).

This case is the paradigm of the "rare" and "exceptional" case *Blum* contemplated. The comprehensive injunctive relief obtained, the extraordinary duration and complexity of the litigation, and the sustained excellence of representation throughout—all specifically documented by the court most familiar with the litigation—provide exactly the kind of "specific evidence" *Blum* demands. 465 U.S. at 899. As the Eleventh Circuit correctly recognized, "the district judge is in the best position to evaluate trial level advocacy," and appellate courts must "afford the district court the deference and discretion to which it is entitled." *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008).

## CONCLUSION

Petitioners ask this Court to adopt a categorical rule that neither *Blum* nor *Delaware Valley I* announced, that no circuit has adopted, and that would strip district courts of the discretion this Court has consistently preserved. The judgment below should be affirmed.
