The Controlled Substances Act does not violate Article I. Under the long-settled standard, Congress may confer discretion on executive officials so long as it supplies an ascertainable policy and standards to guide execution. J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). The CSA easily meets that test. Congress identified the objective—protecting the public from dangerous drugs—specified the criteria the Attorney General must consider, and required consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(a), (b), (c).

This Court has repeatedly upheld broad delegations in technical, rapidly changing regulatory fields. In Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 693-94 (1892), the Court sustained a tariff statute authorizing the President to suspend favorable duties upon specified findings, explaining that Congress may vest authority to ascertain facts on which statutory consequences depend. In Cargo of the Brig Aurora v. United States, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 382, 388 (1813), the Court upheld a statute making trade restrictions turn on a presidential proclamation. In Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), the Court sustained delegation to administrative officers to exclude inferior tea, because Congress had fixed a legislative standard. And in United States v. Eaton, 144 U.S. 677, 687-88 (1892), the Court held that Congress may authorize administrative regulations whose violation carries criminal consequences.

Petitioners’ attempt to invoke Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), fails. Those cases struck down statutes that conferred largely standardless authority. Panama Refining, 293 U.S. at 430; Schechter Poultry, 295 U.S. at 541-42. The CSA is different: it cabins temporary scheduling to an imminent hazard to the public safety, requires consideration of specified factors, and imposes a short duration and notice requirements. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h); see Opp Cotton Mills, Inc. v. Administrator of Wage and Hour Division of Department of Labor, 312 U.S. 126, 145-46 (1941) (upholding delegation where Congress directed agency to act under stated standards). The statute is therefore far closer to the delegations sustained in J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co., 276 U.S. at 409, and Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250, than to the open-ended grants invalidated in 1935.

Nor does the fact that drug scheduling has criminal consequences change the analysis. Congress may attach criminal penalties to violations of validly promulgated administrative rules. United States v. Eaton, 144 U.S. 677, 687-88 (1892); see also Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506 (1911). The constitutional question is whether Congress supplied a sufficient standard, not whether the delegated power can affect criminal liability. J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co., 276 U.S. at 409.

Petitioners’ subdelegation argument likewise fails. Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions” under the drug laws. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). Long before enactment of the CSA, this Court recognized that executive officers may act through subordinates when Congress has entrusted functions to a department. Shurtleff v. United States, 189 U.S. 311, 318-19 (1903); United States v. George, 228 U.S. 14, 24-25 (1913). The DEA Administrator acted within that established administrative structure.

Finally, the Attorney General’s temporary scheduling authority is particularly appropriate because Congress faced a fast-moving problem involving new synthetic drugs and expressly designed an emergency procedure for prompt action. Rapid response in a technical field is a classic basis for upholding delegation. J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co., 276 U.S. at 409; Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250; American Power & Light Co. v. Securities and Exchange Commission, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946). The judgment below should be affirmed.
