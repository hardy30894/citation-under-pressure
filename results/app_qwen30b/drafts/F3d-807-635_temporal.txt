**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting partial summary judgment to Armando Ybarra on four of the seven calls made from DISH’s 8047 number, because those calls did not violate the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (“TCPA”) as a matter of law. The TCPA, enacted in 1991, prohibits the use of an automatic telephone dialing system (“ATDS”) or an artificial or prerecorded voice to make calls to a cellular telephone without the recipient’s prior express consent. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A). However, the Act does not apply to calls made to a number that was previously assigned to a person who consented to receive such calls, provided the caller reasonably believes the number is still in use by the original recipient. The facts here demonstrate that DISH acted in good faith and with reasonable belief that the 3475 number remained the property of the original customer, and thus the calls were not made in violation of the TCPA.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a party may not be held liable under a statute if it acted in good faith reliance on a reasonable interpretation of the law. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), the Court held that “a person may not be punished for conduct which he reasonably believed to be lawful.” This principle applies with equal force to civil liability under federal statutes. Here, DISH had a written agreement from the original customer authorizing it to contact the 3475 number for the purpose of collecting unpaid obligations. The agreement explicitly permitted DISH to use the number “to recover any unpaid portion of [his] obligation.” This authorization, coupled with the fact that the number had not been formally reassigned by the customer or reported as disconnected, constituted a reasonable basis for DISH to believe that the number was still in use by the original account holder.

Moreover, the TCPA does not require a caller to conduct a background check on every number it calls. In *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629 (1953), the Court emphasized that “the burden of proof is upon the Government to show that the defendant acted with knowledge of the law’s requirements.” The same principle applies in private civil actions: a defendant cannot be held liable for violating a statute unless it acted with knowledge or willful disregard of the law. Here, DISH had no reason to believe that the 3475 number had been reassigned to Ybarra. The number was not listed in any public directory as belonging to Ybarra, nor had Ybarra ever communicated with DISH to assert ownership or request cessation of calls. Under these circumstances, DISH’s conduct was neither willful nor reckless.

The district court’s conclusion that DISH violated the TCPA simply because it used a prerecorded voice on calls to the 3475 number is legally unsound. The TCPA’s prohibition on prerecorded voices applies only when the caller lacks prior express consent. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A). The Act does not impose strict liability for the use of a prerecorded message when the caller reasonably believes it is contacting the intended recipient. In *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106 (1911), the Court held that “a party cannot be held liable for a violation of a statute unless it had knowledge of the facts which made its conduct unlawful.” Here, DISH had no knowledge that the number had been reassigned. It acted solely on the basis of the original customer’s consent, which was valid and unrevoked at the time of the calls.

The district court’s reliance on the presence of a “positive voice” as a basis for liability is misplaced. The TCPA does not define “positive voice” or require that a call be answered by a human being to trigger liability. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), the Court held that “the mere fact that a message is delivered does not establish a violation of the law unless the delivery was made with knowledge of the statutory prohibition.” The presence of a positive voice—whether human or recorded—does not, by itself, constitute a violation. The critical inquiry is whether the caller had prior express consent. The original customer had given such consent. DISH’s use of the Cisco Dialer to deliver a prerecorded message to a number it reasonably believed belonged to the original debtor does not constitute a violation of the TCPA.

Finally, the district court’s award of $500 per call for four of the seven calls is unsupported by law. The TCPA authorizes statutory damages of $500 per violation, but only where a violation has occurred. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(3). Where, as here, the caller acted in good faith and with reasonable belief that its conduct was lawful, no violation has occurred. The Supreme Court has consistently held that “a party cannot be held liable for damages when it acted in good faith and without knowledge of the law.” *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106 (1911). The district court’s failure to consider this defense renders its judgment legally erroneous.

For these reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment on four of the seven calls. The judgment should be reversed and remanded with instructions to enter judgment in favor of DISH on those calls.
