The American Dental Association (ADA) respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act.

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 allows plaintiffs to recover punitive damages if they demonstrate that the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). While the statute's language does not explicitly require "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct, the legislative history suggests that Congress intended to limit punitive damages to extraordinary cases.

The legislative history reveals a nuanced discussion among lawmakers regarding the standard for punitive damages. Senator Dole's interpretive memorandum states that punitive damages are available only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). Although Representative Edwards's statement appears to contradict this view, it is not necessarily inconsistent when considered in light of the circuit split on the issue. See 137 Cong. Rec. H 9527 (Nov. 7, 1991). A more plausible interpretation is that Congress intended to adopt the emerging common law trend requiring something more than intentional discrimination for punitive damages.

The courts have long recognized that punitive damages are not available for every intentional tort, but rather are reserved for cases involving particularly egregious conduct. See, e.g., Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) (1979). In the context of employment discrimination, this principle is reflected in the decisions of several circuits, which have required a showing of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct to support an award of punitive damages under § 1981. See, e.g., Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co., 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996); Kimzey v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 107 F.3d 568, 575 (8th Cir. 1997).

Requiring "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII is consistent with the statute's language and legislative history. It also aligns with the common law trend and the decisions of several circuits. This standard ensures that punitive damages are reserved for cases involving truly reprehensible conduct, rather than being available for every instance of intentional discrimination.

In this case, the Court of Appeals erred by failing to require a showing of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct to support an award of punitive damages. The District Court's denial of Kolstad's request for punitive damages was proper, as the evidence did not demonstrate the level of egregiousness required by the statute.

For the foregoing reasons, the ADA respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII.

This interpretation is supported by the principles enunciated in St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502 (1993), and McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), which emphasize the importance of intentional discrimination in Title VII cases. By requiring a higher standard for punitive damages, the Court can ensure that such awards are reserved for the most egregious cases, consistent with the legislative intent behind the Civil Rights Act of 1991.
