**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Government may collect a partnership’s unpaid employment taxes from individual partners without first assessing those partners individually—requires a straightforward application of the Internal Revenue Code and settled precedent. The answer is no. The Government’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year statute of limitations under § 6501(a) bars it from collecting the debt from them, even if the partnership was timely assessed. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the statute of limitations for collection against individual partners is not extended by an assessment against the partnership alone. This conclusion is compelled by the plain language of the Code, the structure of the assessment and collection regime, and binding precedent from this Court and other circuits.

**I. The Statute of Limitations for Collection Against Individual Partners Is Not Extended by an Assessment Against the Partnership**

The Government’s central contention—that a timely assessment against the partnership automatically extends the limitations period for collecting from individual partners—conflicts with the statutory text and the well-established principle that each taxpayer must be separately assessed. Section 6502(a) provides that a tax “may be collected by levy or by a proceeding in court, but only if the levy is made or the proceeding begun—within 10 years after the assessment of the tax.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). The key term is “the assessment of the tax”—not “an assessment of a tax.” The statute applies to the taxpayer against whom the assessment is made. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (“The assessment must be made against the taxpayer.”). Because the individual partners were not assessed, the 10-year period under § 6502(a) did not begin to run as to them.

This interpretation is consistent with the definition of “taxpayer” in the Code. Section 7701(a)(14) defines a “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The Code treats individuals and partnerships as distinct taxpayers. *See United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561, 562 (7th Cir. 1995) (noting that the IRS assessed both the partnership and the individual partners in that case). The IRS’s own regulations confirm this: “An assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer in the office of the Secretary in accordance with rules or regulations prescribed by the Secretary.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The regulation requires identification of the taxpayer. *See id.* An assessment against a partnership does not identify the individual partners as taxpayers, and thus does not satisfy the statutory requirement.

The Government’s argument that the partnership’s assessment “extends” the limitations period to the partners is legally untenable. The statute does not say that an assessment against one taxpayer extends the limitations period for others. *See United States v. W. Prods., Ltd.*, 168 F.Supp.2d 84, 91 (S.D.N.Y. 2001) (holding that the Government may not collect from a general partner without a separate assessment or judgment). The Government’s position would allow it to circumvent the three-year limit in § 6501(a) by simply assessing the partnership, which would be a “loophole” the Code does not permit. *See United States v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960) (affirming district court’s holding that a partner’s liability arises under state law, but collection requires individual assessment or judgment).

**II. The Government Cannot Collect from Individual Partners Without a Judgment or Separate Assessment**

Even if the statute of limitations were not at issue, the Government cannot collect from individual partners without first obtaining a judgment against them. Under California law, a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against a partner. *Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)* provides: “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” *See also 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001)* (“[A] partner must be individually named and served in the action or in a later suit, and judgment entered against that partner, in order to reach the partner’s personal assets.”). This rule is not unique to California. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (affirming district court’s holding that a lien against a partner’s property required a separate assessment or judgment); *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562 (noting that the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners); *United States v. W. Prods., Ltd.*, 168 F.Supp.2d at 91 (holding that the Government could collect from a general partner only after obtaining a judgment against the partner).

The Government’s claim that it can collect under state law is therefore foreclosed. *See United States v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d at 910 (holding that a partner’s liability arises under state law, but collection requires a judgment or assessment). The Government has not obtained a judgment against the individual partners, nor has it made a separate assessment. The time for doing so has expired under § 6501(a). *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (holding that the Government could not collect from a partner without a judgment or assessment).
