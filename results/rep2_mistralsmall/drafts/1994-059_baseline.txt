**ARGUMENT**

**I. Amendment 73 Violates the Exclusive Qualifications Clause of the U.S. Constitution**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Amendment 73’s term limits for federal officeholders violate the Qualifications Clauses of the U.S. Constitution. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2 (House); U.S. Const. art. I, § 3, cl. 3 (Senate). These clauses set forth the *exclusive* qualifications for congressional office, and states may not add or alter them. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (“[T]he Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person duly elected by the people of a State who meets the Constitution’s requirements for membership.”); *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 804 (1995) (“[T]he qualifications for service in Congress established in the text of the Constitution are fixed until changed by the amending process.”).

The text of the Qualifications Clauses is unambiguous. The Constitution prescribes the minimum age, citizenship, and residency requirements for congressional office, and it does not permit states to impose additional restrictions. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 797 (“The Framers decided that the qualifications for membership in Congress were to be fixed in the Constitution and could not be altered by the States.”). The Court in *Thornton* rejected the argument that states may impose term limits on federal officeholders through ballot-access restrictions, holding that such measures are “an indirect attempt to accomplish what the Qualifications Clauses directly prohibit.” *Id.* at 805.

Arkansas’s attempt to circumvent the Qualifications Clauses by restricting ballot access is no different. The Arkansas Supreme Court properly concluded that Amendment 73’s term limits for U.S. Representatives and Senators are unconstitutional because they add qualifications not prescribed by the Constitution. *See id.* at 804-05.

**II. The Arkansas Supreme Court Correctly Declined to Sever the Unconstitutional Provisions**

The Arkansas Supreme Court properly declined to sever Amendment 73’s unconstitutional provisions because the amendment was presented to voters as a single, indivisible package. *SeeCombs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750, 375 S.W.2d 809, 812 (1964) (holding that a severability clause is ineffective if the unconstitutional portion is “so interwoven with the rest of the act that it cannot be separated without destroying the entire act”). The amendment’s preamble and text explicitly linked state and federal term limits, and the voters were not given the opportunity to approve one without the other. *See id.* (noting that the court cannot speculate whether the voters would have approved the constitutional provision without the unconstitutional portion).

The majority’s reliance on *Combs* is misplaced. In *Combs*, the court upheld the severability of a statutory provision because the legislature had clearly indicated its intent to preserve the remainder of the statute. *Id.* Here, by contrast, the voters were not presented with a clear choice to adopt state term limits independently of federal term limits. The amendment’s structure and presentation to the electorate demonstrate that the provisions were inextricably linked. *SeeThornton*, 514 U.S. at 838 (Thomas, J., dissenting) (noting that the “fusion” of state and federal term limits in a single ballot measure rendered severance improper).

**III. The Arkansas Supreme Court Properly Rejected the Enacting Clause Argument**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the absence of an Enacting Clause did not invalidate Amendment 73. Amendment 7 of the Arkansas Constitution requires that initiated bills include an Enacting Clause, but this requirement applies to *legislative* acts, not constitutional amendments. *See* Ark. Const. amend. 7. The Arkansas Supreme Court has consistently held that the Enacting Clause requirement is directory, not mandatory, for constitutional amendments. *SeePlugge v. McCuen*, 310 Ark. 654, 670, 841 S.W.2d 139, 147 (1992) (noting that the Enacting Clause requirement is “not jurisdictional and does not invalidate an amendment for failure to include it”).

Moreover, the voters were not misled by the omission. The full text of Amendment 73 was published prior to the election, and the ballot title clearly described its purpose. *See* Ark. Code Ann. § 7-9-113 (1987). The Enacting Clause requirement is a technicality that should not invalidate an amendment that was otherwise properly presented to the voters. *SeePlugge*, 310 Ark. at 670, 841 S.W.2d at 147.

**Conclusion**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Amendment 73’s term limits for federal officeholders violate the U.S. Constitution and that the unconstitutional provisions could not be severed. The judgment below should be affirmed.
