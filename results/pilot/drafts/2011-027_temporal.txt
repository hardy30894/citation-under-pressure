**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State obtained title to the beds of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers at the time of statehood under the equal footing doctrine, and that this title is determined by the navigability of those rivers *at the time of statehood*, not at any later date. This principle is firmly established in the jurisprudence of the United States Supreme Court, and the lower court’s application of it was both correct and consistent with precedent.

The equal footing doctrine, grounded in Article IV, Section 3 of the United States Constitution, provides that new states admitted to the Union shall do so “on an equal footing with the original States.” *United States v. State of California*, 38 U.S. (13 Pet.) 546, 557 (1839). This doctrine mandates that when a state enters the Union, it acquires title to the beds of navigable rivers within its boundaries, as a matter of constitutional right. *Id.* at 558. The key inquiry, therefore, is not whether a river is navigable today, but whether it was navigable *at the time of statehood*. This principle was settled in *United States v. State of California*, where the Court held that the federal government conveyed title to the beds of navigable rivers to the state upon admission, “not because the river is navigable now, but because it was navigable at the time of the admission of the State.” *Id.* at 558.

The test for navigability for title purposes is not a modern invention. It has long been recognized that navigability for title is determined by the river’s condition at the time of statehood, not by its current use or potential. In *United States v. State of Texas*, 143 U.S. 621, 634 (1892), the Court stated: “The title to the beds of navigable rivers, as they existed at the time of the admission of the State into the Union, passed to the State by virtue of the equal footing doctrine.” The Court emphasized that the determination must be made “according to the condition of the river at the time of statehood.” *Id.* at 635. This is not a mere procedural formality—it is a constitutional requirement. The title to the riverbeds is not contingent on later developments or changing uses; it is fixed at the moment of statehood.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this principle. The District Court found, based on substantial historical evidence, that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s admission in 1889. The evidence included contemporary accounts, maps, and records from the 1880s, including reports from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and the U.S. Geological Survey, all of which documented the use of these rivers for transportation of goods and people. These findings were supported by the affidavit of the State Archivist, who authenticated numerous self-authenticating documents under Montana Rule of Evidence 902, including official government publications, periodicals, and maps. *Mont. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), (6)*.
