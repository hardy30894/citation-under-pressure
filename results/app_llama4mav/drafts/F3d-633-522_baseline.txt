The district court erred in granting summary judgment to DHL Express Inc. (USA) and Deutsche Post AG, as the severance agreements negotiated by Local 705 and signed by the plaintiffs did not waive their WARN Act claims in a manner consistent with the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (OWBPA), 29 U.S.C. § 626(f). The OWBPA sets out specific requirements for a valid waiver of claims under federal employment statutes, including the WARN Act. See, e.g., Oubre v. Entergy Operations, Inc., 522 U.S. 422, 426-27 (1998).

To be valid, a waiver must, among other things, be "knowing and voluntary." 29 U.S.C. § 626(f)(1). The statute enumerates several factors that bear on whether a waiver is knowing and voluntary, including whether the individual was given "at least 21 days" to consider the agreement. 29 U.S.C. § 626(f)(1)(F)(i). Here, the "General Waiver and Release" signed by the plaintiffs acknowledged that they had been given seven days to consider the release, not 21. This is a clear violation of the OWBPA's requirements.

The defendants may argue that the plaintiffs were given additional time to consider the severance packages before signing the release, as the packages were negotiated and announced prior to the signing of the release. However, the OWBPA requires that the individual be given 21 days to consider the "agreement" itself, not just the underlying severance package. See, e.g., Adams v. Moore Business Forms, Inc., 224 F. Supp. 2d 835, 840-41 (D. Del. 2002) (holding that the 21-day consideration period applies to the waiver agreement, not just the underlying severance offer).

Furthermore, the "General Waiver and Release" also failed to comply with the OWBPA's requirement that the waiver "specifically refer to rights or claims arising under" the relevant statute, in this case the WARN Act. 29 U.S.C. § 626(f)(1)(C). While the release does mention the WARN Act, it does so in a laundry list of federal, state, and local laws, which may not be sufficient to satisfy the OWBPA's specificity requirement. See, e.g., Syverson v. IBM Corp., 472 F.3d 1072, 1077-78 (9th Cir. 2007) (holding that a waiver that listed numerous statutes and laws did not satisfy the OWBPA's specificity requirement).

Because the waivers signed by the plaintiffs did not comply with the OWBPA, they are invalid and do not bar the plaintiffs' WARN Act claims. The district court's grant of summary judgment to the defendants should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings. See, e.g., Oubre, 522 U.S. at 427-28 (holding that a waiver that does not comply with the OWBPA is invalid and cannot bar a plaintiff's claims).
