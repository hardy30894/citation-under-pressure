# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT'S EXCLUSION OF LAMB'S CHAPEL CONSTITUTED UNCONSTITUTIONAL VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION AGAINST RELIGIOUS SPEECH.

Once government property is voluntarily opened for use by outside speakers on a broad range of subjects, the government may not thereafter exclude a speaker simply because its message is religious. This principle has deep roots. In *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), this Court recognized that public property held open for public assembly and the exchange of ideas may not be closed selectively to disfavored speakers. The Center Moriches School District's own regulations opened school facilities after hours to community groups for lectures, performances, and civic discussion on subjects ranging from psychology to music. Having thrown open its doors to that range of secular speech, the District could not slam them shut on Lamb's Chapel for no reason other than that its film series addressed family values from a religious perspective.

This Court has repeatedly struck down government efforts to deny religious speakers access to public fora otherwise open to speech on similar subjects. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court held that a city could not deny Jehovah's Witnesses a permit to use a public park for religious meetings while granting such permits to other groups, because doing so amounted to discrimination against a particular religious viewpoint. Likewise, in *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290 (1951), the Court invalidated a licensing scheme that empowered officials to deny permits for street meetings based on the religious content of the speaker's prior remarks, explaining that such discretion invited exactly the sort of content-based suppression the First Amendment forbids. And in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), the Court held that an ordinance could not be enforced against a Jehovah's Witness meeting in a public park while permitting other groups, including religious ones conducting different kinds of services, to use the same park — because such selective enforcement discriminated against the manner in which one faith chose to express itself relative to others.

The teaching of these decisions is unmistakable: once a forum is opened, the state's own preference for secular over religious viewpoints cannot justify exclusion. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), likewise confirms that religious expression enjoys the full protection the First Amendment affords other protected speech, and that government may not single it out for disfavored treatment. The District's Rule No. 7, barring use of school premises for religious purposes, is precisely the kind of categorical, viewpoint-based exclusion condemned in *Niemotko*, *Kunz*, and *Fowler*. The rule does not merely regulate the time, place, or manner of expression; it excludes an entire category of viewpoint — the religious one — from a forum otherwise open to comparable secular expression on the very same subject matter, family life and child-rearing. That is viewpoint discrimination of the clearest kind, and it cannot stand.

## II. GRANTING LAMB'S CHAPEL EQUAL ACCESS TO SCHOOL FACILITIES WOULD NOT OFFEND THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE.

The District defends its exclusion, in part, on the theory that permitting a religious group equal access to its facilities would itself violate the Establishment Clause. That argument inverts the constitutional analysis. Neutral accommodation of religious speech, on the same terms available to secular speech, is not government "establishment" of religion; it is simply the state declining to discriminate against religion.

This Court's decision in *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), is instructive by contrast. There the Court considered a "released time" program permitting public school students to leave school premises during the school day to receive religious instruction elsewhere. Even that far more entangled arrangement — implicating compulsory school attendance and instructional time itself — survived Establishment Clause scrutiny because the accommodation was religion-neutral and did not compel participation. Here, the accommodation sought is far less entangling: Lamb's Chapel asks only for after-hours use of empty school rooms, on the same terms as the Southern Harmonizers or Mr. Huck's lecture series, for a privately sponsored film series open to the public. No student is compelled to attend; no school employee endorses the message; no instructional time is displaced.

By contrast, this Court's decision in *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), condemned a program in which "religious teachers, employed by private religious groups, were permitted to come weekly into the school buildings during the regular hours set apart for secular teaching" to provide religious instruction as a substitute for secular schooling. That is worlds apart from an after-hours film showing that displaces no public instruction and coerces no student. And in *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421 (1962), the constitutional infirmity lay in the State's own composition and sponsorship of a prayer that public schools required students to recite — the State itself becoming the author of religious exercise, such that "the State of New York has adopted a practice wholly inconsistent with the Establishment Clause." Nothing remotely similar is present here. The District would not be authoring, sponsoring, or requiring any religious exercise; it would merely be treating a religious speaker the same as it treats secular speakers who wish to use the same rooms after hours.

Because equal, neutral access works no Establishment Clause violation, the District's Free Speech Clause violation is not excused, and this Court should reverse the judgment below.
