**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Massachusetts Buffer-Zone Statute Is a Content-Neutral, Viewpoint-Neutral Time-Place-Manner Regulation That Satisfies the First Amendment.**

The First Circuit correctly held that Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½ is a permissible regulation of the *time, place, and manner* of speech. *See Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (citing *Heffron v. Int’l Soc’y for Krishna Consciousness*, 452 U.S. 640, 648 (1981)). Such regulations are valid if they (1) are content-neutral, (2) are narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and (3) leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *See id.* (citing *Clark v. Cmty. for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984)).

The statute is content-neutral because it does not discriminate based on the message conveyed. *See Police Dep’t of Chicago v. Mosley*, 408 U.S. 92, 95 (1972) (“[G]overnmental regulation of speech should be content-neutral, and the time, place, and manner of that expression may be controlled only if the controls are necessary to serve a compelling state interest and are narrowly drawn to achieve that end.”). The law applies equally to all speakers, regardless of viewpoint. *See id.* at 96 (“[A]bove all else, the First Amendment means that government has no power to restrict expression because of its message, its ideas, its subject matter, or its content.”). The buffer zone restricts all expressive activity within 35 feet of clinic entrances, without regard to the speaker’s position on abortion. *See McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 176 (1st Cir. 2009), *cert. denied*, 559 U.S. 1047 (2010).

The statute is narrowly tailored to serve the Commonwealth’s significant interest in protecting patients and clinic staff from harassment, intimidation, and obstruction. *See Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of W. N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 376 (1997) (upholding a 15-foot “floating buffer zone” around clinic personnel to prevent obstruction). The 35-foot fixed buffer zone is no broader than necessary to achieve this interest. *See id.* at 377 (“The floating buffer zone sweeps more broadly than necessary to protect the clinics’ patients and staff.”). The statute leaves open ample alternative channels for communication, including sidewalks beyond the buffer zone, where petitioners may speak, distribute literature, and engage in prayer. *See Heffron*, 452 U.S. at 654 (“[T]he availability of alternative forums for the expression of the desired viewpoints substantially diminishes the censorial threat.”).

**II. The First Circuit Correctly Relied on *Hill v. Colorado* in Upholding the Statute.**

The First Circuit properly applied *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), which upheld a statute prohibiting certain activities within 100 feet of healthcare facility entrances. *See McCullen*, 571 F.3d at 181-82. *Hill* remains controlling precedent. *See id.* at 720 (“[T]he First Amendment does not invalidate every incidental burdening of speech that occurs when the government, acting for substantial and legitimate reasons, imposes regulations that indirectly limit such speech.”) (quoting *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799).

The Massachusetts statute is materially indistinguishable from the Colorado statute upheld in *Hill*. Both laws create fixed buffer zones around clinic entrances to prevent harassment and obstruction. *See id.* at 715 (“The statute simply empowers the police to arrest those who violate a regulation of the kind that we have up-held in the past.”). The fact that the Massachusetts statute is narrower (35 feet instead of 100 feet) does not render it unconstitutional. *See id.* at 720 (“The burden on petitioners’ rights is not severe.”).

**III. The Statute Does Not Violate the First Amendment as Applied to the Petitioners.**

The petitioners have failed to demonstrate that the statute prevents them from engaging in effective speech. The record shows that they may speak, distribute literature, and pray outside the buffer zone. *See Heffron*, 452 U.S. at 654 (“[T]he availability of alternative forums for the expression of the desired viewpoints substantially diminishes the censorial threat.”). The statute does not prevent them from communicating their message; it merely requires them to do so at a reasonable distance from clinic entrances. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791.

The petitioners’ reliance on *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147 (1939), is misplaced. *Schneider* struck down a municipal ordinance that banned all leafleting on public sidewalks, holding that the law was not narrowly tailored to serve the city’s interest in preventing littering. *Id.* at 162. Unlike the ordinance in *Schneider*, the Massachusetts statute is narrowly tailored to serve the significant interest of protecting patients and clinic staff from harassment and obstruction. *See Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 376.

**IV. The Statute Does Not Constitute an Unlawful Prior Restraint.**

The statute does not impose a prior restraint on speech. *See Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson*, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931) (“No one would question but that a government might prevent actual obstruction to its streets and sidewalks by interfering with the use of them by others.”). The buffer zone is a content-neutral, time-place-manner regulation that does not prevent speech before it occurs. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791.

**Conclusion**

The Massachusetts buffer-zone statute is a valid time-place-manner regulation that satisfies the First Amendment. The First Circuit correctly upheld the statute, and this Court should affirm its judgment.
