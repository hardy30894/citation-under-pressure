**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), this Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, recognizing that the formulation of rules governing criminal responsibility for the mentally ill is a subject on which the States are entitled to reach differing conclusions without offending due process. *Id.* at 800–01. If a State may constitutionally place the entire burden of proving insanity on the defendant, it follows that a State may also determine the procedural channel through which mental-disease evidence enters the case.

This principle was reinforced in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), where the plurality declined to constitutionalize any single doctrine of criminal responsibility for persons suffering from mental or behavioral abnormality, emphasizing the considerable disagreement among the States and among medical experts over how such conditions should bear on criminal liability, and cautioning against displacing that legislative judgment through the Due Process Clause. *Id.* at 535–37. Arizona's rule confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry is precisely the sort of substantive line-drawing that *Leland* and *Powell* leave to the States.

This channeling approach is also consistent with history. The insanity defense as historically understood, from *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a mechanism for negating the prosecution's proof of intent issue by issue. This Court's decision in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), likewise addressed insanity as a distinct issue governed by its own burden-allocation rules, separate from the prosecution's proof of the elements of the offense. Arizona's rule simply preserves that traditional separation between the insanity inquiry and the mens rea inquiry.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Leland* and *Powell*, Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules; it requires only that such rules not offend "a principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). This Court applied the same standard in *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), confirming that only those procedural guarantees implicit in the concept of ordered liberty are binding on the States through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Consistent with this approach, *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), recognized that the States retain substantial authority to shape their own rules of criminal procedure and evidence, and that a state rule does not offend due process merely because it differs from federal practice or from the rule a defendant would prefer. This Court applied these principles directly to a state evidentiary limitation in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), upholding Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence over the defendant's objection that the rule improperly affected the jury's assessment of guilt, and confirming that state evidentiary rules are entitled to substantial deference so long as the trial as a whole remains fundamentally fair. *Id.* at 563–64.

The touchstone under *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), is whether the state proceeding, viewed as a whole, denied the defendant a fair trial. Here, the trial court permitted Clark to introduce the full range of expert psychiatric testimony and to have it evaluated by the factfinder on the issue of insanity—the very evidence he now claims should also negate intent. Nothing in the authorities above suggests that the Constitution requires evidence relevant to one defense to be simultaneously admissible, unconstrained by that defense's burdens, to defeat an entirely separate element of the prosecution's case.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, freed from the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove beyond a reasonable doubt. That is precisely the kind of procedural and substantive line-drawing sanctioned by *Leland*, *Powell*, *Snyder*, *Twining*, *Spencer*, and *Lisenba*. Arizona's channeling rule does not offend any principle "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people," *Snyder*, 291 U.S. at 105, as to be ranked fundamental, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
