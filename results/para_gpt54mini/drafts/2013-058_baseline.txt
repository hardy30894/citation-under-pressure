**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt an inherited IRA because the Code protects only “retirement funds,” and an inherited IRA is not a fund set aside for the debtor’s retirement. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). As the Seventh Circuit explained, inherited IRAs differ in kind from ordinary IRAs: the beneficiary may not contribute additional funds, may not roll the account over into a personal IRA, and must begin taking taxable distributions immediately, long before retirement. *Clark v. Rameker*, 714 F.3d 559, 561–64 (7th Cir. 2013). Those statutory limits show that the account is a tax-deferral vehicle, not a retirement reserve. *Id.* at 563–65.

A. **Section 522 protects retirement savings, not inherited assets available for present consumption.**

Section 522’s text turns on the character of the funds as “retirement funds.” 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The ordinary meaning of that term is money “set aside for the day when [the owner] stops working.” *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. An inherited IRA does not serve that function. Its beneficiary can withdraw the whole account immediately without penalty, subject only to ordinary income tax; indeed, federal tax law requires distributions to begin within a year and, in many cases, to be completed within five years. *Id.* at 562–63 (citing 26 U.S.C. §§ 402(c)(11)(A), 401(a)(9)(B), 408(d)(3)(C)). That feature is incompatible with retirement savings. As this Court has explained in analogous contexts, statutory terms should be read according to the asset’s actual function, not merely its label. *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987); *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 566–67.

B. **The debtor’s inherited IRA is unlike the IRAs Congress intended to shield.**

An ordinary IRA remains dedicated to the owner’s retirement because the owner cannot freely use it for current consumption without consequences. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 561–62. By contrast, an inherited IRA has no such retirement purpose for the beneficiary. The beneficiary cannot add to the account, cannot treat it as a long-term retirement savings vehicle, and must deplete it on a schedule unrelated to the beneficiary’s retirement age. *Id.* at 562–63. The Fifth Circuit reached the opposite conclusion in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), but that decision improperly treated the historical source of the funds as dispositive. The Seventh Circuit correctly rejected that approach because the Bankruptcy Code asks whether the debtor’s funds are retirement funds now, not whether they once were. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564–66.

C. **The exemption should not be expanded by policy arguments untethered to the statutory text.**

The debtors may argue that exempting inherited IRAs would promote family savings and preserve inherited wealth. But “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” *Rodriguez*, 480 U.S. at 525–26. Congress chose to protect retirement funds, not every asset that can trace its lineage to an IRA. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 567–69. Expanding the exemption to inherited IRAs would allow a debtor to shelter a liquid account that is immediately available for spending, contrary to the Bankruptcy Code’s basic balance between a debtor’s fresh start and creditors’ rights. *Id.* at 564–69.

D. **The better reading is the one adopted by the Seventh Circuit.**

The Seventh Circuit’s analysis is faithful to the statutory text and structure. It recognized that exemptions sometimes depend on an asset’s function in the debtor’s hands, not in a predecessor’s hands. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 565–68. It also observed that Congress used the phrase “retirement funds” rather than “retirement accounts,” confirming that the focus is on the nature of the money itself. *Id.* at 566. Because inherited IRAs are not funds reserved for retirement, they fall outside § 522. *Id.* at 568–69.

For those reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
