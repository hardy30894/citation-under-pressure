**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over uninspected vessels preempts OSHA jurisdiction under Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act. The plain text of Section 4(b)(1), this Court’s precedent, and the statutory scheme compel the conclusion that OSHA may regulate occupational safety and health on uninspected vessels where, as here, the Coast Guard has not exercised its authority to prescribe or enforce standards addressing the working conditions at issue.

**I. Section 4(b)(1) Preempts OSHA Jurisdiction Only Where Another Federal Agency Exercises Its Authority to Regulate the Same Working Conditions**

Section 4(b)(1) provides that the OSH Act does not apply “to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The key word is “exercise.” Congress did not preempt OSHA jurisdiction merely because another agency *could* regulate; it preempted only where another agency *does* regulate.

The Coast Guard has not “exercise[d] statutory authority” over the working conditions at issue on the MR. BELDON. The vessel was an uninspected drilling barge. The Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over uninspected vessels is limited and defined by statute. See 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (authorizing regulations for uninspected vessels only regarding fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and alerting equipment). The Coast Guard has not promulgated standards addressing the hazards that led to the explosion—blowout prevention, well control, and other drilling safety procedures. Those are classic occupational safety and health hazards within OSHA’s expertise.

The Court of Appeals mistakenly relied on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), for the proposition that the Coast Guard has exclusive jurisdiction over all working conditions of seamen. But those cases addressed inspected vessels and did not consider the limited scope of Coast Guard authority over uninspected vessels. This Court has never endorsed such a broad reading of Section 4(b)(1). To the contrary, in *Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Assn.*, 505 U.S. 88, 108 (1992), this Court recognized that Section 4(b)(1) reflects Congress’s intent to avoid “overlapping regulation of working conditions” by multiple federal agencies. Overlapping regulation is not a concern where, as here, the Coast Guard has not regulated the relevant hazards.

**II. The Coast Guard’s Minimal Regulation of Uninspected Vessels Does Not Preempt OSHA’s Comprehensive Occupational Safety Standards**

The Coast Guard’s existing regulations for uninspected vessels are narrow in scope. They address equipment-specific issues like fire extinguishers and life preservers. 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq. The Coast Guard has not issued standards for the types of hazards that caused the explosion on the MR. BELDON—drilling operations, blowout prevention, or well control. OSHA’s regulations fill this regulatory gap.

This Court has made clear that preemption under Section 4(b)(1) requires that the other agency “exercise statutory authority” over the same working conditions. *Industrial Union Dept., AFL-CIO v. American Petroleum Institute*, 448 U.S. 607, 643 n.45 (1980) (plurality opinion). Where the Coast Guard’s regulations do not address the specific hazard, OSHA retains jurisdiction. A contrary rule would create a regulatory vacuum, leaving workers on uninspected vessels without meaningful occupational safety protections. Congress did not intend such a result when it enacted the OSH Act to “assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions.” *American Textile Manufacturers Institute, Inc. v. Donovan*, 452 U.S. 490, 539 (1981) (quoting 29 U.S.C. § 651(b)).

**III. The MR. BELDON Was a “Workplace” Under the OSH Act**

The MR. BELDON was a workplace within the plain meaning of Section 4(a) of the OSH Act, 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The Act applies “with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” *Id.* The drilling barge was located in Louisiana’s territorial waters, and employees performed work there. The OSH Act defines “employer” broadly and contains no exclusion for vessels. *Atlas Roofing Co. v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Comm’n*, 430 U.S. 442, 445 (1977). The term “workplace” must be construed consistently with the Act’s remedial purpose of protecting workers from hazards. *Whirlpool Corp. v. Marshall*, 445 U.S. 1, 12 (1980) (the Act “was designed to prevent the first accident”).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and OSHA’s citations reinstated.
