**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the privileges of conducting activities within the forum state, such that the litigation arises out of or relates to those activities. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). The mere fact that a defendant sells its products in a state, or that those products are later used or sold within the state, does not establish the minimum contacts required for specific personal jurisdiction when the plaintiff’s injury and the claims at issue are entirely disconnected from the defendant’s activities in the forum. Here, Ford Motor Company has no contacts with Montana that caused or related to the death of Markkaya Jean Gullett. The vehicle was manufactured in Kentucky, first sold in Washington, transferred through Oregon, and only later brought into Montana by a private purchaser. No Ford dealership, agent, or representative ever sold or advertised the vehicle in Montana. No Ford employee ever entered the state in connection with the vehicle. No Ford marketing, servicing, or distribution activity occurred in Montana. Under settled precedent, such passive, indirect, and attenuated connections are insufficient to satisfy due process.

The Supreme Court has long held that “the mere foreseeability that the product of a corporation may find its way into the forum state is not enough” to establish jurisdiction. *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 112 (1987) (citing *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985)). In *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980), the Court emphasized that “the foreseeability that the product of a corporation may find its way into the forum state is not enough” to justify jurisdiction. The Court reiterated that “the defendant’s conduct must create a substantial connection with the forum state,” and that “the plaintiff’s claim must arise out of or relate to the defendant’s contacts with the forum.” *Id.* at 297–98. In this case, the claim does not arise out of any contact Ford had with Montana. The tire failure occurred on a Montana highway, but the tire was not manufactured, sold, or distributed by Ford in Montana. The vehicle was not serviced, repaired, or maintained by Ford in Montana. No Ford representative ever interacted with Gullett or her estate in Montana. The claim is entirely derivative of events occurring outside the state and unrelated to any activity by Ford within Montana.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed itself of the privilege of doing business” in Montana by advertising and selling parts in the state is unsupported by the record and contrary to precedent. *See* *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958) (holding that “the mere fact that a corporation has sold goods into a state does not establish sufficient contacts to justify jurisdiction”). The Court in *Hanson* rejected jurisdiction over a Delaware corporation that had sold trust instruments to a resident of Michigan, noting that “the mere fact that a corporation has sold goods into a state does not establish sufficient contacts to justify jurisdiction.” *Id.* at 253. Similarly, in *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985), the Court held that “the foreseeability that the product of a corporation may find its way into the forum state is not enough” to justify jurisdiction. The same principle applies here: the foreseeability that a Ford vehicle might eventually be driven in Montana does not constitute a purposeful availment of the forum.

Moreover, the Due Process Clause requires that the exercise of jurisdiction be reasonable in light of the burden on the defendant, the forum state’s interest, and the plaintiff’s interest in obtaining relief. *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 294. Here, Ford has no offices, employees, or sales in Montana. It has not advertised, marketed, or distributed its products in the state. To subject Ford to jurisdiction in Montana based on the mere fact that a vehicle it manufactured was later driven there would impose an unreasonable burden on the corporation and would open the door to jurisdiction in every state where a Ford vehicle might be driven. Such a rule would violate the fundamental fairness required by due process.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on *Conway v. Benefis Health System*, 2013 MT 73, 297 P.3d 1200, and *Newbury v. State Farm*, 2008 MT 156, 184 P.3d 1021, is misplaced. Those cases involved insurance coverage and collateral source payments in personal injury actions, not personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant. They do not address the constitutional limits on jurisdiction. The Court in *Hanson* made clear that “the mere fact that a corporation has sold goods into a state does not establish sufficient contacts to justify jurisdiction.” *Hanson*, 357 U.S. at 253. The same principle applies here: the fact that Ford sells parts in Montana does not establish the minimum contacts required for jurisdiction over a claim arising from a vehicle accident in a different state.

Finally, the Due Process Clause does not permit jurisdiction based on the “slightest connection” or “the most attenuated chain of events.” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475. The Court has consistently held that jurisdiction must be based on “purposeful availment” and “relatedness” to the claim. *Id.* at 475. Here, there is no such connection. The claim arises from a tire failure in Montana, but Ford had no role in the vehicle’s presence or operation in Montana. The claim does not arise out of or relate to any activity Ford conducted in Montana. To hold otherwise would violate the constitutional guarantee of fair notice and due process.

For these reasons, the Montana Supreme Court erred in concluding that Ford is subject to specific personal jurisdiction in Montana. The Due Process Clause does not permit a state court to exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when none of the defendant’s contacts with the state caused the plaintiff’s claims. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court must be reversed.
