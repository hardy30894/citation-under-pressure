**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE BOARD’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR POLLING IS IRRATIONAL AND INCONSISTENT WITH THE ACT.**

The National Labor Relations Board requires an employer to possess “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” before polling employees. This standard is arbitrary because it demands the same quantum of evidence needed to withdraw recognition—a far more drastic step. The rule creates a logical trap: if an employer already has enough evidence to withdraw recognition, it has no need to poll; if it lacks that evidence, it cannot poll. Polling thus becomes a worthless exercise, defeating the Act’s “policy of promoting industrial peace.” *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 45 (1937).

This Court has never required objective proof of majority loss before an employer may poll. In *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 610 n.27 (1969), the Court stated that an employer may poll employees “if he has a good-faith doubt as to the union’s majority status.” Good-faith doubt is a subjective, reasoned belief; it is not the same as substantial objective evidence. The Board’s more stringent rule conflicts with *Gissel* and with the Act’s recognition that employers and employees need reliable information about union support. *See NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 130–31 (1944) (Board’s construction must be “consistent with the Act”).

The Board’s standard also produces irrational results when compared with its own preference for Board-supervised elections. The Board applies the identical evidentiary threshold to employer polls, to RM elections, and to outright withdrawal of recognition. But an RM election has procedural safeguards; an employer poll does not. And withdrawal of recognition has no safeguards at all. The Board cannot rationally apply one rule to three practices with vastly different risks. *NLRB v. Insurance Agents’ Int’l Union*, 361 U.S. 477, 497–98 (1960) (Board may not impose standards that “frustrate the basic policy of the Act”). The Board’s uniform threshold is particularly indefensible because it makes polling harder to justify than a Board election, inverting the Board’s supposed preference for elections. *NLRB v. Wooster Div. of Borg-Warner Corp.*, 356 U.S. 342, 350 (1958) (Board must act “within the boundaries of the Act”). The Board’s interpretation thus is unreasonable. *NLRB v. Exchange Parts Co.*, 375 U.S. 405, 409 (1964) (Board’s rules must be “carefully tailored” to the Act’s objectives).

**II. EVEN UNDER THE BOARD’S STANDARD, ITS FACTUAL FINDING LACKS SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.**

The Board’s factual conclusion ignores the totality of circumstances. Substantial evidence review requires the court to consider the entire record. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The record here contains overwhelming, unrefuted evidence that a majority of the 32 employees no longer wanted union representation. At least seven employees directly repudiated the union. Two other employees—Bloch and Mohr—told management that the night shift of five or six employees and the entire service department opposed the union. The Board dismissed these statements as unreliable hearsay even though nothing contradicts them.

The Board also improperly disregarded the statements of Ridgick and Marsh. Ridgick said “there was no need for a union” if employees were treated fairly; Marsh complained that union dues were not worth the cost. These are straightforward expressions of anti-union sentiment. The Board’s inference that employees would lie during job interviews because the employer said the company would be non-union is pure speculation. This Court has long held that employers may rely on employee statements indicating opposition to the union. *Brooks v. NLRB*, 348 U.S. 96, 103 (1954). The Board’s insistence on direct, unambiguous declarations contradicts that principle.

Moreover, the Board arbitrarily used the employer’s later decision date to discount employees hired after January 25. An employer’s good-faith doubt is formed when it decides to poll, based on information then available. The Board’s retrospective accounting penalizes the employer for acting promptly. *NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 345–46 (1938) (employer retains control over hiring and business operations). The poll itself—conducted under rigorous safeguards and showing a 19–13 vote against the union—independently corroborates that the employer’s doubt was sincere and reasonable.

**III. THE BOARD’S STANDARD UNDERMINES THE ACT’S CORE PURPOSE.**

The Act guarantees employees the right to select—or reject—a bargaining representative. *Jones & Laughlin*, 301 U.S. at 33. The Board’s rule forces an employer to make an impossible choice: recognize a union that may no longer have majority support, coercing employees into unwanted representation, or withdraw recognition and risk an unfair labor practice charge. Either outcome frustrates employee free choice. The Board’s standard discourages polling, the least disruptive way to resolve doubts about majority status. By making polling practically inaccessible, the Board substitutes its own policy judgment for the Act’s preference for employee self-determination. The statute does not authorize such a result. *Insurance Agents*, 361 U.S. at 497–98.

For these reasons, the Court should set aside the Board’s order and hold that an employer poll is lawful when based on a good-faith doubt of the union’s majority status, or at least that the Board’s factual finding here lacks substantial evidentiary support.
