The Ninth Circuit erred in exempting documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA because they are "predominantly" for internal use and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard is unfounded in the statutory text and Supreme Court precedent.

The FOIA exemptions are "explicitly exclusive" and must be "narrowly construed." United States v. Reynolds, 345 U.S. 1, 8 (1953); Rose v. The statutory language of Exemption 2, which exempts matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency," 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2), does not support a broad interpretation.

The Supreme Court has cautioned against expansive readings of FOIA exemptions. In United States v. Reynolds, the Court emphasized that exemptions must be narrowly construed to effectuate the statute's purpose of promoting transparency. 345 U.S. at 8. Similarly, in Kent v. Dulles, the Court held that administrative regulations must be "construed narrowly" to avoid undermining the statutory scheme. 357 U.S. 116, 129 (1958).

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, which formulated the "predominantly internal" standard, is misplaced because Crooker was decided after 1970. However, the D.C. Circuit's analysis in Crooker was influenced by earlier cases, such as Vaughn v. Rosen, which is not available, however,  Stokes v. Brennan, 476 F.2d 699 (5th Cir. 1973) is not available, however,  Getman v. NLRB, 450 F.2d 670 (D.C. Cir. 1971) is available and relevant. In Getman, the D.C. Circuit emphasized the need for a narrow construction of FOIA exemptions, holding that the exemptions should be applied only where "necessary" to protect agency functions. 450 F.2d at 674.

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's decision is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's holding in United States v. Reynolds, which emphasized the importance of disclosure in promoting accountability and transparency. 345 U.S. at 8. The Court has also recognized the importance of narrowly construing exemptions to prevent agencies from shielding information from the public. See Kent v. Dulles, 357 U.S. at 129.

The Navy's affidavits, which claimed that disclosure of the ESQD arc maps could threaten national security, were not sufficient to justify withholding the documents under Exemption 2. The affidavits did not demonstrate that the information was classified or compiled for law enforcement purposes, as required by other FOIA exemptions. See Kent v. Dulles, 357 U.S. at 129; United States v. Reynolds, 345 U.S. at 8;  Parker v. Brown, 317 U.S. 341 (1943).

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision to exempt the ESQD arc maps from disclosure under FOIA Exemption 2 was erroneous. The court's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard is unsupported by the statutory text and Supreme Court precedent, which emphasize the need for narrow construction of FOIA exemptions. The Navy's failure to classify the documents or demonstrate that they were compiled for law enforcement purposes further undermines the Ninth Circuit's decision. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's judgment and order the disclosure of the ESQD arc maps.
