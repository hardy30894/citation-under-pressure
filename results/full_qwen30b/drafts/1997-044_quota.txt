**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether debts arising from fraud may be discharged under the Bankruptcy Code—requires this Court to interpret the scope of § 523(a)(2)(A), which excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by... actual fraud.” The Third Circuit’s decision in *Cohen v. de la Cruz* misapplies this provision by treating statutorily mandated treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act as non-dischargeable *per se*, despite the absence of any federal statutory or judicial precedent supporting such a result. The Court should reverse, for the following reasons.

**I. Statutory Treble Damages Are Not “Debts Obtained by Fraud” Under § 523(a)(2)(A)**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) does not bar discharge of all damages awarded in a fraud case—only those “obtained by” fraud. *Molzof v. United States*, 502 U.S. 301, 305 (1992), makes clear that the meaning of “obtained by” is a federal question, and must be interpreted in light of the Bankruptcy Code’s purpose: to provide a “fresh start” to the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286–87 (1991). The Court has consistently held that the phrase “obtained by” requires a causal link between the fraud and the *specific debt* at issue. *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 195, 198 (9th Cir. 1991). In *Levy*, the Ninth Circuit held that punitive damages—awarded at the discretion of a jury—were not “obtained by” fraud, because they were not the direct result of the fraudulent conduct but rather a separate judicial penalty. Id. The Court should follow *Levy* here.

The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act’s treble damages are not “obtained by” fraud any more than punitive damages are. They are a statutory penalty, not a compensatory award for the loss caused by the fraud. *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 678 (11th Cir. 1993), is distinguishable: there, the court relied on the “fresh start” policy to bar discharge of punitive damages, but it did so in the context of discretionary jury awards. Here, the treble damages were not discretionary—they were automatic upon a finding of violation of the Act. The debtor could not have avoided them by acting differently; they were a legal consequence of the violation, not a consequence of the fraud itself. Thus, they are not “obtained by” fraud within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A).

**II. The Third Circuit’s “Debt” Theory Conflicts with the Text and Purpose of the Bankruptcy Code**

The Third Circuit held that the treble damages were non-dischargeable because they were part of a “debt” resulting from the fraud. But the Code does not define “debt” in a way that includes all monetary obligations arising from a tort or statutory violation. *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118 (3d Cir. 1995), recognized that the “debt” must be one “obtained by” fraud. The Court in *Cohn* emphasized that the fraud must be the *means* by which the debt was incurred, not merely the *context* in which it arose. Id. at 1118–19. Here, the debt for overcharged rent was obtained by fraud—Cohen charged more than allowed under the rent control ordinance. But the treble damages were not obtained by fraud; they were imposed by statute. The Code does not permit the discharge of debts that are *related to* fraud, only those *obtained by* it.

Moreover, the Court has long held that the Bankruptcy Code must be interpreted to avoid undermining its core purpose: to give honest debtors a “new beginning.” *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 286–87. If a debtor can be held liable for treble damages under a state statute—damages that were not foreseeable at the time of the conduct—then the Code’s promise of a fresh start is hollow. *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d at 198, recognized this danger: “To allow the discharge of the compensatory damages but not the punitive damages would be to create a perverse incentive for debtors to commit fraud.” The same logic applies here: if a debtor can be held liable for treble damages that were not part of the original fraud, then the debtor is punished twice—once by the fraud judgment, and again by the statutory penalty—without any opportunity to discharge the latter. That is not the law of bankruptcy.

**III. The Third Circuit’s Ruling Creates an Unworkable and Unprecedented Rule**

The Third Circuit’s decision opens the door to unlimited non-dischargeability of statutory penalties in bankruptcy. If treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are non-dischargeable, then so too should be treble damages under the RICO Act, the False Claims Act, or any other federal or state statute that provides for enhanced damages. But the Court has never held that such damages are non-dischargeable. *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d at 678, relied on policy, not precedent. The Court should not expand § 523(a)(2)(A) beyond its text and purpose.

The Court has repeatedly cautioned against reading bankruptcy statutes to create new liabilities. *Molzof*, 502 U.S. at 305. The phrase “obtained by” is not a blank check for courts to impose non-dischargeability based on policy preferences. The Third Circuit’s decision does precisely that. It treats the treble damages as if they were part of the original fraud, when they were not. The debtor did not “obtain” the treble damages through fraud; he was *subjected to* them by law.
