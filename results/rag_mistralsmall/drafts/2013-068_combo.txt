**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Massachusetts Buffer-Zone Statute Is a Content-Neutral, Viewpoint-Neutral Time-Place-Manner Regulation That Satisfies Intermediate Scrutiny.**

The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts buffer-zone statute as a constitutional time-place-manner regulation. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I)*, 571 F.3d 167, 176-81 (1st Cir. 2009), *aff’d*, 571 F.3d 167 (2010). Such regulations are permissible if they are (1) content-neutral, (2) narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and (3) leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989); *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984). The Massachusetts statute meets each of these requirements.

First, the statute is content-neutral. It does not target speech based on viewpoint or subject matter but instead regulates the *location* of speech near clinic entrances. *See Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 720 (2000) (upholding a similar Colorado statute as content-neutral because it "does not discriminate based on the viewpoint of the speaker"). The law applies equally to all speakers—pro-life advocates, pro-choice advocates, and others—without regard to their message. *See Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474, 481 (1988) (holding that a content-neutral regulation need not be "the most precise vehicle" for achieving the government’s interest so long as it does not "suppress unpopular ideas").

Second, the statute is narrowly tailored to serve the Commonwealth’s significant interest in ensuring public safety and preventing obstruction of clinic entrances. *See Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 767-68 (1994) (upholding a 36-foot buffer zone around clinic entrances as narrowly tailored to protect patients and staff). The 35-foot buffer zone is no broader than necessary to achieve this interest. *See id.* at 769 (finding that a 36-foot zone was "not unconstitutionally large"). The statute also includes exemptions for clinic employees, patients, and others lawfully using the public way, further demonstrating its narrow tailoring. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 174.

Third, the statute leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. Petitioners may still speak, distribute literature, and engage in expressive conduct outside the buffer zone. *See Frisby*, 487 U.S. at 482 (finding that a residential picketing ban left open "other means of communication"). The record confirms that petitioners can communicate effectively from sidewalks outside the buffer zone. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 176-81. Thus, the statute satisfies intermediate scrutiny.

**II. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls and Should Not Be Overruled.**

The First Circuit correctly relied on *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), in upholding the Massachusetts statute. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181-82. *Hill* expressly approved a 100-foot buffer zone around healthcare facilities, finding it a valid time-place-manner regulation. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715-19. The Court rejected the argument that such zones violate the First Amendment, holding that they burden only "speech in very limited places" and leave open "ample alternative channels for communication." *Id.* at 719.

Petitioners’ attempt to distinguish *Hill* is unavailing. They argue that *Hill* involved a floating buffer zone, while Massachusetts’ statute is fixed. But *Hill* did not rest its holding on the floating nature of the zone. *See id.* at 715-19. The Court’s analysis focused on the government’s interest in protecting patients and staff from harassment and obstruction—not the zone’s mobility. *Id.* The Massachusetts statute advances the same interest and is similarly tailored. *See Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 767-68 (upholding a fixed buffer zone for the same purpose).

Moreover, *Hill* remains good law. This Court has not questioned its reasoning, and no intervening decision has undermined its holding. *See, e.g., Christian Legal Soc. Chapter of Univ. of Cal., Hastings Coll. of Law v. Martinez*, 561 U.S. 661, 685 (2010) (citing *Hill* with approval). Petitioners’ invitation to overrule *Hill* should be rejected.

**III. The Massachusetts Statute Does Not Violate the First Amendment as Applied to Petitioners.**

Petitioners’ as-applied challenge fails because the statute leaves open ample alternative channels for their speech. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The record shows that petitioners can still communicate with clinic patrons outside the buffer zone. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 176-81. The statute does not prevent them from speaking, distributing literature, or engaging in expressive conduct—only from obstructing clinic entrances. *See Frisby*, 487 U.S. at 482.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147 (1939), is misplaced. *Schneider* struck down a ban on leafleting in public ways because it was not narrowly tailored to a significant government interest. *Id.* at 162. Here, by contrast, the statute is narrowly tailored to protect public safety and does not ban speech outright. *See Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 767-68. Thus, *Schneider* does not apply.

**IV. The Massachusetts Statute Does Not Engage in Viewpoint Discrimination.**

Petitioners argue that the statute discriminates based on viewpoint because it burdens only pro-life advocates. But the statute is facially neutral and applies to all speakers equally. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 720. The record does not support a claim of viewpoint discrimination. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 176-81. The First Circuit correctly rejected this argument. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed. The Massachusetts buffer-zone statute is a constitutional time-place-manner regulation that satisfies intermediate scrutiny and does not violate the First Amendment. *Hill v. Colorado* remains controlling, and petitioners’ as-applied and viewpoint-discrimination challenges fail.
