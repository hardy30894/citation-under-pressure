**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SCHOOL DID NOT VIOLATE THE GOOD NEWS CLUB’S FREE SPEECH RIGHTS BECAUSE IT PERMISSIBLY EXCLUDED RELIGIOUS WORSHIP AND INSTRUCTION FROM A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM.**

Milford Central School created a limited public forum by opening its facilities for “social, civic, and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community.” *See* *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985). Within such a forum, the government may restrict speech based on subject matter, so long as the restriction is reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *Id.* at 806. The school’s policy explicitly excludes “religious purposes,” a subject-matter restriction that is both reasonable and viewpoint neutral. This Court has consistently upheld such restrictions when the government did not intend to open its property for religious activities. *See* *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 392-93 (1993).

The Good News Club’s proposed activities—prayer, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as a personal savior—are not merely discussions of morals from a religious viewpoint. Rather, they constitute religious instruction and worship. The Club’s lesson plans explicitly distinguish between “saved” and “unsaved” children and conclude with an “invitation” to “believe on the Lord Jesus Christ” to be saved. As the court of appeals correctly held, these activities “fall outside the bounds of pure ‘moral and character development’” and are “quintessentially religious.” *Good News Club v. Milford Central School*, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000). The school therefore permissibly excluded the Club because it had not opened its forum for religious worship or instruction.

The Club’s claim that it was engaged only in viewpoint discrimination is unavailing. As this Court recognized in *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 831 (1995), viewpoint discrimination occurs when the government “targets not particular subject matter, but particular views taken by speakers on a subject.” Here, the school did not exclude the Club because of its religious perspective on morals; it excluded the Club because the Club’s activities were not about morals at all—they were about religious conversion. The distinction between subject matter and viewpoint is critical. *See* *Arkansas Educ. Television Comm’n v. Forbes*, 523 U.S. 666, 677 (1998) (explaining that a broadcaster’s decision to exclude candidates lacking popular support was a permissible subject-matter decision, not viewpoint discrimination). Because the school’s policy excluded the “subject” of religion, not the “viewpoint” of religious speakers on a secular topic, the exclusion was permissible. *See* *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267 (1981) (noting that a state university may create a limited forum and exclude certain subjects).

**II. ANY INFRINGEMENT OF SPEECH WAS JUSTIFIED BY THE SCHOOL’S COMPELLING INTEREST IN AVOIDING AN ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE VIOLATION.**

Even assuming the exclusion constituted a speech restriction, it was justified by Milford’s legitimate concern that permitting the Good News Club’s activities would violate the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment. Public schools have a “special responsibility” to avoid endorsing religion, *see* *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992), particularly when young children are involved. Holding Club meetings on school premises would convey a message of official endorsement of religion, in violation of the test set forth in *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13 (1971). The Club’s meetings involve prayer, scripture reading, and an explicit invitation to children to accept Jesus Christ—all conducted by a teacher’s husband who is also a reverend. Given the impressionable age of the attendees, a reasonable observer would perceive the school’s sponsorship of such activities as an endorsement of Christianity. *See* *Santa Fe Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290, 305 (2000) (finding that school district’s policy permitting student-led prayers at football games violated the Establishment Clause because it “conveys a message of state endorsement”). Milford’s refusal to open its forum to religious worship was therefore a constitutionally permissible choice to avoid violating its own constitutional obligations. *See* *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 845-46 (noting that the government’s “interest in complying with its constitutional obligations” may justify restrictions on religious speech).

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
