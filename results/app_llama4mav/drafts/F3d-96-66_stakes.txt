ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to the shipowner, Bora Corp., Ltd., as there are genuine issues of material fact regarding the ship's breach of its "active operations" duty under section 5(b) of the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C. § 905(b).

The "active operations" duty, as established by the Supreme Court in Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos, 451 U.S. 156 (1981), requires a vessel to exercise reasonable care to prevent injuries to longshoremen when the vessel is actively involved in the cargo operations. Id. at 167. The duty is triggered when the vessel is "actively involved in the operations" and has "knowledge" of a hazardous condition. Id. at 175-76.

Here, the ship's crew was responsible for opening and closing the hatch covers using a block and pulley system, which included the snatch blocks. The crew had a duty to return the snatch blocks to the up position after opening the hatch covers to allow the removal of cargo below. However, one of the snatch blocks was left in the down position, resting on top of a cargo unit, creating a hazardous condition.

The ship argues that it is not liable because the longshoremen, including Serbin, were responsible for unloading the cargo and that the crew was not actively involved in the operations at the time of the accident. However, the record shows that the crew had previously opened the hatch covers and had left the snatch block in the down position. This is sufficient to establish that the vessel was "actively involved in the operations" and had "knowledge" of the hazardous condition. See Davis v. Portline Transportes Maritime Internacional, 16 F.3d 532, 538 (3d Cir. 1994) (holding that a vessel's active involvement in cargo operations can create a duty to protect longshoremen from hazards).

Furthermore, the fact that Serbin decided to move the snatch block himself does not necessarily absolve the ship of liability. Under Scindia, a vessel's duty to protect longshoremen is not limited to situations where the longshoremen are unable to protect themselves. Rather, the vessel must exercise reasonable care to prevent injuries, even if the longshoremen are aware of the hazard. See Scindia, 451 U.S. at 176-77 (holding that a vessel's duty to protect longshoremen is not limited to situations where the longshoremen are unable to protect themselves).

In this case, there are genuine issues of material fact regarding whether the ship breached its "active operations" duty and whether that breach caused Serbin's injuries. The district court erred in granting summary judgment, and this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.

Howell v. Union Carbide Corp., 899 F.2d 1256 (2d Cir. 1990), is distinguishable. In Howell, the court held that a vessel was not liable for a longshoreman's injuries because the vessel had not actively participated in the cargo operations. Id. at 1261. In contrast, the ship's crew in this case had actively participated in the cargo operations by opening the hatch covers and leaving the snatch block in the down position.

The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
