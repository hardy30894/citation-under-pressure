**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is governed by settled principles of statutory interpretation, rooted in the text, structure, and long-standing judicial construction of the Immigration and Nationality Act. The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of TPS satisfies the “inspected and admitted or paroled” requirement of § 1255(a). That interpretation is irreconcilable with the plain meaning of “admission,” the statutory framework, and binding precedent from this Court. We respectfully submit that Congress did not intend for TPS to serve as a substitute for lawful admission, and that the distinction between “status” and “admission” is both textually and structurally essential.

**I. The Text of the Immigration and Nationality Act Defines “Admission” as a Physical, Procedural Event, Not a Status Grant**

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely a suggestion—it is a precise, functional standard that has been consistently applied by this Court. In *Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185, 188 (1958), the Court held that an alien who is paroled into the country while her admissibility is being determined “has never enjoyed that status” of being “within the United States” for purposes of § 243(h), which authorizes withholding of deportation. The Court emphasized that parole does not alter the alien’s status as “excluded,” and thus she was not “within the United States” in the legal sense. Similarly, in *United States Ex Rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy*, 353 U.S. 72, 75 (1957), the Court affirmed that an alien who entered on a temporary visa and remained beyond the authorized period was not “lawfully admitted” and thus ineligible for suspension of deportation. The Court made clear that “admission” is not a mere label or status conferred by administrative grace, but a factual, procedural event: “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). The grant of TPS, which occurs after an alien is already present in the United States, cannot retroactively transform an unlawful entry into a lawful one.

**II. The Statutory Structure Confirms That “Admission” and “Status” Are Distinct Concepts**

Congress has repeatedly distinguished between “admission” and “status” in the INA. In *Cheung Sum Shee v. Nagle*, 268 U.S. 336, 340 (1925), the Court held that even a Chinese wife of a U.S. citizen—whose marriage would otherwise confer a right to enter—was “mandatorily excluded” under the Immigration Act of 1924 because she was “ineligible to citizenship.” The Court emphasized that “admission” is not automatic upon marriage or status; it is contingent upon statutory eligibility. The same principle applies here: TPS confers a status, but not an admission. The Court in *Chang Chan v. Nagle*, 268 U.S. 346, 350 (1925), similarly held that an alien’s “right of admission” is not established by mere marital ties or administrative leniency. The Court stated that “the Secretary of Labor said— ‘Neither the citizenship of the alleged husband, nor the relationship of the applicant to him, has been investigated for the reason that even if it were conceded that both elements exist she would still be inadmissible.’” This underscores that status and admission are not interchangeable: one may have a status (e.g., spouse of a citizen) but still be “inadmissible” under the law.

**III. The Purpose of TPS Is Temporary Relief, Not a Path to Permanent Residence**

The TPS program is designed to provide temporary protection from removal during extraordinary conditions abroad. It is not a mechanism for lawful entry. In *United States Ex Rel. Stapf v. Corsi*, 287 U.S. 129, 132 (1932), the Court held that an alien who landed in violation of the law was “deemed to be unlawfully in the United States,” even if he was later permitted to remain temporarily. The Court emphasized that “no alien excluded from admission into the United States by any law . . . shall be permitted to land in the United States, except temporarily for medical treatment.” This principle remains valid: temporary permission to remain does not equate to lawful admission. The same logic applies to TPS. As the Court in *Mrvica v. Esperdy*, 376 U.S. 560, 563 (1964), noted, an alien who departs the United States and returns is not automatically “lawfully admitted” unless he undergoes inspection and authorization. The Court rejected the notion that “continuous residence” alone could satisfy the admission requirement. Similarly, TPS does not confer admission; it merely permits continued presence.

**IV. The Distinction Between Admission and Status Is Embedded in the INA’s Framework**
