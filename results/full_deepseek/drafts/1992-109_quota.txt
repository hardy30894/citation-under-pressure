ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Due Process Clause requires a higher “reasoned choice” standard for competence to plead guilty or waive counsel than the standard for competence to stand trial. This Court has consistently defined the constitutional minimum for competence to stand trial as “whether the defendant has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). That same standard governs competence to enter a guilty plea or waive counsel.

**I. The Competence Standard Is Uniform Across All Stages of a Criminal Proceeding**

Nothing in this Court’s precedents suggests a bifurcated competence inquiry. In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975), the Court reiterated the *Dusky* test as the measure for fitness to proceed generally. The Court has applied that test to guilty pleas without modification. *See Henderson v. Morgan*, 426 U.S. 637, 648 n.22 (1976) (noting that a guilty plea is valid only if the defendant “has the requisite competence to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent choice”). A defendant who satisfies the *Dusky* standard—understanding the charges and potential penalties, and able to consult with counsel—necessarily possesses the minimum capacity to make a reasoned decision about whether to plead guilty or proceed pro se.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard conflates competence with the ultimate question whether a waiver is knowing and voluntary. Competence is a threshold inquiry; waiver turns on whether the defendant actually understood the rights relinquished. *See Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975) (waiver of counsel must be “knowing and intelligent”). The competence inquiry does not require that the defendant make a “wise” or “reasoned” choice among options. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 465 (1938) (waiver must be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”). A competent defendant may choose a course that appears irrational to others; the Constitution does not empower courts to second-guess that choice under the guise of a heightened competence standard.

**II. The Trial Court Properly Found Moran Competent Under *Dusky***

The record demonstrates that Moran understood the charges, the penalties, and the rights he was waiving. Two court-appointed psychiatrists evaluated Moran before the plea hearing and found him competent to stand trial. Dr. O’Gorman reported that Moran was “knowledgeable of the charges being made against him,” “can assist his attorney, if he so desires,” and is “fully cognizant of the penalties if convicted.” Dr. Jurasky similarly concluded that Moran understood the proceedings. These findings satisfy *Dusky*.

The Ninth Circuit relied on Moran’s depression, suicide attempt, and medication as evidence of possible incompetence. But this Court has held that “the presence of a mental disturbance is not necessarily inconsistent with a finding of competence” to stand trial. *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105, 108 (1954). The *Dusky* standard requires only a rational and factual understanding, not freedom from emotional distress. Moreover, the trial court specifically asked Moran about his medication; Moran stated he was taking medication but did not indicate any impairment. The court had no duty to inquire further absent clear evidence of incompetence. *See Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966) (duty to hold hearing arises only where there is “bona fide doubt” of competence).

**III. The Post-Conviction Hearing Confirmed Competence**

Even if the trial court erred in failing to sua sponte hold a competence hearing, the error was cured by the full post-conviction hearing at which Moran presented evidence and the state court expressly found him competent. That finding is entitled to a presumption of correctness under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). *See Maggio v. Fulgam*, 462 U.S. 111, 117 (1983) (per curiam) (state court factual findings presumed correct). The state court considered the psychiatric reports and testimony, and applied the correct standard—Moran’s competence at the time of the plea. The Ninth Circuit improperly substituted its own factual assessment for that of the state court.

**IV. This Court’s Precedent Rejects a Heightened Standard**

The reasoning of the Ninth Circuit conflicts with this Court’s decisions in *Dusky*, *Drope*, and *Henderson*. Those cases establish that competence to plead guilty requires no greater mental capacity than competence to stand trial. Indeed, the Court has repeatedly applied the same competence standard in both contexts without distinguishing between them. *See, e.g., Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 398-99 (1993) (holding that the *Dusky* standard is “plainly sufficient” for competence to plead guilty or waive counsel). The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” test imposes an unwarranted additional burden on trial courts and invites endless litigation over the quality of a defendant’s decision-making. This Court should reverse.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
