# ARGUMENT

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) borrows the “civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State” to serve as federal law on fixed platforms only “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). Properly understood against the statute’s structure and this Court’s enclave jurisprudence, that language authorizes resort to state law only where federal law leaves a gap for state law to fill. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading—that state law applies whenever it is not affirmatively inconsistent with federal law—cannot be squared with OCSLA’s text, history, or purpose.

## I. OCSLA Treats the Outer Continental Shelf as a Federal Enclave, Where State Law Serves Only a Gap-Filling Function.

OCSLA extends “the Constitution and laws of the United States” to fixed platforms “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). This Court has confirmed that the statute’s central purpose was “to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf.” Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). Congress did not intend to create a jurisdiction in which state and federal law operate concurrently, as they do within the States themselves; it intended to create exclusive federal jurisdiction, borrowing state law only as “federal law” to supply what Congress had not otherwise addressed.

That structure mirrors the traditional treatment of federal enclaves, where Congress has long assimilated state law only to fill interstices in an otherwise exclusive federal domain. In the criminal context, the Assimilative Crimes Act incorporates state penal law into federal enclaves precisely because federal law is otherwise silent, and this Court has approved that gap-filling incorporation as a valid, non-delegated exercise of congressional power. United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958). OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision was drafted with this enclave model in mind, and this Court’s decision in Rodrigue confirms as much: the Court permitted resort to Louisiana wrongful-death law only after concluding that no federal remedy—neither the Death on the High Seas Act nor general maritime law—covered the workers’ deaths on the fixed platforms. 395 U.S. at 360, 366. Only once the Court determined that admiralty law “no more applies to these accidents actually occurring on the islands than it would to accidents occurring in an upland federal enclave,” id. at 366, did “any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]” fall away, id. Rodrigue thus did not hold that state law applies concurrently with valid federal law; it held that state law fills the void left when no federal law governs the claim at all.

## II. This Court’s Enclave and Preemption Precedents Confirm That Comprehensive Federal Schemes Leave No Room for Supplementary State Regulation.

Where Congress enacts a comprehensive federal regulatory scheme, this Court has consistently held that state law must yield even absent an express conflict. In Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52 (1941), the Court explained that when Congress has adopted “a complete scheme” to govern a field, states may not, “inconsistently with the purpose of Congress,” supplement that scheme with their own regulatory requirements. Id. at 66–67. Pennsylvania v. Nelson, 350 U.S. 497 (1956), likewise held that “when the Federal Government has taken possession of a field” through “a complete penal code,” a State may not add “additional burdens” of its own, even ones not directly conflicting with federal requirements. Id. at 502, 504.

The Fair Labor Standards Act is precisely such a comprehensive scheme. It establishes uniform national minimum-wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements, backed by detailed regulations, and its very completeness leaves no “significant void or gap,” Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), for California’s wage-and-hour law to occupy on the OCS. The Fifth Circuit’s gap-filling test in Continental Oil correctly implements the structure Rodrigue described: state law is borrowed as “the law of the United States” under § 1333(a)(2)(A) only to the extent federal law does not already speak to the subject. Where, as here, the FLSA comprehensively regulates the wages and hours of platform workers, there is no gap for state law to fill, and the inquiry ends there—without regard to whether California law could theoretically coexist without direct conflict.

## III. The Court of Appeals’ Broader Standard Would Undermine the Enclave Structure Congress Chose for the OCS.

The Ninth Circuit’s standard—asking only whether state law is “applicable” to the subject matter and “not inconsistent” with specific federal provisions—collapses OCSLA’s enclave-based incorporation scheme into an ordinary conflict-preemption analysis of the kind applicable within the States. But Congress rejected extending the boundaries of the adjacent States to the Shelf, S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6 (1953), precisely because it wanted a body of law defined by federal, not state, primacy. Reading § 1333(a)(2)(A) to permit any state law that does not directly contradict federal law—rather than one confined to filling actual gaps—would reintroduce the very patchwork of concurrent state regulation that Congress avoided by treating the OCS as an area of “exclusive Federal jurisdiction.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1); Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355–56.

Because the FLSA comprehensively occupies the field of wage-and-hour regulation for OCS platform workers, no gap exists for California law to fill, and Newton’s state-law claims fail as a matter of law.
