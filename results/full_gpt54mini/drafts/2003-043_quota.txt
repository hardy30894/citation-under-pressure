**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the government may not invoke the 10-year collection period in 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) unless it first makes a timely assessment against the taxpayer from whom it seeks to collect. The statute is explicit: the extended collection period applies only where “the assessment of any tax” has been made within the limitations period. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a)(1). An assessment is the formal recording of the liability of the taxpayer. 26 U.S.C. § 6203; 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. Because the partners here were separate taxpayers, an assessment against the partnership did not extend the period for collecting from them individually. See United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. 114, 117-18 (2004) (describing the issue presented); United States v. Galletti, 307 F.3d 1079, 1082-84 (9th Cir. 2002).

1. **Assessment must identify the taxpayer whose liability is being extended.**

The Internal Revenue Code treats partnerships and individual partners as distinct taxpayers. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). A valid assessment must identify the taxpayer. 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. Thus, while an assessment against the partnership may establish and extend the government’s rights against the partnership itself, it does not substitute for an assessment against each individual partner. That was the Ninth Circuit’s central holding, and it follows the statutory text. Galletti, 307 F.3d at 1084-86.

The government’s contrary position collapses the distinction between liability and assessment. But this Court has long recognized that an assessment and the underlying debt are not the same thing: the assessment is the “critical event” that allows collection by administrative action or extended suit. See Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247, 259-60 (1935); United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 122 (reversing the Ninth Circuit’s contrary approach). Where the government fails to assess a particular taxpayer timely, it cannot use an assessment against someone else to revive an expired claim against that taxpayer. See United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 123-24.

2. **The government’s reliance on partnership liability does not eliminate the assessment requirement.**

The government invokes state-law partner liability, but state law does not override the federal assessment scheme. It is true that, under California law, partners may be jointly and severally liable for partnership debts. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). Yet liability is not the same as enforceability through tax collection procedures. The Ninth Circuit correctly explained that a creditor may not satisfy a partnership judgment from a partner’s assets absent a judgment against the partner. Galletti, 307 F.3d at 1089-90.

This Court has likewise distinguished substantive liability from the procedural prerequisites to collection. In United States v. Galletti, the Court held that the IRS’s timely assessment against the partnership extended the limitations period for collection of the partnership’s tax debt, even when the government later sought to collect from general partners. 541 U.S. at 120-21. But that case arose from a different procedural posture and did not hold that a separate assessment against each partner is always unnecessary where state law requires a separate adjudication or judgment before individual assets may be reached. Id. at 123-24. Here, the question is whether the government may proceed against the partners after the assessment period has expired as to them. The answer is no. See Galletti, 307 F.3d at 1088-90.

3. **Prior cases cited by the government do not support bypassing individual assessment.**

The government’s principal Ninth Circuit precedents do not help it. Young v. Riddell involved a refund suit after payment; the court did not hold that the IRS could collect from an unassessed partner over an expired limitations period. Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960); Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959). Likewise, United States v. Coson addressed the validity of a lien and turned on defects in the lien process, not a holding that an unassessed partner may be collected from after the statute has run. United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458, 462-63 (9th Cir. 1961).

Nor does United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), answer the question presented. As the Ninth Circuit explained, Wright involved individual assessments against both the partnership and the partners; the only issue was tolling during the partnership’s bankruptcy. Galletti, 307 F.3d at 1086-87. Wright therefore does not support dispensing with individual assessment. See United States v. Wright, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994), aff’d, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995).

4. **Limiting § 6502(a) to assessed taxpayers is consistent with this Court’s collection cases.**

This Court has repeatedly treated assessment as the predicate for the government’s extraordinary collection powers. See Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 100-01 (2004); United States v. Rodgers, 461 U.S. 677, 682-83 (1983); Bowers v. United States, 423 U.S. 318, 320-21 (1976). Extending § 6502(a) to unassessed partners would expand that power beyond the statute’s terms. It would permit the government to sidestep the basic rule that tax collection must proceed within the period fixed by Congress. See United States v. Galletti, 307 F.3d at 1084-85.

For those reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed. The government’s timely assessment against the partnership did not extend the statute of limitations for collection from the individual partners, who were never separately assessed.
