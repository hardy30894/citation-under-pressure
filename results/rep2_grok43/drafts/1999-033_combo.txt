The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment secures to all persons “the equal protection of the laws.” Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36, 81 (1873). It “was designed to assure that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303, 307-08 (1880). The Clause therefore presupposes a comparison between classes of persons. Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886) (invalidating ordinance applied “with a mind so unequal and oppressive” against a defined class of Chinese laundries).

A plaintiff who alleges neither membership in a class nor differential treatment of similarly situated persons fails to state a claim under the Clause. Plessy v. Ferguson, 163 U.S. 537, 551-52 (1896) (equal protection “does not require the impossible” of identical treatment outside the context of recognized classes). The Court has consistently required that the complainant demonstrate “that the law is being applied differently to persons similarly situated.” Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada, 305 U.S. 337, 350 (1938).

This class-based requirement is not satisfied by an assertion that government action was motivated by personal animus alone. Shelley v. Kraemer, 334 U.S. 1, 22 (1948) (state action violates equal protection only when it denies rights “on the basis of race or color” to members of a protected class). Isolated or individualized treatment, without more, does not implicate the Clause. Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) (segregation “solely on the basis of race” denied equal protection to the class of Negro children).

The same principle governs federal action. Bolling v. Sharpe, 347 U.S. 497, 499 (1954) (“discrimination may be so unjustifiable as to be violative of due process” only when it rests on class considerations). Because respondent Olech alleged neither class membership nor class-based treatment, the complaint states no cause of action under the Equal Protection Clause.
