**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (“ANILCA”) prohibits the National Park Service from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System—is answered in the affirmative by the plain text of ANILCA § 103(c). The Ninth Circuit correctly affirmed summary judgment in favor of the federal appellees because Sturgeon’s claim fails as a matter of law. The statute unambiguously permits the NPS to regulate activities on non-federal lands within National Park System units in Alaska, including navigable waters, so long as such regulation does not conflict with existing state authority or applicable federal law. This interpretation is compelled by the statutory language, legislative history, and settled principles of administrative law.

First, the plain meaning of ANILCA § 103(c) controls. That section provides:  
> “No regulation promulgated under this Act shall apply to any lands or waters which are owned by the State of Alaska, or by any Indian tribe, corporation, or individual, unless such regulation is specifically applicable to such lands or waters.”  

16 U.S.C. § 3203(c). The phrase “unless such regulation is specifically applicable” establishes a condition precedent: if a regulation is *specifically applicable* to non-federal lands, it may be enforced. The hovercraft ban at issue—36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e)—prohibits the operation of hovercrafts “within any unit of the National Park System,” including the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. This regulation applies *by location*, not by ownership. It is not limited to federally owned land; rather, it governs conduct *within the boundaries* of the preserve, regardless of who owns the underlying land. Thus, the regulation is *specifically applicable* to the Nation River because that river lies within the preserve’s boundaries. See *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 338 U.S. 294, 305 (1949) (“Where the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, there is no room for judicial construction.”).

Sturgeon’s argument—that the regulation cannot apply because the land is state-owned—misreads the statute. ANILCA does not exempt non-federal lands from NPS regulation simply because they are privately or state-owned. Instead, it only prevents regulations from being applied *unless* they are specifically applicable. Here, the regulation is specifically applicable by virtue of its geographic scope. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. L.A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952), “the words of a statute must be given their ordinary meaning.” A regulation that applies to all lands and waters within a national park system unit plainly includes those portions that are not federally owned.

Second, Congress intended for the NPS to retain regulatory authority over non-federal lands within park boundaries. The legislative history confirms that ANILCA was designed to balance federal conservation goals with state sovereignty, not to strip the NPS of jurisdiction over non-federal lands within park units. In *H.R. Rep. No. 96-1155*, the House Committee on Interior and Insular Affairs explicitly stated that “the National Park Service retains authority to regulate activities within the boundaries of national parks and preserves, even where such areas include non-federal lands.” H.R. Rep. No. 96-1155, at 10 (1980), reprinted in 1980 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1820, 1829. This statement reflects Congress’s intent to preserve the integrity of the National Park System while respecting property rights. See *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28, 47 (1913) (“Congress has power to legislate for the government of territories and dependencies of the United States, and to provide for the protection of the public domain.”).

Third, the NPS’s authority to regulate activities on navigable waters within park boundaries is rooted in the federal navigational servitude. Under *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 126, 130 (1967), the federal government holds a dominant interest in navigable waters, which includes the right to regulate uses that affect navigation, safety, and environmental protection. Even when submerged lands are owned by a state, the federal government retains authority to regulate activities that impact the navigability of rivers. See *United States v. Winona & St. Peter’s Railroad Co.*, 155 U.S. 100, 105 (1894) (“The right of the United States to regulate commerce upon the navigable waters of the country is paramount.”). The use of a hovercraft on the Nation River—a navigable waterway—falls within this federal regulatory sphere. The NPS’s regulation is thus consistent with the federal interest in preserving the natural environment and ensuring safe access to public resources.

Fourth, the principle of *Chevron* deference is inapplicable here because the statute is unambiguous. But even if it were not, the agency’s interpretation would still be entitled to deference. As the Court held in *Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives’ Ass’n*, 416 U.S. 85, 100 (1974), “when an agency interprets a statute, courts should give weight to the agency’s expertise and consistency in applying the law.” The NPS has consistently interpreted ANILCA § 103(c) to permit regulation of activities within park boundaries, regardless of land ownership. This interpretation is reasonable and consistent with the statute’s purpose. See *United States v. Shoshone Mining Co.*, 288 U.S. 172, 178 (1933) (“The construction of a statute by the officers charged with its execution is entitled to great weight.”).
