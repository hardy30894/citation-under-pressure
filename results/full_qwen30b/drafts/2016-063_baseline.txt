**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to resolve a critical conflict in the interpretation of the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA), 5 U.S.C. § 7702 et seq. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in this case, which holds that such dismissals are reviewable only in the Federal Circuit, is contrary to the Supreme Court’s holding in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596 (2012), and to the statutory text, structure, and purpose of the CSRA. The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), as a basis for distinguishing procedural from jurisdictional dismissals is not only inconsistent with *Kloeckner* but also undermines the very principle of uniformity the CSRA was designed to ensure. This Court should reverse.

The CSRA establishes a bifurcated system for judicial review of MSPB decisions. Under § 7703(b)(1)(A), most MSPB decisions are reviewable in the Federal Circuit. But § 7703(b)(2) creates an exception: “mixed cases”—those in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and [alleges] that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” federal law—are to be reviewed in federal district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The key to determining the proper forum is whether the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” Id. § 7702(a)(1)(A). This threshold question is not a mere procedural formality; it is a substantive inquiry into the nature of the challenged action and the Board’s jurisdiction over it.

In *Kloeckner*, the Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on a *procedural* ground—such as untimeliness—judicial review lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit. 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Court emphasized that the employee had been “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB,” id. at 604, and that the Board’s dismissal did not negate the existence of a mixed case. The Court rejected the argument that the procedural defect rendered the case “non-appealable,” noting that “the Board may excuse procedural defects.” Id. at 605. The Court’s reasoning was grounded in the statutory text: the mixed-case exception applies when the employee is “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB,” not when the Board *concludes* it lacks jurisdiction. Id. at 604.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in this case attempts to distinguish *Kloeckner* by asserting that jurisdictional dismissals—those based on the voluntary nature of the challenged action—are fundamentally different from procedural ones. But this distinction is not supported by the text, structure, or precedent of the CSRA. The D.C. Circuit claims that a jurisdictional dismissal “was never appealable to the MSPB,” and thus falls outside the mixed-case exception. But that is precisely what *Kloeckner* rejected: the idea that a case can be “non-appealable” simply because the Board finds it untimely or jurisdictionally barred. In *Kloeckner*, the Board dismissed the case on timeliness grounds, yet the Court held that the case was still a mixed case because the underlying action—removal—was appealable. 133 S. Ct. at 604. The same logic applies here: Perry was affected by a suspension and retirement—actions that are appealable to the MSPB if involuntary. The fact that the Board later concluded the action was voluntary does not change the fact that the action was appealable *at the time it occurred*. As the Court stated in *Kloeckner*, “the Board’s jurisdictional determination is not dispositive of the mixed-case status.” Id. at 605.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is misplaced. *Powell* predated *Kloeckner* and was based on a narrow interpretation of the CSRA that treated jurisdictional and procedural grounds as categorically distinct. But *Kloeckner* explicitly rejected that dichotomy. The Court did not distinguish between “procedural” and “jurisdictional” grounds in its analysis; it focused instead on whether the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). The Court’s repeated emphasis on the *action*—not the Board’s reasoning—demonstrates that the proper inquiry is not whether the Board *concluded* it lacked jurisdiction, but whether the action itself was appealable. In this case, Perry’s suspension and retirement were such actions. The Board’s conclusion that they were voluntary does not negate that fact.

Moreover, the D.C. Circuit’s rule creates a perverse incentive: employees who are coerced into voluntary resignations may be denied access to district court simply because the Board dismisses their case on jurisdictional grounds. This contradicts the purpose of the mixed-case exception, which is to ensure that discrimination claims are not buried in the Federal Circuit’s procedural maze. As the Court noted in *Kloeckner*, “the purpose of the mixed-case provision is to ensure that discrimination claims are not ‘lost’ in the administrative process.” 133 S. Ct. at 605. The D.C. Circuit’s rule does exactly that.

Finally, the D.C. Circuit’s argument that *Kloeckner* did not “eviscerate” *Powell* because the Court “cited” the Second and Tenth Circuits’ decisions is unpersuasive. The Court’s citation of those decisions was not to endorse their reasoning, but to identify the split it was resolving.
