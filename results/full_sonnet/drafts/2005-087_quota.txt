# ARGUMENT

## I. States Possess Broad Authority to Define and Channel the Use of Mental-Disease Evidence Without Violating Due Process.

This Court has long recognized that "the definition of the elements of a criminal offense is entrusted to the legislature, particularly in the case of state crimes." *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 210 (1977). That authority necessarily includes the power to structure how evidence bearing on mental capacity is presented and to which issue it may be directed. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), this Court upheld a state's choice to require proof of insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, explaining that "the right and power of a state to determine whether the plea of insanity may be an issue in a criminal case, and if so, under what circumstances, is not open to question." *Id.* at 800–01. If a State may condition the very availability of an insanity defense, it may likewise structure the procedural channel through which psychiatric evidence supporting that defense is introduced.

*Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), reinforces this deference. There, a plurality declined to constitutionalize a particular theory of mental disease as a defense to criminal liability, observing that "this whole area of the relationship between disease and criminal responsibility is one that is still not understood." *Id.* at 536–37. The unsettled science of psychiatric causation counsels the same restraint here: Arizona's decision to cabin mens rea psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry, where it is tested under structured burdens and standards, reflects the same institutional caution this Court endorsed in *Powell*.

## II. Arizona's Rule Is a Permissible Evidentiary Channeling Rule Analogous to the Rule Upheld in *Montana v. Egelhoff*.

This case is materially indistinguishable from *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996), where the Court upheld a state statute barring consideration of voluntary intoxication evidence on the question of mens rea. The plurality explained that because "the doctrine at issue here is not deeply rooted," a State's contrary judgment about how to weigh such evidence "is not so fundamental that it can claim the protection of the Due Process Clause." *Id.* at 43, 51 (plurality opinion). Just as Montana permissibly excluded intoxication evidence from the mens rea determination, Arizona permissibly channels mental-disease evidence to the insanity determination rather than allowing it to be used to directly negate mens rea. Both rules reflect a legislative judgment that certain categories of evidence are more reliably and fairly evaluated within a structured affirmative-defense framework than through unstructured jury reasoning about capacity.

*Martin v. Ohio*, 480 U.S. 228 (1987), confirms that such structuring does not offend due process merely because it affects how a defendant may use evidence relevant to intent. The Court there upheld placing the burden of proving self-defense on the defendant even though the evidence "would also have supported...a finding that he did not have the requisite mental state," because "a state may make [that] choice." *Id.* at 233–34. Arizona has made an analogous choice: channeling psychiatric evidence toward the insanity defense, where it is fully considered, rather than permitting it as free-floating mens rea rebuttal.

## III. Due Process Does Not Guarantee an Unrestricted Right to Introduce All Relevant Evidence in Whatever Form the Defendant Prefers.

Clark's asserted right finds no support in this Court's evidentiary due process cases. *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284 (1973), and *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683 (1986), are readily distinguishable. In *Chambers*, the state's evidentiary rules operated to exclude *all* evidence corroborating a third party's confession, leaving the defendant with no means whatsoever to present a defense. 410 U.S. at 302–03. In *Crane*, the trial court excluded evidence of the circumstances of a confession from the jury's consideration entirely. 476 U.S. at 690–91. Arizona imposes no such blanket exclusion. Clark's psychiatric evidence was fully admitted and considered—on the insanity issue for which it was most probative. Arizona has not silenced the evidence; it has assigned it to the analytical framework the State reasonably deems most reliable, consistent with *Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437 (1992), which held that state procedural rules governing competency and capacity issues violate due process only if they "offend some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Id.* at 445.

Finally, *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71 (1992), and *Jones v. United States*, 463 U.S. 354 (1983), confirm that insanity is properly treated as a discrete status with its own burdens and consequences, not merely one evidentiary theory among many for negating intent. *Jones* recognized that "the value of the insanity defense lies in its recognition that some criminal defendants are not culpable," 463 U.S. at 366, precisely because the defense operates through its own structured proof requirements—requirements Arizona is entitled to preserve by declining to let mens rea rebuttal evidence function as an end-run around them.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, Arizona's channeling rule violates no due process right, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
