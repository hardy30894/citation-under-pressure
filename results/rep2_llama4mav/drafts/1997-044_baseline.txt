## Argument

The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are designed to give debtors a fresh start, but this protection is not available to those who commit fraud. The question presented is whether individuals who commit fraud can discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded, under the Bankruptcy Code. The answer is no.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). To prevail under this section, a creditor must show that the debtor obtained money or property through a material misrepresentation, knew the representation was false or made it with gross recklessness, intended to deceive the creditor, and that the creditor reasonably relied on the false representation, resulting in a loss. In re Poskanzer, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992).

The Court of Appeals correctly held that Cohen's debt to the tenants was nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). Cohen's argument that he made no affirmative representation to the tenants is unpersuasive. By charging rents in excess of the lawful amount, Cohen made an implicit representation that the rents were lawful. See In re Cohen, 185 B.R. 171, 172 (Bankr.D.N.J.1994). Cohen's reckless disregard for the truth of his representation is evident from his failure to inform himself about the rent control ordinance, despite being aware of its existence. In re Cohn, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118-19 (3d Cir.1995).

The treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are also nondischargeable. While the Ninth Circuit has held that punitive damages are dischargeable because they are not "obtained by" fraud, In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir.1992), the Eleventh Circuit has correctly held that punitive damages are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) because they are part of the "debt" incurred through fraud. In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672, 678-80 (11th Cir.1993). The St. Laurent view is more cogent, as it aligns with the "fresh start" policy of the Bankruptcy Code, which is intended for "honest but unfortunate debtors." Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991).

In this case, the treble damages award was mandated by statute, making it foreseeable to Cohen at the time he committed the fraud. Therefore, it is properly considered a debt incurred through his conduct, rather than punishment. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed, holding that Cohen's debt to the tenants, including the treble damages award, is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code.
