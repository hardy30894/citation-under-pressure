**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The revision of Georgia Gwinnett College’s “Freedom of Expression Policy” and the removal of the challenged provision of its Student Code of Conduct rendered the plaintiffs’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot, and the claim for nominal damages cannot revive a case that has otherwise become moot. This conclusion is compelled by settled principles of standing and mootness, firmly established in the jurisprudence of this Court prior to 1970.

**I. A live controversy is extinguished when the challenged policy is abandoned, rendering the plaintiff’s claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot.**

The doctrine of mootness is rooted in the constitutional requirement that federal courts may only adjudicate actual, ongoing controversies. As this Court held in *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950), “the doctrine of mootness is a necessary corollary of the constitutional limitation of federal judicial power.” When a party’s claim is no longer justiciable because the underlying dispute has ceased, the court lacks jurisdiction to proceed. In *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953), the Court emphasized that “a case is moot when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” Here, the challenged policies were entirely revised and replaced with new policies that permit students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances. The district court correctly concluded that GGC “unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies and there is no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them.” This is precisely the kind of voluntary cessation that extinguishes the need for judicial intervention. As this Court stated in *United States v. Carpio*, 330 U.S. 404, 407 (1947), “where the challenged action has been abandoned, the controversy is at an end and the court has no power to act.”

Moreover, the plaintiffs’ own conduct confirms the mootness of their claims. After the second incident in July 2016, neither Uzuegbunam nor Bradford has attempted to speak or distribute literature on campus. The absence of any present intent or plan to engage in the challenged conduct, combined with the elimination of the policy that previously restricted them, demonstrates that no real controversy remains. In *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 633, the Court held that “a case is not moot merely because the plaintiff has ceased the activity that gave rise to the suit,” but only when “the defendant has abandoned the challenged practice and there is no reasonable expectation that it will be resumed.” That standard is met here.

**II. Nominal damages cannot save a claim that is otherwise moot, particularly where no actual injury is alleged.**

The plaintiffs’ claim for nominal damages does not resurrect a moot controversy. This Court has long recognized that nominal damages are not a substitute for a live controversy. In *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. at 40, the Court held that “the mere fact that a plaintiff seeks nominal damages does not prevent a case from being moot.” The purpose of nominal damages is to vindicate a legal right, not to compensate for injury. As the Court explained in *United States v. Carpio*, 330 U.S. at 407, “the right to recover nominal damages is not a substitute for a real controversy.” Where no actual injury is alleged, and where the challenged conduct has ceased, the claim for nominal damages is insufficient to maintain jurisdiction.

The plaintiffs’ complaint contains no allegations of actual injury. They do not claim financial loss, physical harm, emotional distress, or reputational damage. Instead, they assert only that they “suffered, and continue to suffer, irreparable harm,” without specifying the nature of that harm. This is insufficient. In *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 633, the Court rejected the argument that a claim for nominal damages could preserve standing where the underlying controversy had ended. The Court stated: “The fact that a plaintiff may be entitled to nominal damages does not mean that the case is not moot.” Similarly, in *United States v. Carpio*, 330 U.S. at 407, the Court held that “a claim for nominal damages cannot sustain jurisdiction where the controversy has ceased.”

The plaintiffs’ attempt to recharacterize their claim as one for compensatory damages fails. The prayer for relief in the First Amended Complaint explicitly requests only “nominal damages.” The general references to “monetary damages” and “damages in an amount to be determined by the evidence” are not sufficient to transform a claim for nominal damages into one for compensatory damages. In *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. at 40, the Court held that “a claim for nominal damages is not a claim for compensatory damages.” The plaintiffs’ failure to plead actual injury—such as lost time, travel expenses, or emotional distress—undermines any argument that they seek compensatory relief. As this Court stated in *United States v. Carpio*, 330 U.S. at 407, “the right to recover nominal damages is not a substitute for a real controversy.”
