# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING THE OPINION OF AMERICAN STEAMSHIP'S EXPERT AS TO THE CAUSE OF THE LINE'S PARTING BECAUSE THAT OPINION WAS NOT EXPERT TESTIMONY.

An expert may assist a jury only with matters that lie beyond a juror's ordinary experience; when a witness merely draws inferences from facts equally available to the jury, his opinion is no more than a guess dressed in the language of expertise. In *Salem v. United States Lines Co.*, 370 U.S. 31 (1962), the Supreme Court confronted precisely this problem. A marine engineer offered his opinion as to how a grating cover had fallen and struck a seaman, even though he had not witnessed the event and possessed no special knowledge of the mechanism that failed. The Court held the testimony should not have been received, explaining that the witness's conclusion rested on the same facts available to the jury and did not require the "assistance of persons possessing special knowledge" to be understood. *Salem*, 370 U.S. at 35.

The same defect infects the testimony admitted below. American Steamship's expert did not testify from specialized knowledge of manilla rope, marine hardware, or metallurgy that would illuminate something beyond common experience; he simply reconstructed, from the same photographs and testimony available to the jury, his theory that the torch used to deice the boom caused the line to part. That is not expertise—it is advocacy in a witness's mouth. Because the opinion "added nothing" that the jury could not have determined for itself from the raw evidence, *id.*, its admission was error, and the error cannot be deemed harmless where the jury's comparative-fault allocation turned squarely on causation.

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK'S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON UNSEAWORTHINESS, BUT NOT ON COMPARATIVE NEGLIGENCE.

### A. Unseaworthiness.

The shipowner's duty to furnish a seaworthy vessel—including seaworthy gear and appliances—is "essentially a species of liability without fault," *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85, 94 (1946), and is "absolute and completely independent of the owner's duty under the Jones Act to exercise reasonable care." *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 549 (1960). That duty is "nondelegable," and the owner cannot excuse its breach by shifting blame to the crew's own conduct in maintaining the ship's equipment. *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96, 100 (1944); *see also The Osceola*, 189 U.S. 158, 175 (1903). The undisputed record establishes that the line supporting the bos'n's chair parted while in ordinary use—a line that had been left rigged through entire summer voyages and was, by the time of the accident, badly worn. A rope that snaps under ordinary lowering conditions is not "reasonably fit for its intended use," the touchstone of seaworthiness. *Mitchell*, 362 U.S. at 550. Because no reasonable jury could conclude on this record that the line was fit for its purpose, the district court should have granted judgment as a matter of law that the vessel was unseaworthy.

### B. Comparative Negligence.

Cook likewise moved for judgment as a matter of law that he bore no comparative fault. That motion was properly denied. Under the Jones Act, a case must go to the jury whenever the evidence would permit a reasonable inference that the employer's negligence, or the seaman's own conduct, "played any part, even the slightest, in producing the injury." *Rogers v. Missouri Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 500, 506 (1957). It is the jury's province, not the court's, to weigh the credibility of witnesses and resolve conflicts in the testimony concerning how Cook used the torch and whether he adequately inspected the rigging; courts "are not free to reweigh the evidence and set aside the jury verdict merely because the jury could have drawn different inferences." *Tennant v. Peoria & Pekin Union Ry. Co.*, 321 U.S. 29, 35 (1944). A directed verdict is warranted only where reasonable minds could not differ, *see Boeing Co. v. Shipman*, 411 F.2d 365, 374 (5th Cir. 1969) (en banc), and comparative fault under maritime law reduces, rather than bars, recovery once some evidence of the seaman's own negligence exists. *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424, 431 (1939). Because conflicting evidence existed as to Cook's use of the torch near the rigged line, the district court correctly left that question to the jury.

## III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK'S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS.

Evidence offered to establish causation or fault must rest on more than speculation; a "complete absence of probative facts" connecting the proffered evidence to the injury cannot support its admission. *Lavender v. Kurn*, 327 U.S. 645, 653 (1946). American Steamship offered no evidence that Cook was impaired on the day of the accident; it introduced only his unrelated medical history to suggest, by innuendo, that he was generally unreliable. Such evidence, unconnected to the events of December 26, 1990, served only to prejudice the jury against Cook's character rather than to illuminate any issue properly before it—precisely the danger against which this Court has cautioned when extraneous character evidence is used to color a jury's assessment of a party. *Cf. Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 475–76 (1948). Its admission requires reversal.
