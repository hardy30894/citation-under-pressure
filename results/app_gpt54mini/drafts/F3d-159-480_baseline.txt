II. ANALYSIS

A. Constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951

Pearson’s first claim—that Congress lacked constitutional authority to enact 18 U.S.C. § 1951—fails under settled Supreme Court and Tenth Circuit precedent. The Hobbs Act is a valid exercise of Congress’s Commerce Clause power because it reaches robberies and extortions that, “in any way or degree,” obstruct, delay, or affect commerce. 18 U.S.C. § 1951(a); see Stirone v. United States, 361 U.S. 212, 215 (1960); United States v. Culbert, 435 U.S. 371, 373-74 (1978).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld § 1951 as a constitutional regulation of activity affecting interstate commerce. In Stirone, the Court recognized that Congress enacted the Hobbs Act in broad terms to protect interstate commerce from interference by robbery and extortion. 361 U.S. at 215-16. In Culbert, the Court rejected efforts to narrow the Act by judicial construction and held that Congress “speaks in broad language” when it prohibits interference with commerce through robbery or extortion. 435 U.S. at 373-74. Under those decisions, the only jurisdictional question is whether the charged conduct had at least a minimal effect on interstate commerce. United States v. Bolton, 68 F.3d 396, 398 (10th Cir. 1995); United States v. Zeigler, 19 F.3d 486, 489-90 (10th Cir. 1994).

That standard is easily satisfied here. The government presented evidence that Mr. Goodcents Subs & Pastas is part of a national chain, purchases goods from Kansas and out-of-state suppliers, and suffered business losses after the robbery and murder. Mr. Peterson further testified that the stolen money would have been used to purchase goods produced outside Kansas. This Court has held that depletion of assets from a business engaged in interstate commerce is sufficient to satisfy § 1951’s commerce element. Bolton, 68 F.3d at 398; Zeigler, 19 F.3d at 489-90. Even a robbery of a local business qualifies when the business buys goods from out-of-state sources or the robbery depletes funds used in interstate commerce. United States v. Merritt, 961 F.2d 362, 366 (2d Cir. 1992); United States v. Wang, 222 F.3d 234, 238-39 (6th Cir. 2000). Pearson therefore cannot show that § 1951 was unconstitutional as applied to his case.

To the extent Pearson suggests that the Hobbs Act exceeds Congress’s Commerce Clause authority categorically, that argument is foreclosed. The Supreme Court has long recognized that Congress may regulate intrastate conduct when, taken in the aggregate, it substantially affects interstate commerce. Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111, 124-28 (1942); see also Gonzales v. Raich, 545 U.S. 1, 17 (2005). Robbery of a commercial establishment that participates in interstate purchasing plainly falls within that principle. See Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146, 150-57 (1971).

Pearson’s remaining claims likewise do not warrant reversal.

B. Refusal to permit a tardy alibi defense

A district court has broad discretion to enforce discovery and scheduling rules governing alibi defenses. Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1; see Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400, 414-16 (1988). Exclusion of an untimely defense witness or theory is permissible where the violation is willful or prejudicial. Id. at 415-18. Pearson has not shown an abuse of discretion, much less prejudice, because he was able to testify to his whereabouts and present corroborating evidence from the jewelry store employee and receipt. See United States v. Wicker, 848 F.2d 1059, 1060-63 (10th Cir. 1988).

C. Sufficiency of the evidence

The evidence was more than sufficient under Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). The testimony of accomplices, if believed by the jury, is enough to sustain a conviction. United States v. Young, 954 F.2d 614, 619 (10th Cir. 1992); United States v. Magallanez, 408 F.3d 672, 682 (10th Cir. 2005). Here, Meyer and Martin provided detailed testimony placing Pearson in the planning and execution of the robbery. Starks identified Pearson as the unarmed robber. Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government, a rational jury could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. Jackson, 443 U.S. at 319; United States v. Delgado-Uribe, 363 F.3d 1077, 1081 (10th Cir. 2004).

D. Felony-murder instruction

Pearson has not shown instructional error. Jury instructions are reviewed as a whole, and reversal is warranted only if the charge, taken together, misled the jury or misstated the law. United States v. Lee, 54 F.3d 1534, 1538 (10th Cir. 1995). A defendant is entitled to an instruction only if it correctly states the law and is supported by the evidence. United States v. Joe, 8 F.3d 1488, 1499 (10th Cir. 1993). The district court’s felony-murder instruction tracked the governing law and was supported by the evidence that a homicide occurred during the commission of the robbery.

E. Lesser-included offense instructions

The district court also properly refused Pearson’s requested lesser-included offense instructions. Such an instruction is required only when the lesser offense is included within the greater, the evidence would permit a rational jury to convict of the lesser while acquitting of the greater, and the proof on the distinguishing element is sufficiently in dispute. Schmuck v. United States, 489 U.S. 705, 716-18 (1989); United States v. Duran, 127 F.3d 911, 914-15 (10th Cir. 1997). Pearson did not satisfy that standard. The evidence, if believed, established participation in the robbery and the resulting homicide; if disbelieved, it supported acquittal rather than a compromise verdict. See United States v. Chanthadara, 230 F.3d 1237, 1254-55 (10th Cir. 2000).

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
