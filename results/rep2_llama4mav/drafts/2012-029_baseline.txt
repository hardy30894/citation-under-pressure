The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-law legal malpractice claim because it necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. The Supreme Court has construed this language to extend to cases in which a state-law claim "necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law." Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988).

The Grable test, as articulated by the Supreme Court, provides a four-part inquiry to determine whether a state-law claim arises under federal jurisdiction: (1) whether resolving a federal issue is necessary to resolution of the state-law claim; (2) whether the federal issue is actually disputed; (3) whether the federal issue is substantial; and (4) whether federal jurisdiction will disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). 

Here, Minton's legal malpractice claim against Gunn necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, specifically the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). To prevail on his malpractice claim, Minton must prove that Gunn's negligence proximately caused his injury, which requires a determination of whether the experimental use exception would have applied to save Minton's patent from invalidity. See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). This determination is a necessary element of Minton's malpractice claim and is actually disputed between the parties.

The Federal Circuit has held that patent law issues, such as the experimental use exception, are substantial and contested in the context of a state-law malpractice claim. See Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed. Cir. 2007); Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007). Furthermore, the federal interest in maintaining uniformity in patent law supports the conclusion that federal jurisdiction is appropriate. See Grable, 545 U.S. at 314.

Finally, exercising federal jurisdiction over Minton's malpractice claim will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. The federal courts have a strong interest in resolving patent law issues, and Congress has vested them with exclusive jurisdiction over cases arising under federal patent law. See 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). Therefore, this Court should reverse the court of appeals' judgment and dismiss the case for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, as the Supreme Court of Texas correctly held.
