**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit’s June 12, 2007 Order Recalling the Mandate Restarted the 90-Day Clock for Filing a Petition for Certiorari, Rendering Buckles’s Petition Timely.**

A. Under Supreme Court Rule 13(3), the 90-day period for filing a petition for certiorari runs from the date of the denial of rehearing *unless* the lower court recalls the mandate. In *United States v. Robinson*, 361 U.S. 220, 221 (1960), the Supreme Court held that a mandate recall “operates to vacate the judgment and to restore the case to its position on the docket as though no judgment had been entered.” Thus, when this Court recalled the mandate on June 12, 2007, it reset the procedural posture of Buckles’s case, and the 90-day period for filing a petition for certiorari began anew. *See also* Sup. Ct. R. 13(3) (providing that the time to file a petition for certiorari runs from the date of the denial of rehearing *unless* the lower court recalls the mandate).

B. This Court has recognized that a mandate recall resets the appellate clock. In *United States v. Cote*, 45 F.3d 1314, 1316 (9th Cir. 1995), the court held that a mandate recall “restarts the time for filing a petition for certiorari.” The same principle applies here. Buckles’s petition for certiorari, filed on September 5, 2007—within 90 days of the mandate recall—was timely. *See also United States v. Smith*, 334 F.3d 780, 782 (9th Cir. 2003) (holding that a mandate recall resets the appellate deadlines).

**II. Buckles’s § 2255 Motion Was Timely Because It Was Filed Within One Year of the Supreme Court’s Denial of Certiorari.**

A. Under 28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)(1), a § 2255 motion must be filed within one year of “the date on which the judgment of conviction becomes final.” The Supreme Court’s denial of certiorari on October 15, 2007, rendered Buckles’s conviction final for purposes of § 2255. *See Clay v. United States*, 537 U.S. 522, 527 (2003) (“A state conviction and sentence become final for purposes of § 2255 when the availability of direct appeal to the state courts has been exhausted and the time for filing a petition for a writ of certiorari has elapsed or a timely petition has been filed and finally denied.”). Buckles filed his § 2255 motion on September 19, 2008—well within one year of October 15, 2007. *See also United States v. Espinoza*, 491 F.3d 1011, 1014 (9th Cir. 2007) (holding that the one-year limitations period under § 2255 begins to run from the date the Supreme Court denies certiorari).

**III. Buckles Is Entitled to Equitable Tolling Because the Ninth Circuit Clerk Misled Him About the Deadline for Filing His Petition for Certiorari.**

A. Equitable tolling is available where a petitioner has been misled by the court or where extraordinary circumstances beyond the petitioner’s control prevented timely filing. *See Irwin v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs*, 498 U.S. 89, 96 (1990) (“[E]quitable tolling is appropriate when a defendant has actively pursued his judicial remedies by filing a defective pleading during the statutory period, or where the plaintiff has been induced or tricked by his adversary’s misconduct into allowing the filing deadline to pass.”). In *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 418 (2005), the Supreme Court held that equitable tolling may apply where a petitioner reasonably relies on misrepresentations by court personnel.

B. Here, Buckles presented evidence that the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office informed his sister that the 90-day deadline would be “stayed” until the mandate was recalled. This misinformation directly caused Buckles to delay filing his petition for certiorari until after the original 90-day period had expired. Under these circumstances, equitable tolling is warranted. *See also Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 651-52 (2010) (holding that equitable tolling may apply where a petitioner’s attorney’s misconduct prevented timely filing); *United States v. Espinoza*, 491 F.3d at 1016 (applying equitable tolling where the petitioner was misled by court personnel).

C. Buckles’s diligence in pursuing his rights further supports equitable tolling. He filed his motion for appointment of counsel within the 21-day window under Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e) and repeatedly sought assistance from retained counsel. His efforts demonstrate that he acted reasonably and in good faith. *See Irwin*, 498 U.S. at 96 (equitable tolling is appropriate where the petitioner has diligently pursued his rights).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Buckles’s § 2255 motion was timely, and the district court erred in dismissing it as untimely. Alternatively, Buckles is entitled to equitable tolling based on the misinformation provided by the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office. The judgment of the district court should be reversed.
