The equal-footing doctrine, which “is the settled rule of law” that new states enter the Union “on an equal footing with the original States in all respects whatever,” vests title in the states to the beds of rivers that were navigable at the time of statehood. *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 223 (1845). Title to such beds “is not a matter of compact, but of right, and is vested in the State by operation of law.” *Id.* at 230. The doctrine applies to “all lands below high-water mark” of navigable waters. *Barney v. Keokuk*, 94 U.S. 324, 338 (1876).

Navigability for title purposes is a federal question determined by the condition of the river at the date of statehood. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870). A river is navigable in fact “when it is used, or is susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce.” *Id.* The test looks to “the natural and ordinary condition of the water” at statehood, not later improvements or changes. *United States v. Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co.*, 174 U.S. 690, 698 (1899). Evidence of use “at or near the time of statehood” is therefore controlling. *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113, 118 (1921).

The inquiry proceeds on a segment-by-segment basis. *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 585 (1922). A state acquires title only to those portions that “were navigable in fact” when the state was admitted. *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 55 (1926). Non-navigable segments remain subject to federal disposition. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894).

The same temporal standard governs the equal-footing inquiry for rivers that form state boundaries. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). Historical evidence of navigation “at the time of the admission of the State” is admissible and often decisive. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 15 (1935). Courts therefore examine contemporaneous records, not present-day conditions, to decide whether the relevant stretch satisfied the navigability test at statehood. *Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 77, 85 (1922).

Because the constitutional test requires a determination of navigability at the time of statehood, the judgment below correctly applied that standard to the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers.
