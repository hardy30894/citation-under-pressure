**ARGUMENT**

**I. California Law, Which Governs the Construction of the Amendment, Treats a Trustee's Discretion Limited by an Ascertainable Support Standard as an Enforceable Right to Income, Not a Mere Expectancy.**

Federal estate tax liability turns first on the property interests that state law creates. As the Supreme Court explained in *Morgan v. Commissioner*, 309 U.S. 78, 80 (1940), "State law creates legal interests and rights. The federal revenue acts designate what interests or rights, so created, shall be taxed." Because the Amendment is a California trust instrument, this Court must determine what interest Mrs. Davis holds under California law before asking whether that interest satisfies section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). *Commissioner v. Estate of Bosch*, 387 U.S. 456 (1967), instructs that a federal court adjudicating a tax controversy must give "proper regard" to the way the state's courts would construe the instrument, even where no controlling decision of the state's highest court exists on the precise facts. *Id.* at 465.

California courts have long held that when a settlor directs a trustee to distribute income "in its discretion" but tethers that discretion to an objective standard—health, support, maintenance, education, comfort, or an accustomed manner of living—the beneficiary's interest is not illusory. In *Estate of Marre*, 18 Cal. 2d 191 (1941), the California Supreme Court reaffirmed that the paramount rule of trust construction is to give effect to the settlor's intent as expressed in the instrument as a whole, not to seize on isolated words of discretion to defeat the evident purpose of providing for a named beneficiary. Applying that principle, *Estate of Ferrall*, 33 Cal. 2d 202 (1948), recognized that a trust for a beneficiary's support and maintenance vests the beneficiary with a real, judicially cognizable interest rather than a hope of bounty dependent on the trustee's unfettered whim. *Estate of Sargavak*, 41 Cal. 2d 314 (1953), went further, holding that where discretion is coupled with an ascertainable standard such as support and maintenance, courts will compel the trustee to make distributions consistent with that standard and will not permit the trustee to withhold income arbitrarily.

The Amendment here fits squarely within this line of authority. Paragraph 2 directs the trustee to pay "all of the net income from the trust estate" for Mrs. Davis's "health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare... in accordance with the surviving spouse's accustomed manner of living." That is precisely the kind of ascertainable, objective standard that California courts have construed as creating an enforceable entitlement to income, not a discretionary gratuity. Under *Marre*, *Ferrall*, and *Sargavak*, Mrs. Davis is "entitled to all the income" within the meaning of I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) because the trustee has no lawful power to withhold, accumulate, or divert that income once the ascertainable standard is met—the very same standard the settlor himself used before his remarriage, when the original 1993 Declaration of Trust made him unconditionally "entitled to all the net income."

This case is unlike those in which courts have denied the marital deduction because the trustee possessed genuinely open-ended discretion to accumulate income or divert it from the spouse altogether. In *Commissioner v. Ellis' Estate*, 252 F.2d 109 (3d Cir. 1958), and *Starrett v. Commissioner*, 223 F.2d 163 (1st Cir. 1955), the instruments permitted the trustee to withhold income from the spouse for reasons unconnected to any objective standard tied to her welfare, leaving the spouse's receipt of income entirely contingent on the trustee's independent judgment. Here, by contrast, the trustee's discretion is not independent of any standard; it is bounded by the very words the settlor used to describe what the spouse is owed.

**II. The Trustee's Narrow Power to Invade Principal Solely for Mrs. Davis's Benefit Does Not Defeat the Qualifying Income Interest.**

Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(II) asks only whether "no person has a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse." Paragraph 5 of the Amendment authorizes invasion of principal only "for the benefit of surviving spouse," and only after she has exhausted the assets of her own trust. No other person—not the daughters, not any remainder beneficiary—may receive principal during Mrs. Davis's lifetime under this provision.

The terminable interest rule embodied in section 2056(b) exists to ensure that property escaping estate tax in the first spouse's estate will be taxed in the survivor's estate, because it remains within the marital unit and under the survivor's effective control. *United States v. Stapf*, 375 U.S. 118, 128 (1963); *Jackson v. United States*, 376 U.S. 503, 510 (1964). A power to invade principal exclusively for the surviving spouse's own benefit does nothing to frustrate that purpose; it keeps the entire corpus available to be taxed, ultimately, in Mrs. Davis's estate. Because the Amendment channels every dollar of income and every permissible invasion of principal to Mrs. Davis alone, it satisfies both the letter and the animating purpose of section 2056(b)(7).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the Amendment gives Mrs. Davis a qualifying income interest for life, and the Tax Court's decision upholding the deficiency should be reversed.
