**Argument**

I. A state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may constitute cause to excuse the procedural default of an underlying constitutional claim.

Under *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), “the exhaustion doctrine . . . generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default.” *Id.* at 488-489. The Court there recognized that an ineffective-assistance claim, if itself defaulted, may nevertheless supply the requisite cause when the prisoner demonstrates that the default of the underlying claim resulted from counsel’s constitutionally deficient performance. *See id.* at 488.

*Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), confirms that a federal habeas court must determine whether a state procedural bar rests on an independent and adequate state ground. *Id.* at 735. Where the state court’s decision does not “clearly and expressly” rely on the procedural default, federal review of the merits remains available. *Id.* at 735-736. Nothing in *Coleman* requires that an ineffective-assistance claim offered as cause itself survive state procedural hurdles before it may excuse another default.

The Sixth Circuit correctly applied these principles. Carpenter’s sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was defaulted on direct appeal. His claim that appellate counsel rendered ineffective assistance by failing to raise that issue supplies cause, even though the ineffective-assistance claim was itself dismissed as untimely in state court. As *Murray* teaches, the purpose of the cause-and-prejudice inquiry is to determine whether the prisoner had a fair opportunity to present his federal claim; it does not demand that every subsidiary claim escape procedural default. 477 U.S. at 488.

*Terrell v. Morris*, 493 U.S. 1 (1989) (per curiam), illustrates the same point. There the Court permitted federal review of an ineffective-assistance claim that had been defaulted under a state rule that postdated the prisoner’s direct appeal. *Id.* at 2-3. The decision underscores that procedural defaults arising from counsel’s failure to anticipate changes in state law or to raise meritorious issues do not categorically bar federal consideration when those defaults themselves constitute the cause for another default.

The State’s contrary rule would erect an insurmountable barrier for prisoners whose appellate counsel defaulted both the underlying claim and the ineffective-assistance claim. Such a regime would undermine the very protection *Murray* and *Coleman* afford against fundamentally unfair state procedural bars. Because the court of appeals properly held that Carpenter’s defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could serve as cause, the judgment granting the writ should be affirmed.
