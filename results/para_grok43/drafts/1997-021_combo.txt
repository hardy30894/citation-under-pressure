The National Labor Relations Act protects employees' right to choose or reject bargaining representatives free from employer coercion. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33 (1937). An incumbent union therefore enjoys only a rebuttable presumption of continued majority support. Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944). That presumption may be overcome by objective evidence sufficient to create a reasonable doubt of majority status. Brooks v. NLRB, 348 U.S. 96, 104 (1954). When such doubt exists, an employer may test employee sentiment by poll, provided the poll is conducted under safeguards that prevent coercion. NLRB v. A.J. Tower Co., 329 U.S. 324, 330 (1946).

The Board's requirement that an employer possess evidence sufficient to justify outright withdrawal of recognition before it may poll imposes a standard that effectively nullifies the statutory right to ascertain employee wishes. International Ass'n of Machinists v. NLRB, 311 U.S. 72, 79 (1940). An employer that already possesses evidence justifying withdrawal has no need to poll; the poll therefore serves no purpose the Act contemplates. NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co., 306 U.S. 292, 297 (1939). The Board's rule thus conflicts with the Act's policy of permitting employers to resolve genuine doubts without committing an unfair labor practice. NLRB v. P. Lorillard Co., 314 U.S. 512, 513 (1942).

The same inconsistency appears in the Board's refusal to credit statements made during hiring interviews or reports by employees concerning the views of others. Such statements, when uncontradicted, constitute objective evidence of loss of support. NLRB v. Virginia Elec. & Power Co., 314 U.S. 469, 477 (1941). The Board may not disregard them without explanation. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Here the record contains multiple unrefuted expressions of opposition from unit employees, together with statements that the entire night shift and service department rejected continued representation. That evidence, taken as a whole, satisfies the reasonable-doubt standard. Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938).

Because the Board's evidentiary standard is irrational and its factual finding lacks substantial support in the record, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
