**ARGUMENT**

**I. Federal Courts Do Not Possess Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Legal Malpractice Claims That Merely Require Application of Patent Law.**

The "arising under" jurisdiction of the federal courts, as defined in 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), extends only to cases where federal patent law creates the cause of action or where the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. This Court has long held that "a suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws." *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Rather, the federal question must be "real and substantial" and not merely incidental. *Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936). The well-pleaded complaint rule requires that the federal issue appear on the face of the plaintiff's complaint, not as a defense or as an element of a state-law claim. *Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908).

Here, Minton's legal malpractice claim is a creature of state law. The complaint alleges that Gunn's negligence caused Minton to lose his patent infringement suit. The patent issue—whether the experimental use exception applies—is merely an element of the causation inquiry. It does not create the cause of action, nor does it present a "real and substantial" dispute over the validity or construction of federal patent law. *See American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916) (holding that a case arises under patent law only when the plaintiff's right to relief depends on the construction or application of the patent laws). Minton's claim depends on whether Gunn acted negligently, not on whether the patent is valid or infringed. The patent issue is a backdrop, not the centerpiece.

**II. The Patent Issue in This Case Is Not Substantial.**

This Court has consistently held that federal jurisdiction is proper only when the federal issue is "substantial" in the sense that its resolution is necessary to the case and the federal interest is "real and weighty." *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180, 199 (1921). In *Hopkins v. Walker*, 244 U.S. 486, 490 (1917), the Court stated that "the mere fact that a state court must decide a federal question does not give the federal courts jurisdiction." The federal issue must be "the real substance of the controversy." *Id.* The experimental use exception is a narrow, fact-specific defense to the on-sale bar. Its resolution in this malpractice case will not affect the validity of Minton's patent or create binding precedent for future patent cases. It will only determine whether Gunn's negligence caused Minton's loss. This is precisely the type of incidental federal question that does not confer federal jurisdiction. *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117-18.

**III. Exercising Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance Between State and Federal Courts.**

Congress has carefully limited federal patent jurisdiction to cases that "really and substantially involve" a dispute over patent law. *Pratt v. Paris Gaslight & Coke Co.*, 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897). State courts have traditionally adjudicated legal malpractice claims, even those requiring reference to federal law. Allowing federal courts to assert exclusive jurisdiction over every state malpractice claim that touches on patent law would flood the federal docket and undermine the role of state courts in resolving professional negligence disputes. This Court has long recognized the importance of maintaining the "constitutional balance" between state and federal judicial responsibilities. *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. 264, 392 (1821).

**IV. The Federal Issue Is Not "Actually Disputed" in the Relevant Sense.**

Even assuming the experimental use exception is a federal issue, it is not "actually disputed" in the sense required for federal jurisdiction. The dispute in this case is whether Gunn's negligence caused Minton's loss—a question of state law. The patent issue is merely a predicate fact, not a contested legal question that requires federal resolution. *See Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117-18 (holding that federal jurisdiction does not arise from "every question of federal law that may be involved in a case"). The parties do not dispute the meaning of the experimental use exception; they dispute whether Gunn should have raised it. That is a question of professional negligence, not patent law.

**V. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings in state court.
