**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE EXECUTION OF MENTALLY RETARDED OFFENDERS DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment must be interpreted according to “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). This Court has consistently held that the “clearest and most reliable objective evidence of contemporary values is the legislation enacted by the country’s legislatures.” *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 331 (1989) (quoting *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 370 (1989)). In *Penry*, this Court expressly considered whether executing mentally retarded offenders was cruel and unusual, and concluded it was not. *Id.* at 340. That holding remains sound and should not be disturbed.

**A. There is No National Consensus Against Executing Mentally Retarded Offenders.**

At the time of *Penry*, only two states (Georgia and Maryland) prohibited the execution of mentally retarded offenders. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 334. Today, only a minority of states have enacted such bans. This lack of legislative action demonstrates that a national consensus has not crystallized. *Cf. Stanford*, 492 U.S. at 370 (looking to “objective indicia that reflect the public attitude toward a given sanction”). Many state legislatures have considered and rejected categorical bars. Moreover, the federal government, which enacted the Federal Death Penalty Act in 1994, did not include a prohibition on executing mentally retarded offenders. See 18 U.S.C. §§ 3591-3598. This silence is telling. As this Court recognized, “the issue is not whether the *Court* believes that executing mentally retarded offenders is a good idea; the issue is whether the *American people* have made that judgment.” *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 335 (emphasis in original). The record reflects no such judgment.

**B. Mental Retardation is a Mitigating Factor, Not a Categorical Bar.**

This Court has long held that the Eighth Amendment requires “individualized sentencing” in capital cases, not categorical exclusions based on a single characteristic. *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978). *Penry* itself held that a jury must be allowed to consider mental retardation as a mitigating factor, but it did not hold that the condition automatically renders a defendant ineligible for the death penalty. 492 U.S. at 340. To the contrary, the Court observed that “mentally retarded persons are not all the same,” and that many possess sufficient cognitive capacity to understand the wrongfulness of their actions and to be held fully accountable. *Id.* at 338-339.

The sentencing scheme in Virginia fully satisfies this requirement. The jury heard expert testimony regarding Atkins’ mental retardation and was instructed that they could consider it as a mitigating circumstance. The jury nonetheless imposed a death sentence. This individualized determination is precisely what the Eighth Amendment demands. *See Jurek v. Texas*, 428 U.S. 262, 276 (1976) (holding that the death penalty may be constitutional when the sentencer considers “the character and record of the individual offender”).

**C. *Penry* Did Not Establish a Per Se Rule and Should Not Be Extended.**

*Penry* squarely rejected the argument that mental retardation, “like insanity, . . . thus renders one unable to form the *mens rea* necessary to be held culpable for a capital crime.” 492 U.S. at 336. This Court further noted that “mental retardation is a factor that may diminish an individual’s culpability but does not do so in all cases.” *Id.* at 338. To adopt the categorical rule urged by petitioner would usurp the role of state legislatures and the jury. It would also ignore the reality that some mentally retarded offenders, like Atkins, commit brutal, premeditated murders that merit the ultimate penalty.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require that every offender with diminished capacity be exempt from execution. In *Thompson v. Oklahoma*, 487 U.S. 815, 823 (1988), this Court struck down the death penalty for offenders under 16, but in *Stanford*, the Court upheld it for 16- and 17-year-olds, emphasizing that “it is for legislatures to decide whether such a prohibition is appropriate.” 492 U.S. at 378. The same deference applies here. The Virginia legislature has not chosen to ban the execution of mentally retarded offenders, and no constitutional imperative compels it to do so.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed. The Eighth Amendment does not bar the execution of mentally retarded offenders, and *Penry v. Lynaugh* controls. The jury in this case properly considered Atkins’ mental retardation as a mitigating factor and, after a full hearing, concluded that death was the appropriate sentence. This Court should decline petitioner’s invitation to replace legislative judgment and jury discretion with a categorical constitutional rule.
