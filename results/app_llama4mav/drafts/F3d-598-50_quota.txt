ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Admitting Prejudicial and Erroneous Testimony from the ADAs and Police Lieutenant

The District Court's admission of testimony from the two Assistant District Attorneys ("ADAs") and a police lieutenant, in which they vouched for the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, opined on the existence of probable cause, and interpreted evidence, was reversible error. It is well-established that witnesses may not vouch for the credibility of other witnesses. United States v. Scop, 846 F.2d 135, 142 (2d Cir. 1988) ("It is not for witnesses to tell the jury whom to believe."). The ADAs' and lieutenant's testimony directly contravened this principle, and their statements were not merely "helpful" or "relevant" but rather an impermissible attempt to influence the jury's assessment of Ramos's and Rivera's credibility. See United States v. Forrester, 60 F.3d 52, 63 (2d Cir. 1995) (holding that a witness's testimony that another witness was "very credible" was improper).

Moreover, the witnesses' opinions on probable cause were inadmissible as legal conclusions. See Hygh v. Jacobs, 961 F.2d 359, 363 (2d Cir. 1992) ("[T]he ultimate legal conclusion is a matter to be determined by the court."). The existence of probable cause is a question for the jury to decide based on the facts presented, not for witnesses to opine on. See Ricciuti v. N.Y.C. Transit Auth., 124 F.3d 123, 128 (2d Cir. 1997) (holding that a witness's testimony on probable cause was "inadmissible as a legal conclusion").

Furthermore, the witnesses' interpretation of evidence was also improper, as it invaded the province of the jury to analyze and weigh the evidence. See United States v. Bilzerian, 926 F.2d 1285, 1294 (2d Cir. 1991) ("[A] witness's opinion as to the meaning or significance of evidence is generally inadmissible."). The jury was capable of evaluating the evidence on its own, and the witnesses' testimony was not necessary to assist the jury in understanding the evidence. See id.

The admission of this testimony was not harmless error. The credibility of Ramos and Rivera was a central issue in the case, and the testimony of the ADAs and lieutenant directly bolstered their credibility. See United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 18-19 (1985) (holding that the admission of improper testimony was not harmless error where it "may have affected the judgment of the jury"). The testimony also spoke directly to the issue of probable cause, which was a critical element of Appellants' claims. See Ricciuti, 124 F.3d at 128.

II. The District Court Should Have Instructed the Jury on the Lawful Arrest Requirement for Obstructing Governmental Administration

Appellants argue that the District Court erred in failing to instruct the jury that, under New York law, the offense of obstructing governmental administration requires that the obstructed officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. This argument is supported by New York case law, which holds that a lawful arrest is an essential element of the offense. See People v. Lupinacci, 191 A.D.2d 589, 590 (N.Y. App. Div. 1993) ("To sustain a charge of obstructing governmental administration, there must be evidence that the defendant intentionally obstructed, impaired or perverted the administration of law or other governmental function being performed by a public servant."). The District Court's failure to so instruct the jury was error, and on remand, the jury should be instructed accordingly.

III. Appellants Were Entitled to a Punitive Damages Instruction

Appellants argue that their version of events, if believed by the jury, would have allowed a jury to infer that Ramos and Rivera acted with malice, entitling them to a punitive damages instruction. Under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, punitive damages are available where the defendant's conduct is "shown to be motivated by evil motive or intent, or when it involves reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others." Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). Appellants' allegations, if credited by the jury, could support a finding of malice or reckless indifference. See Lee v. Edwards, 101 F.3d 805, 808 (2d Cir. 1996) (holding that a jury could infer malice from the defendant's actions). On remand, the District Court should instruct the jury on punitive damages.

In conclusion, the District Court's errors in admitting prejudicial testimony and failing to properly instruct the jury require that the judgment be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
