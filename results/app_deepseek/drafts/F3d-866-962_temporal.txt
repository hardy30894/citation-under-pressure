**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine disputes of material fact exist as to whether BNSF retaliated against appellants for reporting unsafe working conditions. Summary judgment is particularly disfavored where, as here, motive and intent are central issues. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (“summary procedures should be used sparingly” where “motivation for the conduct of the parties is crucial”).

**I. The District Court Erred in Finding No Causal Connection Between Appellants’ Protected Activity and BNSF’s Adverse Actions.**

Appellants engaged in protected activity under 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C) when they reported the unsafe condition of the bridge—lacking side rails and lighting—immediately after coworker John Moore fell. Within days, BNSF initiated an investigation into rule infractions, subjected appellants to increased operations testing, and ultimately disciplined them, including terminating Foster. The close temporal proximity between the protected reports and the adverse actions supports an inference of retaliation. *Radio Officers’ Union v. NLRB*, 347 U.S. 17, 52 (1954) (circumstantial evidence of discriminatory motive may be drawn from timing and surrounding circumstances).

BNSF’s claim that it disciplined appellants solely for rule violations is contradicted by the record. Appellants testified that company officials instructed them to change crews west of the bridge—the very location where Moore fell. The investigating officers, Knutstrom and Dixon, claimed the crew-change point was east of the bridge, but no written instruction existed. This factual dispute goes directly to whether BNSF’s proffered reason was pretextual. *NLRB v. Great Dane Trailers, Inc.*, 388 U.S. 26, 34 (1967) (employer’s conduct may be inherently discriminatory if it undermines protected activity).

**II. The District Court Erred in Concluding That BNSF’s Legitimate Reasons Were Not Pretextual.**

Even assuming BNSF articulated a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason for its actions, appellants presented sufficient evidence that the reason was pretextual. The record shows that BNSF subjected appellants to heightened scrutiny—increased operations testing—after they reported the unsafe bridge. Kline and Snyder testified that they felt compelled to avoid inexperienced coworkers and accepted less lucrative assignments out of fear of discipline. Such evidence of disparate treatment and heightened surveillance supports a finding of pretext. *NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221, 227 (1963) (employer’s actions that “inherently discourage” protected activity may be found retaliatory even if employer offers a business justification).

Moreover, the investigative hearing itself was tainted. The same officials who accused appellants of rule infractions—Dixon and Knutstrom—served as witnesses, and the hearing officer relied on their testimony despite appellants’ contrary accounts. The subsequent decision by the Public Law Board to reinstate Foster, remove Snyder’s reprimand, and remove Kline’s suspension further undermines BNSF’s claim that the discipline was warranted. While the arbitration decision is not binding on this Court, it is evidence that BNSF’s stated reasons lacked merit. *Cf. Poller*, 368 U.S. at 473 (summary judgment improper where evidence presents “a typical jury question” of credibility).

**III. The District Court Improperly Weighed Evidence and Credibility.**

The district court resolved factual disputes in BNSF’s favor, concluding that the discipline was justified and that the increased testing was not retaliatory. But on summary judgment, the court must view all evidence in the light most favorable to appellants. *Poller*, 368 U.S. at 473. Appellants’ testimony that they were told to stop west of the bridge directly contradicts BNSF’s version. The court cannot credit BNSF’s witnesses over appellants’ at this stage. *Radio Officers’ Union*, 347 U.S. at 52 (inferences of discriminatory motive must be drawn from the totality of circumstances).

Because genuine issues of material fact exist as to whether BNSF’s adverse actions were motivated by appellants’ protected reports, summary judgment was improper. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
