**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and the judgment below must be reversed. The record in this case presents substantial evidence of Richard Allan Moran’s mental incapacity at the time he discharged his counsel and pleaded guilty—evidence that triggered a “good faith doubt” about his competency to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of fundamental constitutional rights. Under settled precedent, such a doubt mandates a hearing to assess the defendant’s capacity for “reasoned choice,” not merely a bare rational understanding of the proceedings.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the waiver of constitutional rights—particularly the right to counsel and the right to a jury trial—must be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243, 89 S.Ct. 1709, 1712, 23 L.Ed.2d 274 (1969). This standard is not satisfied by a defendant’s mere ability to understand the charges or the potential penalties. Rather, when a defendant seeks to waive counsel and plead guilty, the law demands more: a capacity for “reasoned choice” among the alternatives available. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir.1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871, 109 S.Ct. 182, 102 L.Ed.2d 151 (1988); *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir.1986) (hereinafter *Evans II*); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir.1981). This higher standard reflects the gravity of the decisions involved and the need to protect against coerced or irrational waivers.

The distinction between competency to stand trial and competency to waive constitutional rights is constitutionally significant. A defendant is competent to stand trial if he has “a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings against him” and can “assist counsel in his own defense.” *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 95 S.Ct. 896, 904, 43 L.Ed.2d 103 (1975) (quoting *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402, 80 S.Ct. 788, 788, 4 L.Ed.2d 824 (1960) (per curiam)). But this standard is insufficient for waiver of counsel or a guilty plea. As the Ninth Circuit correctly observed, “competency to waive constitutional rights requires a higher level of mental functioning than that required to stand trial.” *Evans I*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir.1973); *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518; *United States v. Aponte*, 591 F.2d 1247, 1249 (9th Cir.1978). The “reasoned choice” standard demands that the defendant understand not only the nature of the proceedings but also the consequences of his decisions, including the strategic implications of waiving counsel and refusing mitigating evidence.

Here, the record reveals multiple red flags that should have triggered a competency hearing. Moran had recently attempted suicide, slashing his wrists and shooting himself in the abdomen. He was still recovering from this trauma when he appeared before the court. *See* *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir.1973) (holding that a suicide attempt is “substantial evidence of incompetence” that may require a hearing). Moreover, at the time of the plea, Moran was taking four psychotropic medications: Inderal, Dilantin, Phenobarbitol, and Vistaril—drugs that affect cognition, motor function, and emotional stability. *See* *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (noting that medication use may impair a defendant’s ability to make reasoned choices). Despite Moran’s own admission that he was on medication, the court made no inquiry into its effects. This failure to investigate is fatal under *Evans I*, where the court held that “a good faith doubt about competency should arise if there is substantial evidence of incompetence.” 705 F.2d at 1480.

The trial court’s acceptance of Moran’s waiver was based on monosyllabic responses to leading questions. This is not the hallmark of a reasoned choice. *See* *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518 (holding that a defendant’s “incoherent or monosyllabic responses” to questions about his rights are insufficient to establish a knowing waiver). The court also accepted Moran’s statement that he wanted to prevent any mitigating evidence from being presented—a decision that, if made rationally, would be profoundly self-incriminating. Yet the record shows no indication that Moran understood the strategic consequences of this choice. His behavior was inconsistent with the kind of deliberate, informed decision-making required by due process.

The state court’s postconviction ruling, which relied solely on psychiatric evaluations conducted to determine competency to stand trial, is legally inadequate. *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172, 95 S.Ct. at 904. The reports of Dr. O’Gorman and Dr. Jurasky were not designed to assess the “reasoned choice” standard. They focused on Moran’s ability to understand the charges and assist counsel—precisely the lower standard that does not govern waiver of rights. *See* *Evans I*, 705 F.2d at 1480. The state court’s failure to apply the correct legal standard renders its finding unenforceable under federal habeas review
