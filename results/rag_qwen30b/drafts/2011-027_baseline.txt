**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The equal footing doctrine, a foundational principle of federal constitutional law, mandates that a state acquires title to the beds of navigable waters within its borders at the precise moment it enters the Union. This principle is not subject to post-statehood inquiry or retrospective reevaluation. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s admission to the Union in 1889, and thus the State obtained title to their beds under the equal footing doctrine.

The doctrine’s origins are deeply rooted in the structure of American federalism. As this Court explained in *Utah Division of State Lands v. United States*, “Because all subsequently admitted States enter the Union on an ‘equal footing’ with the original 13 States, they too hold title to the land under navigable waters within their boundaries upon entry into the Union.” 482 U.S. 193, 200 (1987). This principle is not a matter of state law or policy, but a constitutional command. The United States holds submerged lands in the Territories “in trust” for the future States that will be created, and upon statehood, title passes to the new State as a matter of right. *Id.* at 200–01. The moment of statehood is the critical juncture. As this Court emphasized in *Idaho v. United States*, “It is therefore improper for the Court to look to events after Idaho’s admission in order to discern whether Congress had months or years previously intended to divest the entering State of its submerged lands.” 533 U.S. 262, 275 (2001). The same logic applies here: the State of Montana’s title to the riverbeds at issue was determined at the moment of statehood in 1889.

The Court’s decision in *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.* confirms that the determination of navigability for title purposes is a historical inquiry focused on the condition of the waterway at the time of statehood. There, the Court held that the New River’s navigability was a question of fact to be determined by the district court, based on evidence of its use in commerce at the time of the relevant state’s admission. 311 U.S. 377, 385 (1941). The Court did not require a showing of continuous navigability or modern use, but rather an assessment of whether the river was “navigable” in the constitutional sense—capable of being used for commerce—when the state joined the Union. The same standard applies in Montana. The State’s evidence, including historical records, maps, and accounts of commercial activity on the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers in the 1880s, was properly admitted under Montana’s rules of evidence and established that these rivers were navigable at the time of statehood.

PPL’s argument that the State must prove navigability at the time of the dam’s construction or operation is fundamentally flawed. The equal footing doctrine does not permit a state to lose title to submerged lands simply because a private entity later develops them. As this Court held in *Oregon Ex Rel. State Land Board v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co.*, “The issue before us is not what rights the State has accorded private [land] owners in lands which the State holds as sovereign; but, rather, how far the State’s sovereign right extends under the equal-footing doctrine and the Submerged Lands Act—whether the State retains title to the lands formerly beneath the stream of the Colorado River or whether that title is defeasible by the withdrawal of those waters.” 429 U.S. 363, 370 (1977). The doctrine protects the State’s title against erosion by private use or even by natural changes in the river’s course. The fact that PPL’s dams were constructed decades after statehood does not alter the State’s ownership of the riverbeds at the time of statehood.

Moreover, the Federal Power Act does not preempt state title claims. The Court has consistently held that the FPA “recognizes the validity of state property rights and contemplates compensation for property use under state law.” *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Commn.*, 328 U.S. 152, 175–76 (1946). The FPA’s licensing scheme requires applicants to comply with state law regarding “beds and banks and to the appropriation, diversion, and use of water for power purposes,” 16 U.S.C. § 802, and explicitly preserves state ownership of submerged lands. The FPA does not “occupy the field” of property rights, nor does it conflict with state claims for compensation. *See* *Vitullo v. Int. Bhd. of Elec. Workers*, 2003 MT 219, ¶ 14, 317 Mont. 142, 75 P.3d 1250. The State’s claim under the Hydroelectric Resources Act is not preempted; it is a valid exercise of its sovereign authority over lands it owns by virtue of statehood.

In sum, the Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes. The State’s title to the riverbeds of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers vested at the moment of Montana’s admission to the Union in 1889, based on the navigability of those rivers at that time. The trial court’s findings were supported by substantial evidence, and the legal principles governing the equal footing doctrine are firmly established in this Court’s precedent. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
