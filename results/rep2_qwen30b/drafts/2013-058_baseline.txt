**ARGUMENT**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA does not qualify as “retirement funds” under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code, and thus is not exempt from the debtor’s estate. The Court of Appeals’ interpretation is compelled by the plain language of the statute, the statutory context, and the fundamental purpose of the bankruptcy exemption: to protect funds *intended for the debtor’s own retirement*, not assets that have been stripped of their retirement function upon inheritance.

### I. The Bankruptcy Code Exempts Only Funds That Serve the Debtor’s Retirement, Not Those That Are Merely Tax-Deferred

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The key inquiry is not whether the funds were once retirement savings, but whether they *remain* retirement funds in the hands of the current owner. The Seventh Circuit correctly recognized that “retirement funds” is not a term of art defined by the Bankruptcy Code, but its meaning must be derived from context and purpose.

The Internal Revenue Code treats inherited IRAs as *time-limited tax-deferral vehicles*, not retirement savings vehicles. Unlike a traditional IRA, which is designed to preserve wealth for the account holder’s post-employment years, an inherited IRA must be distributed within a fixed period—typically five years or over the beneficiary’s life expectancy—beginning no later than one year after the original owner’s death. 26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A). The beneficiary cannot contribute to the account, cannot roll it over, and cannot use it to fund their own retirement. The funds are not “held for retirement” in any meaningful sense; they are *consumption assets* that must be drawn down *before* the beneficiary reaches retirement age.

This distinction is critical. As the Seventh Circuit observed, “distribution precedes the owner’s retirement.” If the funds were truly retirement savings, they would be protected until the beneficiary retires. But they are not. The very structure of the inherited IRA—mandatory distributions, no rollover, no future contributions—demonstrates that the account is no longer a vehicle for retirement savings. It is a mechanism to *accelerate taxation* on pre-tax dollars that passed from one generation to the next.

### II. The Statutory Framework Does Not Support a Retroactive or Atemporal Interpretation

The Clarks argue that because the funds were once “retirement funds” in Ruth Heffron’s hands, they remain exempt under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the statute does not exempt assets based on their *past* status. The exemption applies to funds that are *currently* retirement funds. The Seventh Circuit correctly rejected the notion that “retirement funds” is a permanent label that survives transfer. As the Court explained, “the money used to be ‘retirement funds’ but isn’t now.” Just as a house that was once a homestead is not exempt if the debtor no longer lives in it, an IRA that no longer serves the purpose of retirement savings is not exempt.

Second, the Clarks’ interpretation would render the exemption meaningless in the context of inherited assets. If any asset that was once retirement savings—whether an IRA, a pension, or even a 401(k) passed by will—were exempt regardless of the beneficiary’s use, then the exemption would apply to *any* bequest. That would allow debtors to shield from creditors any inheritance that once served a retirement purpose, even if the beneficiary has no intention of using it for retirement. This would undermine the entire purpose of the exemption: to protect *current* retirement savings from being seized to pay pre-bankruptcy debts.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to the homestead exemption is instructive. Under §522(b)(3)(A), a debtor may exempt their “domicile” only if they have lived there for at least 730 days before filing. A debtor cannot claim a house as exempt simply because it was once their mother’s home. The exemption depends on *current use*, not *past ownership*. The same principle applies here: the exemption depends on whether the funds are *currently* retirement funds, not whether they once were.

### III. The Fifth Circuit’s Decision in *Chilton* Is Wrong and Creates an Unworkable Rule

The Clarks rely on *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), which held that inherited IRAs are exempt because they are “retirement funds” under the Internal Revenue Code. But *Chilton* misreads the statute by equating “retirement fund” with “account that is tax-deferred under the IRC.” That is not what the Bankruptcy Code says. The Code requires *both* tax deferral *and* the status of a retirement fund. The Fifth Circuit ignored the functional reality that an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund in the beneficiary’s hands.

Moreover, *Chilton* fails to account for the legislative intent behind the exemption. Congress enacted §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) to protect funds that are *intended* for the debtor’s retirement. As the Seventh Circuit noted, “Congress has decided that funds set aside for retirement need not be used to pay pre-retirement debts.” This policy is undermined if the exemption applies to assets that are *not* set aside for the debtor’s retirement, but instead are *required to be spent* before retirement.

### IV. The Conflict Among the Circuits Is Not a Reason to Uphold the Seventh Circuit’s Decision—It Is a Reason to Reconsider the Law

The Clarks argue that the circuit split justifies affirming the Seventh Circuit’s decision. But the conflict is not about whether inherited IRAs are exempt—it is about whether the exemption should be interpreted *atemporally*. The Seventh Circuit’s decision is correct: the exemption applies only to funds that serve the debtor’s retirement. The conflict exists because some circuits have adopted a retroactive interpretation that ignores the functional purpose of the exemption.
