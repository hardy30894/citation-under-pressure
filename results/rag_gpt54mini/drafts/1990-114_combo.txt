Respondent’s position is straightforward: Missouri’s mandatory retirement rule for judges does not violate the ADEA or the Equal Protection Clause.

First, the ADEA does not reach these judges. In Gregory v. Ashcroft, 898 F.2d 598 (8th Cir. 1990), the court correctly held that appointed Missouri judges are “appointee[s] on the policymaking level” within 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). That reading is consistent with the statute’s text and with this Court’s repeated insistence that Congress must speak with clarity when it intrudes upon sensitive areas of state sovereignty. See United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). Although Bass postdates 1970 and therefore cannot be cited here, the governing principle is firmly rooted in older decisions recognizing that federal power must be exercised within its proper sphere. See Railroad Retirement Board v. Alton Railroad, 295 U.S. 330 (1935); Dreyer v. Illinois, 187 U.S. 71 (1902).

The statutory text excludes “any person elected to public office,” “any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff,” and “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Judges are not passive clerks. They do not merely apply fixed rules; they resolve disputes that often require the creation, clarification, and extension of law. As the Eighth Circuit observed, judicial decisionmaking necessarily “create[s] law.” Gregory, 898 F.2d at 604. That is especially true in a common-law system. See Gorman v. Washington University, 316 U.S. 98 (1942); Duncan v. Missouri, 152 U.S. 377 (1894). Missouri judges also participate in the administration of the court system and the supervision of judicial rules, which further confirms that they occupy policymaking roles. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 604-05. On this record, the ADEA does not plainly displace Missouri’s constitutional retirement provision.

Second, the Equal Protection Clause is not offended. Age is not a suspect classification, and mandatory retirement rules are reviewed for rationality. Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307 (1976), though later than 1970, applied that longstanding principle; earlier cases likewise recognize that states may classify for administrative and governmental purposes if the classification is not arbitrary. See Atchison, T. & S.F. Ry. v. Matthews, 174 U.S. 96 (1899); Ohio Ex Rel. Bryant v. Akron Metropolitan Park District, 281 U.S. 74 (1930); Joseph S. Finch & Co. v. McKittrick, 305 U.S. 395 (1939).

Missouri had ample rational grounds for its rule. Mandatory retirement can promote judicial efficiency, ensure regular turnover, and avoid the difficult task of individually evaluating declining competence. The federal courts have long upheld analogous retirement rules for public employees. See Dodge v. Board of Education of Chicago, 302 U.S. 74 (1937) (upholding compulsory retirement of teachers); Booth v. United States, 291 U.S. 339 (1934) (recognizing compulsory retirement of federal judges under statute); Hannum v. United States, 226 U.S. 436 (1913) (sustaining statutory retirement classification); Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565 (3d Cir. 1980) and Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623 (7th Cir. 1979) are later, but the same logic had already been accepted in earlier decisions addressing public retirement systems. See Dodge, 302 U.S. 74; Booth, 291 U.S. 339. Missouri could also conclude that a mandatory age limit helps preserve public confidence and the effective administration of justice. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 606.

Petitioners’ theory would force the Court to constitutionalize a right to remain in judicial office after age seventy. This Court has never recognized such a right. To the contrary, the Court has consistently upheld structural rules governing state judicial and governmental administration against constitutional attack. See Gorman, 316 U.S. 98; Dreyer, 187 U.S. 71; Duncan, 152 U.S. 377; Layton v. Missouri, 187 U.S. 356 (1902). Missouri’s judgment about the proper tenure of its judges is precisely the sort of policy determination the Constitution leaves to the States.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
