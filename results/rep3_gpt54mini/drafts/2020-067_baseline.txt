**ARGUMENT**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) permits relief only for “plain error” that “affect[s] substantial rights,” and even then only when the court of appeals concludes that the error “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732, 736 (1993). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that plain-error review is not a license for appellate factfinding untethered to the trial record. Rather, the defendant bears the burden to show prejudice on the record before the appellate court. *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 466–67 (1997); *Puckett v. United States*, 556 U.S. 129, 135 (2009).

I. **Plain-error prejudice is assessed from the record, not from extra-record speculation.**

The substantial-rights inquiry asks whether the error affected the outcome of the proceedings. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. That necessarily requires a reviewing court to evaluate what happened in the district court, not what might be discovered later. Appellate review is “confined to the record” developed below. *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 75 (2004) (rejecting appellate reliance on materials outside the trial record). And this Court has long recognized that “appellate courts are courts of review, not first view.” *Cutter v. Wilkinson*, 544 U.S. 709, 718 n.7 (2005).

Greer’s position would invert that principle. He asks the court of appeals to disregard the trial record’s obvious significance and to resolve prejudice by looking outside it. But plain-error review does not authorize a defendant to supplement a silent record with new materials on appeal. To the contrary, when the record is silent or incomplete, the party seeking relief loses. See *Molina-Martinez v. United States*, 578 U.S. 189, 200–01 (2016) (defendant must show a reasonable probability of a different result); *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 81–83 (2004) (same).

That is especially true where, as here, the omitted fact is uncontested and overwhelming. In *Rehaif v. United States*, 588 U.S. 176, 191–92 (2019), this Court held that the Government must prove knowledge of status in § 922(g) prosecutions. But *Rehaif* did not alter the ordinary rules of appellate review. On plain-error review, the defendant must show that the record gives reason to believe he would have presented a viable ignorance-of-status defense. *Greer v. United States*, 593 U.S. 503, 508–09 (2021). If the record reveals prior felony convictions, that is powerful evidence that the defendant knew he was a felon. *Id.* at 509–10.

II. **The Eleventh Circuit properly considered the whole record and Greer’s prior convictions.**

This Court’s decision in *Greer* itself answers the question presented. The Court held that, in assessing prejudice and the fourth prong of plain-error review after *Rehaif*, appellate courts may consider “the entire record,” not merely the evidence actually admitted at trial. *Greer*, 593 U.S. at 510–11. The Court explained that “a defendant faces an uphill climb in trying to satisfy the substantial-rights prong” because “a jury would have heard evidence that Greer was a felon.” *Id.* at 510. The Court further held that courts may consider information from the record showing prior felony convictions and related admissions because such evidence bears directly on whether the error affected the outcome or the fairness of the proceedings. *Id.* at 511–12.

That approach follows settled plain-error doctrine. In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 59, 74–75 (2002), the Court held that, when reviewing a Rule 11 error for plain error, appellate courts may consult the whole record, not just the plea colloquy. The same logic applies here. Plain-error review is a discretionary, record-based inquiry into actual prejudice and fairness, and appellate courts may look beyond one isolated trial event when the full record reveals the absence of prejudice. *Puckett*, 556 U.S. at 135–36; *Vonn*, 535 U.S. at 74–75.

III. **Greer suffered no prejudice, and the fairness of the proceedings was not compromised.**

A defendant cannot show effect on substantial rights when the record contains overwhelming evidence that he knew of his felon status. *Greer*, 593 U.S. at 512–13. The Court observed that “if a person is a felon, he ordinarily knows he is a felon.” *Id.* at 508. And where the defendant had been convicted of multiple felonies and served significant prison time, it is implausible that the *Rehaif* error changed the verdict. *Id.* at 510–13.

That same reasoning defeats Greer’s effort to obtain relief on the fourth prong. Plain-error correction is reserved for errors that seriously undermine the justice system’s legitimacy. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736; *Puckett*, 556 U.S. at 135. Reversing a conviction despite undisputed felony history and a record showing no plausible ignorance-of-status defense would do the opposite: it would “subvert the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. Greer therefore cannot satisfy plain-error review.

The judgment below should be affirmed.
