The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case misinterprets the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act's (OCSLA) choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The court below held that state law applies to the Outer Continental Shelf (OCS) as long as it is "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law, without requiring a gap in federal law. However, this interpretation ignores the statutory text and relevant case law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision declares that state law applies to the OCS "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Supreme Court has not directly addressed the issue presented here, but the Fifth Circuit has developed a test for determining when state law applies under the OCSLA. In Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners' Mutual Insurance Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law applies only when there is a "significant void or gap" in federal law.

This "gap-filling" approach is consistent with the OCSLA's purpose, as described in Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969), where the Court noted that Congress enacted the OCSLA to provide a body of law for the OCS, but did not intend to create a comprehensive code that would cover every eventuality. The Court in Rodrigue also emphasized that the OCSLA's choice of law provision is intended to fill gaps in federal law, rather than to supplant it. Id. at 357-58.

Furthermore, the OCSLA's legislative history suggests that Congress intended for federal law to be the primary source of law on the OCS, with state law filling in gaps where necessary. See S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6 (1953). This is consistent with the principle that federal law is generally exclusive in areas of federal jurisdiction, as noted in United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286, 293-94 (1958), where the Court observed that Congress may adopt state law as federal law, but only when there is a need to fill a gap in federal law.

Other cases have also recognized the importance of federal law in areas of exclusive federal jurisdiction. For example, in James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94, 100 (1940), the Court held that federal law governs in areas where the federal government has exclusive jurisdiction, and that state law may be adopted as federal law only when necessary to fill a gap. Similarly, in Pacific Coast Dairy v. Department of Agriculture, 318 U.S. 285, 294 (1943), the Court noted that federal law is generally exclusive in areas of federal jurisdiction, and that state law may be preempted by federal law.

The Ninth Circuit's decision below ignored these precedents and adopted a more expansive view of state law applicability on the OCS. However, this interpretation is not supported by the statutory text or relevant case law. As the Fifth Circuit noted in Continental Oil, 417 F.2d at 1036, state law should only be applied when there is a significant void or gap in federal law.

In this case, the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour claims. The FLSA's savings clause allows for more protective state laws, but this does not mean that state law applies on the OCS in the absence of a gap in federal law. Rather, the FLSA's comprehensive scheme precludes the application of state law on the OCS, as held in cases such as Omohundro v. United States, 300 F.2d 763, 765 (5th Cir. 1962), where the court noted that federal law is exclusive in areas of federal jurisdiction.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of Newton's claims should be reinstated. The OCSLA's choice of law provision requires a gap in federal law before state law can be applied on the OCS, and the FLSA's comprehensive scheme governing wage and hour claims precludes the application of state law in this case.
