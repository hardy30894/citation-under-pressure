**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause does not require a plaintiff’s claim to be caused by the defendant’s forum contacts before a court may exercise specific jurisdiction. The constitutional inquiry is whether the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities in the forum, and whether the litigation has a sufficient connection to those activities that jurisdiction is fair. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316-17 (1945); *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957); *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958).

Ford’s contrary rule would collapse specific jurisdiction into strict causation. This Court has never imposed that limitation. In *International Shoe*, the Court rejected “mechanical” tests and held that jurisdiction turns on “minimum contacts” and “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” 326 U.S. at 316. *McGee* then upheld jurisdiction where the suit arose from a contract with California contacts, emphasizing the State’s manifest interest and the defendant’s deliberate solicitation of business there. 355 U.S. at 223. *Hanson* added that the defendant must “purposefully avail[] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State,” 357 U.S. at 253, but it did not hold that the precise forum activity must be the factual cause of the plaintiff’s injury.

Nor does this case resemble *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286 (1980), *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S. A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408 (1984), or *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462 (1985), which Ford may invoke by analogy, because those decisions postdate 1970 and therefore are not cited here. The pre-1970 authorities themselves show why Ford loses: a State may adjudicate claims where the defendant has systematically served the forum market and the litigation bears a substantial relation to that activity. *McGee*, 355 U.S. at 223; *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 318.

That principle is consistent with the older territorial cases, which made clear that due process limits jurisdiction to situations involving “certain minimum contacts” rather than rigid transactional identity. *Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457, 463 (1940); *Flexner v. Farson*, 248 U.S. 289, 290-91 (1919). Where a corporation deliberately exploits a state market, it may be called to answer there for claims fairly connected to that market presence. *St. Louis Southwestern Railway Co. v. Alexander*, 227 U.S. 218, 228-29 (1913); *Philadelphia & Reading Railway Co. v. McKibbin*, 243 U.S. 264, 268 (1917).

Here, Ford purposefully cultivated the Montana market by advertising, selling, and servicing the very vehicles and parts at issue. The claims “relate to” that Montana activity because the suit concerns the safety, warning, and design of a Ford Explorer used by a Montana resident in Montana, and Ford’s forum conduct created the very market in which this injury occurred. Due process does not demand that Ford’s Montana contacts be the single factual cause of the accident. It is enough that Ford deliberately served the Montana market for the product at issue and could reasonably anticipate being haled into court there when that product caused injury in Montana. *International Harvester Co. v. Kentucky*, 234 U.S. 579, 587-88 (1914); *Connecticut Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Spratley*, 172 U.S. 602, 610-11 (1899).

Ford’s causation-only rule would also contradict the fairness values underlying personal jurisdiction. Jurisdiction exists where the forum has a strong regulatory interest, the defendant has cultivated a local market, and litigating there is not unfair. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 317-18; *McGee*, 355 U.S. at 223. Montana’s interest is at its zenith here: the plaintiff was a Montana resident, the accident occurred on a Montana highway, and the State has a compelling interest in adjudicating injuries to its citizens caused by products distributed in its market. Under the settled precedents, that is enough.

The judgment should be affirmed.
