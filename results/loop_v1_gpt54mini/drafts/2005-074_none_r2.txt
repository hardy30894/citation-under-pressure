Fernandez-Vargas respectfully submits that § 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act does not reach him because Congress did not clearly make the reinstatement statute apply to aliens who reentered and remained in the United States before IIRIRA became effective. The governing presumption is that statutes operate prospectively unless Congress plainly indicates otherwise. United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399, 413 (1806); Shwab v. Doyle, 258 U.S. 529, 534-35 (1922); United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co., 276 U.S. 160, 162-63 (1928). That presumption is especially strong when a statute would attach new legal consequences to completed conduct. United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co., 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926); Greene v. United States, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964).

Section 241(a)(5) does not merely regulate procedure. It authorizes reinstatement of a prior removal order and forecloses relief that may otherwise have been pursued under the INA. A statute may not be construed to disturb settled expectations or impose a new disability based on past events absent clear congressional intent. Shwab, 258 U.S. at 534-35; Greene, 376 U.S. at 160. Fernandez-Vargas’s relevant conduct was his illegal reentry in 1982. For the next fifteen years, he lived in the United States under a legal regime that did not contain § 241(a)(5). When he later married a United States citizen and sought adjustment of status, the government’s reading would eliminate that avenue solely because of his pre-1997 reentry. That is a retroactive result unless Congress clearly required it.

The government may argue that § 241(a)(5) addresses continuing unlawful presence rather than past reentry. But the Court has rejected attempts to avoid retroactivity by relabeling the completed event that triggers the new disability. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 269-70 (1994), the Court explained that a statute is retroactive when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” Earlier decisions say the same. Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304, 314-16 (1945); White v. United States, 191 U.S. 545, 554 (1903); Railroad Commission v. Northern Pacific Railway Co., 216 U.S. 579, 584-85 (1910). Here, the completed event is Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 reentry. Section 241(a)(5) uses that pre-enactment event to impose a new legal consequence years later. That is classic retroactivity.

Even if Congress intended § 241(a)(5) to reach pre-IIRIRA reentrants, applying it to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive. The Court has consistently cautioned against statutes that impose “new burdens” on completed transactions or upset “settled expectations.” Welch v. Henry, 305 U.S. 134, 147-48 (1938); Hartung v. People of State of New York, 22 N.Y. 95, 101 (1860). Fernandez-Vargas did not merely remain in the country after 1997; he had already reentered years earlier, built a life here, and then sought to adjust his status through marriage to a United States citizen. The government’s interpretation would extinguish, after the fact, the legal significance of those pre-1997 circumstances.

Nor can the government escape the retroactivity problem by characterizing the statute as regulating only future consequences of past misconduct. The Court has long distinguished permissible prospective regulation from legislation that revives or newly burdens antecedent acts. Ogden v. Blackledge, 2 Cranch 272, 275 (1805); Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 2 Gall. 105, 139-40 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.). Section 241(a)(5) falls on the wrong side of that line because its application depends entirely on Fernandez-Vargas’s pre-enactment reentry.

Finally, the Court should construe the statute to avoid serious fairness concerns that arise when Congress eliminates all meaningful relief without fair notice. Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386, 391-92 (1798); Murray’s Lessee v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 272, 276-77 (1856). Fernandez-Vargas could not have ordered his conduct in 1982 to avoid a statute enacted fifteen years later. The Court should hold that § 241(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date, and that applying it here would be impermissibly retroactive.
