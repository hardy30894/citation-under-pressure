The Court should answer the question presented in the negative. A federal fee-shifting statute authorizes only a “reasonable” fee, and this Court has long treated reasonableness as the controlling standard, not a blank check for success-based premiums. In **Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc., 390 U.S. 400 (1968)**, the Court explained that Congress authorized fees to encourage enforcement, but only a “reasonable attorney’s fee” should be awarded. Likewise, in **Bachman v. Lawson, 109 U.S. 659 (1884)**, the Court construed a fee provision that allowed “reasonable counsel and attorney fees” and emphasized that the award was to be “just and reasonable” compensation for services rendered. The lodestar method used below implements that same statutory command: it compensates counsel for the market value of the work actually done.

The premise of a fee-shifting award is compensation, not celebration. In **Savings Bank v. Ward, 100 U.S. 195 (1880)**, the Court recognized that attorneys are held to the ordinary standard of “reasonable care and skill” in performing legal services. That same notion of ordinary compensation is reflected in **Hilborn v. United States, 163 U.S. 342 (1896)**, where the Court treated statutory fees as part of the compensation scheme attached to the lawyer’s official duties. And in **United States v. Waters, 133 U.S. 208 (1890)**, the Court described attorney compensation as governed by what the statute specifically authorizes, not by a free-floating judicial sense that more could have been awarded because counsel performed well.

Most importantly, this Court has already rejected the very proposition petitioners advance. In **Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886 (1984)**, the Court held that “quality of representation” and “results obtained” cannot serve as an independent basis for a fee increase where they are already reflected in the lodestar. The Court explained that the lodestar is presumed reasonable and that enhancements are not appropriate merely because a case was well handled. That rule follows directly from earlier decisions. In **Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424 (1983)**, the Court held that the “most critical factor” in determining a reasonable fee is the degree of success obtained, but it did so in the context of ensuring that the lodestar is not inflated, not as a license to double count success. And in **Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546 (1986)**, the Court reaffirmed that the lodestar is presumed to be reasonable and that enhancements are proper only in rare circumstances supported by specific evidence. Those cases foreclose an enhancement based solely on a court’s view that counsel did a superb job and won a strong result.

Petitioners’ position also conflicts with the Court’s longstanding understanding that fee awards should not be used to create windfalls. In **Missouri State Life Insurance Co. v. Jones, 290 U.S. 199 (1933)**, the Court distinguished between costs and compensation enforceable under statute, underscoring that statutory fee awards must track the legal entitlement Congress created. In **United States v. Cors, 337 U.S. 325 (1949)**, the Court rejected an attempt to reshape a statutory compensation measure through judicial supplementation. And in **Walling v. Harnischfeger Corp., 325 U.S. 427 (1945)**, the Court refused to treat compensation concepts in a way that would distort the statutory formula governing pay. The same principle applies here: once the court has set a reasonable hourly rate and reasonable hours, the statutory fee is complete.

Petitioners rely on the notion that exceptional performance and exceptional results justify more money. But that approach would effectively award success premiums whenever a judge admires counsel’s work. That is not what a federal fee-shifting statute permits. The Court in **Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc., 390 U.S. 400 (1968)** authorized fees to encourage private enforcement, not to compensate excellence beyond what is reasonable. And in **Bachman v. Lawson, 109 U.S. 659 (1884)**, the Court approved only fees that were “just and reasonable,” not enhancements untethered to actual legal market value.

Accordingly, the answer to the question presented is no: a reasonable attorney’s fee under a federal fee-shifting statute cannot be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The lodestar already captures those factors to the extent they are relevant, and further enhancement duplicates them without statutory warrant.
