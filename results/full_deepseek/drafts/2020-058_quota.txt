**ARGUMENT**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a does not constitute an “admission” for purposes of adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The plain text, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act compel the opposite conclusion.

**I. The Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Requires That TPS Recipients Be Treated as Admitted**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” This is not merely a passive status designation; it is a deeming provision that equates TPS with lawful nonimmigrant status. For a nonimmigrant, admission is inherent in obtaining that status. As the Ninth Circuit explained, “by the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted.” *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017). The Sixth Circuit similarly held that a TPS grant “amounts to a new lawful entry.” *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548, 553 (6th Cir. 2013). Congress’s use of “considered as” signals an intent to treat TPS recipients as if they had been admitted. *See United States v. Turkette*, 452 U.S. 576, 580 (1981) (courts must give effect to every word of a statute).

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). A TPS grant follows an application process that includes identity verification, background checks, and eligibility determinations—precisely the sort of inspection and authorization that defines admission. The Third Circuit’s rigid distinction between “status” and “admission” ignores that the process of obtaining TPS itself constitutes a new lawful entry. *See In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011) (adjustment of status constitutes an admission). If adjustment of status can create a new admission, so too can a TPS grant.

**II. The Statutory Structure Confirms That TPS Recipients Satisfy § 1255(a)’s Admission Requirement**

Congress knows how to create exceptions to § 1255(a)’s admission requirement, and it did so for special immigrants and visa-eligible aliens. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i). But it also knows how to exclude TPS recipients from that requirement, and it did not. Instead, it used the broad deeming language of § 1254a(f)(4). The Third Circuit’s interpretation renders that language superfluous, because TPS recipients would gain nothing from being “considered as” in lawful nonimmigrant status if they still cannot satisfy the threshold admission requirement. *See Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (statute should be construed so that no part is superfluous).

Moreover, the Third Circuit’s reading conflicts with Congress’s express acknowledgment that TPS recipients may adjust status. Section 1254a(h) requires a supermajority Senate vote to pass legislation adjusting TPS recipients’ status, but that provision addresses *blanket* adjustment, not individual applications under § 1255. The individual adjustment pathway is preserved by § 1254a(f)(4). *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (Congress’s inclusion of language in one section but omission in another is intentional).

**III. The Purpose of TPS Supports Petitioners’ Interpretation**

TPS is a humanitarian program designed to shield nationals of disaster-stricken or dangerous countries from removal. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). The Third Circuit’s rule forces TPS recipients who entered without inspection to leave the United States and reenter lawfully to adjust status—a course that would forfeit TPS and risk return to an unsafe homeland. This “Catch-22” undermines the very purpose of TPS. *See Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964; *see also Kucana v. Holder*, 558 U.S. 233, 249 (2010) (courts should avoid interpretations that lead to absurd results). The Sixth Circuit recognized that requiring TPS recipients to depart and reenter “would impose an unnecessary and potentially dangerous burden on those seeking refuge.” *Flores*, 718 F.3d at 554.

**IV. The Agency’s Contrary Interpretation Is Not Entitled to Deference**

The Government relies on a non-precedential USCIS policy manual stating that TPS does not constitute admission. But that manual is not the result of notice-and-comment rulemaking and is not entitled to *Chevron* deference. *See Pereira v. Sessions*, 138 S. Ct. 2105, 2113 (2018) (agency interpretations in policy statements are not binding on courts). The BIA has not issued a precedential decision on this question, and the USCIS manual’s position conflicts with the plain text of § 1254a(f)(4).

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
