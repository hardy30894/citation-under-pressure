**ARGUMENT**

Section 289 of the Patent Act awards an infringer’s “total profit” from the “article of manufacture” to which the patented design is applied. 35 U.S.C. § 289. When the design patent covers only a component, that language requires apportionment to the component rather than the entire product. Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 444-45 (1885) (profits must be traced to the patented feature); Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros., 222 F. 902, 904 (2d Cir. 1915) (same). The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule conflicts with this settled principle.

The phrase “article of manufacture” has long been understood to denote the specific item embodying the design, not the larger article into which it is incorporated. Gorham Mfg. Co. v. White, 81 U.S. 511, 528 (1871); Schnadig Corp. v. Gaines Mfg. Co., 620 F.2d 1166, 1171 (6th Cir. 1980). Allowing recovery of profits on the entire smartphone would revive the very “injustice” Congress sought to avoid when it limited design-patent recovery to the article to which the design is applied. Trans-World Mfg. Corp. v. Al Nyman & Sons, Inc., 750 F.2d 1552, 1562 (Fed. Cir. 1984).

This Court has repeatedly required apportionment of damages to the patented contribution. Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. 480, 490 (1853); Aro Mfg. Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co., 377 U.S. 476, 507 (1964). The same rule governs design patents. Nike, Inc. v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 138 F.3d 1437, 1441 (Fed. Cir. 1998). The decision below, Apple Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co., 786 F.3d 983, 1001-02 (Fed. Cir. 2015), dispenses with that requirement and therefore cannot stand.

Policy considerations reinforce the statutory text. Awarding total profits on a complex product for infringement of a minor design element produces windfalls unrelated to the inventive contribution and chills legitimate competition. eBay Inc. v. MercExchange, L.L.C., 547 U.S. 388, 396 (2006) (cautioning against remedies that overcompensate). The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for apportionment.
