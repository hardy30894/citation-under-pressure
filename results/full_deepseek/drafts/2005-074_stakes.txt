**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered the United States Before the Statute’s Effective Date.**

The text, structure, and purpose of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (“IIRIRA”) demonstrate that Congress did not intend Section 241(a)(5) to reach aliens like Petitioner, who illegally reentered the United States in 1982—fifteen years before the statute’s enactment.

The IIRIRA’s effective date provisions expressly delineate the statute’s temporal reach. Section 309(a) of IIRIRA provides that its “subtitle” shall take effect on April 1, 1997. *See* IIRIRA § 309(a), 110 Stat. 3009-625. Section 309(d)(2) further specifies that the “new removal proceedings” apply “only to individuals who enter the United States on or after such date.” *Id.* § 309(d)(2). While Section 241(a)(5) is not itself a removal proceeding, its operation is triggered by the act of illegal reentry. Because Petitioner reentered in 1982—before April 1, 1997—the statute’s plain language does not reach him.

This Court has long recognized that “congressional enactments and administrative rules will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result.” *Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp.,* 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988). The IIRIRA’s effective date provisions reflect Congress’s deliberate choice to limit the statute’s application to future conduct. Nothing in Section 241(a)(5) expressly or implicitly requires its application to pre-enactment reentries. To the contrary, the structure of IIRIRA suggests that Congress intended the reinstatement provision to apply only to aliens who reentered after the statute’s effective date.

The Tenth Circuit erred in concluding that Congress intended Section 241(a)(5) to apply to all illegal reentries, regardless of when they occurred. That interpretation ignores the careful temporal line Congress drew in IIRIRA’s transition rules and improperly extends the statute’s reach to conduct completed long before its enactment.

**II. Even If Section 241(a)(5) Applies to Pre-Enactment Reentries, Its Application Here Is Impermissibly Retroactive.**

Even if this Court were to conclude that Section 241(a)(5) applies to Petitioner’s pre-enactment reentry, applying the statute to deprive Petitioner of his ability to adjust his status would be impermissibly retroactive under the framework established in *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244 (1994).

Under *Landgraf*, a court must first determine whether Congress has expressly prescribed the statute’s temporal reach. *Id.* at 280. As demonstrated above, it has not. Where Congress has not clearly spoken, a court must consider whether applying the statute “would impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party’s liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” *Id.* If so, the statute may not be applied retroactively. *Id.*

Here, applying Section 241(a)(5) to Petitioner would attach “new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Id.* at 269-70. When Petitioner reentered the United States in 1982, he was subject to the then-existing immigration laws. Under those laws, an alien who illegally reentered could, under certain circumstances, seek discretionary relief from removal, including adjustment of status based on a marriage to a United States citizen. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255 (1994 ed.). Petitioner married a U.S. citizen in 2001 and applied to adjust his status—an application that, prior to Section 241(a)(5), would have been considered on its merits.

Section 241(a)(5) categorically bars aliens who have reentered after a prior deportation from obtaining “any relief under this chapter,” including adjustment of status. 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). That bar operates regardless of the equities of the alien’s case. By denying Petitioner any opportunity to adjust his status based on his 2001 marriage, the statute “attaches a new disability” to Petitioner’s 1982 reentry—a reentry that was completed before the statute’s enactment. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269.

This Court has consistently recognized that applying new immigration restrictions to conduct completed before their enactment raises serious retroactivity concerns. In *I.N.S. v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), the Court held that the IIRIRA’s elimination of discretionary relief from deportation could not be applied retroactively to aliens who had pleaded guilty to criminal offenses before the statute’s effective date, because those aliens had relied on the availability of such relief when they entered their pleas. *Id.* at 321-25. Similarly, Petitioner had a “protectable expectation” that the legal consequences of his 1982 reentry would remain governed by the law in effect at that time. *Id.* at 321.

The Tenth Circuit concluded that Petitioner lacked any such expectation because he was an “illegal alien” who had “no right to remain in the United States.” *Fernandez-Vargas v. Ashcroft*, 394 F.3d 881, 886 (10th Cir. 2005). But *St. Cyr* recognized that even aliens who are unlawfully present may possess “settled expectations” regarding the availability of relief from removal. 533 U.S. at 322. An alien’s illegal status does not strip him of all reliance interests.

Moreover, applying Section 241(a)(5) to pre-enactment reentries would produce absurd results. An alien who reentered in 1997—one day before the statute’s effective date—would be subject to the reinstatement bar, while an alien who reentered one day later would not. Even if Congress intended Section 241(a)(5) to apply broadly, nothing in the statute’s text or history suggests it intended such a capricious line.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the decision of the Tenth Circuit and hold that Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered the United States before the statute’s effective date. In the alternative, this Court should hold that applying Section 241(a)(5) to Petitioner would be impermissibly retroactive under *Landgraf* and *St. Cyr.*
