# ARGUMENT

## THE EXECUTION OF MENTALLY RETARDED OFFENDERS DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT

### A. The Eighth Amendment's "Cruel and Unusual" Clause Is Properly Measured by Objective Indicia of Consensus, and No Such Consensus Exists

This Court has long held that the Eighth Amendment "draw[s] its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). But that inquiry is not a license for the Judiciary to substitute its own judgment for that of the People acting through their legislatures. As this Court held in *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), the appropriate measure of consensus is objective evidence of "the actions of legislatures." *Id.* at 331. Applying that test in *Penry*, this Court squarely rejected the identical claim Atkins presses here, holding that "there is insufficient evidence of a national consensus" against executing the mentally retarded, because "[o]nly one state legislature has enacted a statute specifically providing for this exemption." *Id.* at 334.

*Penry* controls this case. The Fourteenth Amendment and stare decisis counsel strongly against overruling a considered constitutional holding absent a fundamental change in circumstances. While a number of States have since enacted exemptions, mere legislative activity in a minority of jurisdictions cannot supply the "national consensus" this Court's precedents require. In *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361 (1989), decided the same Term as *Penry*, this Court rejected a similar Eighth Amendment challenge to the execution of 16- and 17-year-old offenders, explaining that the clause "must be applied with reference to the operative facts surrounding" it, and that a punishment is not unconstitutional merely because "a number of leading voices in the profession" or advocacy groups oppose it. *Id.* at 377. The Court emphasized that "it is not, we think, absolutely necessary that a punishment be universally decried" for it to survive Eighth Amendment scrutiny where legislative practice does not reflect consensus. *Id.* Virginia's own General Assembly—the body constitutionally entrusted with weighing the culpability of offenders and the penological needs of the Commonwealth—considered and rejected a categorical exemption for mentally retarded capital defendants, opting instead to allow juries, informed by expert testimony, to weigh mental retardation as a mitigating factor case-by-case. That legislative judgment is entitled to substantial deference. *See Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 175 (1976) (plurality opinion) (noting that "legislative judgment weighs heavily" in Eighth Amendment analysis).

### B. Individualized Sentencing Adequately Accounts for Mental Retardation as a Mitigating Circumstance

This Court's capital jurisprudence already provides the correct mechanism for addressing diminished culpability: individualized sentencing that allows the jury to consider "any relevant mitigating evidence," including evidence of mental impairment. *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978); *see also Eddings v. Oklahoma*, 455 U.S. 104, 113–14 (1982) (sentencer must be permitted to consider evidence of the defendant's mental and emotional development). In *Penry*, this Court held that the Eighth Amendment's concerns are satisfied so long as "the sentencer [is permitted] to consider fully" a defendant's mental retardation "as a mitigating factor," and that this consideration—not a categorical exemption—is the constitutionally required safeguard. 492 U.S. at 328.

Respondent's own sentencing proceedings demonstrate that this framework functioned exactly as intended. Atkins was afforded not one but two capital sentencing hearings; at each, his forensic psychologist testified at length regarding his intellectual limitations. The Commonwealth, in turn, presented contrary evidence bearing on Atkins' actual capacities and moral culpability. The jury, having heard both sides, twice returned a sentence of death. This is the individualized, evidence-based process that *Penry* contemplated, and it protects against the risk of arbitrary or disproportionate punishment far more precisely than a categorical rule keyed to an inherently imprecise clinical diagnosis. As this Court has recognized, mental retardation exists on a spectrum, and the mildly retarded—the category into which Atkins' own expert placed him—may nonetheless retain the capacity for premeditation, deliberation, and appreciation of wrongdoing. A categorical bar would treat offenders as fungible based on an IQ score or diagnostic label without regard to the individualized circumstances of the crime, contrary to the "individualized consideration" this Court has demanded in the capital context. *Lockett*, 438 U.S. at 605.

### C. Proportionality Review Does Not Support a Categorical Exemption

Even under the two-pronged approach used in cases such as *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584 (1977), and *Enmund v. Florida*, 458 U.S. 782 (1982), the death penalty as applied to mentally retarded offenders is not disproportionate. Those cases involved offenders whose *conduct*—rape without homicide, or minimal participation in a felony without intent to kill—rendered the death penalty categorically excessive regardless of the offender's mental state. *Coker*, 433 U.S. at 592; *Enmund*, 458 U.S. at 798. Atkins, by contrast, was convicted of the deliberate, calculated murder of his victim after abduction and armed robbery—conduct at the core of what the death penalty is designed to punish. Nothing in *Coker* or *Enmund* suggests that an offender's diagnostic classification, standing alone, renders the sentence for such conduct categorically excessive; rather, culpability turns on the individual's actual mental state and conduct at the time of the offense, matters the jury weighed and resolved against Atkins twice.

### Conclusion

For these reasons, *Penry* remains sound law, no national consensus has emerged sufficient to override it, and Virginia's individualized capital sentencing procedure adequately protects mentally retarded defendants from disproportionate punishment. The judgment below should be affirmed.
