The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The non-delegation doctrine, as reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), permits Congress to delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform." Id. at 372 (quoting J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The CSA provides sufficient guidance to the Attorney General, listing eight factors to consider when adding or removing substances from the schedules, including the substance's potential for abuse, its pharmacological effect, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). The Attorney General must also obtain a "scientific and medical" evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services, which is binding if the Secretary recommends against scheduling the substance. Id. § 811(b).

The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), is similarly guided by intelligible principles. The Attorney General may temporarily schedule a substance only to "avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety" after considering specific factors related to the substance's history, current pattern, and significance of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. Id. § 811(h)(1), (3). The temporary scheduling is also limited in duration to one year with a possible six-month extension. Id. § 811(h)(2). These guidelines provide a sufficient framework for the Attorney General to exercise the delegated authority.

The Attorney General's delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is also authorized. The CSA explicitly grants the Attorney General the power to delegate his functions under the Act to other officers or employees of the Department of Justice. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General has promulgated regulations subdelegating his authority to the DEA Administrator, which is a valid exercise of his authority under the statute. See 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989).

The Toubys argue that a more stringent non-delegation analysis applies when the delegatee is empowered to create crimes. However, the Supreme Court has not adopted such a heightened standard. In Mistretta, the Court upheld a delegation of authority to the Federal Sentencing Commission to promulgate sentencing guidelines, despite the significant impact on individual liberty. The Court's analysis focused on the presence of intelligible principles guiding the delegatee's authority, rather than the specific consequences of the delegated power.

The Third Circuit correctly held that the CSA's delegation to the Attorney General is constitutional, and the Attorney General's subdelegation to the DEA Administrator is authorized. The Toubys' convictions should be affirmed.
