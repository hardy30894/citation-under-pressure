**ARGUMENT**

I. **States may not add qualifications for Members of Congress beyond those the Constitution itself prescribes.**

The Constitution fixes the qualifications for the House and Senate, and those qualifications are exclusive. Article I provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, have been seven years a citizen, and, when elected, be an inhabitant of the state from which chosen. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2. A Senator must be at least thirty years old, have been nine years a citizen, and, when elected, be an inhabitant of the state from which chosen. U.S. Const. art. I, § 3, cl. 3. The Constitution also states the age, citizenship, and residency requirements for federal office in terms that leave no room for state supplementation. See U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2–3.

This Court’s precedents recognize that the Constitution’s textual qualification scheme is controlling. In **Powell v. McCormack**, 395 U.S. 486, 522-48 (1969), the Court held that the House may not exclude a duly elected Member who satisfies the Constitution’s enumerated qualifications, explaining that the Framers “carefully constructed” the qualifications and that the House was not free to add others. The Court later reaffirmed that principle in **U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton**, 514 U.S. 779, 783-84, 827-37 (1995), holding that states lack power to impose term limits on federal legislators because the qualifications contained in Article I are exclusive. The Court explained that the Framers intended the listed qualifications to be the “exclusive qualifications,” and that allowing the States to add to them would “erode the structure of the Constitution.” Id. at 827, 837.

The Arkansas amendment does exactly what the Constitution forbids. It declares that persons who have served the specified number of terms “shall not be certified as a candidate” and “shall not be eligible” for the ballot. That is a state-imposed disqualification from congressional office. No matter how the restriction is labeled, it operates as an additional qualification for federal office. See **Cook v. Gralike**, 531 U.S. 510, 523-24 (2001) (invalidating Missouri ballot labels designed to influence congressional elections and emphasizing that states may not use ballot machinery to dictate electoral outcomes for federal offices).

Nor can Arkansas justify its amendment as an exercise of the State’s reserved powers. The Tenth Amendment does not authorize states to alter federal qualifications because the power to set those qualifications was not reserved to them in the first place. **Thornton**, 514 U.S. at 802-05. The Constitution created a national legislature with national qualifications, and the States may not “supplement” those qualifications by initiative or otherwise. Id. at 827-37; **Powell**, 395 U.S. at 540-48.

II. **The amendment’s ballot-access label does not save it.**

Arkansas contends that its measure merely regulates ballot access, not eligibility. But the Constitution forbids states from doing indirectly what they cannot do directly. In **Cook v. Gralike**, 531 U.S. at 523-24, the Court held that state rules governing the mechanics of federal elections are invalid when they are designed to “dictate electoral outcomes” or favor a class of candidates. Here, the amendment’s design is even more direct: it bars otherwise qualified candidates from appearing on the ballot if they have served the designated number of terms.

A ballot-access rule that excludes an entire class of constitutionally qualified candidates is not a neutral procedural regulation. See **Anderson v. Celebrezze**, 460 U.S. 780, 788-89 (1983) (state election regulations burdening constitutional rights must be justified by legitimate and sufficiently weighty interests); **Cook**, 531 U.S. at 523-24. Arkansas’s asserted interest in term limits cannot overcome the Constitution’s express allocation of qualifications to the national charter. **Thornton**, 514 U.S. at 832-33.

III. **If any part of Amendment 73 falls, the congressional term-limit provisions are not severable from the state term-limit provisions as enacted.**

Although Arkansas law looks to whether the measure would have passed without the invalid portion, see **Combs v. Glens Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964)**, the record here shows a single unified term-limits package presented to voters as one amendment. The state and federal term limits were coupled throughout the text and ballot title. Under such circumstances, the invalid congressional restrictions infected the enactment as a whole. See **Levy v. Parker**, 478 U.S. 921 (1986) (mem.) (memorable for the principle that courts must respect the electorate’s actual enactment and not reconstruct a different measure absent clear severability); **Thornton**, 514 U.S. at 883-84 (Scalia, J., dissenting) (acknowledging the severability inquiry depends on voter choice).

But even if the state term-limit provisions could stand independently as a matter of state law, that question does not cure the constitutional defect in Section 3. The State may regulate its own officers, but it may not condition access to Congress on prior service limits. **Thornton**, 514 U.S. at 787-95. The judgment should therefore be affirmed insofar as it invalidates the congressional term-limit provisions.
