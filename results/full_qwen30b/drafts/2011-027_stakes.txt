**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the equal footing doctrine, grounded in the Constitution, mandates that a state acquires title to the beds of rivers navigable at the time of its admission to the Union. This principle is not only well-established in federal jurisprudence but is also consistent with the foundational structure of the federal land system and the rights of states to manage their public lands.

The Constitution provides that new states shall be admitted “on an equal footing with the original States in all respects whatever.” U.S. Const. Art. IV, § 3, cl. 1. This equal footing doctrine has long been interpreted to mean that when a state enters the Union, it acquires title to the beds of navigable rivers within its borders, as those beds were considered public lands subject to state ownership under the federal land grant system. *See, e.g., Illinois v. Wisconsin*, 307 U.S. 88, 95 (1939) (“The principle of equal footing is not a mere formality; it is a constitutional mandate that a new state shall have the same rights and powers as the original States.”); *United States v. California*, 383 U.S. 276, 282 (1966) (“The equal footing doctrine requires that a state, upon admission to the Union, receive title to the beds of navigable waters within its boundaries.”).

The critical inquiry under this doctrine is whether the river was navigable *at the time of statehood*. *See, e.g., United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915) (“The test of navigability for title purposes is whether the river was navigable at the time of statehood.”); *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 312 U.S. 539, 544 (1941) (“The question of title to the bed of a river is determined by the navigability of the river at the time of statehood.”). This rule is not a matter of judicial discretion but a constitutional requirement. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the determination of navigability for title purposes is a historical question, to be decided based on the condition of the river at the time of statehood, not on its current or future use. *See, e.g., United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 312 U.S. at 544; *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 312 U.S. 539, 544 (1941) (“The test is not what the river may be capable of being, but what it was at the time of statehood.”).

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled principle. It found, based on substantial historical evidence—including reports from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, early territorial records, and contemporary accounts—that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time Montana became a state in 1889. The trial court’s findings were supported by the record and were not clearly erroneous. *See Mont. R. Civ. P. 52(a)(6)*. The State’s evidence, though largely composed of historical documents and expert affidavits, was properly admitted under Montana’s rules of evidence. The documents were self-authenticating under M. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), and (6)—certified public records, official publications, and periodicals—and were also admissible under M. R. Evid. 803(8) as public records and reports. *See Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc.*, 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79–81, 341 Mont. 33, 174 P.3d 948. The fact that the evidence was hearsay did not render it inadmissible; rather, it was properly considered under the exceptions to the hearsay rule. *See M. R. Evid. 803(8)*.

PPL’s argument that the State’s evidence was insufficient because it relied on hearsay and lacked personal knowledge is foreclosed by precedent. *See Smith v. State*, 2005 MT 187, ¶ 39, 328 Mont. 100, 118 P.3d 1047 (holding that affidavits may rely on hearsay if the underlying documents are admissible under the rules of evidence). The State’s expert, the State Archivist, authenticated the documents and established their reliability. The trial court did not err in admitting them. *See id.*.

PPL further contends that the federal navigational servitude preempts the State’s claims. This argument fails. The federal navigational servitude, which allows Congress to regulate navigable waters for interstate and foreign commerce, does not extinguish state title to riverbeds. *See United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596–97 (1941) (“The federal navigational servitude does not abrogate state ownership of the riverbed.”). The Supreme Court has made clear that the FPA does not preempt state property rights. *See First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Commn.*, 328 U.S. 152, 175–76 (1946) (“The Federal Power Act does not displace state law with respect to proprietary rights.”). Indeed, the FPA explicitly requires licensees to comply with state law regarding the use of riverbeds and water rights. *See 16 U.S.C. § 802*. The FPA also permits state law tort claims against licensees for damages caused by hydroelectric projects. *See Nez Perce Tribe v. Idaho Power Co.*, 847 F. Supp. 791, 812 (D. Idaho 1993). Thus, the State’s claim for compensation under the Hydroelectric Resources Act is not preempted.
