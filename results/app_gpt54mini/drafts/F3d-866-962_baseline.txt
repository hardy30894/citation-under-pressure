## Argument

### I. The district court applied an unduly narrow causation standard and ignored evidence that BNSF disciplined appellants because they reported safety concerns.

The Federal Railroad Safety Act protects railroad employees who “report, in good faith, a hazardous safety or security condition.” 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). Congress enacted the FRSA to “promote safety in every area of railroad operations,” and its retaliation provision must be applied in light of that remedial purpose. Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co., 768 F.3d 786, 791–92 (8th Cir. 2014). To prevail, an employee need only show that protected conduct was a “contributing factor” in the adverse action. 49 U.S.C. § 20109(d)(2)(A); Kuduk, 768 F.3d at 791. That standard is “broad” and “easily” satisfied by circumstantial evidence. Palmer v. Canadian Nat’l Ry./Ill. Cent. R.R. Co., 446 F. App’x 9, 13 (7th Cir. 2011); Araujo v. N.J. Transit Rail Operations, Inc., 708 F.3d 152, 158–60 (3d Cir. 2013).

Appellants presented ample evidence that they engaged in protected activity when they reported the bridge’s dangerous conditions, including the lack of side rails and lighting, in written and recorded statements to BNSF management and claims personnel. See 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C); Kuduk, 768 F.3d at 791. BNSF then initiated a discipline process shortly after those reports, held appellants in an investigative posture for months, increased operations testing, and ultimately imposed discipline after management officials who had already heard appellants’ safety complaints reviewed the evidence. Temporal proximity may support causation when coupled with other evidence of retaliatory motive. See Kuduk, 768 F.3d at 792–93; Araujo, 708 F.3d at 160–61.

The record also supports an inference of retaliatory animus because the very officials who were implicated in the safety dispute, Trainmaster Dixon and Road Foreman Knutstrom, served as witnesses against appellants at the hearing. The hearing evidence centered on whether employees had been instructed to change crews east or west of the bridge, and appellants testified that company officials directed them to the west side, where the unsafe conditions existed. A factfinder could reasonably conclude that BNSF’s discipline was not a neutral response to rule infractions, but a reaction to employees who had complained about a hazardous location and then disputed management’s version of events. Under the FRSA, an employee need not prove retaliation was the sole or primary cause; it is enough that it contributed in any way to the adverse action. Kuduk, 768 F.3d at 791; Gunderson v. BNSF Ry. Co., 850 F.3d 962, 968–69 (8th Cir. 2017).

### II. The district court erred by resolving disputed issues of motive and credibility at summary judgment.

Summary judgment is inappropriate where the record permits competing inferences about why the employer acted. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). In FRSA cases, courts may not weigh witness credibility or choose between competing versions of disputed events; those are classic jury questions. Araujo, 708 F.3d at 158–60; Rogers v. Union Pac. R.R. Co., 816 F.3d 864, 869–70 (8th Cir. 2016).

Here, the parties sharply disputed the key facts: where the crew was instructed to change, whether BNSF had previously identified the bridge as dangerous, and whether appellants were disciplined because of actual rule violations or because management wanted to punish employees who had reported unsafe conditions. Appellants’ statements repeatedly identified the bridge’s hazards, and Foster specifically told BNSF that employees had previously reported the location as dangerous. Yet the district court effectively credited BNSF’s witnesses over appellants’ testimony, even though the hearing record showed that company officials gave conflicting accounts of the crew-change location.

The fact that the Public Law Board later reinstated Foster, removed Snyder’s reprimand, and removed Kline’s suspension further underscores the disputed nature of BNSF’s discipline. While arbitration does not itself establish retaliation, subsequent reinstatement is relevant circumstantial evidence from which a factfinder may infer that the employer’s initial charges were overstated or pretextual. See Kuduk, 768 F.3d at 793; Greene v. BNSF Ry. Co., 726 F.3d 1230, 1238–39 (10th Cir. 2013).

### III. BNSF failed to prove by clear and convincing evidence that it would have taken the same action absent the protected activity.

Even if appellants made their prima facie showing, BNSF could prevail only by proving “by clear and convincing evidence” that it would have disciplined them absent the protected conduct. 49 U.S.C. § 42121(b)(2)(B)(iv); 49 U.S.C. § 20109(d)(2)(A)(i). That burden is demanding. Kuduk, 768 F.3d at 791–92. BNSF did not meet it.

The discipline rested on rule infractions identified after a contested hearing in which appellants were represented by the union and permitted to question witnesses. But that process does not itself establish clear and convincing proof of independent causation. The hearing record remained hotly contested, especially on the disputed crew-change location and the reason appellants were where they were when the accident occurred. Moreover, BNSF imposed materially different sanctions: Snyder received a reprimand, Kline received a record suspension and probationary review, and Foster was fired only because she already happened to be in a prior review period. Those individualized and escalating sanctions, imposed after appellants repeatedly reported dangerous bridge conditions, are not the type of undisputed evidence required to carry BNSF’s heavy burden. See Araujo, 708 F.3d at 160–61; Kuduk, 768 F.3d at 791–93.

Because the record contains ample evidence that protected safety reporting was a contributing factor in the discipline, and because BNSF did not prove as a matter of law that it would have taken the same actions anyway, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
