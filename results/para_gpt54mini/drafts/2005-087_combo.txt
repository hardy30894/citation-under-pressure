## Argument

Arizona’s rule does not violate due process. The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees a fair trial, but it does not require a State to recognize every theory by which a defendant might seek to negate culpability. The Constitution leaves wide latitude to the States to define crimes, allocate burdens, and regulate the admissibility and use of evidence. See *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952); *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895); *Fox v. State of Ohio*, 46 U.S. (5 How.) 410 (1847).

1. **The Due Process Clause permits Arizona to confine mental-health evidence to the insanity defense.**  
In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld a state rule requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court explained that “the Due Process Clause does not require the State to adopt one insanity test rather than another.” *Id.* at 798. That principle controls here. If due process does not dictate the State’s definition and burden for insanity, it likewise does not compel Arizona to permit psychiatric evidence to operate under a separate, judge-created doctrine of “partial” or “diminished” responsibility. Arizona’s decision to limit such evidence to the insanity defense is a substantive rule governing criminal liability and proof, not a denial of fundamental fairness. See *Mullins v. Oregon*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 13 (1853); *Patsone v. Pennsylvania*, 232 U.S. 138 (1914).

2. **The State may require proof of intent through legally defined evidence rules without violating due process.**  
The Court has repeatedly recognized that a State may “regulate the competency of witnesses” and the modes of proof so long as it does not transgress fundamental fairness. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 487 (1895); *Hawes v. Georgia*, 258 U.S. 1 (1922). Arizona’s rule does not relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt; it simply determines what evidence may be used for which legal purpose. That is a classic state-law judgment about evidence. See *Strother v. Lucas*, 37 U.S. (12 Pet.) 410 (1838); *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908).

3. **The Constitution does not create a right to introduce expert psychiatric evidence to negate mens rea apart from insanity.**  
At common law, insanity was an affirmative excuse; it was not the same thing as disproving intent. The Court’s pre-1970 cases never constitutionalized a separate diminished-capacity defense. Indeed, *Davis v. United States* treated insanity as a distinct issue that could excuse otherwise criminal conduct, while still leaving the government to prove the elements of the offense. 160 U.S. at 484-88. Likewise, *Davis* does not suggest that due process forces a State to admit psychiatric opinion on intent whenever the defendant wishes to contest mens rea. To the contrary, the Court’s treatment of criminal responsibility reflects deference to state evidentiary and substantive law. See *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950); *Patterson v. Colorado*, 205 U.S. 454 (1907).

4. **Arizona’s rule does not shift the burden on intent or presume guilt.**  
Clark remains entitled to insist that the State prove every element of the offense. But Arizona’s limitation on the use of psychiatric evidence does not alter that burden. The jury still had to decide whether the prosecution proved the requisite intent from the evidence admitted under state law. Due process forbids conviction without proof of each element, but it does not guarantee a defendant the evidentiary vehicle of his choice. See *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952); *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97 (1934); *West Virginia Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624 (1943). And where the State’s rule is rationally connected to a legitimate trial function—here, preventing psychiatric testimony from confusing the jury’s separate inquiry into insanity—it is constitutionally permissible. See *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516 (1884).

5. **Fundamental fairness does not require admission of all defense evidence.**  
The Court has long held that due process is not a license for federal courts to second-guess every state rule of criminal procedure. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), and *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), both reject the idea that due process incorporates every assertedly desirable rule of evidence or trial practice. Arizona’s rule is especially defensible because psychiatric evidence of “intent” is uniquely susceptible to overvaluation and confusion. States may reasonably require that such evidence be channeled through the established insanity defense, rather than permitting it to become a second, partially overlapping mental-state defense. See *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952); *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895).

6. **Clark’s contrary position would constitutionalize a defense the Court has never recognized.**  
His theory would convert a State’s evidentiary choice into a federal constitutional command: whenever the defendant offers psychiatric testimony, due process would require the jury to consider it on mens rea as well as insanity. No pre-1970 decision supports that leap. The Court has instead emphasized that criminal-law definitions and burdens are primarily for the States, absent a departure from fundamental fairness. *Patterson v. Colorado*, 205 U.S. 454 (1907); *Patsone v. Pennsylvania*, 232 U.S. 138 (1914); *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952). Arizona’s rule therefore falls comfortably within constitutional limits.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
