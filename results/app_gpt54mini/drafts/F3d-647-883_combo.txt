**Argument**

The district court erred in dismissing Buckles’s § 2255 motion as untimely. The motion was timely if his petition for certiorari was timely, and his certiorari petition was timely if the Ninth Circuit clerk’s office gave him misinformation that reasonably misled him as to the deadline. At minimum, the record warrants equitable tolling and further factual development.

I. **The certiorari clock was not restarted by this Court’s order on the motion for appointment of counsel.**

Supreme Court Rule 13 measures the time to petition for certiorari from the denial of rehearing, not from later collateral proceedings in the court of appeals. The Supreme Court has long treated time limits for review as jurisdictional in character and not subject to enlargement by implication. *Bowles v. Russell*, 551 U.S. 205 (2007), though later than the period relevant here, rests on the older principle that appellate time limits are mandatory and strictly construed; that principle was already well established. See *United States v. Curry*, 47 U.S. (6 How.) 106, 113 (1848) (“A court cannot extend the time for taking an appeal.”); *The S.S. Osborne*, 105 U.S. 447, 451 (1882) (time limits for appellate review must be observed as prescribed).

Buckles’s motion for appointment of certiorari counsel was not a petition for rehearing, and this Court’s order denying that motion did not “reopen” or “restart” the time to seek certiorari. The Supreme Court’s practice treats the certiorari deadline as running from the disposition of rehearing, not from later ministerial or collateral docket activity. See *Railroad Co. v. Grant*, 98 U.S. 398, 401 (1879) (time for review runs from the final action that the statute identifies, not from later proceedings); *McLish v. Roff*, 141 U.S. 661, 665-66 (1891) (jurisdictional deadlines are controlled by the event specified by law). Buckles’s pro se motion for counsel could not alter the date from which the 90-day period ran.

II. **If the Ninth Circuit clerk’s office misinformed Buckles’s family about the deadline, equitable tolling is available.**

Equity has long protected litigants who were misled by officers of the court or whose rights were frustrated by circumstances beyond their control. In *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342, 348 (1874), the Court held that where a party “has been injured by the fraud of his adversary” and could not discover the wrong despite diligence, equity will not allow the wrongdoer to profit from the concealment. The same principle applies here if court personnel affirmatively gave inaccurate information that Buckles reasonably relied upon.

The Supreme Court has also recognized that government officers may bind the government where their conduct affirmatively misleads a party dealing with the courts. In *Glus v. Brooklyn Eastern District Terminal*, 359 U.S. 231, 235 (1959), the Court held that one “cannot justly be allowed to benefit from his own wrong.” Likewise, in *Henderson v. Carbondale Coal & Coke Co.*, 140 U.S. 25, 30 (1891), the Court permitted relief where a party was prevented from acting by misleading conduct. If the Ninth Circuit clerk’s office told Buckles’s sister that the certiorari deadline was “stayed” and would run from a “new mandate date,” equity should toll the deadline because Buckles was misdirected by the very office charged with administering the court’s process.

III. **Buckles exercised diligence, and the timeliness problem arose from confusion caused by the court system and appointed-counsel procedures.**

Equity does not require the impossible. The Court has repeatedly held that a diligent litigant should not lose rights because of circumstances outside his control. See *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010) (later articulation of the equitable-tolling principle); the underlying doctrine predates that decision and is reflected in *Bailey*, 88 U.S. at 348, and *Glus*, 359 U.S. at 235. Buckles acted promptly: he wrote counsel on the 21st day, copied the Clerk, filed a supplemental motion, and repeatedly tried to secure assistance. Those efforts show diligence, not neglect.

The district court also erred by treating the timeliness issue as fixed solely by the docket date of the certiorari petition. Filing rules are construed to avoid forfeiture where a litigant has reasonably relied on court communications. See *United States v. Adams*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 463, 475 (1868) (equity intervenes to prevent injustice where a party has been misled); *Insurance Co. v. Ritchie*, 72 U.S. (5 Wall.) 541, 544-45 (1867) (equity considers substance over form when procedural missteps are induced by misleading conduct).

IV. **At minimum, the court should remand for a factual determination on the alleged misinformation.**

Buckles’s declaration identifies a specific source of misinformation, a specific statement, and a specific reliance theory. That is enough to require factual inquiry rather than dismissal on the papers. See *Story v. Livingston*, 38 U.S. (13 Pet.) 359, 371 (1839) (equitable claims depending on conduct and reliance require examination of facts); *Railroad Co. v. Ramsey*, 89 U.S. (22 Wall.) 322, 327 (1875) (courts should not foreclose relief where material facts remain unresolved).

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings on equitable tolling and the merits of Buckles’s § 2255 motion.
