**ARGUMENT**

**I. Buckles's Petition for Certiorari Was Timely Because This Court's Order Recalling the Mandate Reset the Ninety-Day Clock.**

A federal conviction becomes final, for purposes of measuring the time to seek certiorari and to file a § 2255 motion, "when this Court affirms a conviction on the merits and denies a timely filed petition for rehearing." *Clay v. United States*, 537 U.S. 522, 527 (2003). Supreme Court Rule 13(3) confirms that where "the lower court appropriately entertains an untimely petition for rehearing," the ninety-day period "runs from the date of the denial of rehearing." Sup. Ct. R. 13(3). The animating principle of Rule 13 is that a litigant should not be forced to seek certiorari while a motion addressed to the very tribunal below remains pending, because the judgment has not achieved the repose that finality requires. *See Clay*, 537 U.S. at 527 (finality attaches only once "the availability of appeal" has been "exhausted").

That principle applies with equal force here. Buckles timely invoked Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e) to request appointment of certiorari counsel. This Court recalled its mandate specifically to adjudicate that request, and did not reissue the mandate until June 20, 2007 — after resolving the motion on June 12, 2007. While the mandate was recalled, Buckles's case remained, in every practical sense, unresolved in this Court; there was no final judgment from which the certiorari clock could sensibly run. Just as Rule 13(3) tolls the certiorari period during the pendency of a properly entertained rehearing petition, the certiorari period should not begin to run while this Court has withdrawn its own mandate to consider whether counsel must be appointed to prosecute the very certiorari petition at issue. To hold otherwise would require a defendant to file for certiorari, or forfeit the right to do so, before this Court has even decided whether he is entitled to counsel to prepare that petition — a result inconsistent with the orderly disposition of appeals that the mandate rule is designed to protect.

Because the recall of the mandate restarted the ninety-day period, Buckles's September 5, 2007 petition, filed within ninety days of the June 20, 2007 reissuance, was timely. A timely certiorari petition means Buckles's conviction did not become final until the Supreme Court denied certiorari on October 15, 2007, *Buckles v. United States*, 552 U.S. 982 (2007), rendering his subsequent § 2255 motion timely under 28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)(1).

**II. In the Alternative, Buckles Is Entitled to Equitable Tolling Because He Diligently Pursued His Rights and Was Misled by This Court's Own Clerk's Office.**

Even if the limitations period is deemed to have run from the original denial of rehearing, Buckles is entitled to equitable tolling. A habeas petitioner is entitled to equitable tolling of a statutory deadline where he shows "(1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way and prevented timely filing." *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010) (quoting *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 418 (2005)). Equitable tolling is available in the § 2255 context for the same reasons it is available under § 2254, because "the same body of equitable principles" governs. *Holland*, 560 U.S. at 649.

Buckles satisfies both prongs. On diligence: he mailed his retained counsel a request for assistance on the very day Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e) required, copied this Court's Clerk, followed up by telephone, and — when counsel proved unresponsive — filed his own pro se "Supplemental Motion for Appointment of Counsel." When his Rule 4-1(e) motion remained unresolved, his sister telephoned the Clerk's office directly to ascertain the governing deadline. Buckles then filed his certiorari petition consistent with the information he received. This is not the conduct of a petitioner who "sat on his rights"; it is the conduct of a petitioner acting with "reasonable diligence" under trying circumstances. *Holland*, 560 U.S. at 653.

On extraordinary circumstances: Buckles alleges that an employee of this Court's own Clerk's office told his sister that "the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued." Misinformation from a court officer regarding a filing deadline, if credited, is precisely the kind of circumstance that can warrant equitable tolling, because it is an external impediment beyond the petitioner's control that induced the very delay at issue. Attorney misconduct or abandonment has likewise been recognized as capable of constituting an extraordinary circumstance justifying tolling where it goes beyond "garden variety" negligence. *See Holland*, 560 U.S. at 651–52. Misleading advice from the tribunal's own Clerk is at least as compelling. Cf. *Irwin v. Dep't of Veterans Affairs*, 498 U.S. 89, 96 (1990) (equitable tolling appropriate where a claimant "has been induced or tricked by his adversary's misconduct into allowing" a filing deadline to pass).

Because the record does not conclusively resolve whether the Clerk's office made the statement Buckles attributes to it, or whether counsel's own conduct independently caused the delay, this Court should, at minimum, remand for an evidentiary hearing to determine whether equitable tolling applies. *See Holland*, 560 U.S. at 654 (remanding for further factual development of an equitable tolling claim). Dismissal of Buckles's § 2255 motion without such a hearing was error.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's dismissal of Buckles's § 2255 motion as untimely, or, in the alternative, remand for an evidentiary hearing on equitable tolling.
