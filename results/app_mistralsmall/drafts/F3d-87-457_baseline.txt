**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS OF CHAPTER 93-121 ARE UNCONSTITUTIONALLY OVERBROAD AND FACIALLY INVALID UNDER THE FIRST AMENDMENT.**

The disclosure provisions of Chapter 93-121 sweep far too broadly, chilling constitutionally protected lobbying activity. As this Court has recognized, "[a] facial challenge to a legislative Act is, of course, the most difficult challenge to mount successfully." *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987). To succeed, the League must demonstrate that the statute’s overbreadth is "substantial," not merely that some applications of the statute may be unconstitutional. *Id.* at 745; *see also Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973) ("[O]verbreadth adjudication has been employed sparingly and only as a last resort.").

Chapter 93-121’s disclosure requirements are facially invalid because they criminalize a substantial amount of protected speech. The statute mandates disclosure of all lobbying expenditures, including "food and beverages, entertainment, research, communication, media advertising, publications, travel, and lodging." Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a). This sweeping requirement encompasses not only direct lobbying expenditures but also incidental or ancillary activities that are plainly within the ambit of First Amendment protection. *See Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, 66 (1976) (recognizing that "expenditures for the purpose of influencing the outcomes of elections" are protected by the First Amendment).

Moreover, the statute’s lack of a de minimis exception or a meaningful scienter requirement exacerbates its overbreadth. Unlike the disclosure provisions upheld in *Buckley*, which were narrowly tailored to apply only to expenditures above a certain threshold, Chapter 93-121 imposes disclosure obligations on even the most minor expenditures. *See id.* at 83-84 (upholding disclosure requirements for expenditures above $100). The absence of such safeguards renders the statute unconstitutionally overbroad. *See Vill. of Schaumburg v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 444 U.S. 620, 637 (1980) (striking down ordinance requiring disclosure of all fundraising activities without a de minimis exception).

**II. THE CONTINGENCY-FEE BAN VIOLATES THE FIRST AMENDMENT’S PROTECTION OF COMMERCIAL SPEECH.**

The contingency-fee ban in Chapter 93-121, Fla. Stat. § 11.047, is an unconstitutional restriction on commercial speech. The Supreme Court has made clear that "commercial speech is constitutionally protected." *Central Hudson Gas & Elec. Corp. v. Public Serv. Comm’n of N.Y.*, 447 U.S. 557, 562-63 (1980). To survive First Amendment scrutiny, a restriction on commercial speech must satisfy the four-part test set forth in *Central Hudson*: (1) the speech must concern lawful activity and not be misleading; (2) the asserted governmental interest must be substantial; (3) the regulation must directly advance the asserted interest; and (4) the regulation must be no more extensive than necessary to serve that interest. *Id.* at 566.

Here, the contingency-fee ban fails the fourth prong of *Central Hudson*. The statute prohibits lobbyists from receiving fees contingent on legislative outcomes, ostensibly to prevent corruption or the appearance of corruption. However, the ban is not narrowly tailored to achieve that goal. As the Supreme Court has recognized, "the First Amendment’s concern for the speech itself is of no less importance than its concern to see that speech is not drowned out by the chilling effect of a censorship regime." *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552, 570 (2011). The contingency-fee ban sweeps too broadly, criminalizing arrangements that pose no real risk of corruption. *See id.* at 571-72 (striking down law restricting data-mining for pharmaceutical marketing as overbroad).

Furthermore, the Eleventh Circuit has previously acknowledged the tension between the contingency-fee ban and modern commercial speech jurisprudence. *See United States v. Nat’l Ass’n of Realtors*, 844 F.2d 1565, 1572 (11th Cir. 1988) (noting that "recent cases cast doubt on the constitutionality" of such bans). This Court is bound by the Supreme Court’s precedents, not its own predictions of how the Court might rule in the future. *See Agostini v. Felton*, 521 U.S. 203, 237 (1997) ("We are bound not only by the holdings of our prior cases, but also by their reasoning."). The contingency-fee ban is unconstitutional under *Central Hudson* and must be struck down.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the disclosure provisions of Chapter 93-121 are unconstitutionally overbroad, and the contingency-fee ban violates the First Amendment’s protection of commercial speech. The judgment of the district court should be reversed.
