Santander is not a “debt collector” when it seeks to collect debts it owns for its own account. The FDCPA’s text draws a basic line between creditors and debt collectors. As the Fourth Circuit correctly recognized, the Act “generally does not regulate creditors, when they collect debt on their own account,” and Santander became a creditor when it purchased the loans before the challenged conduct. Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 135-36 (4th Cir. 2016), aff’d, 137 S. Ct. 1718 (2017).

The statutory definition confirms that result. Section 1692a(6) covers a person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). That language requires collection for a third party, not for one’s own account. Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995). The Act’s structure likewise distinguishes a “creditor,” defined as a person to whom the debt is owed, from a “debt collector.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4), (6). Congress wrote the FDCPA to target “abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors,” not to regulate ordinary creditors collecting their own debts. 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e); see also Selden v. Equitable Trust Co., 94 U.S. 419, 424 (1877) (distinguishing business categories by the nature of the transactions they conduct).

Petitioners’ contrary theory would invert that text by making default status dispositive. But the phrase “debts owed or due another” refers to the debt at the time of collection, not to the identity of the original lender years earlier. Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1318 (11th Cir. 2015); Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013). As the Fourth Circuit explained, “the default status of a debt has no bearing on whether a person qualifies as a debt collector under the threshold definition” in § 1692a(6). Henson, 817 F.3d at 136. Petitioners’ reading would also collapse the distinction Congress preserved by excluding from the definition persons collecting nondefaulted debts for others. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii); see S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977).

Other courts of appeals have drawn the same line. In Ruth v. Triumph Partnerships, 577 F.3d 790, 796-97 (7th Cir. 2009), the Seventh Circuit held that a company collecting debts it owned was not a debt collector merely because it had acquired defaulted accounts. The Sixth Circuit reached the same conclusion in Bridge v. Ocwen Federal Bank, FSB, 681 F.3d 355, 359 (6th Cir. 2012), and the Third Circuit likewise recognized the creditor/debt-collector distinction in FTC v. Check Investors, Inc., 502 F.3d 159, 173 (3d Cir. 2007). The Eleventh Circuit’s analysis in Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1314-18, reinforces that the inquiry focuses on who owns and is collecting the debt at the time of collection.

Petitioners’ attempt to rely on the phrase “owed or due another” is defeated by ordinary grammar. As the Fourth Circuit noted, “the word ‘another’ modifies both ‘owed or due’ and ‘asserted to be owed or due.’” Henson, 817 F.3d at 136-37. That reading follows the ordinary rule that qualifying language applies to all antecedents when the syntax so indicates. Paroline v. United States, 572 U.S. 434, 447 (2014); Porter v. Hall, 328 U.S. 21, 26 (1946). It also fits the statutory context, which defines a debt collector as one who “regularly” collects debts “owed or due another,” not one who once serviced a debt for someone else. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6).

Petitioners’ policy argument fares no better. Congress did not create a loophole; it drew a line between entities collecting for others and entities collecting for themselves. The FDCPA’s purpose is to curb abusive practices by those acting as debt collectors. 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e); Heintz, 514 U.S. at 294. Santander may have collected defaulted accounts, but once CitiFinancial sold the receivables, Santander was collecting debts it owned. A business that “collects its own debts” is not transformed into a debt collector merely because it also engages in other lending or servicing activities. Henson, 817 F.3d at 137; Selden, 94 U.S. at 424. That is especially true where, as here, the complaint alleged that the challenged communications began only after the sale. Under the FDCPA, liability attaches only when a defendant is acting as a debt collector in connection with the challenged conduct. 15 U.S.C. § 1692e; Heintz, 514 U.S. at 294.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
