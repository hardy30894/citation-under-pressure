**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction in any case involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between federal patent policy and the constitutional allocation of judicial power. The Supreme Court of Texas erred in concluding that federal courts possess exclusive subject-matter jurisdiction over petitioner Vernon Minton’s state-law legal malpractice claim simply because the claim *implicates* federal patent law. That conclusion misapplies the Supreme Court’s framework in *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363, 162 L.Ed.2d 257 (2005), and disregards the foundational principle that “federal question jurisdiction exists only where a federal issue is both necessary and substantial to the resolution of the claim.” *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 809, 108 S.Ct. 2166, 100 L.Ed.2d 811 (1988). Here, the patent issue is neither necessary nor substantial to the malpractice claim, and federal jurisdiction would disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. The Court should reverse.

### I. The Patent Issue Is Not Necessary to the Malpractice Claim

Petitioner’s legal malpractice claim rests on state law: the duty of an attorney to exercise reasonable skill and diligence in representing a client. To prevail, Minton must prove that his attorneys’ failure to raise the experimental use exception proximately caused his loss of the patent infringement suit. *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex.2004). This requires a “case within a case” showing that, but for the attorney’s negligence, Minton would have prevailed in federal court. *Schaeffer v. O’Brien*, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex.App.-Eastland 2001, pet. denied). The resolution of that “case within a case” depends not on whether the experimental use exception *applies*, but on whether the attorneys *knew* of the facts that would have made it viable.

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed.Cir.2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed.Cir.2007), are not binding on this Court, and their reasoning is flawed. They treat the mere *existence* of a federal patent issue as sufficient to trigger exclusive federal jurisdiction, ignoring the Supreme Court’s directive in *Grable* that federal jurisdiction requires a federal issue that is “actually disputed and substantial” and that “a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. Here, the experimental use exception was not “actually disputed” at the time of the malpractice suit. The facts—such as the commercial lease terms, the revenue-sharing structure, and the lack of disclosure to Stark—were known to Minton and his attorneys. The exception was not a viable defense under the facts as they existed. Thus, the patent issue is not necessary to the malpractice claim; it is a hypothetical, not a live controversy.

### II. The Patent Issue Is Not Substantial

Even if the patent issue were necessary, it is not substantial. The Supreme Court has made clear that “a federal issue is substantial only if it is of sufficient importance to justify federal adjudication.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. In *Grable*, the Court held that a state-law quiet title action involving a federal tax lien was not substantial because the federal issue “would not affect the validity of the federal tax lien or the federal government’s interest in it.” Id. Similarly, here, the resolution of the experimental use exception would not affect the validity of the patent or the federal government’s interest in patent law uniformity. The patent was already invalidated in federal court. The malpractice claim does not seek to enforce or challenge the patent; it seeks to recover damages for the alleged failure to assert a defense. As the Fifth Circuit recognized in *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 384, 388 (5th Cir.2008), “the mere fact that a federal issue is involved does not make it substantial.” The issue here is “merely incidental” to the malpractice claim. *New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 702 N.W.2d 336, 342 (Neb.2005).

### III. Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities

The final prong of *Grable* requires that federal jurisdiction not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. State courts are well-equipped to adjudicate legal malpractice claims, which are inherently local in nature and involve state standards of professional conduct. To vest exclusive federal jurisdiction in such cases would “open the door to federal jurisdiction in a vast array of state-law claims” that merely touch on federal law. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313, 125 S.Ct. 2363. The Federal Circuit’s approach in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept*—which treats any patent-related malpractice claim as automatically federal—creates precisely that risk. It would allow federal courts to preempt state courts in every case where a patent issue is *potentially* relevant, undermining the federalism principles that *Grable* was designed to protect.
