**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and the judgment should be affirmed. The record in this case presents substantial evidence of Richard Allan Moran’s mental incapacity at the time he discharged his counsel and pleaded guilty—evidence that triggered a good faith doubt about his competency and necessitated a hearing under established precedent. The state court’s failure to conduct such a hearing violated due process, and its subsequent post-conviction findings, based on an erroneous legal standard, cannot cure that constitutional defect.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the waiver of fundamental constitutional rights—such as the right to counsel and the right to a jury trial—must be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243, 89 S.Ct. 1709, 1712, 23 L.Ed.2d 274 (1969). This standard is not satisfied by mere surface compliance with procedural formalities. Instead, due process demands “utmost solicitude” from the court when a defendant seeks to waive such rights. *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir.1973) (citing *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 243, 89 S.Ct. at 1712). The Court has emphasized that “a waiver of constitutional rights is not to be presumed from a silent record,” and that “the burden is on the State to show that the waiver was made with an understanding of the nature of the charges, the statutory penalties, and the consequences of the plea.” *McCarthy v. United States*, 394 U.S. 459, 466, 89 S.Ct. 1166, 1171, 22 L.Ed.2d 418 (1969).

Crucially, the standard for competency to waive counsel or plead guilty is not the same as the standard for competency to stand trial. While a defendant need only possess a “rational and factual understanding” of the proceedings and the ability to assist counsel to be competent to stand trial, *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 95 S.Ct. 896, 904, 43 L.Ed.2d 103 (1975), the law requires a higher threshold for waiving constitutional rights: the capacity for “reasoned choice.” *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir.1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871, 109 S.Ct. 182, 102 L.Ed.2d 151 (1988); *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir.1986) (hereinafter *Evans II*); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir.1981). This “reasoned choice” standard demands that the defendant understand the nature and consequences of the alternatives available to him—including the risks of proceeding pro se, the potential for mitigating evidence, and the finality of a guilty plea—and be able to make a deliberate, informed decision among them. *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214.

The record here demonstrates that Moran lacked this capacity. He had recently attempted suicide, was hospitalized, and was taking four psychotropic medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—all of which affect cognition, mood, and motor function. *See* *Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics*, 7th ed. (1985) (citing *Physicians’ Desk Reference*). The court was informed that Moran was on medication, yet made no inquiry into its effects. This failure to investigate is fatal. As this Court has held, “a court must initiate a competency hearing, on its own motion if necessary, whenever it has or should have a good faith doubt about a defendant’s competency.” *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (citing *Evans I*, 705 F.2d 1479 (9th Cir.1973); *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518). The presence of suicidal ideation, recent self-harm, and polypharmacy constitutes substantial evidence of incompetence sufficient to trigger such a duty.

Moreover, Moran’s conduct at the plea hearing was inconsistent with reasoned choice. His responses were monosyllabic, and he expressed a desire to prevent any mitigating evidence from being presented—despite the fact that such evidence could have spared him the death penalty. This self-destructive motive, combined with his mental state, suggests not a reasoned decision, but a disoriented or coerced surrender. *See* *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir.1981), cert. denied, 456 U.S. 946, 102 S.Ct. 2013, 72 L.Ed.2d 469 (1982) (a good faith doubt arises when the record shows “substantial evidence of incompetence”).

The state court’s post-conviction ruling, which relied solely on psychiatric evaluations conducted to determine competency to stand trial, is legally insufficient. Those evaluations were not designed to assess “reasoned choice” and said nothing about the effects of medication or Moran’s ability to weigh alternatives. *See* *Evans I*, 705 F.2d at 1480 (competency to waive counsel requires a higher standard than competency to stand trial).
