**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CLAIM DOES NOT “ARISE OUT OF” FORD’S MONTANA ACTIVITIES**

The Due Process Clause requires that a court have specific jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only if the plaintiff’s claim “arise[s] out of or relate[s] to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum state. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980). Here, the claim does not arise out of Ford’s activities in Montana. The accident was caused by a manufacturing defect in the vehicle’s tire, not by Ford’s act of advertising or selling parts in Montana. The vehicle was sold in Washington State and Oregon before it ever reached Montana. The Montana sale was a mere resale by a third party, and the injury occurred in Montana solely because the vehicle was driven there. The claim is rooted in the product’s design and manufacturing, not in Ford’s specific commercial activities within the state.

**II. THE CLAIM DOES NOT “RELATE TO” FORD’S MONTANA ACTIVITIES**

Even if the claim does not “arise out of” the defendant’s contacts, it must still “relate to” those contacts to support specific jurisdiction. *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297. The “stream of commerce” theory is not a shortcut to jurisdiction; the claim must still have a substantial connection to the defendant’s forum-related activities. *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 925 (2011). Ford’s contacts with Montana—advertising and selling parts—do not create a substantial connection to the claim that a tire defect caused an accident in Montana. The claim is about the product’s condition at the time of the accident, not about the Montana sales transaction. The Montana Supreme Court erred in finding jurisdiction based on the mere availability of Ford’s products in the state. As the Supreme Court has held, a defendant’s contacts with the forum must be such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). Here, Ford’s contacts with Montana are too attenuated to satisfy this standard.

**III. FORD IS NOT “AT HOME” IN MONTANA**

Finally, the Montana Supreme Court’s decision improperly conflates general jurisdiction with specific jurisdiction. A corporation is “at home” in a state only for purposes of general jurisdiction where it is incorporated or maintains its principal place of business. *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117, 128 (2014). Ford is incorporated in Delaware and maintains its headquarters in Michigan. It is not “at home” in Montana. While a state may have specific jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant, it cannot assert general jurisdiction over a corporation that is not “at home” there. The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on Ford’s general availability in the state to find specific jurisdiction is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s holding in *Daimler*. Because Ford’s contacts with Montana are insufficient to support specific jurisdiction, the district court erred in denying Ford’s motion to dismiss.
