### ARGUMENT

Selam is entitled to habeas relief because the tribal proceedings violated rights secured by the Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”), 25 U.S.C. § 1302. The district court erred in concluding both that Selam was not denied compulsory process and that his Confrontation Clause claim was waived.

#### I. Selam was denied compulsory process.

ICRA guarantees that no Indian tribe shall “deny to any person in a criminal proceeding the right, at his own expense, to have the assistance of counsel for his defense” and related due-process protections. 25 U.S.C. § 1302. This Court has long recognized that fundamental fairness in criminal adjudication requires a meaningful opportunity to present a defense. See Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 294 (1973) (“The rights to confront and cross-examine witnesses and to call witnesses in one’s own behalf have long been recognized as essential to due process.”); Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967) (the right to present witnesses “is a fundamental element of due process of law”).

Here, the record shows that Selam was advised of his right to compel witnesses, but the practical reality was that he was not able to secure the testimony necessary to defend against the charges. He provided counsel with five potential character witnesses; counsel located three, two of whom stated their testimony would be damaging. No defense witnesses were presented. In a case turning on credibility, the denial of a meaningful ability to secure witnesses is constitutionally significant. Washington, 388 U.S. at 19; Chambers, 410 U.S. at 294.

The district court’s ruling effectively converted a theoretical right into a hollow one. A defendant’s failure to ask for subpoenas does not forfeit compulsory process where the surrounding circumstances show the defense lacked a meaningful opportunity to investigate and present witnesses. See Crane v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986) (the Constitution guarantees “a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense”). Selam was represented by a lay spokesperson, not licensed counsel. He was incarcerated in tribal proceedings, had difficulty assisting in witness contact, and was left to rely on a process that did not yield any favorable defense testimony. Under these circumstances, the tribal court’s failure to ensure that Selam could present witnesses deprived him of a fair opportunity to defend himself. See Chambers, 410 U.S. at 302-03.

At minimum, the habeas record required careful scrutiny of whether the tribal courts afforded Selam an effective opportunity to obtain witnesses. The district court’s contrary conclusion should be reversed.

#### II. Selam did not waive his Confrontation Clause claim.

The district court also erred in holding that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by not presenting it to the Warm Springs Tribal Court of Appeals. Exhaustion in the tribal-court context is a prudential doctrine, not a jurisdictional bar, and federal courts retain authority to consider unexhausted claims where the interests of justice so require. See National Farmers Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow Tribe of Indians, 471 U.S. 845, 856-57 (1985); Iowa Mutual Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 19-20 (1987).

Nothing in the tribal appellate dismissal establishes a knowing, intentional waiver of the Confrontation Clause claim. Waiver of a constitutional right is not lightly presumed. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver is “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”). Selam raised his constitutional challenge in federal habeas proceedings after the tribal conviction became final. The district court treated the failure to present the claim in tribal appellate proceedings as an absolute forfeiture, but that approach is inconsistent with the flexible nature of tribal habeas review recognized by the Supreme Court. National Farmers Union, 471 U.S. at 856-57; Iowa Mutual, 480 U.S. at 19-20.

Moreover, the Confrontation Clause claim goes to the core of the reliability of the conviction. In the second trial, the five-year-old victim did not testify. Instead, the prosecution introduced her statements through four witnesses. The right of confrontation is designed to preserve the truth-seeking function of criminal trials. See Pointer v. Texas, 380 U.S. 400, 403-05 (1965). Where the State relies heavily on hearsay statements in place of live testimony, a defendant’s confrontation objection is not a technicality; it is a challenge to the integrity of the adjudication itself. See Idaho v. Wright, 497 U.S. 805, 813-16 (1990).

Because Selam’s confrontation claim was not knowingly waived, and because the tribal proceedings implicate fundamental rights protected by ICRA, the district court should have reached the merits and granted relief. At a minimum, the court’s waiver ruling should be reversed and the case remanded for merits consideration.

#### CONCLUSION

The judgment denying the petition for habeas corpus should be reversed. Selam was denied a meaningful opportunity to present witnesses in his defense, and his Confrontation Clause claim was improperly deemed waived. The Court should remand with instructions to grant appropriate habeas relief.
