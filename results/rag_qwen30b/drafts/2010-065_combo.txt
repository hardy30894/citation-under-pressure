**ARGUMENT**

Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. The principle that private individuals may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to attack federal legislation as an overreach of federal power is firmly established in our jurisprudence. This Court has long held that the Tenth Amendment does not confer standing upon private parties to litigate federalism concerns. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939), the Supreme Court explicitly rejected a challenge to federal action by private utility companies, stating: “The sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment. As we have seen, there is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states, and, if this were not so, the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” This holding remains binding precedent and controls the outcome here.

Bond’s attempt to circumvent *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* by asserting that the Tenth Amendment protects individual rights is foreclosed by *Heller v. Connecticut*, 389 U.S. 902 (1967), where the Court emphasized that constitutional rights are not self-executing in the absence of a party with a direct stake in the outcome. The Court observed that “the question of voluntariness was conclusively resolved against the petitioner by the verdict of the jury at trial,” and that the constitutional claims raised were not cognizable in the absence of a proper party. Similarly, in *Giaccio v. Pennsylvania*, 382 U.S. 399, 401 (1965), the Court held that a defendant could not challenge a state statute imposing costs upon acquittal unless he had a personal stake in the outcome. The Court stated: “The Act of 1860, set out below, provides among other things that: ‘. . . in all cases of acquittals by the petit jury on indictments for [offenses other than felonies], the jury trying the same shall determine, by their verdict, whether the county, or the prosecutor, or the defendant shall pay the costs.’” The Court did not entertain the constitutional challenge because the defendant lacked standing to assert a generalized grievance.

The principle that standing requires a concrete injury is further reinforced by *Lang v. New Jersey*, 209 U.S. 467, 475 (1908), where the Court held that “the State, separates criminal defendants into classes, to wit, those who are accused before the finding of the indictment and those who are accused afterwards, giving to the first a privilege of challenge which is denied to the second.” The Court rejected the claim that this classification violated the Equal Protection Clause because the plaintiff failed to demonstrate a personal injury. The Court reasoned that “the protection of the statute was addressed to the furtherance of the due and efficient administration of justice for the protection of those against whom crimes might be committed as well as those who” were accused. Thus, the plaintiff’s interest was too abstract to support standing.

This Court has consistently held that a private party cannot assert the Tenth Amendment as a shield against federal criminal prosecution. In *Premier-Pabst Sales Co. v. Grosscup*, 298 U.S. 226, 230 (1936), the Court affirmed that “the discrimination complained of” in a state licensing statute was not actionable by a private corporation unless it could demonstrate a direct and personal injury. The Court stated: “The case was heard before three judges upon application for a preliminary injunction. The facts were stipulated; and it was agreed that the hearing should be deemed also a final hearing upon the application for a permanent injunction. The court denied the injunctions and dismissed the bill as wanting in equity.” The Court emphasized that “the discrimination complained of” did not confer standing upon the plaintiff.

The same principle applies here. Bond’s claim that § 229 violates federalism is not a personal injury but a generalized grievance about the scope of federal power. In *Jenkins v. McKeithen*, 395 U.S. 411, 414 (1969), the Court held that “the other matters sought to be raised in the complaint were merely potential defenses to the pending criminal charges and that appellant had not alleged any basis for restraining prosecution of those charges.” The Court concluded that the plaintiff lacked standing to challenge the constitutionality of a state law in the absence of a direct injury. Similarly, in *Davis v. North Carolina*, 384 U.S. 737, 740 (1966), the Court emphasized that “the question whether it was not a crime within the statute was one which the District Court was competent to decide.” The Court held that the issue was properly before the court, but only because the defendant had a personal stake in the outcome.

The principle that standing requires a personal stake is also reflected in *Serra v. Mortiga*, 204 U.S. 470, 472 (1907), where the Court stated: “Whether an act charged in an indictment is or is not a crime by the law which the court administers [in this case the statute law of the United States], is a question which has to be met at almost every stage of criminal proceedings; on motions to quash the indictment, on demurrers, on motions to arrest judgment, etc. The court may err, but it has jurisdiction of the question.” The Court held that the issue was properly before the court because the defendant had a personal stake in the outcome. In *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581, 590 (1900), the Court reiterated that “the claim was made in the case (and referred to in the opinion) that the adoption of the Fourteenth Amendment provided an additional security to the individual against oppression by the States themselves, and limited their powers to the same extent as the amendments theretofore adopted.” The Court rejected the claim because the defendant had not demonstrated a personal injury.
