**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Appeals correctly held that a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) is subject to harmless-error review and that such error is harmless where the defendant possessed actual knowledge of his appeal right and elected not to appeal. Rule 32(a)(2) requires the court to “advise the defendant of any right to appeal the sentence.” The rule is designed to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights. It does not create a substantive right independent of the defendant’s knowledge. When the defendant already knows of his right, the purpose of the rule is fully served, and no prejudice flows from the omission.

**I. A violation of Rule 32(a)(2) is not a structural error and is subject to harmless-error analysis.**

This Court has consistently held that procedural errors do not warrant automatic reversal unless they fall within a narrow class of structural defects that undermine the framework of the trial. *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 309–310 (1991). The failure to advise a defendant of his appeal right is not such a defect. It does not affect the integrity of the guilt-determining process, nor does it deprive the defendant of a fair trial. *See United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 784 (1979) (holding that a formal violation of Rule 11 is not grounds for collateral relief absent a showing of prejudice). Accordingly, the harmless-error standard of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) applies. *See United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993) (holding that unpreserved errors are subject to plain-error review, but preserved errors are reviewed for harmlessness). Here, petitioner preserved his claim, so harmless-error review is appropriate.

**II. The error was harmless because petitioner knew of his right to appeal and chose not to appeal.**

The district court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that petitioner “knew of his right to appeal when the sentencing hearing occurred.” The Court of Appeals affirmed that finding, concluding that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) “had been served since Peguero was aware of his right to appeal.” Where a defendant has actual knowledge of his appeal right, the failure to provide the formal advisory works no prejudice. *Cf. Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (holding that a violation of Rule 32(a) regarding the right of allocution does not warrant habeas relief absent a showing of prejudice). In *Hill*, the Court emphasized that the rule “is not a rigid formula” and that the defendant suffered no prejudice because he had nothing to say. *Id.* at 428–429. Similarly, here, petitioner had full knowledge of his appeal right; he simply chose not to exercise it. There is no claim that the omission caused him to forgo a meritorious appeal or that he would have appealed had the court advised him. The error therefore had no effect on his substantial rights.

**III. This Court has rejected per se habeas relief for technical rule violations.**

In *Timmreck*, the Court held that a Rule 11 violation—failure to properly advise a defendant of the consequences of a guilty plea—did not, standing alone, entitle the defendant to habeas relief. 441 U.S. at 784. The Court reasoned that collateral relief is available only when the error “resulted in a complete miscarriage of justice” or “was inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure.” *Id.* at 783–784. The same standard applies here. The district court’s failure to recite the advisory was a technical omission, not a fundamental defect. Because petitioner possessed actual knowledge, the proceedings were fundamentally fair. *See United States v. Morrison*, 449 U.S. 361, 364 (1981) (holding that even constitutional violations do not require “automatic reversal” if the defendant suffered no prejudice).

**IV. The Court of Appeals correctly applied harmless-error review.**

The Court of Appeals engaged in the proper inquiry: it examined whether the error “affected substantial rights.” *See Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 637 (1993) (harmless-error standard on habeas review requires a showing of “actual prejudice”). Petitioner failed to show that the omission caused him to lose an appeal he would have taken. His habeas motion sought only to vacate his sentence, not to appeal. Indeed, he waited over three years after sentencing to raise the claim, demonstrating that the lack of a formal advisory did not deprive him of the opportunity to appeal. The error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
