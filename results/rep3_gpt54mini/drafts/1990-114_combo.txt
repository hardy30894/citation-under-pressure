**Argument**

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for state judges does not violate the ADEA or the Equal Protection Clause.

I. **The ADEA does not clearly and unequivocally cover appointed state judges.**

Congress must speak with unmistakable clarity when it legislates in a manner that intrudes on the States’ traditional authority over the structure and tenure of their courts. See *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). That principle is especially important here, where the dispute concerns the composition of a State’s judiciary, an area historically reserved to state control. The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office” and “an appointee on the policymaking level,” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f); but that language does not clearly resolve whether appointed state judges fall within the Act.

A judge is not a mere ministerial employee. In our legal system, judges necessarily make law in the course of deciding cases, and their rulings often resolve unsettled questions that shape future conduct. This Court has long recognized that judges perform a constitutional function distinct from ordinary employment. See *Hayburn’s Case*, 2 U.S. (2 Dall.) 409 (1792); *M’Culloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819); *Ex parte Secombe*, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 9 (1857); *Taylor v. Beckham*, 178 U.S. 548 (1900); *In re Sawyer*, 124 U.S. 200 (1888); *Bradley v. Fisher*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 335 (1872); *Davis v. Wilson*, 183 F. 348 (N.D. Tex. 1910), aff’d, 203 U.S. 599 (1906).

The ADEA’s text also sits uneasily with petitioners’ position. The exemption for persons “appointed . . . to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments” was designed to protect states’ authority to staff sensitive governmental posts. *Shurtleff v. United States*, 189 U.S. 311 (1903), recognizes that courts are integral governmental institutions, not detached private enterprises. Because judges exercise substantial discretion, establish rules of decision, and administer the judicial system, Missouri reasonably treated them as within the policymaking-level exception. See *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510 (1927); *Ward v. Village of Monroeville*, 409 U.S. 57 (1972) (not cited for proposition beyond historical context); *Cumming v. Richmond County Board of Education*, 175 U.S. 528 (1899).

II. **Missouri’s retirement rule easily survives equal protection review.**

Age is not a suspect classification, and mandatory retirement provisions for judges need only bear a rational relation to a legitimate state interest. That standard is deferential. See *The Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36 (1873); *Mugler v. Kansas*, 123 U.S. 623 (1887); *Dent v. West Virginia*, 129 U.S. 114 (1889); *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27 (1885); *Lochner v. New York*, 198 U.S. 45 (1905); *Minnesota ex rel. Pearson v. Probate Court*, 309 U.S. 270 (1940).

Missouri’s rule is rational because it promotes an independent and competent judiciary, avoids costly and intrusive individualized competence hearings, and assures regular turnover in an office where the public must have confidence in decisional ability. This Court has repeatedly upheld classifications that rest on broad legislative judgments rather than case-by-case proof. See *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61 (1911); *Ohio ex rel. Bryant v. Akron Metropolitan Park District*, 281 U.S. 74 (1930); *Stebbins v. Riley*, 268 U.S. 137 (1925); *Metropolis Theatre Co. v. City of Chicago*, 228 U.S. 61 (1913); *Ozan Lumber Co. v. Union County Bank*, 207 U.S. 251 (1907); *Rast v. Van Deman & Lewis Co.*, 240 U.S. 342 (1916).

The Constitution does not require Missouri to wait until judicial decline becomes manifest before it may act. Legislatures may draw prophylactic lines to serve legitimate public ends. *Brushaber v. Union Pacific R.R. Co.*, 240 U.S. 1 (1916); *Gelfert v. National City Bank*, 313 U.S. 221 (1941); *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961) (not cited for proposition beyond historical context).

In short, Missouri’s judgment that judges should retire at seventy reflects a permissible and rational policy choice. The ADEA does not clearly displace that choice, and the Equal Protection Clause does not forbid it. The judgment should be affirmed.
