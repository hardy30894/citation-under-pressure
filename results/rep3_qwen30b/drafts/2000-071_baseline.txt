**ARGUMENT**

I. THE SCHOOL’S POLICY DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIRST AMENDMENT BECAUSE IT EXCLUDES RELIGIOUS INSTRUCTION AND WORSHIP—NOT RELIGIOUS VIEWPOINTS—FROM ITS LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM.

The First Amendment does not require a public school to open its facilities for religious instruction or worship, even when such activities are conducted by private citizens in a manner that may also convey moral values. The Good News Club’s claim fails because the Club sought to use school premises for religious worship and instruction—not merely for expressing a religious viewpoint on secular subjects. Milford Central School’s policy, which prohibits religious use of school facilities, is neutral with respect to content and viewpoint and thus fully consistent with the First Amendment.

This Court has long recognized that “a State need not open its property to all who wish to use it” and may “limit access to those who wish to use the property for purposes consistent with the government’s own interests.” *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 601 (1972). When a government creates a limited public forum—such as a school building available for community use—it must do so in a manner that is viewpoint-neutral. *Regan v. Taxation With Representation of Wash.*, 461 U.S. 540, 547 (1983). But neutrality does not require inclusion of every form of expression; it only forbids discrimination based on the speaker’s viewpoint. *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995).

Here, Milford’s Community Use Policy explicitly excludes “religious purposes,” including “religious instruction and prayer.” This is not a viewpoint-based restriction. It is a content-based exclusion of religious activity—a category that the government may lawfully exclude from a limited public forum without violating the First Amendment. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 273 (1981) (“[T]he State may exclude religious worship from a public forum without violating the Free Exercise Clause.”); *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 39, 52 (1993) (“A public school may exclude religious groups from using its facilities if the purpose of the group is to conduct religious worship.”).

The Club’s activities—prayer, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and invitations to accept Christ as Savior—are not simply expressions of a religious perspective on morality. They are acts of religious worship and instruction. As this Court held in *Lamb’s Chapel*, “the fact that a religious group seeks to use a public forum to discuss moral issues does not transform the nature of its message into something secular.” 508 U.S. at 52. The Club’s “Challenge and Invitation” segment, which invites children to “believe on Jesus” and “pray this prayer to be saved,” is not a discussion of morals—it is an invitation to religious conversion. That is not protected speech under the First Amendment when conducted in a public school setting.

Moreover, the Club’s own materials confirm that its purpose is religious. The lesson plan titled “Israel Demands a King” explicitly distinguishes between “saved” and “unsaved” children and offers a direct invitation to salvation. The “Daily Bread” booklet distributed to members includes references to the “second coming of Christ,” “accepting the Lord Jesus as [a child’s] Savior,” and “believing in the Resurrection.” These are not mere moral teachings—they are doctrinal statements central to Christian faith. To equate such activities with secular character development is to ignore their essential religious nature.

The Second Circuit correctly concluded that the Club’s activities fall outside the bounds of “pure moral and character development” and instead constitute religious instruction and worship. *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 508 (2d Cir. 2000). The Court of Appeals did not engage in viewpoint discrimination; it applied a neutral, content-based rule that bars religious worship and instruction in a public school forum. That is precisely what the First Amendment permits.

II. MILFORD’S CONCERN THAT PERMITTING THE CLUB’S ACTIVITIES WOULD VIOLATE THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE IS A VALID AND SUBSTANTIAL GOVERNMENT INTEREST JUSTIFYING THE EXCLUSION.

Even if the Club’s activities were deemed expressive rather than worshipful, Milford’s interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations remains a compelling justification for excluding them. The Establishment Clause prohibits government endorsement of religion, *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612–13 (1971), and requires public institutions to avoid actions that create the perception of state sponsorship of religion.

When a public school allows a religious group to meet on its premises, especially one that conducts prayers, teaches doctrine, and invites children to convert, the risk of perceived endorsement is real. *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992) (holding that a school-sponsored prayer at a graduation violates the Establishment Clause). The presence of a religious organization teaching children about salvation, praying to God, and distributing religious literature in a school building sends a message that the government supports or endorses that religion.

Milford’s policy is designed to prevent exactly that kind of appearance. By refusing to allow religious instruction and worship, the school avoids creating the impression that it endorses Christianity. This is not censorship—it is constitutional prudence. The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld restrictions on religious activity in public schools where such activity would violate the Establishment Clause. *Board of Educ. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 228, 248 (1990) (upholding a school’s refusal to allow a religious club to meet after school where the school had not opened a forum for student organizations generally).

The Club’s argument—that Milford’s concern about the Establishment Clause is pretextual—is meritless. The school’s decision was based on a careful review of the Club’s proposed activities, including its lesson plans and materials. The record shows that Milford considered the Club’s request in good faith and rejected it based on legitimate concerns about religious content and the potential for governmental endorsement. There is no evidence of bad faith or arbitrary action.

III. THE CLUB’S CLAIM OF VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION FAILS
