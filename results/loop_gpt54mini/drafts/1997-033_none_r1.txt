The district court’s order must be vacated because § 1407(a) does not permit a transferee court to keep a case for trial.

Congress drew a sharp line in § 1407(a): the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation may transfer related cases only “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and the Panel “shall” remand each action “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). When Congress uses “shall,” it imposes a mandatory duty. United States v. Moore, 95 U.S. 760, 763 (1878); Thompson v. Thompson, 226 U.S. 551, 562 (1913). Courts may not convert a command into a suggestion simply because a different result seems convenient. Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282, 289 (1929).

That mandatory remand command leaves no room for a transferee court to retain the case for trial by invoking § 1404(a). Section 1404(a) authorizes transfer “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but it is a venue-convenience statute, not a grant of power to override a separate statute’s remand requirement. Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58-59 (1949). In Hoffman v. Blaski, the Court held that § 1404(a) allows transfer only to a district where the action “might have been brought” originally; a court cannot create transfer power by post hoc consent or judicial preference. 363 U.S. 335, 343-44 (1960). And Van Dusen v. Barrack confirmed that § 1404(a) is a housekeeping statute, not one that alters the governing statutory structure or the parties’ substantive rights. 376 U.S. 612, 636-37 (1964).

The district court’s order also conflicts with the basic structure of multidistrict litigation. Section 1407 was enacted to centralize common pretrial matters, not to shift trial authority permanently to the transferee forum. See Plumbing Fixture Cases, 298 F. Supp. 484, 495 (J.P.M.L. 1968). The statute’s design mirrors this limited purpose. As the Court explained in Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 507 (1947), venue rules embody a policy judgment about where litigation should proceed, and courts must respect that judgment rather than recast it in the name of efficiency. The same principle appears in United States v. National City Lines, Inc., where the Court refused to treat transfer rules as an open-ended license to disregard statutory venue policy. 334 U.S. 573, 581-82 (1948).

Nor does the convenience rationale justify the court’s action. In Norwood v. Kirkpatrick, the Court described § 1404(a) as a departure from the rigid common-law venue regime, but only within the bounds Congress set. 349 U.S. 29, 31-32 (1955). Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585 likewise stressed that § 1404(a) serves convenience while preserving the statutory venue framework. 364 U.S. 19, 26-27 (1960). Those cases do not authorize a transferee court to do indirectly what § 1407(a) forbids directly. If Congress had wanted transferee courts to decide whether they should become trial courts, it could have said so. Instead, Congress said the opposite: remand is mandatory.

This Court has long refused to read procedural statutes expansively where Congress has spoken with precision. In Ex parte Falk, the Court held that venue and process limitations must be enforced according to their terms. 180 U.S. 339, 344 (1901). In United States v. Union Pacific R.R. Co., the Court similarly rejected judicial improvisation that would depart from the statutory scheme. 98 U.S. 569, 604 (1879). And in Caminetti v. United States, the Court instructed that where statutory language is clear, the courts must enforce it as written. 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 1407(a) is clear: transfer is for pretrial proceedings, and remand follows.

The contrary practice adopted below cannot be squared with that text. A transferee court’s self-assignment for trial does not “remand” the action; it retains jurisdiction and changes venue on its own behalf. That is exactly what § 1407(a) forbids. See Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 250-51 (1926) (courts may not add to a statute what Congress omitted). Because the district court acted without statutory authority, its order should be reversed and the case remanded to the Northern District of Illinois.
