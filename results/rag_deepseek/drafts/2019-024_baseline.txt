ARGUMENT

**I. The Ninth Circuit’s “actual awareness” standard misconstrues the text of § 1113(2).**

Section 1113(2) bars an ERISA action commenced more than three years after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The “breach or violation” is the fiduciary’s act—here, the decision to allocate plan assets to alternative investments. The statute does not require knowledge of the legal conclusion that the act was imprudent. As the Ninth Circuit held in *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985), the limitations period “is triggered by the [trustees’] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law.” A plaintiff who knows the facts of the fiduciary’s conduct has “actual knowledge of the breach,” even if he does not know that the conduct gives rise to a claim.

The Ninth Circuit departed from this principle by requiring Sulyma to know both “that those investments occurred, and that they were imprudent.” The second requirement is a knowledge-of-the-law requirement in disguise—precisely what *Blanton* rejected. It is also inconsistent with the objective standard of prudence in 29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1), which requires fiduciaries to exercise “the care, skill, prudence, and diligence . . . that a prudent man acting in a like capacity and familiar with such matters would use.” Whether an investment was imprudent is an objective question; a plaintiff’s subjective view of the investment’s prudence is irrelevant to the merits and should be equally irrelevant to the limitations inquiry.

**II. The Ninth Circuit erred by allowing Sulyma’s self-serving testimony of non-recollection to defeat summary judgment.**

The Ninth Circuit held that Sulyma’s declaration that he was “unaware” of the alternative investments and did “not recall” seeing the disclosures created a genuine dispute of material fact. That holding is wrong.

First, it conflates “actual knowledge” with “current recollection.” The record shows that Sulyma accessed the very websites on which the disclosures were hosted. The Fund Fact Sheets disclosed both the fact of the alternative investments and the basic strategy behind them. A plaintiff who has accessed information has “actual knowledge” of that information as a matter of law; his later failure to recall it does not retroactively erase that knowledge. To hold otherwise would allow any plaintiff to manufacture a factual dispute by asserting a failure of memory, making summary judgment virtually impossible in any ERISA case where the defendant made disclosures.

Second, the Ninth Circuit’s rule rewards willful blindness. A plaintiff who chooses not to read the disclosures that the defendant provided cannot claim that he lacked “actual knowledge” of the information those disclosures contained. This Court has recognized that ERISA’s limitations periods serve the interests of certainty and repose. *See Heimeshoff v. Hartford Life & Accident Ins. Co.*, 571 U.S. 99 (2013). The three-year period in § 1113(2) reflects Congress’s judgment that fiduciaries should not be exposed to stale claims. The Ninth Circuit’s decision undermines that judgment by permitting a plaintiff to extend the limitations period indefinitely by asserting ignorance of documents he had every opportunity to read.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s standard reads the three-year limitations period out of the statute.**

Congress enacted § 1113 with two distinct limitations periods: a six-year period running from the date of the breach, and a three-year period running from the date of actual knowledge. 29 U.S.C. § 1113(1), (2). The three-year period would be superfluous if it required knowledge of the legal conclusion of breach, because a plaintiff who knows the facts of the fiduciary’s conduct could always wait until the six-year period expired and then claim he only recently learned that the conduct was imprudent. The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation thus “essentially reads the ‘actual knowledge’ standard out of the statute.” *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509, 520 (9th Cir. 1991).

This Court has held that ERISA imposes on fiduciaries “a duty of care with respect to the management of existing trust funds, along with liability for breach of that duty.” *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink*, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996). But that duty is not without limit. The balance Congress struck in § 1113(2) is that a fiduciary who discloses all relevant information to a participant is entitled to repose after three years. The Ninth Circuit’s decision upsets that balance and exposes fiduciaries to liability for conduct that was fully disclosed years earlier.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
