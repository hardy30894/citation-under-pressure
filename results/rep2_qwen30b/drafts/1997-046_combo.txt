I. An inmate possesses a constitutionally protected liberty interest in clemency proceedings under the Fourteenth Amendment, and Ohio’s clemency process, by creating a meaningful opportunity for relief from a death sentence, triggers due process protections.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects “life or liberty” interests that are not merely created by statute, but are “fundamental” in nature and “deeply rooted in our traditions and conscience.” *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). While the Supreme Court has long recognized that the power to grant clemency is a sovereign prerogative, it has also consistently held that when a state creates a procedure that may affect a person’s liberty, due process applies. In *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86, 97 (1923), the Court emphasized that “the right to a fair trial is a fundamental right” and that “no state may deprive a person of liberty without due process of law.” This principle extends not only to the trial itself but to any stage of the criminal process where the state’s action may result in a deprivation of liberty.

In *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. 303, 315 (1946), the Court stated that “the right to be free from arbitrary governmental action is a basic element of due process,” and that “the Constitution protects the individual against arbitrary or capricious action by the government.” When a state establishes a clemency process that may result in the commutation of a death sentence, it creates a liberty interest that is not merely procedural but substantive. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency review is not a mere formality; it is a mechanism through which the state may alter the finality of a death sentence. As such, it implicates a liberty interest protected by the Fourteenth Amendment.
