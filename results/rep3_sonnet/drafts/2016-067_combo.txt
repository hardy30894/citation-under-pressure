ARGUMENT

I. The Plain Text of the FDCPA Confines "Debt Collector" Status to Those Who Collect Debts Owed to Others.

The starting point of statutory construction is the text Congress enacted. As this Court has long recognized, "[t]he intention of the legislature is to be collected from the words they employ. Where there is no ambiguity in the words, there is no room for construction." United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95-96 (1820). Section 1692a(6) defines a "debt collector," in relevant part, as one "who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). That language is unambiguous: it requires that the debt be owed to someone other than the collector itself. When "the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning[,] the duty of interpretation does not arise." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Santander, having purchased petitioners' accounts outright, collected debts owed to itself—not "to another." It therefore falls outside the plain text of the operative definition.

Petitioners attempt to avoid this conclusion by treating the word "another" as modifying only the phrase "owed or due," while ignoring its equal application to "asserted to be owed or due." That reading violates the settled rule that "[w]hen several words are followed by a clause which is applicable as much to the first and other words as to the last, the natural construction of the language demands that the clause be read as applicable to all." Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920). Under that rule, "another" governs the entire clause, confirming that Congress asked whether the debt is owed to a third party at the time of collection—not whether it once was.

II. The Structure of Section 1692a(6) Confirms that Exclusions Cannot Expand the Definition's Coverage.

Petitioners' theory—that a purchaser of defaulted debt must be a "debt collector" because it does not fit within the creditor exclusion—inverts the statute's structure. Exclusionary clauses narrow an already-satisfied definition; they do not themselves create liability. This Court has consistently held that specific statutory carve-outs take their meaning from, and cannot override, the general terms they qualify. See Russell Motor Car Co. v. United States, 261 U.S. 514, 519-20 (1923) (construing enumerated statutory terms according to their association with surrounding words and general purpose). Section 1692a(6)(F)(iii)'s exclusion for debts "not in default at the time it was obtained" protects loan servicers of current accounts; it does not convert every purchaser of defaulted debt into a "debt collector" merely because the exclusion does not apply to it. A party must first satisfy the affirmative definition before any exclusion is relevant at all.

III. Petitioners' Interpretation Would Extend the Act Beyond Its Evident Purpose.

Even were the text less clear, resort to purpose confirms the same result. "There is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes," United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), but where necessary this Court has also looked to legislative history to illuminate an otherwise technical scheme. See Helvering v. Stockholms Enskilda Bank, 293 U.S. 84, 93-94 (1934). Here both sources point the same direction. The Senate Report accompanying the FDCPA explained that Congress meant to reach "third persons who regularly collect debts for others," not creditors collecting their own accounts. S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977). Congress's purpose was to check abuses by professional collection agents who have no direct stake in the underlying debt and thus no natural incentive toward accuracy or restraint—not to regulate every finance company that purchases receivables and thereafter services them as owner. Reading "debt collector" to include any assignee of a once-defaulted account, regardless of its status as owner, would sweep in ordinary creditors and stretch the statute well beyond the "spirit" Congress intended, even if some isolated language might be read otherwise. Cf. Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) ("a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers").

IV. Petitioners' Reading Would Also Violate the Settled Rule that Statutory Exemptions and Exclusions Are Construed According to Their Terms, Not Expanded by Implication.

Finally, petitioners ask this Court to treat the absence of an exclusion as an affirmative source of liability—effectively expanding coverage through negative inference. But this Court has cautioned that remedial exemptions must be construed according to their own terms and purposes, not manipulated to expand or contract liability beyond what Congress specified. See A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945); Addison v. Holly Hill Fruit Prods., Inc., 322 U.S. 607, 617 (1944). Those principles apply with equal force here: the (F)(iii) exclusion cannot be read backward to manufacture debt-collector status for every purchaser of defaulted paper.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, and those stated by the court of appeals, the judgment below should be affirmed.
