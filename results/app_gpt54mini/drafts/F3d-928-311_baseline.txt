## ARGUMENT

### I. Furlow’s South Carolina crack-cocaine conviction is not a predicate “serious drug offense” or “controlled substance offense.”

The ACCA applies only if a defendant has three prior convictions for a “violent felony” or “serious drug offense.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1). A “serious drug offense” must be “an offense under State law, involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance” and carrying a maximum term of at least ten years. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Guidelines similarly require a prior “controlled substance offense” that prohibits the manufacture, distribution, or possession with intent to distribute a controlled substance, punishable by more than one year. USSG § 4B1.2(b).

To determine whether a prior conviction qualifies, courts apply the categorical approach and compare the elements of the state offense to the generic federal definition. Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575, 600-02 (1990); Descamps v. United States, 570 U.S. 254, 260-61 (2013). If a statute sweeps more broadly than the federal definition, it cannot serve as a predicate. Descamps, 570 U.S. at 261. Where a statute is divisible, courts may use the modified categorical approach only to identify the element set of conviction, not to rewrite the offense. Mathis v. United States, 579 U.S. 500, 505-06 (2016).

Furlow’s South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine does not categorically match the ACCA or Guidelines definitions if the South Carolina statute reaches conduct beyond the federal definitions. South Carolina’s drug statutes have been construed to criminalize a broader universe of conduct and substances than the federal predicate definitions require, and a conviction under an overbroad statute cannot qualify. See Mathis, 579 U.S. at 517-18; Descamps, 570 U.S. at 261. The government therefore may not rely on the label “distribution of crack cocaine” alone; it must show that the elements of conviction match the federal definitions. Id.

Because the record does not establish a qualifying categorical match, the district court erred in counting this conviction as both a serious drug offense and a controlled substance offense. See United States v. McLeod, 808 F.3d 972, 976-78 (4th Cir. 2015) (rejecting predicate status where the state statute covered broader conduct than the federal definition); United States v. Dozier, 848 F.3d 180, 183-84 (4th Cir. 2017) (same). Without this conviction, Furlow lacks the drug predicate needed for the ACCA and career-offender enhancements.

### II. Furlow’s Georgia first-degree arson conviction does not qualify as generic “arson.”

The ACCA and Guidelines both enumerate “arson” as a qualifying predicate. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). But the generic offense is not whatever a state chooses to call arson. Courts must compare the state elements with the generic contemporary meaning of arson. Taylor, 495 U.S. at 598-99; United States v. Castleman, 572 U.S. 157, 162-63 (2014) (statutory terms are interpreted by their ordinary, generic meanings).

A state arson statute that criminalizes conduct broader than generic arson cannot support an enhancement. See United States v. Knight, 606 F.3d 171, 173-75 (4th Cir. 2010); United States v. Misleveck, 735 F.3d 983, 989-91 (7th Cir. 2013). Georgia’s first-degree arson statute is broader than generic arson if it covers burning of occupied or unoccupied structures, vehicles, or other property beyond the scope of generic arson’s core focus on malicious burning of a dwelling or structure. See Taylor, 495 U.S. at 598; United States v. Stitt, 139 S. Ct. 399, 404-05 (2018) (confirming the need to identify the generic offense’s elements before comparing state law).

The district court therefore erred in treating the Georgia conviction as an ACCA and career-offender predicate without first determining whether the statute of conviction matches generic arson. Mathis, 579 U.S. at 517-18. Because the record does not show that the Georgia offense is the generic crime of arson, it cannot be counted. See United States v. White, 836 F.3d 437, 445-47 (4th Cir. 2016).

### III. Because Furlow lacks the required predicates, his sentence must be vacated.

The ACCA enhancement required three qualifying prior convictions. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1). The career-offender provision required at least two prior convictions for a crime of violence or controlled substance offense. USSG § 4B1.1(a). If either the South Carolina drug conviction or the Georgia arson conviction is excluded, the sentence cannot stand under the applied enhancements.

A sentence based on an erroneous career-offender or ACCA designation is procedurally unreasonable and must be vacated. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 51 (2007); United States v. Winbush, 922 F.3d 227, 230-32 (4th Cir. 2019). Furlow respectfully requests reversal of the predicate rulings and remand for resentencing without the ACCA and career-offender enhancements.
