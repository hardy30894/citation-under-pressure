# ARGUMENT

## I. Death row inmates possess no constitutionally protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings.

The Due Process Clause protects only interests that arise either from the Constitution itself or from state law. *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 226 (1976). Executive clemency satisfies neither condition. This Court settled the question in *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), holding that a Connecticut inmate whose repeated commutation applications were denied without explanation possessed no due process interest triggering judicial review. The Court explained that "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." *Id.* at 464. That principle governs here with even greater force, because Ohio's clemency scheme, like Connecticut's, vests "unfettered discretion" in the executive. A "hope" of executive clemency, however sincerely held, is "not a constitutionally protected 'liberty' interest." *Id.* at 465.

*Dumschat* forecloses any argument that clemency, once codified in procedural regulations, becomes a "liberty interest" of constitutional dimension. The mere existence of state procedures governing how clemency applications are processed does not convert the underlying discretionary act of mercy into an entitlement. As Chief Justice Burger's opinion in *Dumschat* recognized, the Constitution "does not entitle the respondent to any 'process' " where the substantive decision remains committed to unreviewable executive grace. 452 U.S. at 465.

Nor does *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), rescue respondent's position. *Evitts* held only that a State which creates a first appeal as of right must administer that appeal consistently with due process, because the appeal is "an integral part of the … system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant." *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956), quoted in *Evitts*, 469 U.S. at 393. Clemency, unlike a first appeal, is not part of the adjudicatory process at all — it operates after the judgment of conviction has become final and serves an entirely different constitutional function: extending mercy outside the ordinary system of justice. This Court has never extended *Evitts*'s logic to executive clemency, and the *Evitts* Court itself cautioned against extending procedural protections indefinitely, noting that the right to counsel does not even extend to habeas corpus proceedings, which "form an integral part of the adjudicative system much more clearly than executive clemency proceedings do." *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974), discussed in *Evitts*, 469 U.S. at 394.

This Court's later admonition in *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472 (1995), confirms that courts should not mechanically extend due process analysis to every ancillary procedure a State chooses to create. *Sandin* observed that lower courts applying *Hewitt v. Helms*, 459 U.S. 460 (1983), had pushed the logic of *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1 (1979), too far, thereby creating "disincentives for States to codify prison management procedures in the interest of uniform treatment." 515 U.S. at 483. The same disincentive would arise here: if adopting a voluntary interview procedure exposes Ohio to constitutional litigation and judicially imposed minimum procedures, the State will have every reason to abandon the option altogether — to the detriment of condemned inmates. The Sixth Circuit's holding that "minimal" due process protections attach to clemency, however "barely perceptible," cannot be reconciled with *Dumschat*'s clear teaching that clemency decisions are simply not judicially reviewable.

## II. Ohio's voluntary clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination.

Respondent's Fifth Amendment claim fails for the independent reason that Ohio's procedure compels nothing. The regulation gives the inmate the *option* of a personal interview; it does not require him to answer any question, and it does not penalize silence. This is fundamentally unlike a scheme that forces a choice between incriminating oneself and forfeiting some independent benefit.

This Court's decision in *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), controls. There, the Court rejected as premature a claim that adverse consequences might someday flow from a prisoner's silence, explaining that relief addressing hypothetical future prejudice was unwarranted where no such consequence had yet occurred. *Id.* at 324. The same is true here. Respondent has not been compelled to speak, has not spoken, and has not identified any pending or contemplated criminal proceeding in which a clemency statement could be used against him. Indeed, respondent's own complaint disclaims that this suit will affect his existing conviction or sentence. Any risk of self-incrimination is, in the words of *Baxter*, "premature." 425 U.S. at 324.

Because the interview is voluntary, "as far as the regulation discloses, a prisoner … remains free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes." The Fifth Amendment prohibits *compelled* self-incrimination; it does not create a right to be shielded from every situation in which a defendant, of his own free will, might choose to speak. Absent compulsion, there is no constitutional violation—and certainly no basis for a federal court to fashion advance safeguards against speculative future prosecutions that may never occur.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed insofar as it recognized a protected liberty interest in clemency proceedings and remanded for further Fifth Amendment analysis.
