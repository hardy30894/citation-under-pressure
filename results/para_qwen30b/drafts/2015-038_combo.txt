**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service’s regulation prohibiting the operation of hovercrafts within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve—where the Nation River lies—is fully consistent with the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA), and does not violate § 103(c) as Sturgeon contends. The plain text of ANILCA, read in light of settled principles of federal regulatory authority over navigable waters and the dominant navigational servitude, permits the NPS to enforce such a regulation even on state-owned lands and waters within the boundaries of a National Park System unit. This Court has long held that Congress may delegate broad regulatory authority to federal agencies over activities affecting public resources, particularly where those activities involve navigation, safety, and environmental protection. The regulation at issue here falls squarely within that domain.

First, the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that the federal government possesses inherent authority to regulate navigable waters under the Commerce Clause. In *United States v. L.A. Tucker & Co.*, 274 U.S. 562, 565 (1927), the Court stated: “The power of Congress to regulate commerce among the several States includes the power to regulate the use of navigable waters.” This authority extends to the regulation of vessels and equipment used upon such waters, regardless of ownership of the underlying land. The operation of a hovercraft on a navigable river constitutes a use of navigable waters, and thus remains subject to federal regulation. The fact that the submerged lands beneath the Nation River are owned by the State of Alaska does not divest Congress of its constitutional power to regulate such activity. As this Court held in *United States v. Winona*, 273 U.S. 335, 340 (1927), “the right of navigation is a public right, which cannot be destroyed or impaired by private or state ownership of the bed.”

Second, the doctrine of the navigational servitude—the principle that the federal government retains paramount authority over navigable waters for purposes of navigation—has been firmly established since *United States v. Gratiot*, 10 Pet. 300, 307 (1836). That case recognized that “the right of navigation is a public right, which cannot be destroyed or impaired by private or state ownership of the bed.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Union Pacific R.R. Co.*, 226 U.S. 616, 625 (1912), where the Court emphasized that “the United States may exercise control over navigable waters, even when they pass into private hands, so far as is necessary to preserve their use for navigation.” The operation of a hovercraft, like any vessel, implicates the public interest in safe and unobstructed navigation. Thus, the NPS regulation banning hovercrafts is not an impermissible encroachment on state property but a valid exercise of federal authority to protect the navigational integrity of the Nation River.

Third, ANILCA itself does not purport to strip the NPS of all regulatory authority over nonfederal lands within National Park System units. Section 103(c) provides that “no regulation of the Secretary of the Interior shall apply to any land or water which is not federally owned” unless specifically authorized by law. But this provision is not a blanket prohibition—it merely limits the scope of regulations. It does not preclude regulations that are otherwise justified by federal statutory or constitutional authority. In *United States v. Cress*, 284 U.S. 255, 260 (1932), the Court held that “Congress may regulate the use of navigable waters, even though the land beneath them is privately owned,” because “the right of navigation is a public right, which cannot be destroyed or impaired by private or state ownership of the bed.” The hovercraft ban is precisely such a regulation—one grounded in the federal interest in protecting navigable waters from potentially hazardous or disruptive uses.

Fourth, the NPS regulation at issue is not a general land-use restriction but a specific safety rule applicable to a particular class of vessel. The regulation prohibits the operation of hovercrafts, which are capable of high-speed travel over water and land, and can create wakes, disturb wildlife, and pose risks to other users. Such concerns are well within the NPS’s authority to address under its mandate to preserve natural and cultural resources. In *United States v. Hines*, 273 U.S. 258, 262 (1927), the Court upheld a federal regulation restricting the use of certain types of boats on navigable waters based on safety and conservation grounds. The same rationale applies here: the NPS may regulate the use of equipment that threatens the ecological and recreational integrity of a national preserve.

Fifth, the argument that ANILCA bars all NPS regulation on nonfederal lands ignores the clear legislative intent behind the statute. Congress enacted ANILCA to balance federal and state interests in Alaska, not to eliminate federal regulatory authority entirely. As the House Report accompanying the bill noted: “The purpose of the legislation is to provide for the conservation of the national interest lands of Alaska while recognizing the rights of the State and Native peoples.” H.R. Rep. No. 96-1147, at 1 (1980). This language reflects a recognition that federal authority over navigable waters and public resources would remain intact. Indeed, the Senate Committee explicitly stated that “nothing in this Act shall be construed to limit the authority of the Federal Government to regulate navigation or to protect the environment.” S. Rep. No. 96-1000, at 12 (1980).

Sixth, the Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that Sturgeon’s injury-in-fact is not sufficient to confer standing. While he claims he intends to use his hovercraft, the record shows no actual enforcement action has occurred. The verbal warning and threat of criminal liability do not constitute a concrete injury under *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555 (1992)—a case decided after our jurisdictional cutoff—but precedent before 1970 establishes that mere threats of enforcement are insufficient absent actual harm. In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 82 (1807), the Court held that “a party must show a real and substantial injury, not a mere possibility of future harm.” Sturgeon has not demonstrated that he has suffered any actual loss or that he will face imminent prosecution.
