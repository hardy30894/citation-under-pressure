## Argument

Bond lacks standing to press her Tenth Amendment attack on § 229. The Supreme Court has long held that private parties may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to invalidate federal action absent a cognizable injury beyond the assertion that the federal government has acted too broadly. In Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939), the Court rejected private utilities’ Tenth Amendment challenge to federal competition and explained that, “absent the states or their officers, [they] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” That holding remains controlling. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 523 U.S. 83, 101-02 (1998) (jurisdictional issues must be resolved before merits); see also California v. United States, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978) (treating standing and merits analysis in Tenth Amendment context).

This Court should follow Tennessee Electric Power, not the outlier cases Bond invokes. The circuits that have denied private Tenth Amendment standing have done so correctly because the Amendment protects the states’ reserved sovereign interests, not an individual defendant’s generalized objection to federal prosecution. See United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004); Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005); Brooklyn Legal Services Corp. v. Legal Services Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); Oregon v. Legal Services Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009). By contrast, the decisions Bond relies on either predate important limiting authority or do not grapple with Tennessee Electric Power’s explicit no-standing language. See Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. U.S. Department of Energy, 666 F.2d 1359, 1368 n.16 (11th Cir. 1982); Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis, 185 F.3d 693, 703-04 (7th Cir. 1999), cert. denied, 528 U.S. 1116 (2000). Even the Eleventh Circuit has since expressed doubts about those earlier decisions. See Dillard v. Chilton County Commission, 495 F.3d 1324, 1335 (11th Cir. 2007); Seniors Civil Liberties Association v. Kemp, 965 F.2d 1030, 1034 n.6 (11th Cir. 1992).

Bond’s injury here is not the kind that creates standing to litigate the federal-state allocation of power. She was indicted and convicted under a criminal statute that applies on its face to her conduct; her grievance is that the government chose federal rather than state prosecution. But “the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of [its] delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States.” Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945). That principle describes federalism’s structure; it does not confer an individual right to insist on state prosecution. Bond therefore seeks to assert only the sort of abstract federalism objection the Court has repeatedly declined to entertain absent the state’s own challenge. See Tennessee Electric Power Co., 306 U.S. at 144; Steel Co., 523 U.S. at 94-95.

Nor does Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432-33 (1920), help Bond on standing. Holland addressed Congress’s power to enact treaty-implementing legislation, not whether a criminal defendant may invoke the Tenth Amendment to defeat her own conviction. And later cases confirm that treaty implementation may rest on national authority even where the subject matter would otherwise be local. See United States v. Lara, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004); United States v. Lue, 134 F.3d 79, 84-85 (2d Cir. 1998); United States v. Ferreira, 275 F.3d 1020, 1027-28 (11th Cir. 2001). Whether § 229 is ultimately valid under the Treaty Power or Necessary and Proper Clause is therefore a merits question, not a standing question.

Bond’s contrary position would also expand federal jurisdiction beyond Article III limits. Standing requires a litigant’s own legally cognizable interest, not a request to vindicate the structural preferences of a sovereign that has not objected. See Steel Co., 523 U.S. at 103-04; Tennessee Electric Power Co., 306 U.S. at 144. Here, Pennsylvania has not challenged § 229, and Bond cannot convert her criminal conviction into a vehicle for litigating abstract federalism principles. As the First Circuit observed, the states, not private defendants, are the proper parties to press such structural objections. Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 35.

Bond’s standing theory should be rejected, and the judgment below affirmed.
