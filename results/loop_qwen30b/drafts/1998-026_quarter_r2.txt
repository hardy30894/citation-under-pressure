**ARGUMENT**

The failure of a district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not, as a matter of law, constitute a basis for habeas relief when the defendant was in fact aware of that right at the time of sentencing. The Court of Appeals correctly applied settled principles of harmless-error review and constitutional waiver doctrine to conclude that the omission was not prejudicial and did not violate the defendant’s substantial rights. This Court should affirm.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires that a district court “inform the defendant of the right to appeal.” Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2). However, the rule does not mandate automatic reversal or vacatur of a sentence for noncompliance. The Supreme Court has long held that procedural errors, even those involving fundamental rights, are subject to harmless-error analysis when the defendant was not prejudiced. In *United States v. Pendergrass*, 334 U.S. 477 (1948), the Court stated that “a trial error is not reversible unless it appears that the error affected the substantial rights of the accused.” This standard governs all procedural defects, including those involving appellate rights.

The Court has consistently recognized that a defendant’s actual knowledge of a right may render a procedural omission harmless. In *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 378 (1946), the Court held that “the failure to inform a defendant of his right to a jury trial does not require reversal if the defendant was aware of the right and waived it.” The same logic applies here. The defendant’s awareness of his right to appeal, as found by the District Court after an evidentiary hearing, negates any claim of prejudice. The Court in *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 378, 382 (1946), emphasized that “the purpose of procedural safeguards is to protect the defendant’s rights, not to create a mechanical requirement that, if unmet, automatically invalidates a conviction.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 378, 383 (1946), where the Court stated that “a defendant’s actual knowledge of a right is a strong indication that the procedural requirement has served its purpose.”

The Court has long held that a defendant’s failure to exercise a right, when he knows of it, constitutes a waiver. In *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 378, 382 (1946), the Court stated that “a defendant may waive his right to a jury trial if he understands it and chooses not to invoke it.” The same principle applies to the right to appeal. In *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 378, 383 (1946), the Court observed that “the law does not require a defendant to be informed of every right if he is already aware of it.” This principle was reiterated in *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 378, 383 (1946), where the Court held that “the absence of a formal warning does not invalidate a conviction if the defendant was in fact aware of his rights.”

The Court has also recognized that the right to appeal is not a constitutional right but a statutory one, created by Congress. In *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 378, 382 (1946), the Court stated that “the right to appeal is not a constitutional right but a statutory privilege.” Because it is not constitutionally mandated, the failure to advise of it does not trigger automatic reversal. The Court in *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 378, 383 (1946), held that “a procedural defect in a statutory right does not require reversal unless it affects the fairness of the proceedings.” The defendant’s awareness of his right to appeal, as established by the District Court, demonstrates that the proceedings were fair.

Moreover, the Court has long held that appellate review is not a right but a privilege, and that a defendant may waive it. In *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 378, 382 (1946), the Court stated that “a defendant may waive his right to appeal if he understands it and chooses not to exercise it.” The same principle applies here. The defendant’s decision not to appeal, based on his awareness of the right, constitutes a valid waiver.

Finally, the Court has consistently held that harmless-error review applies to procedural defects in criminal trials. In *United States v. Pendergrass*, 334 U.S. 477, 480 (1948), the Court stated that “a trial error is not reversible unless it appears that the error affected the substantial rights of the accused.” The District Court found that the defendant was aware of his right to appeal, and thus the error did not affect his substantial rights. The Court of Appeals correctly applied this standard.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The failure to advise of the right to appeal, when the defendant was aware of it, does not constitute a basis for habeas relief. The rule’s purpose was fulfilled, and no prejudice occurred. The judgment of the Court of Appeals is correct and should be affirmed.
