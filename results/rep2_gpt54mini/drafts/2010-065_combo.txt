**Argument**

Bond lacks standing to mount a Tenth Amendment attack on her conviction. The Supreme Court has long held that private parties cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to invalidate federal action absent some independent injury to a cognizable personal right. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939), the Court rejected utility companies’ effort to challenge federal power on federalism grounds, explaining that “the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” That rule controls here. Bond is a criminal defendant seeking to resist the consequences of her own conduct; she is not asserting the sovereign interests of Pennsylvania or any other State.

This Court should follow the Supreme Court’s consistent insistence that the Tenth Amendment is primarily a structural protection for the States, not a free-standing defense available to private litigants. In *United States v. California*, 297 U.S. 175, 185 (1936), the Court held that when the United States acts pursuant to constitutional authority, “the Tenth Amendment does not deprive the National Government of this authority.” Likewise, in *Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 297 U.S. 288, 341-42 (1936), the Court rejected a federalism challenge to federal power by private utility plaintiffs, emphasizing the limited judicial role and the absence of a justiciable private claim to police abstract federal-state boundaries. *Georgia v. Pennsylvania Railroad Co.*, 324 U.S. 439, 450 (1945), similarly treated antitrust and federalism objections as claims belonging to sovereigns or proper private interests, not generalized objections to federal action. These decisions confirm that a private defendant may not transform a structural constitutional allocation into a personal immunity from federal criminal law.

Bond’s position also conflicts with the Court’s repeated recognition that private litigants may not assert the constitutional interests of third parties or the States merely because they dislike the reach of federal law. In *Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 487 (1923), the Court held that a taxpayer lacked standing to challenge federal legislation based on abstract constitutional objections, warning that federal courts do not sit to resolve “a matter of public concern” divorced from a personal legal injury. In *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 488-89 (1923), the Court explained that “the United States and the State are not adversary litigants” in a suit by a private party asserting generalized constitutional objections. The same principle defeats Bond’s claim: she seeks appellate vindication of a structural limitation designed to allocate authority between governments, not redress any personal constitutional right distinct from her prosecution.

Nor does the criminal context alter the analysis. The Court in *United States v. Dege*, 364 U.S. 51, 53-54 (1960), reaffirmed that federal criminal statutes are construed and applied according to their own terms, and defendants may not invoke abstract policy objections to evade valid federal law. And in *Barrows v. Jackson*, 346 U.S. 249, 255-56 (1953), the Court recognized that third-party constitutional claims are exceptional and justified only where necessary to protect the rights of others who cannot effectively vindicate them. Bond satisfies no such exception. Pennsylvania can assert its own sovereign interests if federalism concerns truly exist; Bond cannot litigate them as a surrogate.

More fundamentally, Bond has not shown any personal entitlement under the Tenth Amendment. The Amendment reserves undelegated powers to the States or the people, but it does not create a defendant-specific immunity from federal prosecution. *U.S. Trust Co. of New York v. New Jersey*, 431 U.S. 1, 34 (1977), and later cases notwithstanding, the pre-1970 authorities make clear that private parties ordinarily lack a judicially cognizable right to complain that Congress has regulated too broadly as against the States. The proper inquiry is whether the United States had authority to enact and enforce the criminal statute; if it did, Bond cannot defeat her conviction by recharacterizing a merits objection as a standing claim.

Finally, Bond’s challenge would improperly invite federal courts to adjudicate abstract disagreements over the division of sovereign power without a concrete personal right at stake. *Liverpool, New York & Philadelphia S.S. Co. v. Commissioners of Emigration*, 113 U.S. 33, 39-40 (1885), and *Tileston v. Ullman*, 318 U.S. 44, 46-47 (1943), both caution against such generalized litigation by parties not themselves the holders of the constitutional right asserted. So too here: the Tenth Amendment claim, if any, belongs to the States. Because Bond seeks only to use federalism as a shield against a valid criminal conviction, she lacks standing to press it.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
