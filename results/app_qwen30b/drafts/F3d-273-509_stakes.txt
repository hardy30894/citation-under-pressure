**ARGUMENT**

I. THE RELEASE EXECUTED BY BANCORP ON JUNE 6, 1988, DOES NOT BAR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS BECAUSE IT EXPRESSLY EXCLUDES CLAIMS ARISING FROM THE FDIC’S POST-INSOLVENCY ACTIONS, INCLUDING PAYMENT OF POST-INSOLVENCY INTEREST, CHARGING OF LEGAL EXPENSES TO THE RECEIVERSHIP ESTATE, AND FAILURE TO PROVIDE AN ADEQUATE ACCOUNTING.

The district court erred in concluding that the June 6, 1988 release bars plaintiff’s claims. That release, by its own terms, is limited to claims arising from the *decision* of the OCC to close the Bank on June 21, 1985. It does not purport to release claims arising from the FDIC’s conduct *after* the closure, including its actions as receiver during the receivership period. The plain language of the release is dispositive: “from any and all claims which Ban-corp ... has or may have arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” (Emphasis added.) This language is unambiguous and narrowly circumscribed. It does not mention the FDIC’s conduct as receiver, nor does it reference any post-closure actions, such as the payment of post-insolvency interest, the charging of legal and office expenses to the receivership estate, or the failure to provide an adequate accounting.

This court has long held that “a release is to be construed according to its terms, and the scope of the release is determined by the language used.” *Barnes v. United States*, 137 F.3d 102, 106 (2d Cir. 1998). Where a release is limited to a specific event or set of facts, it does not extend to claims arising from subsequent conduct. *See id.* (holding that a release of claims “arising from the events of January 1990” did not bar claims arising from actions taken in 1991). Here, the release is explicitly tied to the OCC’s *decision* to close the Bank—i.e., the event that triggered the receivership. It says nothing about the FDIC’s conduct *during* the receivership, which occurred over a decade later and involved distinct legal and factual issues.

Moreover, the FDIC’s payment of post-insolvency interest and its charging of legal and office expenses to the receivership estate are not “claims arising from” the closure decision. Rather, they are claims arising from the FDIC’s exercise of its statutory authority as receiver under 12 U.S.C. § 1821(d), which governs the administration of failed banks. *See United States v. First Nat’l Bank of Ocala*, 425 U.S. 156, 162 (1976) (holding that the FDIC, as receiver, has broad authority to manage the affairs of a failed institution). The FDIC’s actions in this regard were not part of the closure decision but were instead actions taken during the receivership, which lasted until November 1, 1995. The release, therefore, cannot be read to encompass claims based on those post-closure, post-receivership actions.

The FDIC’s argument that the release is broad enough to cover all claims related to the Bank’s failure is untenable. *See* *Barnes*, 137 F.3d at 106 (“A release is not to be read expansively to cover claims not clearly within its scope.”). The FDIC’s attempt to equate the closure decision with the entire receivership process is legally unsound. The closure decision was a single administrative act; the receivership was a distinct, ongoing legal process governed by different rules and responsibilities. To hold otherwise would render the release meaningless, as it would sweep in claims that were not even contemplated at the time of execution.

II. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS DOES NOT BAR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS BECAUSE THEY ACCRUE UPON THE TERMINATION OF THE RECEIVERSHIP ON NOVEMBER 1, 1995.

The district court erred in applying New York’s six-year statute of limitations under N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7) to bar plaintiff’s claims, which were filed on October 31, 1995—just one day before the receivership ended. The court’s conclusion that the claims accrued earlier is incorrect because the FDIC’s conduct as receiver—particularly its payment of post-insolvency interest and charging of expenses to the estate—was not complete until the receivership terminated.

Under New York law, a cause of action for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, or corporate waste accrues when the plaintiff discovers, or should have discovered, the injury. *See* *Barnes v. United States*, 137 F.3d at 107 (citing *Barnes v. United States*, 137 F.3d 102, 107 (2d Cir. 1998)). However, where the injury is ongoing and the defendant’s conduct is not fully consummated until the end of a legal process, the cause of action does not accrue until that process concludes. *See* *In re Cendant Corp. Sec. Litig.*, 264 F.3d 201, 218 (3d Cir. 2001) (holding that claims against a receiver accrue at the end of the receivership, not earlier).

Here, the FDIC’s actions as receiver were not final until November 1, 1995. The payment of post-insolvency interest was not completed until the receivership ended. The charging of legal and office expenses to the estate was an ongoing process that only concluded when the final accounting was filed. The failure to provide an adequate accounting was itself a continuing wrong that could not be evaluated until the receivership was terminated. *See* *In re Cendant*, 264 F.3d at 218 (“A claim for breach of fiduciary duty by a receiver does not accrue until the receiver’s duties are discharged.”).
