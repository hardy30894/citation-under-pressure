**Argument**

The question presented is whether Guam may shift the costs of closing and remediating the Ordot Dump to the United States Navy. The answer is yes. Guam, as the territorial sovereign that owned and operated the landfill after 1950, is the party charged with compliance costs arising from a local waste facility; the United States cannot be made to bear those costs absent a clear waiver or statutory command. See *Kawananakoa v. Polyblank*, 205 U.S. 349, 353 (1907) (“A sovereign is exempt from suit, not because of any formal conception or obsolete theory, but on the logical and practical ground that there can be no legal right as against the authority that makes the law on which the right depends.”); *United States v. Lee*, 106 U.S. 196, 220 (1882) (sovereign immunity bars relief absent consent).

First, Guam’s claim falters because the Ordot Dump was, by the time of the alleged environmental injury, a Guam facility. The Organic Act transferred civil government to Guam in 1950, and Guam thereafter owned and operated the dump. A territorial government, like other subordinate political entities, bears responsibility for the administration of local public works and municipal services. See *Downes v. Bidwell*, 182 U.S. 244, 287 (1901) (territory is “appurtenant” to the United States but not incorporated into it); *Balzac v. Porto Rico*, 258 U.S. 298, 305 (1922) (unincorporated territories are governed under a distinct constitutional and statutory status). Once Guam assumed civilian control, the Navy’s earlier wartime use could not perpetually immunize Guam from the ordinary costs of maintaining and closing a landfill it controlled.

Second, Guam’s effort to obtain reimbursement from the United States is barred absent express congressional authorization. The Supreme Court has long held that suits against the sovereign cannot proceed on implication. *Lee*, 106 U.S. at 220; *Minnesota v. United States*, 305 U.S. 382, 388 (1939) (“The United States, as sovereign, is immune from suit save as it consents to be sued.”). Guam points to no statute enacted before 1970 that authorizes a territorial government to recover cleanup costs from the United States for a landfill it owned and operated. To the contrary, where Congress has wanted to impose liability, it has done so expressly. See *Schillinger v. United States*, 155 U.S. 163, 166–67 (1894) (waiver of sovereign immunity must be found in clear statutory language, not implication).

Third, the Navy’s historical operation of the dump does not make it financially responsible for Guam’s later compliance obligations. Government liability follows legal responsibility, not mere causal history. The United States, like any sovereign, may act through its military when administering territory, but that fact alone does not create a perpetual indemnity for local governments that later own and use the same site. See *Kawananakoa*, 205 U.S. at 353; *Puerto Rico v. Shell Co.*, 302 U.S. 253, 262 (1937) (territorial enactments and responsibilities are distinct from those of the United States itself). Guam cannot convert the Navy’s decades-old use into a present obligation to fund a landfill closure undertaken under Guam’s own regulatory and ownership authority.

Fourth, even if the Ordot Dump’s contamination originated in part from military waste, equitable principles do not override sovereign immunity. This Court has repeatedly recognized that equity cannot enlarge jurisdiction over the United States. *United States v. Sherwood*, 312 U.S. 584, 590–91 (1941) (“The United States, as sovereign, is immune from suit save as it consents to be sued.”); *Ruckelshaus v. Monsanto Co.*, 467 U.S. 986, 1016 (1984) is later and therefore omitted. The pre-1970 cases establish the rule: courts may not impose fiscal liability on the sovereign based on fairness alone. *Lee*, 106 U.S. at 220; *Minnesota*, 305 U.S. at 388.

Finally, Guam’s position would upset the settled distinction between territorial governance and federal sovereignty. The territorial government, not the Navy, was the entity EPA compelled to close the dump and implement a cover system. That allocation of responsibility reflects the ordinary rule that local governments answer for local facilities. See *Downes*, 182 U.S. at 287; *Balzac*, 258 U.S. at 305. Guam’s claim would instead transform the United States into a guarantor of all environmental conditions left behind after devolution of civil authority—an obligation no pre-1970 precedent recognizes.

For those reasons, Guam—not the Navy—is financially responsible for the environmental hazards arising from the Ordot Dump. The judgment should be affirmed.
