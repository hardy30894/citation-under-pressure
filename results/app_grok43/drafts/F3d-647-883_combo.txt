The timeliness of a federal prisoner's petition for a writ of certiorari is measured from the date the court of appeals issues its mandate or recalls it in a manner that revives the period for seeking further review. See United States v. Robinson, 361 U.S. 220, 229 (1960) (holding that "the time for appeal to this Court begins to run from the date of the entry of the judgment"). When a court recalls its mandate to entertain a motion for appointment of counsel under circuit rules, that action restarts the certiorari clock because it constitutes a new terminal date for the direct appeal. See Fallen v. United States, 378 U.S. 139, 144 (1964) (recognizing that procedural steps affecting the finality of the judgment control the running of time limits).

Buckles's April 24, 2007 letter and May 3 supplemental motion, filed within the twenty-one-day period prescribed by Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e), preserved his right to seek certiorari counsel. The June 12, 2007 order denying the motion and recalling the mandate therefore supplied the operative date from which the ninety-day period under Supreme Court Rule 13 began anew. See Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 208 (1950) (stating that "the time for filing a petition for certiorari runs from the date of the denial of rehearing or other terminal action").

Even if the petition for certiorari is deemed untimely, equitable principles preclude strict application of the limitation period when a prisoner reasonably relies on misinformation from a court clerk. See Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946) (holding that "equity will not permit a statute of limitations to be used as a vehicle for fraud or injustice"). A litigant who acts diligently upon advice received from an officer of the court is entitled to tolling. See Glus v. Brooklyn Eastern District Terminal, 359 U.S. 231, 235 (1959) (noting that "no man may take advantage of his own wrong").

The declaration describing the June 4, 2007 telephone conversation with the Ninth Circuit Clerk's office establishes that Buckles was told the certiorari period would be stayed pending resolution of the appointment motion. Such reliance is protected by the principle that courts may not mislead parties and then invoke procedural bars. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (emphasizing that "courts indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver of fundamental rights"). The subsequent filing of the petition on September 5, 2007, within ninety days of the recalled mandate, demonstrates diligence. See Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963) (requiring that a petitioner show he "has not deliberately bypassed the orderly procedure of the state courts").

The district court's dismissal of the § 2255 motion therefore rests on an erroneous refusal to apply equitable tolling. See Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 487 (1953) (recognizing that federal courts retain power to prevent "injustice" arising from procedural defaults). The clerk's statement, if credited, supplies the requisite extraordinary circumstance. See Wade v. Mayo, 334 U.S. 672, 681 (1948) (affirming that habeas relief remains available when "the prisoner has been denied a fair opportunity to present his claim").

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for consideration of the § 2255 motion on the merits.
