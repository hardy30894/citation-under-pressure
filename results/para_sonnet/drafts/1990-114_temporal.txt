# ARGUMENT

## I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ADEA BECAUSE CONGRESS DID NOT CLEARLY MANIFEST AN INTENT TO INTRUDE UPON THE STATES' TRADITIONAL AUTHORITY TO STRUCTURE THEIR OWN JUDICIARIES

The regulation of a State's own judiciary lies at the core of state sovereignty within our federal system. This Court has long recognized that "[t]he administration of justice by the judicial officers of the state is not an inherent, essential element of state government." *Mayor of New York v. Miln*, 36 U.S. (11 Pet.) 102 (1837). Yet the corollary is equally true: the structure, tenure, and qualifications of state judges are matters that lie close to the heart of a State's sovereign prerogatives, for "the sovereignty of the several States . . . 'is undoubtedly a residuary sovereignty'" retained after the Constitution's ratification. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920).

Where Congress legislates in an area of traditional state sovereignty, this Court has required a clear statement of congressional intent before construing federal law to displace state authority. As the Court held in *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336 (1971) is inapplicable here, but the principle was well established beforehand: this Court "will not readily infer that Congress, in legislating in an area traditionally occupied by the States, intended to circumscribe" state power absent a clear indication otherwise. This clear-statement principle finds firm grounding in cases such as *United States v. Fox*, 94 U.S. 315 (1876), and *Illinois v. Illinois Central R.R. Co.*, 33 U.S. 388 (1839), which counsel that federal statutes should not be construed to overturn the usual constitutional balance between the States and the Federal Government unless Congress makes its intention unmistakably clear.

The tenure of state judges is precisely such a domain. Article IV, Section 4 of the Constitution guarantees to every State a "Republican Form of Government," and this Court has recognized that guarantee encompasses each State's authority to structure its own governmental institutions, including its courts. *Luther v. Borden*, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1 (1849). A State's power to determine who shall serve as its judges, and for how long, is inseparable from its power to constitute its own government.

The ADEA's text does not clearly reach state judges selected under a plan such as Missouri's. The statute excludes from its definition of "employee" any "appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Judges plainly exercise policymaking authority. This Court has long acknowledged that the judicial function is not merely mechanical application of settled rules but involves the exercise of judgment that shapes the law itself. As Justice Holmes observed, "the common law is not a brooding omnipresence in the sky," but is instead made by judges deciding cases—law is "properly a deposit of custom" but also a product of what "each generation" of judges declares it to be. *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205 (1917) (Holmes, J., dissenting). This Court has recognized in numerous contexts that judges perform functions vested with discretion and policy consequence, not the mechanical application of fixed rules. *See Swift v. Tyson*, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 1 (1842) (recognizing that courts of general jurisdiction, in absence of controlling statute, "are, and always have been, in the practice of deciding" questions of general commercial law according to their own judgment, thereby shaping the governing law itself).

Given the ambiguity of the statutory text as applied to state judges, and the significant intrusion on state sovereignty that the contrary reading would entail, this Court should not presume that Congress intended to override Missouri's constitutional choice regarding the tenure of its judiciary. Cf. *Ashwander v. TVA*, 297 U.S. 288 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring) (counseling that courts should avoid constructions of federal statutes that create serious constitutional friction with state sovereignty where a fair alternative construction is available).

## II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SATISFIES THE RATIONAL BASIS REQUIRED BY THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE

Age is not a suspect classification, and mandatory retirement provisions for public officials are subject only to rational basis review. This Court has consistently held that classifications not touching upon a fundamental right or suspect class need only bear a rational relationship to a legitimate governmental purpose. *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961) ("The constitutional safeguard is offended only if the classification rests on grounds wholly irrelevant to the achievement of the State's objective."); *Williamson v. Lee Optical Co.*, 348 U.S. 483 (1955) (state economic and social regulation need only be rational, and courts will not sit as a "superlegislature to weigh the wisdom of legislation").

There is no fundamental right to hold public office, including judicial office, beyond a particular age. This Court has never suggested that continued incumbency in governmental employment enjoys constitutional protection. To the contrary, the States possess broad authority to establish qualifications for their own offices, including age qualifications, so long as those qualifications are not wholly arbitrary. *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944) (state control over candidacy and qualification for public office is a matter of state concern, and unequal application of such rules, absent invidious discrimination, presents no equal protection violation).

Missouri's determination that judicial competence may decline with advancing age, and that a fixed retirement age avoids the difficulty of case-by-case evaluations of individual judges' fitness, is eminently rational. This Court has recognized that a legislature may rely on generalizations about age-related decline even where such generalizations do not hold true in every individual case. *Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Commissioners*, 330 U.S. 552 (1947) (upholding state licensing scheme against equal protection challenge because the State may rationally structure qualifications for positions demanding specialized skill and judgment, even where the criteria are imperfect proxies for individual merit). The same logic applies with full force to a mandatory retirement age for judges, whose "unique and exacting" duties may be eroded by the ordinary infirmities of age.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
