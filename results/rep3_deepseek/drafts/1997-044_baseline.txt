**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DEBTOR’S LIABILITY FOR TREBLE DAMAGES, ATTORNEY’S FEES, AND COSTS IS DISCHARGEABLE UNDER § 523(a)(2)(A) BECAUSE THOSE AMOUNTS WERE NOT “OBTAINED BY” FRAUD.**

The plain text of 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only “any debt . . . for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by . . . actual fraud.” The critical phrase is “to the extent obtained by.” This language limits nondischargeability to the value of the money, property, or services that the debtor actually received through fraud. Treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs are not “obtained by” the debtor; they are imposed by statute or court order as a penalty. They therefore fall outside the scope of § 523(a)(2)(A).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir. 1991), correctly interprets this language. There, the court held that punitive damages awarded in connection with a fraud claim were dischargeable because they “were not obtained by fraud” but rather “were imposed as punishment.” *Id.* at 199. The court reasoned that the phrase “to the extent obtained by” requires a direct causal link between the fraud and the specific debt. Only the compensatory damages—the amount the debtor actually took—satisfy that requirement. *Id.* The same logic applies here. The treble damages, fees, and costs awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are not sums that Cohen “obtained” from the tenants. They are statutory penalties designed to deter and punish. They are therefore dischargeable.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), is misplaced. *St. Laurent* held that punitive damages are nondischargeable because the term “debt” in § 523(a)(2)(A) is broad enough to encompass any liability arising from fraud, including punitive awards. *Id.* at 678-79. But *St. Laurent* ignored the statute’s limiting language. The phrase “to the extent obtained by” does not sweep in every debt that has some connection to fraud; it reaches only the specific property or value the debtor fraudulently acquired. *Levy*, 951 F.2d at 199. The *St. Laurent* approach reads the critical limiting language out of the statute, contrary to the principle that courts must “give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute.” *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955).

Moreover, *St. Laurent*’s reliance on the “fresh start” policy is unavailing. That policy protects only the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991). But the policy does not authorize courts to expand the statutory text. Congress carefully defined the scope of nondischargeability in § 523(a)(2)(A). If Congress had intended to render all fraud-related liabilities—including punitive awards—nondischargeable, it could have said so. It did not. This Court must enforce the statute as written. *See Connecticut Nat’l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992) (“courts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says”).

**II. THE DEBTOR’S CONDUCT DID NOT CONSTITUTE “ACTUAL FRAUD” UNDER § 523(a)(2)(A).**

Even if the treble damages were nondischargeable, the underlying debt for the rent overcharges is dischargeable because Cohen did not commit “actual fraud” within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A). The bankruptcy court found that Cohen made no affirmative misrepresentation to the tenants. He simply charged rents that exceeded the limits set by the Hoboken Rent Control ordinance. The court nonetheless inferred a “false representation” from Cohen’s silence, reasoning that by setting the rent, he implicitly represented that the amount was lawful. This inference is legally erroneous.

“Actual fraud” under § 523(a)(2)(A) requires a showing that the debtor made a false representation with intent to deceive. *Field v. Mans*, 516 U.S. 59, 70-71 (1995). Silence can constitute a false representation only where the debtor had a duty to disclose. *See, e.g., In re Van Horne*, 823 F.2d 1285, 1288 (8th Cir. 1987). Here, no such duty existed. The tenants did not ask about rent control, and Cohen had no obligation to volunteer information about the ordinance. The bankruptcy court’s conclusion that Cohen’s silence amounted to fraud improperly expands the statute beyond its plain meaning.

Furthermore, the record shows that Cohen was unaware of the rent control limits. He believed he could charge market rates for vacant apartments. His ignorance, while perhaps negligent, does not rise to the level of “actual fraud,” which requires “a knowing and intentional misrepresentation.” *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118-19 (3d Cir. 1995). The lower courts’ reliance on a “reckless disregard” standard conflates negligence with fraud and undermines the statutory requirement of intent.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed. The debt, including the treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs, is dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A).
