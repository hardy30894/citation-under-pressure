**Argument**

**I. The district court abused its discretion by denying Winbush’s timely request for a fingerprint expert and a continuance, depriving him of a fair opportunity to present his defense.**

An indigent criminal defendant is entitled to the “basic tools” of an adequate defense, including expert assistance when it is necessary to test the government’s proof. *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 76-77 (1985). That principle applies not only at trial but to the preparation of a meaningful defense where expert evidence is central to the government’s case. *Caldwell v. Mississippi*, 472 U.S. 320, 323 n.1 (1985); *Little v. Armontrout*, 835 F.2d 1240, 1243-44 (8th Cir. 1987).

Here, the government affirmatively put fingerprint evidence in issue by disclosing experts to testify that no latent prints of value were recovered and to explain why such results were unsurprising on the items seized. Winbush sought a defense fingerprint expert to examine the evidence and explain the significance of the absence of prints. That request went directly to the reliability and weight of the government’s forensic presentation. Refusing expert assistance in these circumstances was an abuse of discretion. See *United States v. Moore*, 997 F.2d 55, 59 (5th Cir. 1993); *United States v. Durant*, 545 F.2d 823, 827-28 (2d Cir. 1976).

The denial was especially unreasonable because the court grounded its ruling on timing alone, despite the absence of any showing that the request was a tactic for delay. A continuance must be granted when denial is arbitrary and substantially impairs the defense. *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964); *Morris v. Slappy*, 461 U.S. 1, 11-12 (1983). Although trial courts enjoy broad discretion over scheduling, that discretion is not unlimited where a denial prevents a defendant from securing essential evidence. *United States v. Sellers*, 645 F.3d 830, 834-35 (7th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Valenzuela-Bernal*, 458 U.S. 858, 867 (1982).

The prejudice was concrete. The government relied on the absence of fingerprints to bolster its account of the search and seizure of the firearm and crack cocaine. A defense expert could have explained that the absence of prints says little about possession, handling, or ownership. Expert assistance also would have supported cross-examination of the government’s forensic witnesses and countered any improper inference that lack of prints corroborated the prosecution’s theory. See *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 324-31 (2006); *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302-03 (1973). On this record, the last-minute denial of both expert assistance and a brief continuance “arbitrarily” cut off a material line of defense. *Ungar*, 376 U.S. at 589.

**II. The evidence was insufficient to prove possession of the Kentucky crack, the Tennessee crack, or possession of the firearm in furtherance of drug trafficking beyond a reasonable doubt.**

A conviction cannot stand unless, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government, any rational jury could find the elements proven beyond a reasonable doubt. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). Even under that deferential standard, the government must establish a legally sufficient nexus between the defendant and each charged object. *United States v. Kitchen*, 57 F.3d 516, 520-21 (7th Cir. 1995).

The record did not permit a rational finding that Winbush possessed the Kentucky crack or Tennessee crack. Police recovered the Kentucky crack from a jacket found along the pursuit route after Winbush had fled; they found the Tennessee crack one block away on another street. No witness saw Winbush discard either bag, and no forensic evidence tied him to the narcotics. Constructive possession requires proof of both power and intent to control the contraband. *United States v. Richardson*, 208 F.3d 626, 632 (7th Cir. 2000); *United States v. Kitchen*, 57 F.3d at 520-21. Mere proximity or presence near discarded drugs is not enough. *United States v. Thomas*, 321 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 2003); *United States v. Jackson*, 598 F.3d 340, 349-50 (7th Cir. 2010). At most, the evidence showed that Winbush ran along a route where drugs were later recovered.

The same flaw defeats the § 924(c) count. To prove possession of a firearm “in furtherance of” drug trafficking, the government must show more than contemporaneous possession; it must prove that the firearm advanced or helped forward the drug crime. *United States v. Castillo*, 406 F.3d 806, 813-14 (7th Cir. 2005); *United States v. Brown*, 726 F.3d 993, 997-98 (7th Cir. 2013). A gun merely present during a drug offense does not suffice. *United States v. Haynes*, 582 F.3d 686, 692-93 (7th Cir. 2009). Because the firearm was recovered only after Winbush abandoned the vehicle and jacket, and because the government offered no evidence that the gun protected, emboldened, or facilitated the prior sale, the evidence did not satisfy § 924(c). See *Castillo*, 406 F.3d at 813-14.

**III. The judgment should be reversed, or at minimum the case remanded for a new trial.**

At minimum, the denial of expert assistance and a continuance warrants a new trial. Where a defendant is prevented from presenting a meaningful defense, reversal is required if the error is not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967); *United States v. Lane*, 474 U.S. 438, 449 (1986). Here, the government’s case depended heavily on inferences about possession and forensic significance. Because the court foreclosed the defense from testing those inferences with expert evidence, the convictions cannot stand.
