**ARGUMENT**

**I. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Comports with the Sixth Amendment’s Jury Trial Guarantee.**

The Sixth Amendment secures to every defendant the right to “a trial, by an impartial jury.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right, however, does not extend to the manner in which a jury’s verdict is reached, so long as the verdict is the product of a unanimous jury’s deliberation. *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930) (“The requirement of unanimity . . . is not an essential element of the Sixth Amendment.”). In *Patton*, the Court upheld a federal statute permitting convictions by a three-fourths jury vote, rejecting the argument that the Sixth Amendment mandated unanimity. *Id.* at 292. The Court reasoned that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee “does not require a verdict of guilty by twelve, but only that the finding of guilt shall be by a jury of twelve.” *Id.* at 288. Thus, so long as the jury’s verdict is unanimous, the Sixth Amendment is satisfied.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme adheres to this principle. The jury’s advisory sentence is the product of a unanimous vote, and the trial court—acting as the ultimate sentencer—weighs the aggravating and mitigating factors before imposing a sentence. *Spaziano v. Florida*, 434 U.S. 1301, 1313 (1977) (Rehnquist, J., in chambers) (noting that Florida’s scheme “permits the jury to render an advisory verdict, which the trial judge is free to accept or reject”). This structure ensures that the jury’s role is not diminished; rather, it is the trial court that makes the ultimate factual findings necessary for a death sentence. *Id.* at 1314 (“The trial judge, not the jury, makes the critical findings of fact as to the aggravating and mitigating circumstances.”). Thus, Florida’s scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee.

**II. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment’s Prohibition Against Cruel and Unusual Punishment.**

The Eighth Amendment forbids punishments that are “cruel and unusual,” but it does not impose rigid procedural requirements on the states. *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 313 (1972) (Powell, J., dissenting) (“The Eighth Amendment is not a static concept; it must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.”). In *Furman*, the Court struck down Georgia’s death penalty statute because it failed to provide meaningful guidance to the sentencer, leading to arbitrary and capricious imposition of the death penalty. *Id.* at 313-14. However, the Court did not hold that the Eighth Amendment required a particular sentencing procedure, only that the procedure must not be arbitrary or discriminatory.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme avoids the arbitrariness condemned in *Furman*. The statute requires the trial court to find at least one statutorily enumerated aggravating factor before imposing a death sentence. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(3) (1974). The aggravating factors are narrowly defined, and the trial court must weigh them against any mitigating factors before imposing a sentence. *Spaziano*, 434 U.S. at 1314. This structured sentencing process ensures that the death penalty is not imposed arbitrarily, but rather only after careful consideration of the individual circumstances of the offense and the offender.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require that a jury, rather than a judge, make the ultimate sentencing decision. *McGautha v. California*, 402 U.S. 183, 208 (1971) (“The Constitution does not prohibit a judge from imposing a heavier sentence than he would have imposed if he had been sitting without a jury.”). In *McGautha*, the Court upheld California’s capital sentencing scheme, which permitted the trial judge to impose a death sentence after considering the jury’s advisory verdict. *Id.* at 186. The Court reasoned that the Sixth Amendment does not require jury sentencing, and the Eighth Amendment does not impose additional procedural requirements. *Id.* at 208. Thus, Florida’s scheme—like California’s—does not violate the Eighth Amendment.

**III. The Jury’s Role in Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Satisfies the Eighth Amendment’s Demand for Reliability.**

The Eighth Amendment requires that the death penalty be imposed only after “the fullest consideration of the factors relevant to the individual defendant and the circumstances of the crime.” *Furman*, 408 U.S. at 313 (Powell, J., dissenting). Florida’s scheme ensures this reliability by requiring the jury to consider the aggravating and mitigating factors before rendering an advisory verdict. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2) (1974). The trial court then conducts an independent weighing of these factors before imposing a sentence. *Spaziano*, 434 U.S. at 1314. This two-step process ensures that the death penalty is not imposed without careful consideration of the individual circumstances of the case.

Furthermore, the Eighth Amendment does not require that the jury’s verdict be binding. *McGautha*, 402 U.S. at 208. The trial court’s role as the ultimate sentencer ensures that the death penalty is imposed only after a thorough and individualized consideration of the aggravating and mitigating factors. *Id.* Thus, Florida’s scheme satisfies the Eighth Amendment’s demand for reliability.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
