**Argument**

The Eleventh Circuit’s judgment should be vacated because *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), announced a new substantive rule that applies retroactively on collateral review. The court below treated *Johnson* as if it were merely an interpretation of the Armed Career Criminal Act’s residual clause in a pending case. But *Johnson* did far more: it held that imposing an enhanced sentence under the residual clause violates due process because the clause is unconstitutionally vague. 576 U.S. at 606. That holding alters “the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes,” and thus fits squarely within the framework for retroactivity on collateral review. *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351–52 (2004).

Under *Teague v. Lane*, new substantive rules generally apply retroactively, while new procedural rules generally do not. 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989); *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190, 198 (2016). Substantive rules include decisions that “narrow the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms” or that place “certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 351–52 (citation omitted). *Johnson* did precisely that. By invalidating the ACCA residual clause, the Court removed one entire category of prior convictions from the statute’s reach. After *Johnson*, a defendant cannot be sentenced under that clause at all. 576 U.S. at 606. A rule that “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes” is substantive and retroactive. *Montgomery*, 577 U.S. at 198.

This Court’s retroactivity precedents confirm the point. In *Welch v. United States*, the Court held that *Johnson* announced a substantive rule because “[b]efore Johnson, the ACCA could reach certain conduct; after Johnson, it cannot.” 578 U.S. 120, 129 (2016). The Court explained that *Johnson* “changed the substantive reach of the Armed Career Criminal Act, altering ‘the range of conduct or the class of persons that the [Act] punishes.’” Id. at 130 (quoting *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353). That is the very definition of a substantive rule. Id. at 129–30. The Court further held that *Johnson* is retroactive because it had “more than just procedural significance”; it “affected the reach of the underlying statute rather than the judicial procedures by which the statute is applied.” Id. at 130–31.

The government cannot avoid retroactivity by characterizing *Johnson* as a mere vagueness decision. This Court has repeatedly recognized that a rule invalidating a criminal provision for vagueness is substantive when it removes an entire category of punishment. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 602–06; *Welch*, 578 U.S. at 129–31. That is unlike a rule “regulating only the manner of determining the defendant’s culpability,” which is procedural. *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353. *Johnson* did not regulate factfinding; it eliminated the ACCA residual clause altogether. 576 U.S. at 606. Once that clause was invalidated, prior convictions that qualified only under the residual clause could no longer support the ACCA’s 15-year mandatory minimum. *Welch*, 578 U.S. at 129–31.

Nor does finality justify denying relief. When the Court announces a substantive rule, “there is no interest in finality sufficient to justify denying retroactive effect.” *Montgomery*, 577 U.S. at 200. That principle is especially compelling here, where the mandatory minimum sentence at issue exceeds the ordinary statutory maximum absent the ACCA enhancement. See 18 U.S.C. § 924(a)(2); *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 606. Welch’s sentence was enhanced by a clause the Court has held void. He is therefore serving a sentence the law no longer permits.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary approach cannot stand. A constitutional rule that “places particular conduct or persons covered by the statute beyond the State’s power to punish” is substantive and retroactive. *Montgomery*, 577 U.S. at 198. Because *Johnson* invalidated the ACCA residual clause and narrowed the statute’s scope, it applies retroactively to Welch’s collateral challenge. *Welch*, 578 U.S. at 129–31.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse and hold that *Johnson* applies retroactively on collateral review.
