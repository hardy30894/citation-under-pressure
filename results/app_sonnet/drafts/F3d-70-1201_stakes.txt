# ARGUMENT

## I. Rule 407 Does Not Bar Evidence of Subsequent Remedial Measures in a Strict Products Liability Action, and, in Any Event, the Evidence Here Was Independently Admissible for Impeachment.

Federal Rule of Evidence 407 provides that "[e]vidence of subsequent remedial measures is not admissible to prove negligence or culpable conduct in connection with the event," but the Rule expressly "does not require the exclusion of evidence of subsequent measures when offered for another purpose, such as proving ownership, control, or feasibility of precautionary measures, if controverted, or impeachment." Fed. R. Evid. 407. The policy underlying the Rule—encouraging manufacturers to make voluntary safety improvements without fear that doing so will be used as an admission of fault—has substantially less force in a strict liability action, where the focus is on the condition of the product rather than the reasonableness of the manufacturer's conduct. See Ault v. International Harvester Co., 528 P.2d 1148 (Cal. 1974) (holding that the rationale for excluding subsequent remedial measures does not extend to strict products liability claims because such claims do not turn on the culpability of the defendant's conduct). This Circuit has not yet decided whether Rule 407 applies with equal force to strict liability claims, and Wood submits that it should not be applied to exclude evidence bearing on the product's design where, as here, the manufacturer's own conduct places the safety of that design squarely in issue.

The Court need not resolve that question definitively to reverse, however, because the evidence at issue was independently admissible under Rule 407's impeachment exception. Morbark's counsel told the jury in opening statement that "the evidence will indicate that after Hurricane Andrew the Army Corps of Engineers ordered thirty machines just like the one that is involved in this case," R4-141-22, and on cross-examination of Infinger elicited testimony that the City of DeFuniak Springs "still" used "that Morbark chipper machine," id. at 44—plainly inviting the jury to infer that the seventeen-inch infeed chute was unchanged and was the safest design available. Courts have long recognized that once a defendant controverts the feasibility or continued use of a safer design, or otherwise leaves the jury with a false impression on that subject, the plaintiff may introduce evidence of subsequent remedial measures to correct that impression, even though such evidence would otherwise be barred. See Grenada Steel Indus., Inc. v. Alabama Oxygen Co., 695 F.2d 883 (5th Cir. 1983); Chase v. General Motors Corp., 856 F.2d 17 (4th Cir. 1988); Anderson v. Malloy, 700 F.2d 1208 (8th Cir. 1983); Herndon v. Seven Bar Flying Serv., Inc., 716 F.2d 1322 (10th Cir. 1983). The district court correctly applied that principle when it overruled Morbark's objection and allowed Wood's counsel to elicit testimony that the chute had in fact been lengthened, explaining that Morbark had "opened the door" to "fair rebuttal" by "making suggestions to the jury that the city was still using that chipper" without change. R4-141-56. That ruling was correct, and the evidence was properly before the jury for impeachment purposes.

## II. The District Court's Instruction Directing the Jury to Disregard the Properly Admitted Impeachment Testimony Was Reversible Error Requiring a New Trial.

Having correctly admitted Infinger's testimony that the infeed chute had been lengthened after the accident, the district court erred by later instructing the jury to disregard that same evidence. Once evidence has been properly admitted for a permissible purpose under Rule 407, a court may not thereafter withdraw it from the jury's consideration without working precisely the prejudice the Rule of Evidence was designed to avoid on one side while inflicting it on the other. The court itself recognized, in the colloquy outside the jury's presence, that Morbark had invited the very testimony it now sought to strike, and that permitting Morbark to benefit from an instruction erasing that testimony would allow Morbark "to tak[e] unfair advantage of the Court's ruling." R4-141-56. An instruction that tells the jury to disregard properly admitted evidence, after the jury has already heard testimony directly rebutting a misleading impression created by the opposing party, cannot be reconciled with the court's own recognition that the door had been opened.

This error was not harmless. The jury was told in opening statement that the chipper's design remained unchanged and was still in governmental use nationwide, and on cross-examination was led to believe the same. The only evidence correcting that false impression—Infinger's testimony that the chute had been lengthened—was then withdrawn from the jury's consideration by the court's admonition, leaving the jury with the very misimpression Rule 407's impeachment exception exists to prevent. Because the erroneous instruction went to the central contested issue in the case—whether the seventeen-inch infeed chute was defective and unreasonably dangerous—the error affected Wood's substantial rights, and the district court abused its discretion in denying her motion for a new trial.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
