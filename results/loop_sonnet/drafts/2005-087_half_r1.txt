**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, holding that "the process of formulating a rule of criminal responsibility... is one in which the legislature of each State has wide latitude," and that due process does not compel adoption of any particular test of criminal responsibility. *Id.* at 800–01. If a State may constitutionally place the entire burden of proving insanity on the defendant, it follows a fortiori that a State may also determine the channel through which mental-disease evidence enters the case.

This principle was reinforced in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), where the plurality declined to constitutionalize any single doctrine of criminal responsibility, observing that "traditional common-law concepts of personal accountability" remain matters "of state law," and that the Court should be "reluctant to take this step in the absence of some indication... that a change in the direction is required." *Id.* at 536. Arizona's rule confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry is precisely the sort of substantive line-drawing that *Leland* and *Powell* leave to the States.

This channeling approach is also consistent with history. The insanity defense as historically understood, from *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a mechanism for negating the prosecution's proof of intent on a fact-by-fact basis. Arizona's rule simply preserves that traditional separation.

Moreover, this Court has already sustained the exclusion of similar categories of psychiatric evidence offered to negate intent. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the Court upheld a trial court's refusal to instruct the jury that evidence of the defendant's mental deficiency could reduce murder to manslaughter by negating premeditation, reasoning that the District of Columbia was entitled to adhere to "the practical difficulties of ingrafting the doctrine of partial responsibility onto our jurisprudence" without violating due process. *Id.* at 475–76. If the exclusion of mental-deficiency evidence offered to negate premeditation was constitutional in *Fisher*, Arizona's more modest rule—which still permits the evidence to be considered on insanity—is a fortiori permissible.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Leland* and *Powell*, Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules. As Justice Cardozo explained in *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), the relevant inquiry is whether the challenged practice "violates some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Id.* at 328.

The States' latitude to fashion criminal procedure is further reflected in *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516 (1884), where the Court sustained a state prosecution by information rather than grand-jury indictment, explaining that "any legal proceeding enforced by public authority, whether sanctioned by age and custom, or newly devised in the discretion of the legislative power, in furtherance of the general public good, which regards and preserves these principles of liberty and justice, must be held to be due process of law." *Id.* at 537. Arizona's evidentiary channeling rule is precisely such a considered legislative and judicial judgment about how psychiatric evidence should be weighed in the criminal process; it does not exclude the evidence but directs it to the historically established inquiry for which it is most probative.

Nor does the exclusion of Clark's evidence from the mens rea determination amount to the kind of fundamental unfairness this Court condemned in *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941). There, the Court explained that "the aim of the requirement of due process is not to exclude presumptively false evidence, but to prevent fundamental unfairness in the use of evidence, whether true or false." *Id.* at 236. Arizona did not prevent the use of Clark's evidence; it permitted full presentation of the expert testimony on the insanity claim, the very issue to which such evidence has traditionally been addressed. This is not a case, as in *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), where the defendant was denied any meaningful opportunity to be heard on a matter central to his defense.

Finally, *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), which addressed the allocation of the burden of proof on insanity under federal law, confirms that mental-disease evidence has historically been evaluated within the insanity framework, not as an independent means of negating specific elements of the offense. Nothing in that decision, or in the due process principles articulated above, requires that evidence relevant to one recognized defense must also be admissible, unconstrained by that defense's structure, to defeat an element the prosecution must otherwise prove.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove. That is precisely the kind of rule the above authorities sanction. Arizona's channeling rule does not offend any "principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people" as to be fundamental, *Palko*, 302 U.S. at 328, and this Court should affirm.
