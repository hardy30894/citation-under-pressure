**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Denying Appellant’s Motion for a Fingerprint Expert and Motion to Continue**

A. The District Court Erred in Denying Appellant’s Motion for a Fingerprint Expert

Appellant’s timely motion for a fingerprint expert was improperly denied. The denial of a motion for expert assistance is reviewed for abuse of discretion. *United States v. Baines*, 573 F.3d 979, 988 (7th Cir. 2009). The Supreme Court has recognized that “[t]he Constitution guarantees criminal defendants a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense.” *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986). This right includes the ability to present expert testimony when it is “relevant to a critical issue in the case.” *United States v. Ross*, 33 F.3d 1507, 1521 (11th Cir. 1994) (citing *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 76 (1985)).

Here, the government’s case relied heavily on the physical evidence recovered from the scene, including the firearm and narcotics. The government’s expert, Michael Shay, testified that no latent fingerprints of value were discovered on the evidence. Appellant sought a fingerprint expert to challenge this testimony and explain the significance of the absence of fingerprints. The district court’s denial of this request deprived Appellant of the opportunity to present a complete defense.

The Seventh Circuit has held that “[w]hen a defendant makes a showing that his or her constitutional right to present evidence in his or her defense is at stake, the district court must consider whether the proposed evidence is relevant and whether the testimony of the proposed expert is necessary to assist the jury in understanding the evidence.” *United States v. Hall*, 473 F.3d 917, 922 (7th Cir. 2007). The district court’s refusal to allow Appellant to present expert testimony on the significance of the absence of fingerprints was an abuse of discretion.

B. The District Court Erred in Denying Appellant’s Motion to Continue

Appellant’s motion to continue was also improperly denied. The denial of a motion to continue is reviewed for abuse of discretion. *United States v. Canino*, 949 F.2d 928, 936 (7th Cir. 1991). The Supreme Court has held that “[t]he right to a speedy trial does not include the right to a trial at the earliest possible moment.” *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514, 521 (1972). The Seventh Circuit has recognized that “[a] continuance may be necessary to allow a defendant to prepare his defense, particularly when the government’s case is complex or when the defendant has had insufficient time to review the evidence.” *United States v. Moya-Gomez*, 860 F.2d 706, 732 (7th Cir. 1988).

Here, Appellant filed his motion for a fingerprint expert and motion to continue only six days before trial, after the government had already disclosed its expert witnesses. The district court denied the motion, stating that the request was “woefully late.” However, the Seventh Circuit has held that “[t]he timeliness of a motion for a continuance is not determined solely by the date it is filed, but also by the reasons for the delay and the prejudice to the opposing party.” *United States v. Salazar*, 425 F.3d 800, 805 (7th Cir. 2005). The district court’s denial of Appellant’s motion to continue was an abuse of discretion.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Support Appellant’s Conviction for Possession of a Firearm in Furtherance of a Drug Trafficking Crime**

Appellant’s conviction for possession of a firearm in furtherance of a drug trafficking crime, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 924(c), must be reversed. The sufficiency of the evidence is reviewed de novo. *United States v. Witherspoon*, 449 F.3d 773, 778 (7th Cir. 2006). To sustain a conviction under § 924(c), the government must prove that the defendant “possessed a firearm in furtherance of” a drug trafficking crime. *Smith v. United States*, 508 U.S. 223, 238 (1993).

Here, the government failed to present sufficient evidence to establish that Appellant possessed the firearm in furtherance of a drug trafficking crime. The firearm was recovered approximately two blocks from where the drug transaction occurred, and Appellant was no longer in possession of the firearm when he was apprehended. The government’s theory that Appellant possessed the firearm in furtherance of a drug trafficking crime is speculative and unsupported by the evidence.

The Seventh Circuit has held that “[t]o prove that a firearm was possessed ‘in furtherance of’ a drug trafficking crime, the government must show a nexus between the firearm and the drug trafficking crime.” *United States v. Mack*, 345 F.3d 779, 785 (7th Cir. 2003). The government’s evidence here is insufficient to establish such a nexus. Therefore, Appellant’s conviction for possession of a firearm in furtherance of a drug trafficking crime must be reversed.
