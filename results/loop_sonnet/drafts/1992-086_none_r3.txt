# ARGUMENT

The Center Moriches Union Free School District, having opened its facilities to community groups for years, singled out religious speech for exclusion by refusing Lamb's Chapel access to show a film on family values. That selective exclusion violates the First Amendment for four independent reasons: it discriminates against protected expression in a forum the District itself created; it discriminates specifically on the basis of religious viewpoint; the school setting provides no license for such discrimination; and the Establishment Clause, properly understood, poses no bar to equal treatment.

## I. Once the District Opened Its Facilities to Community Groups for Expressive Activity, It Could Not Selectively Exclude Speech Because of Its Religious Content.

The First Amendment's guarantee of free expression is not confined to the streets and parks that "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public and, time out of mind, have been used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). When government opens property to the public for expressive use, it assumes an obligation of even-handedness among speakers and subjects. The District did exactly that here, permitting civic organizations, musical performers, and outside lecturers to use its auditorium after school hours over an extended period. Having thrown open its doors to community expression on secular topics, the District could not slam them shut solely because Lamb's Chapel's message on family life and child-rearing happened to be presented from a religious perspective.

That the Chapel's message took the form of a film does not diminish its claim to protection. This Court has long recognized that motion pictures are "a significant medium for the communication of ideas," *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495, 501 (1952), entitled to the same constitutional solicitude as the spoken or printed word. The District cannot escape its obligation of neutrality by treating a film about family values differently than it would treat a live lecture on the identical subject, merely because the film was produced from a religious point of view.

## II. The District's Exclusion of Religious Speech, While Tolerating Comparable Secular Speech, Constitutes Impermissible Viewpoint Discrimination.

This Court has repeatedly struck down government action denying access to public facilities precisely because a speaker's message was religious, while permitting similar secular uses. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court invalidated the denial of a park permit to a religious group where other groups had been allowed to use the same park, holding that the denial rested not on neutral criteria but on official disapproval of the group's beliefs. The Court reached the same result in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), and in *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290 (1951), where unbridled licensing discretion was used to silence a speaker because officials disagreed with the content of his religious message. See also *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948) (condemning a licensing scheme that left officials free to pick and choose among speakers without reference to objective, content-neutral standards).

The District's own correspondence confirms the identical defect here. Its clerk denied Lamb's Chapel's application because the film "does appear to be church related," even while the District permitted a "Psychology and the Unknown" lecture series touching on parapsychology and metaphysics, and a musical performance carrying religious overtones by the Southern Harmonizers. As in *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, and *Kunz*, the District drew its line not along neutral, content-independent criteria, but precisely along the forbidden axis of religious viewpoint. No government official "can prescribe what shall be orthodox in politics, nationalism, religion, or other matters of opinion." *West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943).

## III. That the Forum Is School Property Does Not License Suppression of Disfavored Viewpoints.

Public school property is no exception to these settled principles. This Court has made clear that "state-operated schools may not be enclaves of totalitarianism," and that those who use school facilities do not "shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate." *Tinker v. Des Moines Indep. Community Sch. Dist.*, 393 U.S. 503, 506, 511 (1969). Nor does the collective and congregational character of Lamb's Chapel's request diminish the protection due its speech; the right of like-minded citizens to advance shared beliefs through joint activity and assembly is itself protected by the First and Fourteenth Amendments. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 430 (1963). The District cannot invoke the school setting to justify discrimination that would be plainly unconstitutional if practiced in a park or on a street corner.

## IV. Equal Access for Lamb's Chapel Would Vindicate, Not Violate, the Establishment Clause's Command of Neutrality.

Anticipating an Establishment Clause defense, the District errs in assuming that equal treatment of religious speech amounts to state endorsement of religion. This Court's Establishment Clause jurisprudence demands neutrality, not hostility, toward religious expression. Government "cannot exclude" citizens from generally available benefits "because of their faith, or lack of it." *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947). And in *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952), the Court explained that to bar accommodation of religious activity "would be to find in the Constitution a requirement that the government show a callous indifference to religious groups." Extending to Lamb's Chapel the same access afforded secular civic and cultural organizations would reflect the very neutrality the Establishment Clause commands—not offend it.

## CONCLUSION

The District's categorical exclusion of religious speech from a forum otherwise open to comparable secular expression cannot be reconciled with *Hague*, *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, *Kunz*, *Saia*, *Burstyn*, *Barnette*, *Tinker*, *Button*, *Everson*, or *Zorach*. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
