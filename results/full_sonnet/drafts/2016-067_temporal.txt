# ARGUMENT

## I. The FDCPA's Plain Text Makes the Debt Collector/Creditor Distinction Turn on Ownership of the Debt at the Time of Collection, Not on the Debt's History.

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act defines a "debt collector," in relevant part, as any person "who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The word "another" governs both halves of that clause, because "[w]hen several words are followed by a clause which is applicable as much to the first and other words as to the last, the natural construction of the language demands that the clause be read as applicable to all." *Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor*, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920). The statute's coverage thus hinges on a single variable: whether the debt collected belongs to someone other than the collector.

Santander does not fit that description. It purchased petitioners' accounts outright from CitiFinancial Auto and thereafter collected the debts for its own account, not on CitiFinancial's behalf. Because it owned the debts, its collection activity was activity to collect money owed to *itself*, not "another." That plain reading should resolve the case: "It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

## II. Petitioners' Default-Status Test Has No Footing in the Statute's Words.

Petitioners ask this Court to graft a default-status rule onto § 1692a(6)'s threshold definition by borrowing language from an exclusion, § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), that applies only "to the extent" a person is "collecting or attempting to collect any debt . . . owed or due . . . another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) (emphasis added). By its own terms, that exclusion presupposes the person is already collecting debt owed to someone *else*; it says nothing about a person, like Santander, who purchases and thereafter collects debt owed to itself. An exclusion cannot expand the affirmative definition it qualifies. Cf. *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Prods. Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (specific statutory provisions control rather than being read more broadly than their own text permits).

Nor can petitioners rescue their theory through the definition of "creditor" in § 1692a(4), which excludes "any person to the extent that he receives an assignment or transfer of a debt in default solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another." That exclusion, again, applies only to persons collecting "for another"—a category petitioners' own complaint places Santander outside of once it purchased the receivables for its own portfolio. "[T]here is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress's words here draw the line at ownership, not default status.

## III. Santander's Status Must Be Judged at the Time of the Challenged Conduct, When It Was Collecting Its Own Debt.

Whatever role Santander played before it purchased petitioners' loans—petitioners allege it once serviced the accounts for CitiFinancial Auto—that prior role is irrelevant to whether Santander was a "debt collector" when it engaged in the conduct petitioners challenge. Statutory terms take their meaning from the context and moment of application, for "[a] word is not a crystal, transparent and unchanged[;] it is the skin of a living thought and may vary greatly in color and content according to the circumstances and the time in which it is used." *Boston Sand & Gravel Co. v. United States*, 278 U.S. 41, 48 (1928). By December 1, 2011, when Santander purchased the loans and began the collection activity at issue, it was no longer acting as anyone's agent; it was collecting on debt it owned outright. Its earlier servicing-era relationship to petitioners' accounts cannot convert its post-purchase conduct—collection of its own receivables—into the collection of "debts . . . due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6).

## IV. Conclusion

Congress regulated those who collect debts on behalf of others; it did not regulate those, like Santander, who purchase debts and thereafter collect them for their own account. Petitioners' default-status rule would require this Court to disregard the plain words Congress chose, *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485, and to treat an exclusionary clause as though it created the very category it merely qualifies, *Porto Rico Ry.*, 253 U.S. at 348. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
