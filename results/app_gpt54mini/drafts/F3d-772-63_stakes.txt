**ARGUMENT**

### I. Foley plausibly alleged that Wells Fargo breached enforceable settlement obligations owed to him as a class member.

A complaint survives dismissal if it states a claim that is plausible on its face. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009); *Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007). On a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the court must accept well-pleaded facts as true and draw reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Medina-Velázquez v. Hernández-Gregorat*, 767 F.3d 103, 108 (1st Cir. 2014); *Watterson v. Page*, 987 F.2d 1, 3 (1st Cir. 1993).

Foley alleged that he was a member of the Pick-a-Payment settlement class and that the settlement required Wells Fargo first to consider him for a HAMP modification and, if he did not qualify, then to consider him for a MAP2R modification under the settlement’s waterfall process. Those allegations plausibly state a claim for breach of contract or breach of the settlement agreement. A settlement agreement is a contract, and its interpretation is governed by ordinary principles of contract law. *United States v. ITT Cont’l Baking Co.*, 420 U.S. 223, 238 (1975); *In re Air Crash Disaster at Boston, Mass. on July 31, 1973*, 399 F. Supp. 1106, 1110 (D. Mass. 1975), aff’d, 742 F.2d 93 (1st Cir. 1984). A nonparty class member may enforce a class settlement that confers benefits on the class. *Langford v. Chrysler Motors Corp.*, 513 F.2d 1121, 1123–24 (2d Cir. 1975); *In re Prudential Ins. Co. of Am. Sales Practices Litig.*, 148 F.3d 283, 325–26 (3d Cir. 1998).

Here, the complaint alleges that Wells Fargo continued to tell Foley for months that HAMP was the only program for which he might qualify, failed for months to send the promised application, failed to process his completed application, and proceeded toward foreclosure without the contractual consideration of modification that the settlement required. Those facts are enough at the pleading stage to support a reasonable inference that Wells Fargo did not perform the sequential consideration process promised in the settlement. See *Massachusetts v. Mylan Labs.*, 357 F. Supp. 2d 314, 318–19 (D. Mass. 2005) (contract claim survives where complaint alleges nonperformance of specific obligations).

### II. Foley adequately stated claims under Massachusetts General Laws chapter 93A.

Chapter 93A prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices in trade or commerce. Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A, §§ 2, 11. Conduct may be actionable even if no traditional tort is proved, so long as it falls within “the penumbra of some common-law, statutory, or other established concept of unfairness,” is “immoral, unethical, oppressive, or unscrupulous,” or causes substantial injury. *PMP Assocs., Inc. v. Globe Newspaper Co.*, 366 Mass. 593, 596 (1975); *VMark Software, Inc. v. EMC Corp.*, 37 Mass. App. Ct. 610, 620 (1994).

Massachusetts courts have repeatedly held that deceptive loan-servicing and foreclosure-related conduct can violate chapter 93A. *Miller v. Cotter*, 448 Mass. 671, 681–82 (2007); *Drum v. Sanofi-Aventis U.S., LLC*, 2010 WL 1302962, at *5 (Mass. Super. Ct. Mar. 29, 2010) (applying chapter 93A to unfair business conduct in consumer transaction). Foley alleged that Wells Fargo misrepresented the status of his modification review, repeatedly failed to provide the promised application, ignored his submissions, and then sent misleading communications about short sale and deed in lieu options while foreclosure loomed. At minimum, those allegations plausibly describe deceptive and unfair conduct in mortgage servicing. See *Carter v. Hilliard*, 383 Mass. 550, 552–53 (1981); *Casavant v. Norwegian Cruise Line Ltd.*, 460 Mass. 500, 506–07 (2011).

Wells Fargo’s preemption argument does not defeat the chapter 93A claims at the pleading stage. Federal law preempts state law only when Congress clearly intended that result. *Cipollone v. Liggett Grp., Inc.*, 505 U.S. 504, 516 (1992); *English v. Gen. Elec. Co.*, 496 U.S. 72, 78–79 (1990). Even where federal banking law occupies part of the field, state laws of general application are not displaced absent a direct conflict. *Watters v. Wachovia Bank, N.A.*, 550 U.S. 1, 11 (2007); *Barnett Bank of Marion Cty., N.A. v. Nelson*, 517 U.S. 25, 31 (1996). Chapter 93A is a law of general applicability, and Foley’s claims concern deceptive servicing and contract performance, not the regulation of interest rates or underwriting standards. Wells Fargo has not shown that compliance with both federal law and chapter 93A is impossible. See *Kurns v. R.R. Friction Prods. Corp.*, 565 U.S. 625, 630–31 (2012).

### III. Foley’s chapter 244 foreclosure-related claim was improperly dismissed.

Massachusetts foreclosure law imposes strict prerequisites on a mortgagee’s right to foreclose. *U.S. Bank Nat’l Ass’n v. Ibanez*, 458 Mass. 637, 647–48 (2011). The statutory scheme requires compliance with the mortgage’s power of sale and with applicable notice requirements. Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, §§ 14, 35A; *Pinti v. Emigrant Mortg. Co.*, 472 Mass. 226, 233–34 (2015). A foreclosure taken without adherence to those requirements is void or voidable depending on the defect. *Ibanez*, 458 Mass. at 651.

Foley alleged that Wells Fargo scheduled foreclosure while failing to carry out the settlement-mandated modification review and while sending him communications that obscured rather than clarified his status. Those allegations plausibly support a claim that Wells Fargo was not entitled to proceed as it did under chapter 244 and the settlement’s servicing commitments. At the very least, Foley was entitled to discovery before dismissal of this claim. See *Orr v. Kinder Morgan Energy Partners, L.P.*, 350 F.3d 1, 10 (1st Cir. 2003).

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
