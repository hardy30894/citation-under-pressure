**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING EXPERT TESTIMONY ON DECEPTIVE BEHAVIOR WITHOUT A PROPER FOUNDATION UNDER FEDERAL RULE OF EVIDENCE 702.**

Appellant challenges the admission of FBI Agent Jones’s expert testimony regarding deceptive behavior, arguing it lacked the requisite foundation under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), and Fed. R. Evid. 702. The Supreme Court has made clear that expert testimony must be “both reliable and relevant,” *id.* at 589, and that trial courts must act as “gatekeepers” to exclude unreliable science. *Id.* at 597.

Agent Jones’s testimony failed this standard. He claimed that certain behaviors—such as invoking faith or expressing a preference to die rather than face charges—were indicative of deception. However, this methodology lacks scientific validation. Courts have consistently rejected expert testimony on credibility where it is based on subjective assessments rather than scientifically validated principles. *See United States v. Velasquez*, 64 F.3d 844, 851 (7th Cir. 1995) (excluding expert testimony on deceptive behavior as unreliable); *United States v. Brown*, 505 F.3d 85, 93 (1st Cir. 2007) (holding that expert testimony on credibility must be grounded in reliable methodology).

Moreover, Agent Jones’s opinion that “never in [his] career” had he seen an innocent person make a particular statement is inherently unreliable. Such anecdotal assertions do not constitute expert methodology under *Daubert*. *See Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 150 (1999) (extending *Daubert* to all expert testimony, not just scientific). The trial court abused its discretion in admitting this testimony.

**II. THE ADMISSION OF THIS TESTIMONY WAS PLAIN ERROR BECAUSE IT DEPRIVED APPELLANT OF A FAIR TRIAL.**

Even if the testimony were admissible, its admission constituted plain error under Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b) because it likely affected the jury’s verdict. *See United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993) (plain error requires a showing of (1) error, (2) that is plain, and (3) that affects substantial rights).

Agent Jones’s testimony directly impugned Appellant’s credibility by suggesting his statements were deceptive. The jury heard that invoking God to support truthfulness was a “deceptive statement” and that his behavior during interrogation was “common among the criminal element.” This improper bolstering of the government’s case likely swayed the jury’s assessment of Appellant’s guilt. *See United States v. Roach*, 28 F.3d 729, 734 (8th Cir. 1994) (finding plain error where expert testimony improperly bolstered government witnesses).

**III. APPELLANT’S CONDUCT DURING HIS ARREST AND INTERROGATION WAS NOT SUFFICIENT TO OVERCOME THE PLAIN ERROR.**

The government argues that Appellant’s use of a false name and initial refusal to disclose his identity justify the admission of Agent Jones’s testimony. However, this conduct does not render the error harmless. *See Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 307-08 (1991) (harmless error analysis requires examining the impact of the error on the verdict). Appellant’s misidentification was brief and occurred before any formal charges were filed. The improper expert testimony, by contrast, directly attacked his credibility at trial, a critical issue in the case.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse his conviction and remand for a new trial.
