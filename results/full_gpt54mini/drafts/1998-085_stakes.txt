**Argument**

Punitive damages under Title VII do not require proof that the employer’s conduct was independently “egregious” or “outrageous” apart from the state-of-mind showing Congress enacted. The statutory text controls. Congress authorized punitive damages when the plaintiff proves the defendant “engaged in a discriminatory practice . . . with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Nothing in that language adds a freestanding requirement that the underlying conduct be especially shocking, brutal, or outrageous. The operative inquiry is the defendant’s mental state. See *Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n*, 139 F.3d 958, 966-68 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (rejecting an independent “egregiousness” requirement and focusing on malice or reckless indifference).

This Court’s Title VII decisions likewise tie punitive damages to intentional misconduct and culpable awareness, not to a separate threshold of extraordinary wrongfulness. In *St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993), the Court explained that once a plaintiff establishes a prima facie case and discredits the employer’s stated reasons, “the factfinder may infer the ultimate fact of discrimination.” That framework identifies discriminatory intent; it does not impose a heightened “egregiousness” hurdle for remedies. Similarly, *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994), recognized that the Civil Rights Act of 1991 expanded the remedies available for intentional discrimination, including punitive damages, because Congress found that “additional remedies” were needed to deter unlawful discrimination. 42 U.S.C. § 1981 note; *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 255-58.

The court of appeals’ contrary rule cannot be squared with the ordinary meaning of the statute. Congress chose “malice” and “reckless indifference,” terms that already cabin punitive awards to blameworthy state of mind. See *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (punitive damages available under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 when defendant acts “with reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others”). In *Smith*, the Court did not require a separately outrageous act; it held that punitive damages turn on the defendant’s conscious disregard of protected rights. Id. at 51-56. Congress used the same core concept in § 1981a(b)(1), and this Court should give the statutory words the same effect. See *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130, 158 (1967) (opinion of Harlan, J.) (punitive damages depend on the defendant’s “knowledge that [it] was acting in violation of law”).

The D.C. Circuit’s approach also conflicts with the structure of Title VII. Liability for intentional discrimination and availability of punitive damages are distinct questions. A plaintiff proves liability by showing discriminatory intent under *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802-05 (1973), but punitive damages under § 1981a require the additional showing specified by Congress: malice or reckless indifference. Congress did not add an extra “egregiousness” requirement on top of that standard. When Congress intends to require aggravated conduct, it says so. See *Gebser v. Lago Vista Independent School District*, 524 U.S. 274, 290-91 (1998) (rejecting liability rule untethered from the statute’s actual notice and deliberate indifference requirements). Here, too, the statute itself supplies the threshold.

Nor does legislative history justify judicially grafting an additional element onto the text. Although some floor statements referred to “extraordinarily egregious cases,” other legislative materials emphasize the enacted standard of “malice” or “reckless indifference.” The controlling law is the enacted text, not isolated snippets from debate. See *Connecticut National Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992) (courts must presume that “a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there”). Where Congress has spoken plainly, a court may not add a requirement that the statute does not contain. Id. at 254.

Respondent therefore should prevail. The question is not whether the employer’s conduct was independently “egregious,” but whether the employer acted with the culpable mental state Congress identified. That is the rule reflected in the statute, in *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), and in this Court’s Title VII and punitive-damages precedents. The judgment below should be reversed.
