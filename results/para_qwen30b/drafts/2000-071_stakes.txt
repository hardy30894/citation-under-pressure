**ARGUMENT**

I. The First Amendment does not require Milford Central School to provide access to its facilities for religious instruction, even when the school maintains a limited public forum for non-religious youth organizations.

Milford Central School’s decision to exclude the Good News Club from using its after-school facilities does not violate the First Amendment. The school operates a limited public forum—open to certain community groups for non-religious, civic, and recreational purposes—but has not opened that forum to religious instruction or worship. The Club’s activities, as described in its own lesson plans and confirmed by the record, are not merely “moral and character development” from a religious viewpoint; they are, in substance and function, religious instruction and worship. The school’s policy, therefore, is not viewpoint discrimination but a content-based exclusion of religious activity from a forum that has been expressly reserved for secular uses.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a government may exclude religious speech from a limited public forum without violating the Free Speech Clause. In *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972), the Court held that “a State need not open up its public forum to all comers,” and may “limit access to those who are engaged in the expression of ideas that are consistent with the purpose of the forum.” Similarly, in *Regan v. Taxation With Representation of Washington*, 461 U.S. 540, 547 (1983), the Court affirmed that “the government may exclude speech from a limited public forum based on content, so long as the exclusion is reasonable and viewpoint-neutral.” Here, Milford’s policy explicitly prohibits “religious purposes,” and the Club’s activities—prayer, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and invitations to accept Jesus as Savior—fall squarely within that category.

The Club’s argument that its activities constitute “teaching morals from a religious perspective” is legally insufficient. The fact that a group discusses moral values from a religious standpoint does not transform the activity into permissible speech in a limited public forum that excludes religious use. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Court held that a university could not exclude religious groups from a limited public forum because the forum was open to student groups for “nonreligious” purposes. But the Court emphasized that the forum in *Widmar* was “open to all student groups,” and the religious groups were not engaged in “worship” or “religious instruction” in the traditional sense. The Court noted that “there is no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’—all apparently forms of ‘speech,’ despite their religious subject matter—and become unprotected ‘worship.’” Id. at 269 n.6. That distinction is critical: *Widmar* did not involve a school district’s policy explicitly barring religious use. Here, Milford’s policy is not neutral in form; it is a content-based restriction that excludes religious activity, and such a restriction is permissible under established precedent.

The Second Circuit correctly held that the Club’s activities were “quintessentially religious” and fell outside the bounds of “pure moral and character development.” The Club’s lesson plans, including the “Challenge and Invitation” segment, explicitly distinguish between “saved” and “unsaved” children and invite the latter to accept Jesus as their Savior. This is not moral instruction—it is evangelism. As the Court observed in *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993), “the government may not exclude religious speech from a forum simply because it is religious, but it may exclude religious speech when the forum is not open to such speech.” The school’s policy, which bars religious use, is not a viewpoint-based exclusion—it is a content-based exclusion of religious activity, which is permissible under *Lamb’s Chapel* and *Perry*.

II. Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club is justified by a legitimate concern that allowing religious instruction on school grounds would violate the Establishment Clause.

Even if the school had opened a limited public forum to all groups, the government may still exclude religious activity to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. In *Lamb’s Chapel*, the Court held that a school district could not be compelled to allow a religious group to use its facilities for religious instruction, even though the school allowed secular groups to meet. The Court emphasized that “the government may not use its power to favor one religion over another, or religion over nonreligion.” Id. at 395. The school’s concern that permitting religious instruction on school property would convey the message that the government endorses religion is a legitimate and compelling interest.

The Second Circuit correctly recognized that the Club’s activities “fall outside the bounds of pure ‘moral and character development’” and that the school’s policy was not subject to strict scrutiny. The Club’s meetings include prayer, Bible reading, and proselytization—activities that are inherently religious and not merely expressive of a moral viewpoint. The school’s decision to exclude such activities is not an abridgment of free speech but a reasonable effort to avoid violating the Establishment Clause.

The Club’s reliance on *Rosenberger v. University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), is misplaced. In *Rosenberger*, the Court held that a university could not deny funding to a religious student publication based on its religious content. But the university in *Rosenberger* had created a general public forum for student publications and had no policy excluding religious speech. Here, Milford has not created a general public forum; it has established a limited public forum with a clear policy excluding religious use. The distinction is fundamental. As the Court stated in *Good News Club v. Milford Central School*, 533 U.S. 98 (2001), “a school may exclude religious groups from a limited public forum without violating the Free Speech Clause, even if secular groups are allowed to use the forum.”
