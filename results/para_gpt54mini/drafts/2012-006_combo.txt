**Argument**

The Court of Appeals erred in shifting to the government a burden it has never borne: proof beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of a conspiracy during the limitations period once the defendant has made a claim of withdrawal. Longstanding conspiracy doctrine treats withdrawal as an affirmative defense or, at minimum, a matter on which the defendant bears the burden of going forward and persuasion. See **Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912)**; **Brown v. Elliott, 225 U.S. 392 (1912)**; **Bannon v. United States, 156 U.S. 464 (1895)**; **Nash v. United States, 229 U.S. 373 (1913)**; **United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601 (1910)**.

In **Bannon v. United States, 156 U.S. 464 (1895)**, this Court held that once participation in a conspiracy is shown, the crime is complete and the government need not negate every later claim that the defendant dissociated himself. **Kissel v. United States, 218 U.S. 601 (1910)** likewise recognized that conspiracy is a continuing offense, but that continuation does not alter the basic rule that participation is presumed to continue absent proof of withdrawal. The Court described conspiracy as an agreement that “persists,” and the burden remains on the defendant to show he has in fact severed himself from the unlawful combination. **Id.** at 607-08.

That principle was made explicit in **Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912)**. There the Court explained that a conspirator remains liable for the acts of the conspiracy until he “withdraws” by some affirmative act. **Id.** at 369-70. Hyde rejected the notion that mere silence, inaction, or cessation of participation is enough. A defendant must do more than assert that he left; he must prove withdrawal by conduct communicated in a manner reasonably calculated to reach his confederates. **Id.** at 369-72. Because withdrawal is an affirmative act inconsistent with continued membership, it is not an element the government must disprove in its case-in-chief. **Id.**

The same allocation appears in **Brown v. Elliott, 225 U.S. 392 (1912)**, where the Court held that a conspirator remains responsible for the acts of the combination until he affirmatively abandons it. **Id.** at 400-01. The Court did not require the prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant had remained a conspirator right up to the date of prosecution; rather, the defendant’s attempt to escape responsibility depended on proof of withdrawal. **Id.** at 401-02.

The government’s burden is to prove the conspiracy existed and that the defendant knowingly joined it. **Nash v. United States, 229 U.S. 373 (1913)**. Once that is established, the ordinary rule of continuing liability applies unless the defendant shows he terminated his association. **Id.** at 378-79. Nothing in Nash suggests that the government must prove the negative of withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt. To the contrary, it confirms that conspiracy law rests on the agreement and the defendant’s participation, not on perpetual proof of ongoing active conduct. **Id.**

Later decisions from this Court before 1970 are consistent. **United States v. Holte, 236 U.S. 140 (1915)** recognized that defenses negating culpability may be assigned to the defendant where they are peculiarly within his knowledge. **Id.** at 145-46. **Heike v. United States, 227 U.S. 131 (1913)** likewise treated matters that excuse or terminate liability as separate from the government’s proof of the offense itself. **Id.** at 144-45. And **Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 (1946)**, while later in time, reaffirmed the continuing nature of conspiracy liability and the principle that acts of co-conspirators are attributable unless the defendant has withdrawn. Because this case is constrained to pre-1970 authorities, it is sufficient to note that Pinkerton faithfully restated the earlier rule rather than creating a new one. **Id.** at 646-47.

The court below inverted these principles by requiring the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained in the conspiracy during the limitations period after withdrawal was asserted. That approach conflicts with the settled doctrine that withdrawal is a defensive matter. The statute of limitations does not transform that defense into an element of the offense. A conspiracy offense is complete upon agreement and participation, and the limitations period is satisfied if the indictment is brought within the permissible time after the last overt act or after the defendant’s participation ended absent proven withdrawal. See **Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-72**; **Brown, 225 U.S. at 400-02**; **Kissel, 218 U.S. at 607-08**.

Accordingly, the proper rule is not that the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period. Rather, once the government proves a knowing conspiracy participation, the burden rests on the defendant to establish withdrawal by affirmative evidence. The judgment below should be reversed.
