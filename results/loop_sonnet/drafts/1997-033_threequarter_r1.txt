# ARGUMENT

## I. The plain, mandatory text of Section 1407(a) forecloses any transferee court's use of Section 1404(a) to retain a case for trial.

Section 1407(a) provides that the Panel "shall" remand each transferred action at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings. Congress's choice of the word "shall" is not incidental. This Court has long recognized that mandatory statutory language of this kind imposes an obligation that admits no judicially created exception. *Escoe v. Zerbst*, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935). Nor may a court disregard that obligation because compliance seems inconvenient in a particular case. As this Court explained in *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917), "[i]t is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, ... the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." Nothing in Section 1407(a) qualifies the remand duty for cases in which a transferee judge concludes, under the separate discretionary standard of Section 1404(a), that retention would be convenient.

The statute's own words supply the most reliable evidence of Congress's design; where the text of a statute unambiguously commands a result, courts are not free to substitute a competing judgment about sound administration for the judgment Congress itself made. *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940). Respondent's countermotion asked the District of Arizona to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent one by invoking Section 1404(a)'s general convenience standard. But Congress did not leave the duration of a Panel transfer to the discretion of the transferee judge; it fixed that duration by statute, and courts have no license to nullify a mandatory command because they judge that departure serves efficiency.

## II. Because Congress specifically restricted multidistrict transfer to pretrial proceedings, the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a) cannot be used to override that specific limitation.

Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) serve different purposes and were drafted with different scope. Section 1404(a) is "a federal judicial housekeeping measure," generally applicable to ordinary civil litigation and addressed to the convenience of the parties in selecting among proper venues. *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 636 (1964). It operates only where the transferee forum is one in which the action "might have been brought" by the plaintiff in the first instance—a limitation this Court has strictly enforced even against arguments grounded in convenience. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343–44 (1960). Section 1407(a), by contrast, is a specialized mechanism enacted for the narrow purpose of coordinating pretrial proceedings among related actions pending in different districts, subject to the Panel's remand obligation. *Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960) (discussing the limited venue-transfer standard governing federal civil actions generally).

Where a specific statutory provision addresses a particular subject, it is not displaced by a general provision addressing the same general subject matter. As this Court has explained, "however inclusive may be the general language of a statute, it will not be held to apply to a matter specifically dealt with in another part of the same enactment." *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (quoting *Rodgers v. United States*, 185 U.S. 83, 87–89 (1902)). Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the temporal scope of a Panel-ordered transfer; that specific command cannot be displaced by resort to the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a), a statute of general applicability that says nothing about multidistrict litigation at all.

Moreover, Section 1404(a)'s general purpose has never been understood to authorize a court to alter the plaintiff's chosen forum for trial absent satisfaction of the statute's own criteria, developed for ordinary venue disputes between forums in which an action might independently have been brought. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 60–61 (1949). Lexecon selected the Northern District of Illinois as its forum. Nothing in Section 1404(a)'s convenience calculus permits a court sitting by virtue of the Panel's pretrial assignment to substitute its own trial-venue preference for the plaintiff's chosen forum, particularly when doing so directly contradicts the specific remand command of Section 1407(a).

## III. The Ninth Circuit erred in treating the conflict as unresolved.

The court below acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate" yet declined to enforce it, resting instead on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and non-binding circuit precedent. But an agency rule cannot rewrite the words Congress chose, and no court may relax a mandatory statutory command because compliance is inconvenient. Under *American Trucking*, 310 U.S. at 543–44, and *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485, the "sole function" of a reviewing court when statutory language is plain is to enforce it—not to weigh policy trade-offs Congress itself resolved. Because Section 1407(a) required remand of this action to the Northern District of Illinois at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings, and because Section 1404(a) supplies no authority to override that specific command, the judgment below should be reversed.
