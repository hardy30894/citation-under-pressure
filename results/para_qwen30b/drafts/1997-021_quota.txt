**ARGUMENT**

The National Labor Relations Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employer polling is irrational, inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act, and unsupported by substantial evidence. The Board’s rigid application of this standard—requiring objective indications sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition before an employer may poll—renders polling a legally meaningless exercise and undermines the Act’s purpose of facilitating stable collective bargaining. This Court should reverse the Board’s decision and uphold Allentown Mack’s right to conduct a poll under a less stringent, more practical standard grounded in precedent and statutory policy.

**I. THE BOARD’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD IS IRRATIONAL AND CONTRARY TO THE ACT’S PURPOSE**

The Board’s insistence that an employer may poll only when it already has “objective indications” raising a “reasonable doubt” about the union’s majority status is fundamentally irrational. It creates a paradox: an employer may not poll unless it already has sufficient evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition, yet the sole purpose of polling is to *obtain* such evidence. As the Board itself acknowledged in *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989), polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling,” and thus should be used sparingly. But if polling is only permitted when the employer already has the evidence it seeks, then polling becomes superfluous. The Board’s standard thus renders polling “marginally useful,” *id.*, and effectively eliminates its utility.

This irrationality is compounded by the Board’s own preference for Board-supervised elections (*RM* elections) over employer polls. See *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061. If the Board believes elections are the preferred method for testing union support, then the evidentiary standard for polling should be *higher*, not identical, to that for elections. Yet the Board applies the same standard to both. This inconsistency undermines the Board’s own rationale and violates the principle that administrative agencies must act rationally. *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983) (“An agency must examine the relevant data and articulate a satisfactory explanation for its action, including a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made.”).

The courts of appeals have correctly recognized this flaw. In *A.W. Thompson, Inc.*, 651 F.2d 1139, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981), the court held that an employer may poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. The court emphasized that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for employers in “sincere doubt” about union support. *Id.* at 1145. The same conclusion was reached in *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984), where the court held that polling is permissible “if the employer complies with the Struksnes conditions and procedural safeguards, and if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support.” *Id.* at 1299. The Sixth Circuit echoed this in *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982), stating that an employer may poll “if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient in itself to justify withdrawal.” *Id.* at 867.

These decisions reflect a sound understanding of the Act’s purpose: to allow employers to test union support without committing unfair labor practices. The Board’s contrary standard—requiring the employer to already have the evidence it seeks—defeats that purpose. It forces employers to choose between risking an unfair labor practice by bargaining with a minority union or forgoing polling altogether. The Act does not require such a Hobson’s choice.

**II. THE BOARD’S FACTUAL DETERMINATION THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE**

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked “objective reasonable doubt” is not supported by substantial evidence. The Board discounted statements from seven employees who expressed opposition to the union, relying on technicalities and speculative inferences. But the record contains overwhelming objective evidence of a loss of union support.

First, the Board erred in excluding the statements of Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack because they were not in the bargaining unit on January 25, 1991. The Board’s use of that date as a cutoff is arbitrary. The company’s decision to poll was made *after* January 25, and the employees’ statements were made *before* the sale. The relevant inquiry is whether the employer had a good-faith basis for doubt *at the time of the poll*, not whether the employees were still employed on a specific date. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37 (1987) (incumbent union enjoys rebuttable presumption of majority support, but presumption may be overcome by objective evidence).

Second, the Board improperly discounted the statements of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh. Ridgick told a manager that “as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union.” This is not “equivocal” but a clear expression of anti-union sentiment.
