**Argument**

The Texas Supreme Court erred in holding that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over this legal-malpractice suit merely because it may require application of patent law. The question under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) is not whether patent law appears somewhere in the background, but whether the state-law claim “really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect” of patent law, and whether exercising jurisdiction would upset the federal-state balance. *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988); *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).

This Court has long rejected an expansive view of “arising under” jurisdiction. A case does not arise under federal law simply because it “takes its origin in the laws of the United States.” *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Rather, federal jurisdiction exists only when the federal issue is necessary, actually disputed, and substantial. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314; *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 809.

Minton’s malpractice claim does not satisfy that test. His cause of action arises under Texas tort law. As the Texas Supreme Court recognized, he must prove duty, breach, and causation under state standards. See *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). The patent issue is embedded only because Minton must show that, but for his lawyers’ alleged error, he would have prevailed in the underlying patent case. That “case within a case” feature is common to legal-malpractice actions and does not transform every malpractice suit touching on federal law into an action “arising under” federal law. *Schaeffer v. O’Brien*, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied).

Most importantly, the patent question here is not substantial in the jurisdictional sense. In *Grable*, the Court found jurisdiction because the federal issue—whether the IRS had given adequate notice under federal tax law—was a pure question of federal law, actually disputed, and significant to the federal system as a whole. 545 U.S. at 315. By contrast, Minton’s patent issue concerns only the outcome of one private malpractice dispute. Any construction of the on-sale bar or experimental-use exception would affect only whether Minton can prove proximate cause in this case. That sort of fact-bound, case-specific issue is not the kind of federal question that warrants exclusive federal jurisdiction. See *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936).

The Supreme Court has also cautioned that federal jurisdiction cannot be expanded when doing so would disturb the division of responsibilities between state and federal courts. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. State courts routinely adjudicate malpractice claims, even when they require consideration of federal law as an element of proof. Nothing in § 1338(a) suggests Congress intended to displace that traditional state function whenever patent law is implicated. The patent laws create exclusive federal jurisdiction over suits “for patent infringement,” not over state tort claims that merely require patent-law analysis as evidence. 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a); *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808-09.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), should not control. That case treated patent-law malpractice claims as categorically federal if they required resolution of patent issues, but *Grable* requires a more searching inquiry into substantiality and federalism. 545 U.S. at 314. The better-reasoned decisions have rejected exclusive federal jurisdiction in this setting. See *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 334, 340 (5th Cir. 2008); *New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 270 Neb. 264, 702 N.W.2d 336 (2005); *E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 189 Cal. App. 4th 1140, 117 Cal. Rptr. 3d 516 (2010); *Roof Technical Servs., Inc. v. Hill*, 679 F. Supp. 2d 749 (N.D. Tex. 2010).

Indeed, this Court’s own cases confirm that the plaintiff’s well-pleaded complaint controls. *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 809. Minton’s complaint pleads legal malpractice under Texas law, not patent infringement or patent invalidity. The federal issue is merely a component of proving causation. That is not enough. See *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313-14; *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569.

This Court should hold that federal courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction over every case that involves patent law. Where, as here, patent law is only an embedded element of a state-law malpractice claim, jurisdiction remains with the state courts. The judgment of the Texas Supreme Court should be reversed.
