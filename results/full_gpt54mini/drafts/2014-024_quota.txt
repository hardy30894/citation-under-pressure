**Argument**

Section 875(c) does not criminalize speech unless the Government proves the defendant *subjectively* intended to make a threat. The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—asking only whether a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement as threatening—collides with this Court’s criminal-law presumption of scienter, with the Court’s “true threats” doctrine, and with the constitutional need to avoid sweeping in protected speech. See *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250–52 (1952); *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605–06 (1994); *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72–78 (1994).

A. **Section 875(c) requires mens rea beyond mere reasonable foreseeability.**  
Congress knows how to create strict-liability offenses, but this Court has repeatedly held that silence on mens rea does not ordinarily eliminate a culpability requirement. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250–63; *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 605–16. Section 875(c) contains no express mental-state term, but it criminalizes transmitting “any communication containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another,” 18 U.S.C. § 875(c), not simply transmitting words that a listener might construe as threatening. Where a statute is silent, courts presume Congress included a scienter requirement sufficient to separate wrongful from innocent conduct. *X-Citement Video*, 513 U.S. at 72–78; *Carter v. United States*, 530 U.S. 255, 269–70 (2000). The Third Circuit’s objective standard erases that distinction.

B. **The First Amendment “true threats” exception turns on intent, not just effect.**  
This Court has recognized that “true threats” are unprotected because they are serious expressions of an intention to commit unlawful violence, not because they merely cause alarm. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam); *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359–60 (2003). In *Black*, the Court explained that “‘true threats’ encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” 538 U.S. at 359. That formulation is incompatible with conviction based solely on how a reasonable recipient might react, because it focuses on what the speaker meant to communicate. See *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359–60; *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708.

The Third Circuit relied on its own prior decision in *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557–58 (3d Cir. 1991), but *Kosma* predates *Black* and cannot survive this Court’s later articulation of the doctrine. The better reading of *Black* is that the First Amendment permits punishment only where the defendant subjectively intended his statement as a threat. See *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359–60; *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708.

C. **An objective standard chills protected speech.**  
The First Amendment does not allow punishment of ambiguous, political, artistic, or emotional speech merely because a jury later deems it threatening in context. This Court has repeatedly insisted that standards governing speech must avoid overbreadth and vagueness. See *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 432–33 (1963); *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104, 108–09 (1972); *Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition*, 535 U.S. 234, 253–54 (2002). That concern is acute here, where Elonis’s posts included rap-style lyrics, hyperbole, and references to public controversies. A purely objective standard invites punishment of “political hyperbole” and other protected expression. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708.

D. **The Government’s contrary position is not compelled by precedent.**  
The Third Circuit treated fear in the recipient as the core of the offense, but this Court has never held that the “true threats” exception dispenses with mens rea. To the contrary, criminal statutes are construed to avoid “criminalizing otherwise innocent conduct,” *X-Citement Video*, 513 U.S. at 71–72, and to preserve the line between unlawful threats and protected speech. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359–60; *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708. Because § 875(c) lacks any clear statement authorizing strict liability, and because the First Amendment requires a narrow construction of the threats exception, the conviction cannot stand absent proof that Elonis intended his communications as threats. See *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250–52; *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 605–06; *Carter*, 530 U.S. at 269–70.

The judgment should be reversed.
