**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’s 35-foot buffer zone as a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time-place-manner regulation that leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), squarely controls and should not be overruled.

**I. The Act Is a Content-Neutral Time-Place-Manner Regulation**

The Act restricts only the location of speech—not its content or viewpoint. It applies equally to all persons, regardless of message. *See Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (regulation is content-neutral if it “serves purposes unrelated to the content of expression”). The government’s interests—ensuring public safety, protecting access to healthcare, and preventing obstruction—are “justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech.” *Clark v. Cmty. for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984). Petitioners’ claim of viewpoint discrimination fails because the Act does not target any particular message; it applies to all speech within the buffer zone. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 723–24.

**II. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored to Serve Significant Government Interests**

Massachusetts has a compelling interest in “ensuring the public safety and order, promoting the free flow of traffic on streets and sidewalks, [and] protecting property rights.” *Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 768 (1994). The buffer zone is narrowly tailored because it is “not substantially broader than necessary to achieve the government’s interest.” *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 800. The 35-foot zone is far smaller than the 100-foot zone upheld in *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 707, and it exempts clinic employees, law enforcement, and persons merely passing through. The Act does not burden substantially more speech than necessary; it targets only the area immediately adjacent to clinic entrances where congestion and confrontation are most acute. *See Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of W. N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 380 (1997) (upholding fixed buffer zones around clinic entrances).

**III. The Act Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels**

Petitioners retain numerous means to communicate: they may speak, pray, display signs, distribute literature, and engage in sidewalk counseling from outside the 35-foot zone. They may also use amplified sound, as the record shows. The First Circuit correctly found that “oral speech of varying degrees of volume and amplification, distribution of literature, displays of signage and symbols, wearing of evocative garments and costumes, and prayer alone and in groups” remain available. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 759 F. Supp. 2d 133, 145 (D. Mass. 2010). The Constitution does not guarantee “the most effective” means of communication, only “adequate” ones. *Clark*, 468 U.S. at 295. Petitioners’ inability to engage in one-on-one conversations at close range does not render the regulation unconstitutional. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 726–27.

**IV. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls and Should Not Be Overruled**

*Hill* upheld a Colorado statute that created a 100-foot buffer zone around healthcare facilities, prohibiting “knowingly approach[ing]” within eight feet of another person to engage in “oral protest, education, or counseling.” 530 U.S. at 707. The Court held the law was content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and left open ample alternative channels. *Id.* at 725–30. The Massachusetts Act is even more modest: it creates a fixed 35-foot zone with no “approach” restriction, allowing speech from any distance outside the zone. *Hill* is directly on point and remains good law. Petitioners’ invitation to overrule *Hill* is meritless. *Hill* did not create a “special” exception for abortion clinics; it applied ordinary time-place-manner analysis. *See id.* at 714–15. Subsequent decisions like *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), involved content-based restrictions on commercial data mining, not content-neutral buffer zones. *Sorrell* does not undermine *Hill*.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
