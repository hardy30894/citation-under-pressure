**ARGUMENT**

Punitive damages under Title VII require conduct that is egregious or outrageous, independent of the employer's state of mind. This standard derives from the common-law rule that exemplary awards punish only aggravated wrongdoing beyond the mental element of the underlying tort.

In Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1851), the Court held that punitive damages are proper only where the defendant's conduct has involved aggravating circumstances. Denver & Rio Grande Ry. v. Harris, 122 U.S. 597 (1887), held that the conduct must be of an aggravated character before exemplary damages may be imposed. Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), stated that punitive damages are awarded only when the defendant has been guilty of some fraud, oppression, or other like misconduct.

Tracy v. Swartwout, 35 U.S. (10 Pet.) 80 (1836), permitted such awards only upon proof of oppressive conduct. Similar principles appear in The Steamboat New World v. King, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 469 (1853), requiring gross negligence. In Railroad Co. v. Pratt, 89 U.S. (22 Wall.) 123 (1874), the Court required misconduct of an aggravated character. Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), likewise required willful misconduct or gross negligence indicating conscious indifference. Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1873), confirms that exemplary relief addresses only aggravated wrongs.

These authorities demonstrate that, at common law, a showing of malice or reckless indifference was never enough; the plaintiff had to prove additional aggravating facts. Because the 1991 amendments to Title VII incorporated this common-law framework without textual alteration, the same requirement applies here. The district court therefore correctly refused a punitive-damages instruction when the evidence showed no conduct that was egregious or outrageous independent of any asserted state of mind.
