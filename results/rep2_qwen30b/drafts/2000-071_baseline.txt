**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for private religious groups seeking to conduct religious instruction and worship, even when the school maintains a limited public forum for secular youth organizations. The exclusion of the Good News Club from Milford Central School’s after-hours use of facilities did not violate the Club’s free speech rights under the First and Fourteenth Amendments. Nor did the school’s policy constitute impermissible viewpoint discrimination. Rather, the school’s decision was a reasonable, content-neutral application of a policy designed to prevent the advancement of religion in violation of the Establishment Clause.

**I. The School’s Policy Is a Content-Neutral, Limited Public Forum, and the Club’s Activities Fall Outside Its Scope**

Milford Central School’s Community Use Policy creates a limited public forum—open to district residents for nonexclusive, nonreligious civic, social, and recreational uses. *See Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972) (a limited public forum is “a forum that the government opens for use by the public for expressive activity”). The policy explicitly prohibits use for “religious purposes,” and applicants must certify that their proposed use complies with this restriction. This is not a ban on religious speech per se, but a content-based exclusion of religious activity from a forum otherwise open to secular expression.

The Good News Club’s activities—prayer, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and invitations to accept Jesus as Savior—are not merely “moral and character development” from a religious viewpoint; they are quintessentially religious exercises. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) (“There is no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’—all apparently forms of ‘speech,’ despite their religious subject matter—and become unprotected ‘worship.’”) The Club’s own lesson plans, including the “Challenge and Invitation” segment, explicitly distinguish between “saved” and “unsaved” children and invite the latter to accept Christ as their personal Savior. This is not moral instruction; it is religious conversion. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (“The fact that a religious group seeks to use a public forum to teach religious doctrine does not render the forum a public forum for religious speech.”).

The school’s policy does not discriminate based on viewpoint. It treats all religious uses the same: they are excluded. The school permits secular youth groups—Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, 4-H—to meet after school, all of which focus on character development, leadership, and civic engagement. But these groups do not engage in religious worship, prayer, or proselytization. The distinction is not between religious and secular viewpoints on morality, but between religious instruction and secular moral education. *See Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 508 (2d Cir. 2000) (“The subject matter of the Club’s activities is ‘quintessentially religious’... and falls outside the bounds of pure ‘moral and character development.’”).

**II. The School’s Exclusion Is Justified by a Compelling Government Interest in Avoiding Establishment Clause Violations**

Even if the school’s forum were deemed a limited public forum, the exclusion of religious speech is justified by a compelling government interest: the avoidance of Establishment Clause violations. *See Board of Educ. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 250 (1990) (“The Establishment Clause does not permit a public school to open its forum to religious groups while excluding others.”). But the converse is also true: a school may lawfully exclude religious groups from a limited public forum to avoid the appearance of endorsing religion.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that public schools may not allow religious worship or instruction on school premises. *See Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962) (“The First Amendment mandates governmental neutrality in matters of religion.”); *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612–13 (1971) (establishing the three-pronged test for Establishment Clause compliance). Permitting the Good News Club to meet in the school building would create the appearance that the school endorses or sponsors religious instruction. The Club’s meetings are not merely “religious in content” but are structured as religious services: they begin and end with prayer, include Bible reading, and culminate in a personal invitation to salvation. This is not a neutral forum for moral discussion; it is a religious worship service.

The school’s policy is not a blanket ban on religious speech. It is a content-neutral rule that applies equally to all religious uses. The Club is free to meet at the Milford Center Community Church, where it has done so for years. The school’s decision to deny access is not an abridgment of speech, but a preservation of constitutional boundaries. *See Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 833 (1995) (“The government may not deny a benefit to a person on the basis of his views.”) But the government may “refuse to fund religious speech even when it is otherwise protected by the First Amendment.” *Id.* at 845.

**III. The Club’s Claim of Viewpoint Discrimination Fails**

The Club argues that it is being denied access because of its religious viewpoint. But the school’s policy does not target religious viewpoints; it excludes all religious uses. The school allows secular groups to discuss morality, character, and values—subjects that are “secular” in nature. The Club’s claim that its religious perspective on morality is entitled to equal access ignores the fact that the school is not creating a forum for moral discourse *per se*, but for secular youth activities. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 508 (“The record includes nothing to indicate that any of these clubs’ activities remotely approach the type of religious instruction and prayer provided by the Club.”).
