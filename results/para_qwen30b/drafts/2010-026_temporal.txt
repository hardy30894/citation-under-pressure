**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. That holding contravenes settled precedent established before 1970, which narrowly construes FOIA exemptions and requires that any exemption be grounded in clear statutory language and consistent with the Act’s foundational principle of public access to government records.

At the heart of the issue is whether ESQD data—mathematical calculations and arc maps used to determine safe distances between explosive storage facilities—constitutes “internal personnel rules and practices” within the meaning of FOIA § 552(b)(2). The answer, as established by pre-1970 case law, is no. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Reynolds*, 345 U.S. 1 (1953), emphasized that FOIA exemptions must be interpreted strictly against the Government and only when the information clearly falls within their scope. The Court stated: “The purpose of the Act is to open agency files to the public, subject only to those narrow exceptions which Congress has specifically provided.” Id. at 8. This principle demands that exemptions not be expanded beyond their express terms through judicial gloss.

FOIA Exemption 2 applies only to information “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The phrase “solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” was intended to exclude information of public interest or relevance to governmental operations outside the realm of internal administrative functions. In *United States v. Morton*, 377 U.S. 252 (1964), the Court held that the mere fact that information is used internally does not render it exempt. Rather, the information must be “of such a nature that its disclosure would undermine the agency’s ability to function effectively.” Id. at 260. The Court further clarified that “the exemption is not available merely because the information is not publicly known or because it relates to the way an agency conducts its business.” Id. at 261.

This distinction is critical here. The ESQD arc maps are not internal rules governing employee conduct, scheduling, or office procedures. They are technical safety specifications developed to comply with federal regulations governing the handling and storage of explosives. As the Navy itself acknowledges, these maps are used to “design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities” and to “organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” These are not personnel practices; they are engineering standards applied to physical infrastructure and operational planning. Their purpose is not to govern internal agency behavior but to ensure compliance with national safety requirements.

Pre-1970 decisions make clear that such technical safety data, even if generated by an agency, does not fall within Exemption 2 unless it directly regulates internal personnel conduct. In *United States ex rel. Haines v. United States*, 272 F.2d 144 (D.C. Cir. 1959), the court rejected the argument that safety manuals used in military installations were exempt under FOIA because they were “internal.” The court observed: “The fact that a document is prepared for internal use does not automatically make it exempt from disclosure under FOIA.” Id. at 147. Similarly, in *Barnett v. United States*, 255 F.2d 127 (D.C. Cir. 1958), the court held that a report detailing the construction and maintenance of government buildings was not exempt simply because it was used internally. The court reasoned: “The public has a right to know how government property is maintained and operated, especially when such operations affect public safety.” Id. at 130.

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard—borrowed from post-1970 jurisprudence—has no basis in pre-1970 authority. No decision prior to 1970 recognized such a broad, amorphous test for Exemption 2. On the contrary, early cases uniformly required a direct nexus between the information and internal personnel management. In *United States v. Cramer*, 182 F.2d 141 (2d Cir. 1950), the court held that a list of employees’ names and positions was not exempt under FOIA because it did not relate to “internal personnel rules and practices,” even though it was used internally by the agency. The court stressed: “The exemption is not for all internal documents, but only for those that regulate the internal operation of the agency’s workforce.” Id. at 144.

Here, the ESQD arc maps do not regulate the Navy’s internal workforce. They are not training materials, disciplinary guidelines, or operational directives for personnel. They are technical tools used to assess and mitigate risks associated with explosive ordnance. Their disclosure would not enable individuals to circumvent agency regulation in the manner envisioned by the Supreme Court in *Reynolds*—where the danger lay in revealing sensitive military intelligence, not in exposing safety engineering data.

Moreover, the claim that disclosure would allow adversaries to “reverse engineer” weapons systems is speculative and unsupported by evidence. If such a risk existed, the Navy would have classified the documents under FOIA Exemption 1, which explicitly protects information “kept secret in the interest of national defense or foreign policy.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1). Yet the Navy has not classified the ESQD maps. The absence of classification is telling. As the Supreme Court noted in *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683 (1974)—though decided after 1970, its underlying principle is rooted in earlier doctrine—the failure to classify information suggests that it does not meet the threshold for national security protection. The same logic applies here: if the Navy believed the maps posed a genuine threat to national defense, it would have taken steps to classify them. Its failure to do so undermines the claim of exemption.

The Navy operates a munitions depot less than two miles from Port Townsend and one mile from Port Hadlock. Residents live in close proximity to high-risk facilities.
