**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Held That Ford’s Extensive In-State Activities Give Rise to Specific Jurisdiction Because the Claims “Arise Out of or Relate to” Those Contacts.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017). This standard is satisfied when, as here, the defendant has “purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State,” *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958), and the plaintiff’s suit is “related enough” to those activities, *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court*, 592 U.S. 351, 362 (2021).

Ford does not dispute that it extensively markets, sells, and services vehicles in Montana. It maintains 36 authorized dealerships, advertises nationally and locally, and sells replacement parts directly to Montana consumers. These contacts are not random or attenuated; they represent a deliberate effort to serve the Montana market. *See World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (jurisdiction proper where defendant “delivers its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State”).

The claims in this case—design defect, failure to warn, and negligence—directly “relate to” Ford’s Montana activities. The vehicle at issue was a Ford Explorer, a model Ford designed, manufactured, and marketed for nationwide sale, including in Montana. Ford’s Montana advertising and sales network created a market for its vehicles, and the defective tire was part of a product line Ford sold and serviced in the state. The connection is not merely “but-for” causation; it is a substantial link between Ford’s in-state conduct and the harm suffered. *See Ford*, 592 U.S. at 362 (specific jurisdiction exists when “the defendant’s suit-related conduct . . . create[s] a substantial connection with the forum State”).

**II. Ford’s Argument That Jurisdiction Requires a Causal Connection Between the Defendant’s Contacts and the Plaintiff’s Claims Is Contrary to This Court’s Precedent.**

Ford contends that specific jurisdiction requires a showing that the plaintiff’s claims “arose from” the defendant’s in-state contacts, demanding a direct causal nexus. But this Court has repeatedly rejected such a rigid formulation. In *Ford*, the Court held that the phrase “arise out of or relate to” encompasses both causal and relational connections. *Id.* at 362. The Court explained that “relate to” is broader than “arise out of,” and that jurisdiction is proper when “there is a strong relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation.” *Id.* (quoting *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984)).

Ford’s reliance on *Bristol-Myers Squibb* is misplaced. There, the Court held that specific jurisdiction could not be based on the defendant’s general contacts with the forum when the plaintiffs’ claims had no connection to those contacts. *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, 582 U.S. at 265. Here, by contrast, the claims are directly connected to Ford’s Montana activities: Ford sold the same model of vehicle in Montana, advertised its safety features to Montana consumers, and provided replacement parts and service in the state. The defective tire was part of a product line Ford actively marketed and maintained in Montana. This is not a case where the defendant’s only contact with the forum is unrelated to the suit. *Cf. Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011) (general jurisdiction requires “continuous and systematic” contacts).

**III. The Montana Supreme Court’s Decision Comports with the Fairness and Reasonableness Requirements of Due Process.**

Even if Ford’s contacts with Montana were insufficient to establish specific jurisdiction, the exercise of jurisdiction is nonetheless reasonable and fair. *See Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 476 (1985) (jurisdiction must comport with “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice”). Ford is a global corporation with the resources to defend itself in Montana. The state has a strong interest in providing a forum for its residents injured by defective products. *See Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 776 (1984) (forum state has “a significant interest in redressing injuries that occur within the State”). And Montana’s courts are the most convenient forum for the plaintiff, who resides there and suffered her injury there. *See McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957) (jurisdiction proper where plaintiff’s “interest in having a convenient forum” is strong).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
