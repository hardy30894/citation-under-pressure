**ARGUMENT**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA does not qualify as an exempt “retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), and therefore is not shielded from creditors in bankruptcy. The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for retirement funds is not a relic of the original owner’s intent or a mere label attached to an account; it is a functional protection for funds *currently* dedicated to the debtor’s future retirement. The Court of Appeals’ interpretation—rooted in statutory text, structure, and policy—ensures that the exemption serves its intended purpose: preserving assets for post-employment financial security, not enabling the use of inherited funds for current consumption.

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The key inquiry is not whether the funds *once* served as retirement savings, but whether they *currently* function as such in the hands of the debtor. As the Seventh Circuit emphasized, “money counts as ‘retirement funds’ only when held for the owner’s retirement.” *Clark v. Rameker*, 705 F.3d 725, 730 (7th Cir. 2013). The inherited IRA, by its very design, fails this test.

Unlike a traditional IRA, which is intended to accumulate wealth for the account holder’s own retirement, an inherited IRA is a time-limited vehicle for *distribution*, not *preservation*. Under 26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A), the beneficiary must begin withdrawals within one year of the original owner’s death, and the account must be fully distributed within five years (or over the life expectancy of the beneficiary, if permitted). These mandatory distributions are not optional; they are triggered by the death of the original owner, not the beneficiary’s retirement. The funds are not held for the beneficiary’s future use but are instead *required to be spent*—and taxed—during the beneficiary’s working years. As the Seventh Circuit observed, “distribution precedes the owner’s retirement.” Id. at 731. This is not retirement savings; it is a mechanism for *accelerated taxation*.

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary holding in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and the Eighth Circuit’s *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), rest on a flawed premise: that the term “retirement funds” is a fungible label that survives transfer. But the Bankruptcy Code does not exempt “funds that were once retirement funds.” It exempts “retirement funds” *in the hands of the debtor*. The absence of the word “debtor’s” in §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) does not mean the exemption is untethered to the debtor’s current use. As the Seventh Circuit correctly noted, the Code’s other exemptions *do* use temporal and personal qualifiers—e.g., “the debtor’s interest,” “the debtor’s domicile”—and the absence of such language in the retirement fund provision reflects a *different* legislative intent: to allow *ongoing* contributions to a retirement account during bankruptcy, not to extend the exemption to assets that no longer serve a retirement function.

The Clarks’ argument that the exemption should apply because the funds were once retirement savings is foreclosed by *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987), which holds that “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” To interpret the exemption as applying to any account that once held retirement funds would eviscerate the distinction between savings for retirement and assets available for current use. It would allow a debtor to inherit a $300,000 IRA, withdraw the entire balance within a year, and still claim the funds as exempt—despite the fact that the money is no longer “retirement funds” and is being used for immediate consumption. That result is inconsistent with the Code’s purpose: to protect funds *intended* for future retirement, not to shield assets that have already lost their retirement character.

Moreover, the Seventh Circuit’s analogy to the homestead exemption is instructive. Under §522(b)(3)(A), a debtor may exempt a “domicile” only if it was the debtor’s home for at least 730 days before filing. A debtor cannot claim a house as exempt simply because it was once the home of a parent. *Clark*, 705 F.3d at 731. The same logic applies here: an inherited IRA is not exempt merely because it once served as a retirement account for the decedent. The exemption depends on the *current* use and function of the asset.

The Clarks’ policy argument—that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage saving and bequests—is not a valid basis for statutory interpretation. As *Rodriguez* reminds us, “deciding what competing values will or will not be sacrificed to the achievement of a particular objective is the very essence of legislative choice.” 480 U.S. at 525. Congress chose to protect *current* retirement savings, not *past* savings or *future* bequests. To extend the exemption to inherited IRAs would reward the very behavior the Code seeks to discourage: using retirement accounts as vehicles for wealth transfer without regard to the beneficiary’s actual need for retirement security.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with the text, structure, and purpose of the Bankruptcy Code. It preserves the integrity of the exemption by ensuring that only funds *currently* serving a retirement function are protected. The judgment should be affirmed.
