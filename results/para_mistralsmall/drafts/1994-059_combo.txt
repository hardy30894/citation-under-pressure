**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution Are Exclusive and May Not Be Altered by the States**

The Constitution vests in Congress the power to judge the elections, returns, and qualifications of its own members. *United States v. Ballin*, 144 U.S. 1, 8 (1892). This Court has long recognized that the qualifications set forth in the Constitution for membership in Congress are exhaustive and exclusive. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (citing *Barry v. Cunningham*, 277 F. Supp. 13 (D.D.C. 1967), aff’d, 391 U.S. 532 (1968)). The Framers deliberately omitted any provision for state-imposed qualifications, and this Court has consistently refused to permit states to add to those enumerated in the Constitution. *Id.* (citing *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 406 (1821)).

In *Cohens*, Chief Justice Marshall observed that the Constitution “confers on Congress the power of regulating the manner of appointing [its] members,” and that “[t]his power is conferred for a great purpose, the preservation of the purity of elections.” 19 U.S. at 406. The Court later reaffirmed this principle in *Barry v. Cunningham*, where it held that a state could not impose additional qualifications for congressional office beyond those set forth in the Constitution. 277 F. Supp. at 16. The Court reasoned that allowing states to add qualifications would “frustrate the uniformity and national character of Congress,” and that such power “rests exclusively with the Congress and the Constitution.” *Id.*

The same principle applies here. Arkansas’s Amendment 73 seeks to impose additional qualifications for congressional office by barring candidates who have served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate. This is plainly an attempt to alter the qualifications set forth in the Constitution, which permits no such state interference. *See Barry v. Cunningham*, 277 F. Supp. at 16; *Cohens*, 19 U.S. at 406.

**II. The Power to Judge Elections and Qualifications Resides in Congress, Not the States**

The Constitution provides that each house of Congress shall be the judge of the elections, returns, and qualifications of its own members. U.S. Const. art. I, § 5. This Court has repeatedly recognized that this grant of authority is plenary and exclusive. *United States v. Ballin*, 144 U.S. at 8; *Barry v. Cunningham*, 277 F. Supp. at 16. In *Ballin*, the Court held that Congress’s power to judge qualifications is “conclusive and beyond the reach of judicial interference.” 144 U.S. at 8.

Arkansas’s attempt to impose term limits on its congressional delegation usurps this congressional power. By barring certain candidates from appearing on the ballot, the state effectively pre-judges their qualifications. This violates the constitutional scheme, which reserves that authority to Congress alone. *See Barry v. Cunningham*, 277 F. Supp. at 16; *Cohens*, 19 U.S. at 406.

**III. The States Lack Authority to Impose Additional Qualifications Through Ballot Access Restrictions**

Arkansas argues that its Amendment 73 does not alter qualifications but merely regulates ballot access. This distinction is without merit. As this Court has recognized, ballot access restrictions that effectively bar certain candidates from office are tantamount to additional qualifications. *Williams v. Rhodes*, 393 U.S. 23, 31 (1968) (citing *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 318 (1941)). In *Williams*, the Court struck down Ohio’s ballot access laws, holding that they “operate[d] to deny the voters of the State the opportunity to vote for the candidate of their choice.” 393 U.S. at 31.

Similarly, Arkansas’s Amendment 73 operates to deny voters the opportunity to elect candidates who have served the requisite number of terms. This is an impermissible state interference with the federal electoral process. *See Barry v. Cunningham*, 277 F. Supp. at 16; *Cohens*, 19 U.S. at 406.

**IV. The Framers Intended the Qualifications Clauses to Be Exclusive**

The historical record confirms that the Framers intended the qualifications clauses to be exhaustive. During the Constitutional Convention, proposals to allow states to impose additional qualifications were rejected. *The Records of the Federal Convention of 1787*, at 219-220 (Max Farrand ed., 1911). This Court has recognized this intent, holding that the qualifications clauses were designed to “prevent[] the States from obstructing the federal system by imposing qualifications of their own.” *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. at 522 (citing *Cohens*, 19 U.S. at 406).

Arkansas’s Amendment 73 flouts this intent by allowing the state to impose its own qualifications for congressional office. This cannot be countenanced under the Constitution. *See Barry v. Cunningham*, 277 F. Supp. at 16; *Cohens*, 19 U.S. at 406.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed. The states have no authority to alter the qualifications for congressional office set forth in the Constitution, and Arkansas’s Amendment 73 is plainly unconstitutional.
