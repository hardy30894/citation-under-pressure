**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Search Warrant Failed to Establish a Nexus Between the Crime Alleged and the Place to Be Searched, Violating the Fourth Amendment.**

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant “particularly describ[e] the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized.” U.S. Const. amend. IV. A warrant must demonstrate a “‘nexus’ between the item to be seized and criminal behavior.” *United States v. Fucci*, 805 F.3d 73, 78 (1st Cir. 2015) (quoting *Maryland v. Garrison*, 480 U.S. 79, 84 (1987)). Here, the affidavit did not establish a sufficient nexus between Joubert’s alleged crimes and his parents’ home.

The affidavit recited general assertions that offenders “often maintain possession” of evidence but did not link that evidence to Joubert’s parents’ residence. *See id.* at 79 (“Bare assertions of likely evidence are insufficient.”). The fact that Joubert occasionally stayed at the home or moved there during the investigation does not suffice. *Cf. United States v. Lora-Cornejo*, 54 F.4th 10, 22 (1st Cir. 2022) (finding nexus where defendant stored contraband in his bedroom). The warrant thus violated the particularity requirement. *Fucci*, 805 F.3d at 78.

**II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Admitting Uncharged Child Molestation Evidence Under Rule 404(b).**

Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) permits evidence of other crimes only if it is “(1) relevant to a material issue; (2) similar to the charged offense; (3) committed relatively close in time to the charged offense; and (4) supported by clear and convincing evidence.” *United States v. De La Cruz–Benitez*, 923 F.3d 17, 25 (1st Cir. 2019). The district court erred in admitting evidence of uncharged molestation.

The government offered evidence of Joubert’s alleged abuse of SJ, who was not a victim in the indictment. The incidents occurred decades earlier and lacked the coercive context of the charged conduct. *See id.* at 26 (finding abuse where prior acts were “strikingly similar” in method and proximity). The evidence was unduly prejudicial under Rule 403. *Id.* at 27. The admission was an abuse of discretion.

**III. The Federal Statutes Under Which Joubert Was Convicted Exceed Congress’s Commerce Clause Authority.**

The Commerce Clause empowers Congress to regulate only (1) channels of interstate commerce, (2) instrumentalities of interstate commerce, or (3) activities substantially affecting interstate commerce. *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 609 (2000). Joubert’s conduct—local possession and exploitation—does not satisfy the third prong.

The government relied on *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995), which upheld the Gun-Free School Zones Act, but that case involved a jurisdictional element. *Id.* at 561. Here, § 2251(a) and § 2252A(a)(5)(B) lack such a nexus. *See Morrison*, 529 U.S. at 613 (invalidating Violence Against Women Act for lacking substantial effect on commerce). The statutes exceed Congress’s authority as applied to Joubert.

**IV. Joubert’s 480-Month Sentence Was Substantively Unreasonable.**

A sentence is substantively unreasonable if it “cannot be located within the range dictated by the [§ 3553(a)] factors.” *United States v. Martin*, 520 F.3d 87, 92 (1st Cir. 2008). Joubert’s sentence was 20 years above the Guidelines range, despite his lack of a criminal record and the absence of physical force in the offense. *See id.* at 93 (finding substantively unreasonable a sentence “greater than necessary” to reflect § 3553(a) factors). The district court gave inadequate weight to these factors. The sentence is unreasonable.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Joubert’s conviction and sentence should be reversed.
