I. The District Court Plainly Erred in Admitting Agent Jones’s Expert Testimony That Stanley’s Statements Were Deceptive and Indicative of Guilt.

Because Stanley did not object at trial, this Court reviews for plain error. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). Reversal is required if there is (1) error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affects substantial rights, and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Id. at 732-36.

Expert testimony is admissible only if it “will help the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue.” Fed. R. Evid. 702(a). An expert may not “usurp the jury’s function” by offering opinions on credibility. United States v. Dazey, 403 F.3d 1147, 1171 (10th Cir. 2005). “The jury is the lie detector in the courtroom.” United States v. Toledo, 985 F.2d 1462, 1470 (10th Cir. 1993). Consequently, “expert testimony which does nothing more than vouch for the credibility of another witness” is inadmissible. United States v. Charley, 189 F.3d 1251, 1267 (10th Cir. 1999).

Agent Jones’s testimony crossed this line. He stated that Stanley’s answers “[did] not make sense,” displayed “evasive behaviors common among the criminal element,” and that “Never in my career have I seen that with an innocent person.” He further opined that invocations of faith were “deceptive statements.” These assertions directly told the jury that Stanley was lying and guilty—precisely the credibility determinations reserved for the jury. See United States v. Samara, 643 F.2d 701, 705 (10th Cir. 1981) (expert may not “tell the jury what result to reach”).

The error is plain under Charley and Toledo. It affected Stanley’s substantial rights because the testimony struck at the heart of his defense—that his post-arrest statements were truthful—and the government’s case turned on discrediting those statements. Finally, allowing an FBI agent to testify that a defendant’s words and demeanor prove guilt “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Olano, 507 U.S. at 736. The conviction must be reversed.
