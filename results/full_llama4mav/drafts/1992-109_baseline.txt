The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. Moran v. Godinez, 972 F.2d 263 (9th Cir. 1992). The circuit court properly distinguished between the standard for competency to stand trial and the standard for competency to waive constitutional rights, holding that the latter requires a "reasoned choice" among available alternatives. Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); see also Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211, 214-15 (9th Cir. 1973).

The trial court's failure to apply this heightened standard when accepting Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty pleas violated his due process rights. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a defendant's competency to stand trial is not the same as their competency to waive constitutional rights. See Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171-72 (1975); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966); Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). While a defendant need only have a "rational and factual understanding of the proceedings" to be competent to stand trial, Dusky, 362 U.S. at 402, a higher level of mental functioning is required to waive constitutional rights, see Evans v. Raines, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1983).

The state court's post-conviction ruling, which relied on psychiatric evaluations conducted to determine Moran's competency to stand trial, did not cure this error. Those evaluations did not assess Moran's ability to make a "reasoned choice" among available alternatives, and therefore did not provide a sufficient basis for determining his competency to waive counsel and plead guilty. See Moran, 972 F.2d at 270-71. The trial court's failure to hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive constitutional rights, combined with the state court's reliance on the wrong legal standard in its post-conviction ruling, requires that Moran's convictions be vacated.

The Ninth Circuit's decision should be affirmed, as it correctly applied the relevant case law to the facts of Moran's case. The Due Process Clause requires that a trial court apply a heightened standard to determine a defendant's competency to waive constitutional rights, and the state court's failure to do so in this case resulted in a violation of Moran's constitutional rights.
