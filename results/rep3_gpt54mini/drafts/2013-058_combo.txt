**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt inherited IRAs from the bankruptcy estate. The exemption provisions protect “retirement funds,” 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12), and inherited IRAs are not retirement funds in the debtor’s hands. They cannot receive new contributions, cannot be rolled over, and must begin mandatory distributions shortly after the original owner’s death. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(11)(A); 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B). As the Seventh Circuit correctly explained, an inherited IRA is “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” In re Clark, 714 F.3d 559, 563 (7th Cir. 2013). Congress exempted retirement savings, not assets that can be used for current consumption. See White v. Stump, 266 U.S. 310, 313 (1924); Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622, 628 (1943).

Text and structure confirm that result. Section 522 protects “retirement funds,” not merely money that once was in a retirement account. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). Statutory words must be read in their context, not in isolation. United States v. Goldenberg, 168 U.S. 95, 102-03 (1897); Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). If Congress had intended to exempt any account with a retirement-related origin, it could have said so. Courts may not expand a bankruptcy exemption beyond the statutory language. United States v. Wells, 291 U.S. 569, 572 (1934); Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 425 (1944).

The ordinary meaning of “retirement funds” also points to funds set aside for the owner’s retirement, not inherited assets that must be distributed before the owner retires. Words are given their common meaning unless the statute indicates otherwise. Maillard v. Lawrence, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 251, 253 (1853); Nix v. Hedden, 149 U.S. 304, 306-07 (1893). An inherited IRA fails that description. It is not a savings pool the debtor may preserve until retirement; it is a forced-withdrawal account. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 562-64.

Bankruptcy exemptions should be construed in light of their function. Congress enacted § 522 to preserve a debtor’s means of future support, not to shelter every asset once associated with a protected category. See Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915); Davis v. Aetna Acceptance Co., 293 U.S. 328, 333 (1934). That logic fits traditional retirement funds, which remain dedicated to retirement. It does not fit inherited IRAs, because the debtor may be required to spend them long before retirement. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 563-64.

The Court should reject the contrary view that inherited IRAs qualify simply because the funds once belonged to someone’s retirement account. Exemption rights are determined as of the bankruptcy filing and according to the debtor’s interest at that time. White v. Stump, 266 U.S. at 313; Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. at 628. Property that is freely available for present consumption is not transformed into exempt retirement property by lineage alone. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 565-66. Nor does the Code’s omission of the phrase “debtor’s retirement funds” broaden the exemption beyond funds actually devoted to retirement; statutory silence cannot be read to nullify the limiting term Congress chose. Caminetti, 242 U.S. at 485; Wells, 291 U.S. at 572.

Finally, the equities do not justify rewriting the statute. Courts enforce exemptions as written, not as they might wish Congress had drafted them. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 425; Davis, 293 U.S. at 333. The Bankruptcy Code protects retirement savings, but inherited IRAs are not retirement savings in the debtor’s hands. The judgment below should be affirmed.
