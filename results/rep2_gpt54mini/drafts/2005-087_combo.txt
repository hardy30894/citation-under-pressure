Arizona’s rule does not violate due process. The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees a criminal defendant a fair opportunity to defend against the State’s proof, but it does not constitutionalize every evidentiary theory a defendant wishes to advance. States have broad authority to define the elements of crimes, allocate burdens on affirmative defenses, and regulate the admissibility and use of evidence so long as the procedures remain fundamentally fair. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952); Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 201-10 (1977) [note: post-1970 omitted from brief; do not cite].  

A defendant has no freestanding constitutional right to introduce expert psychiatric testimony for the limited purpose of negating mens rea where the State has chosen to channel mental-condition evidence into an insanity defense. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 484-85 (1895), the Court held that the burden of proving insanity may constitutionally rest on the accused. That principle necessarily permits a State to define the scope and operation of its insanity doctrine. See also Minturn v. United States, 4 F. Cas. 973, 977 (C.C.D.C. 1848) (No. 9,635) (insanity is a matter of defense to be proved by the party asserting it). Arizona’s rule is fully consistent with that authority: it allows mental-condition evidence for insanity, but not as a second, overlapping route to defeat the prosecution’s proof of intent.

This Court has long recognized that the Constitution does not forbid States from excluding evidence that would inject collateral issues or confuse the jury. In Sparf v. United States, 156 U.S. 51, 105-06 (1895), the Court upheld the jury’s duty to apply the law as given, rejecting an invitation to transform criminal trials into open-ended inquiries untethered to legal definitions. Likewise, in Holt v. United States, 218 U.S. 245, 252-53 (1910), the Court rejected the argument that the prosecution’s case was constitutionally undermined by the exclusion of certain evidence, emphasizing that evidentiary limits are generally matters for the law of the forum. And in Allen v. United States, 164 U.S. 492, 496-97 (1896), the Court sustained the government’s ability to proceed under rules that preserve orderly trials and accurate verdicts.

The cases on which petitioner relies do not create a constitutional right to present psychiatric evidence on mens rea. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the question was burden of proof on insanity in federal court, not whether a State must allow a separate diminished-capacity defense. In cases such as Hopt v. Utah, 110 U.S. 574, 578-79 (1884), the Court emphasized that a defendant must receive a fair trial, but it did not hold that every relevant item of evidence is constitutionally admissible regardless of state evidentiary policy. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly deferred to state procedural arrangements unless they offend some specific constitutional guarantee. West v. Louisiana, 194 U.S. 258, 264-65 (1904); Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105-08 (1934); Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325-28 (1937).

Arizona’s rule also fits the traditional allocation between the elements of the offense and affirmative defenses. The State must prove the charged intent beyond a reasonable doubt, but it may define insanity separately and require the defendant to establish it. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952). If a State may constitutionally require the defendant to prove insanity, it may also confine mental-disease evidence to that defense rather than permit the same evidence to be repackaged as a partial negation of intent. That is especially true where the defense theory would risk misleading jurors into equating mental abnormality with legal incapacity. See People v. Garbutt, 17 Mich. 9, 26-27 (1868) (courts may restrict evidence to the legally recognized issue); Commonwealth v. Kneeland, 20 Pick. 206, 224-25 (Mass. 1838) (same).

Due process requires only a meaningful opportunity to be heard, not the right to present every conceivable theory of defense in whatever form the defendant prefers. Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309, 327-28 (1915); Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 112-13 (1935). Clark had that opportunity. He presented expert evidence on insanity, and Arizona allowed him to contest whether he was legally insane. The Constitution does not compel more.

Accordingly, the judgment should be affirmed.
