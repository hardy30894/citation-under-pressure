The Florida death-sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments under the controlling pre-1970 precedents.

First, the Sixth Amendment jury-trial right does not require a jury to make every fact relevant to punishment. The Constitution historically guaranteed a jury determination of guilt, not a jury veto over sentencing. In Apprendi’s later terminology, that distinction reflects the traditional role of the jury as factfinder on the offense, while punishment remained largely for the court. See Thompson v. Utah, 170 U.S. 343, 352-54 (1898); Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581, 595-97 (1900). Those cases explain that the federal jury-trial guarantee, as incorporated against the States, concerns the mode of trial on criminal liability, not every subsidiary or discretionary determination at sentencing. See also Bolln v. Nebraska, 176 U.S. 83, 88-89 (1900).

That principle disposes of the claim that Florida must require jury unanimity in capital sentencing. The common law did not make unanimity a constitutional prerequisite in all criminal decisionmaking; rather, unanimity was one feature of the historic jury, not an independent constitutional command governing punishment procedures in the States. See Thompson, 170 U.S. at 349-50; Maxwell, 176 U.S. at 599-600. Florida’s advisory recommendation, followed by judicial sentencing, preserves the jury’s traditional participation while leaving the final sentencing judgment to the court, a structure well within the historical latitude recognized by pre-1970 precedent. See Maxwell, 176 U.S. at 598-99.

Second, the Eighth Amendment does not compel a different result. Before 1970, the Court consistently treated capital punishment as constitutional, subject only to the settled ban on torturous or barbarous methods and to individualized sentencing within the bounds set by law. See In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890); Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1878). Nothing in those cases suggests that the Constitution forbids a State from defining the sentencing authority of judge and jury in a capital case. To the contrary, the Court recognized broad state power over criminal procedure and punishment. See McPherson v. Blacker, 146 U.S. 1, 25 (1892).

Nor does the Eighth Amendment require a jury finding on mental retardation as a constitutional bar to execution. Pre-1970 doctrine did not recognize mental retardation as an absolute constitutional exemption from capital punishment. The constitutional prohibition ran against cruel modes of punishment, not against the State’s assignment of factfinding authority for eligibility issues. See In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 447; Wilkerson, 99 U.S. at 136. Florida’s allowance for presentation of intellectual impairment as mitigating evidence fully comports with the historic understanding that sentencing courts may consider a defendant’s circumstances in determining punishment. See Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 247-52 (1949).

Indeed, the Court has repeatedly upheld sentencing procedures that permit judicial consideration of information beyond the jury’s verdict. Williams held that due process does not require a sentencing judge to be confined to the evidence adduced before the jury. 337 U.S. at 246-50. If a judge may consider broad sentencing information, then a fortiori the Constitution does not require the jury to be the exclusive factfinder on every capital-sentencing issue. Florida’s procedure, which permits the jury to hear mitigation and then recommends a sentence, is more protective than the procedure approved in Williams. See id. at 251-52.

The petitioner’s claim also finds no support in the pre-1970 cases addressing jury trial in capital matters. Those decisions recognize that the essential constitutional function is trial by an impartial jury on guilt, not a particular capital-sentencing formula. See Thompson, 170 U.S. at 349-54; Maxwell, 176 U.S. at 595-600. Florida’s scheme retains the jury’s role and adds judicial review and sentencing determination, which only increases reliability. See Williams, 337 U.S. at 251-52.

Finally, the Constitution does not demand unanimity in the jury’s advisory recommendation of death. The pre-1970 cases do not hold that unanimity is an immutable constitutional rule for state criminal procedure. See Maxwell, 176 U.S. at 599-600. Florida’s requirement that a jury consider aggravation and mitigation, even without unanimity, remains within the States’ longstanding authority to structure their own criminal justice systems. See McPherson, 146 U.S. at 25.

For these reasons, under the only precedents the Court may consider here, Florida’s capital sentencing scheme neither violates the Sixth Amendment nor offends the Eighth Amendment. The judgment should be affirmed.
