# ARGUMENT

## I. The Beyond-a-Reasonable-Doubt Standard Governs Every Element of the Charged Conspiracy, Including Its Temporal Scope.

It is axiomatic that a criminal conviction may rest only on proof that establishes guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. In *Howard v. Fleming*, 191 U.S. 126 (1903), this Court sustained a trial court's charge to the jury on the reasonable-doubt standard, holding that a state conviction is not rendered unconstitutional where the trial judge properly instructs on the government's obligation to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt before the jury may convict. *Id.* at 126. That standard does not evaporate merely because the element in question concerns the *duration* of an agreement rather than its formation. Where, as here, the charged offense is a conspiracy that the indictment alleges continued into a period governed by the statute of limitations, the government must prove that continuation to the same exacting standard that governs every other element of the crime.

Conspiracy itself has long been understood as an offense built on the persistence of an unlawful combination. In *Haas v. Henkel*, 216 U.S. 462 (1910), the indictment charged that the defendants "conspired in the District of Columbia to defraud the United States," *id.* at 462, and this Court's analysis proceeded on the premise that the conspiracy's temporal and geographic contours defined the very offense charged. The same is true here: petitioners were charged with membership in a conspiracy whose life allegedly extended into the limitations period, and the government's burden must be measured against that specific temporal allegation, not against proof of some earlier, since-abandoned combination.

## II. A Conspiracy Is a Continuing Combination That Persists Only So Long As Its Members Remain Bound Together; Proof of an Antecedent Split Defeats the Inference of Continued Membership.

This Court has repeatedly described conspiracy in terms of a "combination," a relationship that exists only while its participants remain "acting in combination." *McDaniel v. Traylor*, 212 U.S. 428, 428 (1909) (describing defendants who, "by combination and conspiracy," acted together, and who, "acting in combination," tied their claims together). The corollary of that description is equally clear: when the combination dissolves, the conspiracy—as a legal and factual matter—dissolves with it. Once petitioners came forward with evidence that the conspiracy's leaders, Moore and Gray, had split apart before the limitations period began, they rebutted the premise that the "combination" continued to exist in the form charged.

The continuing character of a conspiracy is precisely what allows it to be prosecuted across shifting circumstances of place and personnel. In *Hyde v. Shine*, 199 U.S. 62 (1905), this Court observed that "the conspiracy in this case was triable in California or Oregon, as well as in the District of Columbia," *id.* at 62, because the unlawful agreement was understood to persist as a single, identifiable combination wherever its members acted. But that same continuing character cuts both ways: if the combination can be proved to persist across jurisdictions, it can likewise be shown to have terminated at a point in time. Once petitioners introduced evidence of the leaders' split, the government could no longer rely on an undifferentiated inference that the earlier combination simply carried forward; it was obliged to prove, to the jury's satisfaction beyond a reasonable doubt, that the *charged* conspiracy—not some predecessor arrangement—persisted into the limitations window. The precision demanded of an indictment's temporal and substantive scope, and the jeopardy consequences that attach to it, were emphasized in *In re Belt*, 159 U.S. 95 (1895), where the prisoner argued that the empanelling of a jury "put the prisoner in jeopardy in respect of all the offences charged... within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment," *id.* at 95—illustrating that the specific offense charged, not some broader course of conduct, defines what the government must prove and what jeopardy attaches to.

## III. Once Withdrawal Evidence Is Introduced, the Burden of Persuasion on Continued Membership Falls to the Government.

Analogous burden-shifting principles confirm that once a defendant produces evidence undermining a presumption of continuity, the proponent of that presumption must affirmatively reestablish it. In the civil-conspiracy context, this Court has recognized that a prior judgment may serve as "prima facie evidence of the conspiracy," *Partmar Corp. v. Paramount Pictures Theatres Corp.*, 347 U.S. 89, 89 (1954), but a prima facie showing is precisely that—one that can be rebutted, at which point the burden of persuasion returns to the party asserting the fact. Similarly, in *First Nat. Bank of Ariz. v. Cities Service Co.*, 391 U.S. 253 (1968), the Court considered whether the courts below had erred in allocating to the party opposing summary judgment the burden of demonstrating a genuine issue for trial, *id.* at 253, underscoring that burdens do not remain static once one side has come forward with contrary evidence.

That same logic dooms the government's position here. The danger of treating an entire course of dealing as a single, undifferentiated conspiracy without careful proof of its temporal scope was recognized in *Emich Motors Corp. v. General Motors Corp.*, 340 U.S. 558 (1951), where the Court confronted an indictment charging "a single conspiracy to perform some twenty-six different acts" and noted that where the government "did not offer evidence to support all of the acts," it becomes "impossible upon a general verdict of guilty to determine" the conspiracy's actual scope. *Id.* at 558. The probative limits of evidence offered to prove a continuing combination were likewise recognized in *Price v. Henkel*, 216 U.S. 488 (1910), where the Court held that the "probative weight" of evidence is "necessarily limited" to what it actually establishes. *Id.* at 488. Once petitioners proved the leaders' split, the government's evidence of the pre-split combination lost its probative force as to the limitations period, and the burden of persuasion—not merely production—fell squarely on the government to prove membership beyond a reasonable doubt during the relevant window.
