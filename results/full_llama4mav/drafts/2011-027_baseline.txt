The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the trial court properly applied the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes. The equal footing doctrine, which grants states title to the beds of navigable rivers upon admission to the Union, is a well-established principle of constitutional law. Under this doctrine, a river is considered navigable if it was navigable at the time of statehood. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75-76 (1931); Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 77, 86-87 (1922).

The test for navigability for title purposes is a factual inquiry that considers whether a river was "used or susceptible of being used" for commerce at the time of statehood. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870). This inquiry is not limited to evidence of actual commercial use, but also considers whether the river was capable of supporting such use. United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 407-08 (1940). The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this test, relying on historical evidence to determine whether the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of Montana's statehood in 1889.
