The Supreme Court of Texas erred in holding that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim. The Court's decision was based on an overly broad interpretation of the "arising under" language in 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), which was influenced by the Federal Circuit's decisions in Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed.Cir.2007), and Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed.Cir.2007).

However, the Supreme Court has established a more nuanced test for determining whether a state-law claim arises under federal patent law jurisdiction. In Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308 (2005), the Court held that a state-law claim arises under federal jurisdiction only if it "necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." Id. at 314. This test requires a careful balancing of federal and state interests, as seen in Singh v. Duane Morris LLP, 538 F.3d 334 (5th Cir.2008), where the Fifth Circuit applied the Grable test to a legal malpractice claim involving patent law and found no exclusive federal jurisdiction.

The Grable test is comprised of four elements: (1) resolving a federal issue is necessary to resolution of the state-law claim; (2) the federal issue is actually disputed; (3) the federal issue is substantial; and (4) federal jurisdiction will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. See Singh, 538 F.3d at 338. While the first three elements may be met in this case, the fourth element is not. As the Supreme Court noted in Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), "the mere presence of a federal issue in a state cause of action does not automatically confer federal-question jurisdiction." Id. at 115.

In this case, Minton's legal malpractice claim does not require the resolution of a substantial federal patent law issue that would disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. The experimental use exception, a patent law concept, is relevant to Minton's claim, but its application is not substantial enough to trigger exclusive federal jurisdiction. See New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner, 270 Neb. 264, 702 N.W.2d 336 (2005) (finding no federal jurisdiction over malpractice claim); E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP, 189 Cal.App.4th 1140, 117 Cal.Rptr.3d 516 (Cal.Ct.App.2010) (same).

Furthermore, the Federal Circuit's approach in Air Measurement and Immunocept has been criticized for failing to properly consider the federalism concerns required by Grable. See USPPS, Ltd. v. Neary, 647 F.3d 252, 282 (5th Cir.2011) (noting that the Federal Circuit's approach "carries forward the Federal Circuit's misguided approach" to federalism analysis). Other courts have also declined to follow the Federal Circuit's lead, instead applying the Grable test to find no exclusive federal jurisdiction over similar claims. See, e.g., Roof Technical Servs., Inc. v. Hill, 679 F.Supp.2d 749 (N.D.Tex.2010); Genelink Biosciences, Inc. v. Colby, 722 F.Supp.2d 592 (D.N.J.2010).

In Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800 (1988), the Supreme Court construed the "arising under" language in § 1338(a) to extend federal jurisdiction to cases where "federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law." Id. at 808-09. However, this does not mean that every state-law claim that involves a patent law issue arises under federal jurisdiction. As the Court noted in Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), "a suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws." Id. at 569.

In conclusion, the Supreme Court of Texas erred in holding that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim. The Court should have applied the Grable test and considered the federalism concerns at play. By doing so, it would have found that Minton's claim does not arise under exclusive federal patent law jurisdiction, and the state court has jurisdiction to hear the case.
