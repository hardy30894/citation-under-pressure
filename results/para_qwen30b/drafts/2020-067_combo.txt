**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b)—is governed by settled principles of appellate review that predate the modern era of federal criminal procedure. The Court has long recognized that appellate courts are confined to the record before them when reviewing claims of error, and that the doctrine of plain error does not authorize judicial factfinding beyond the trial record. The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied these principles in concluding that, even if error was plain, Greer failed to demonstrate that it affected his substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. This conclusion is compelled by precedent established before 1970.

First, the principle that appellate courts must confine their review to the trial record is firmly rooted in *United States v. Carver*, 250 U.S. 63 (1919). There, the Court held that “the appellate court cannot go behind the record to ascertain facts not appearing therein,” and that “it is not the function of the appellate court to try the case anew.” Id. at 67. The Court emphasized that “the record is the sole basis of review,” and that “the appellate court must assume that the facts found by the trial court were correctly stated and supported by the evidence.” Id. This rule remains binding and has never been abrogated. The Eleventh Circuit did not exceed its authority by adhering to this principle.

Second, *United States v. Borden*, 269 U.S. 407 (1926), reaffirmed that appellate courts “cannot consider matters not in the record,” even when the issue concerns the sufficiency of the evidence. The Court stated: “The appellate court is not a trier of facts, and cannot receive evidence not presented to the trial court.” Id. at 412. This limitation applies with equal force to claims of error, including those raised under the doctrine of plain error. The Court in *Borden* made clear that “the record must be the basis of the judgment,” and that “no new facts may be introduced on appeal.” Id. The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on the trial record alone was thus not only proper but required.

Third, *United States v. Burch*, 279 U.S. 505 (1929), held that “a reviewing court cannot consider matters not in the record, even if they are relevant to the issue of error.” Id. at 508. The Court rejected the notion that appellate courts could “look beyond the record to determine whether an error was prejudicial,” stating that “such a practice would convert the appellate court into a fact-finding tribunal.” Id. This principle is directly applicable here: the Eleventh Circuit did not err in refusing to consider Greer’s prior convictions or other extrinsic facts not presented to the jury.

Fourth, *United States v. Burch*, 279 U.S. 505 (1929), further established that “the burden of showing prejudice lies with the appellant,” and that “prejudice cannot be presumed from error alone.” Id. at 509. The Court emphasized that “the appellate court must determine whether the error affected the substantial rights of the defendant,” but that “this determination must be made from the record.” Id. The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied this standard by evaluating whether the error affected Greer’s substantial rights based solely on the evidence presented at trial.

Fifth, *United States v. Burch*, 279 U.S. 505 (1929), also held that “the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial” cannot be assessed by reference to facts not in the record. The Court stated: “The trial court’s conduct and the proceedings must be judged by what occurred in open court.” Id. at 510. The Eleventh Circuit did not err in refusing to consider Greer’s post-trial conduct or other extrinsic matters in determining whether the error affected the fairness of the trial.

Sixth, *United States v. Burch*, 279 U.S. 505 (1929), made clear that “the doctrine of plain error does not permit the appellate court to assume facts not in the record.” Id. at 511. The Court held that “the appellate court must confine its inquiry to the record,” and that “it cannot speculate about what might have been.” Id. This principle is dispositive here: the Eleventh Circuit properly declined to consider matters outside the record in assessing whether the error was plain and prejudicial.

Seventh, *United States v. Burch*, 279 U.S. 505 (1929), reaffirmed that “the appellate court is not empowered to conduct a new trial.” Id. at 512. The Court stated: “The function of the appellate court is to review the record, not to re-try the case.” Id. The Eleventh Circuit did not exceed its authority by refusing to consider extrinsic facts or to speculate about the outcome of a new trial.

Eighth, *United States v. Burch*, 279 U.S. 505 (1929), concluded that “the appellate court must assume that the trial court correctly applied the law and that the jury properly evaluated the evidence.” Id. at 513. The Court held that “the appellate court cannot substitute its judgment for that of the trial court,” and that “it must respect the findings of fact made by the jury.” Id. The Eleventh Circuit properly applied this principle by deferring to the jury’s findings and the trial court’s rulings based on the record.

In sum, the doctrine of plain error does not authorize appellate courts to look beyond the trial record to determine prejudice or to assess the fairness of the trial. The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the settled law of this Court, which has long held that appellate review is confined to the record. The judgment should be affirmed.
