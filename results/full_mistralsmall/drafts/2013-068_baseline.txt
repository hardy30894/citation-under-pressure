**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE MASSACHUSETTS BUFFER ZONE STATUTE IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL, NARROWLY TAILORED TIME, PLACE, AND MANNER REGULATION THAT SURVIVES INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY.**

The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts buffer zone statute as a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I)*, 571 F.3d 167, 176-81 (1st Cir. 2009), *cert. denied*, 561 U.S. 1027 (2010). Such regulations are permissible under the First Amendment if they are (1) content-neutral, (2) narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and (3) leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). The Massachusetts statute meets each of these requirements.

**A. The Statute Is Content-Neutral.**

The statute is content-neutral because it does not discriminate based on the message of the speaker. *See Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 720 (2000). It applies equally to all individuals, regardless of their viewpoint, and prohibits all expressive activity within the buffer zone. *See id.* The plaintiffs’ contention that the statute is viewpoint-discriminatory is belied by its facially neutral terms and the absence of any evidence that it has been applied in a discriminatory manner. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 177-78.

**B. The Statute Is Narrowly Tailored to Serve a Significant Governmental Interest.**

The Commonwealth has a significant interest in protecting the safety and privacy of patients and clinic employees seeking medical care. *See id.* at 178. The buffer zone directly advances this interest by preventing congestion, harassment, and potential violence at clinic entrances. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715-16. The statute is narrowly tailored because it is the least restrictive means of achieving this interest. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798-99. The 35-foot buffer zone is no broader than necessary to protect patients and employees while still allowing ample space for alternative communication. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 179-80.

**C. The Statute Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels for Communication.**

The plaintiffs have not been deprived of all means of communication. They remain free to speak, distribute literature, and engage in expressive activity outside the buffer zone. *See id.* at 180. The First Circuit correctly found that the plaintiffs retain “adequate communicative channels,” including oral speech, signage, and literature distribution. *Id.* at 181. The plaintiffs’ argument that they cannot effectively communicate at a “normal conversational distance” is unavailing, as the Supreme Court has rejected similar claims in *Hill*. *See* 530 U.S. at 723-24.

**II. THE FIRST CIRCUIT PROPERLY APPLIED *HILL v. COLORADO* AND THERE IS NO BASIS TO LIMIT OR OVERRULE THAT DECISION.**

The plaintiffs argue that *Hill v. Colorado* should be limited or overruled. This argument is meritless. *Hill* upheld a 100-foot buffer zone around healthcare facilities, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly reaffirmed its reasoning. *See id.* at 715-20. The Massachusetts statute is even more narrowly tailored than the Colorado statute, as it imposes a smaller buffer zone and explicitly exempts clinic employees and patients. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181-82. There is no justification for departing from *Hill*, and this Court should reaffirm its holding.

**III. THE STATUTE DOES NOT IMPOSE AN UNLAWFUL PRIOR RESTRAINT ON SPEECH.**

The plaintiffs’ prior restraint claim fails because the statute does not prohibit speech in advance but merely regulates the time, place, and manner of expressive activity. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 183-84. The buffer zone does not prevent the plaintiffs from speaking; it merely requires them to do so outside the restricted area. *See id.* This is not a prior restraint. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 795-96.

**IV. THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE BARS RELITIGATION OF THE FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY OF THE STATUTE.**

The plaintiffs’ attempt to relitigate the facial constitutionality of the statute is barred by the law of the case doctrine. *See United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011). The First Circuit already upheld the statute in *McCullen I*, and the plaintiffs have not shown any of the narrow exceptions to the doctrine apply. *See id.* at 14. Their reliance on recent Supreme Court decisions is unavailing, as those cases do not alter the First Amendment framework applicable to time, place, and manner regulations. *See Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011); *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed. The Massachusetts buffer zone statute is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that does not violate the First Amendment. *Hill v. Colorado* remains good law, and the plaintiffs’ arguments to the contrary should be rejected.
