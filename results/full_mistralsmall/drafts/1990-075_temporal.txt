**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL IS CONSTITUTIONAL.**

The Controlled Substances Act’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General does not violate the non-delegation doctrine. The Supreme Court has long held that Congress may delegate authority to the Executive so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegate’s discretion. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The temporary scheduling provision of 21 U.S.C. § 811(h) satisfies this standard.

Congress has supplied clear standards governing the Attorney General’s exercise of temporary scheduling authority. Section 811(h) requires that a substance be temporarily scheduled only if it poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety” and meets at least three of the eight factors enumerated in § 811(c)—specifically, the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of such abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). These criteria are sufficiently definite to cabin the Attorney General’s discretion. *See Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 785 (1948) (upholding delegation where Congress required that prices be “fair and equitable”); *Farmers Irrigation Co. v. Farmers’ Irrigation Co.*, 258 U.S. 477, 484 (1922) (finding delegation valid where statute required rates to be “just and reasonable”).

The temporary scheduling provision also contains procedural safeguards that further constrain the Attorney General’s discretion. Before temporarily scheduling a substance, the Attorney General must publish notice in the *Federal Register* and consider the Secretary of Health and Human Services’ evaluation. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). These requirements ensure that the Attorney General’s decision is informed by expert input and subject to public scrutiny. *See Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944) (upholding delegation where statute required consideration of “fair and equitable” prices and provided for public hearings).

The Toubys’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In those cases, the Court invalidated delegations because Congress had failed to provide any meaningful standards to guide the Executive’s discretion. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415 (finding delegation invalid where statute provided “no standard whatever”); *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 542 (same). Here, by contrast, Congress has supplied specific criteria and procedural safeguards. *See Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372-73 (1989) (distinguishing *Panama Refining* and *Schechter Poultry* on the ground that those cases involved “broad and unconfined” delegations).

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S SUBDELEGATION OF AUTHORITY TO THE DEA IS AUTHORIZED.**

The Attorney General’s subdelegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is consistent with settled principles of administrative law. The Supreme Court has recognized that “the Attorney General is the chief law enforcement officer of the Government” and may “utilize his powers through appropriate officers and agencies of the Department of Justice.” *Hecht v. Bomas*, 357 U.S. 572, 583 (1958). The Attorney General’s authority to subdelegate is particularly appropriate where, as here, the delegation involves technical and scientific determinations best handled by an expert agency. *See United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911) (upholding subdelegation of authority to regulate public grazing lands to the Secretary of Agriculture).

The subdelegation here is expressly authorized by 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), which provides that the Attorney General “may promulgate rules and regulations to carry out the purposes of this subchapter.” The DEA Administrator’s exercise of temporary scheduling authority is thus a valid exercise of the Attorney General’s delegated power. *See United States v. Chemical Foundation*, 272 U.S. 190, 200 (1926) (upholding subdelegation where statute authorized the Commissioner of Patents to “perform any acts and issue any regulations necessary for the administration” of patent laws).

**III. THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DEPRIVE DEFENDANTS OF DUE PROCESS.**

The Toubys’ due process challenge fails because the temporary scheduling provision provides adequate procedural protections. Before a substance is temporarily scheduled, the Attorney General must provide notice and an opportunity for public comment. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). These procedures ensure that the Government’s action is not arbitrary or capricious. *See Opp Cotton Mills v. Administrator*, 312 U.S. 126, 152-53 (1941) (upholding delegation where statute required notice and hearing before fixing wages and hours).

Moreover, the temporary scheduling provision is limited in duration—no more than one year, with a possible six-month extension. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). This temporal limitation ensures that the Government’s action is not unduly burdensome. *See Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (upholding delegation where statute provided for periodic review of regulations).

**CONCLUSION**

The delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General and his subdelegation to the DEA are constitutional. The statute provides an intelligible principle to guide the Attorney General’s discretion, and the subdelegation is expressly authorized by statute. The Toubys’ constitutional challenges therefore fail as a matter of law.
