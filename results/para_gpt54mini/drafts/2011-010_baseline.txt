**Argument**

I. **Section 1333(b) does not require that the injury occur on the outer continental shelf itself.**

The text of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore Act coverage to “the disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Congress thus tied compensability to the *cause* of the injury—operations on the shelf—not to the *place* where the injury happens. That reading follows the statute’s ordinary structure: the phrase “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” links coverage to a causal relationship between shelf operations and the injury, not to a geographic situs requirement. See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b); see also Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 n.2 (1986) (noting that § 1333(b) is the only provision that “superimposes a status requirement” on OCSLA’s otherwise situs-based scheme).

The Ninth Circuit correctly rejected the Benefits Review Board’s situs-of-injury rule and held that § 1333(b) applies where there is a “substantial nexus between the injury and extractive operations on the shelf.” Valladolid v. Pacific Operations Offshore, LLP, 668 F.3d 1137, 1140-41 (9th Cir. 2012). That construction best fits the statutory language and the Act’s purpose to provide workers’ compensation protection for injuries caused by outer-shelf operations. See id. at 1145-46.

II. **Tallentire does not compel a situs-of-injury limitation.**

Petitioner’s contrary rule rests primarily on a footnote in Tallentire, but that footnote cannot bear the weight she places on it. The Supreme Court in Tallentire addressed OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, § 1333(a)(2)(A), not the compensation provision in § 1333(b). Tallentire, 477 U.S. at 209, 219 n.2. The Court expressly stated that § 1333(b) “has no bearing on this case.” Id. at 219 n.2. Dicta of that sort is not controlling, particularly where the issue was not briefed or decided. See Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44, 67 (1996); United States v. Montero-Camargo, 208 F.3d 1122, 1132 n.17 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc).

Nor does Tallentire’s reference to OCSLA’s “general scope” being determined “principally by locale” resolve the separate question presented by § 1333(b). Tallentire, 477 U.S. at 219. Section 1333(a) uses situs to define surrogate state law; § 1333(b) instead extends federal compensation benefits to injuries resulting from offshore operations. See id. at 219 n.2; 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The provisions serve different functions and use different language.

III. **The better reading is the one adopted by the Third Circuit, not the Fifth Circuit.**

The Third Circuit held in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), that a claimant need only show that the injury would not have occurred “but for” operations on the outer continental shelf. Id. at 809-11. That approach honors the statutory text’s causal focus and avoids inserting a geographic limitation Congress did not include. Id. at 810-11.

By contrast, the Fifth Circuit’s situs-of-injury rule in Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), adds words to the statute. Mills requires that the injury occur on the shelf or over shelf waters, even though § 1333(b) says only that the disability or death must result from an injury occurring “as the result of operations” on the shelf. Id. at 362. Courts should not rewrite an unambiguous statute by adding a location element Congress omitted. See Lamie v. United States Trustee, 540 U.S. 526, 538 (2004).

IV. **The Ninth Circuit’s substantial-nexus test is faithful to the statute and this Court should adopt it.**

The Ninth Circuit’s construction avoids both extremes. It does not extend coverage to any injury merely connected in some attenuated way to offshore work; instead, it requires a substantial nexus between the injury and shelf operations. Valladolid, 668 F.3d at 1148-49. That standard tracks the statutory phrase “as the result of” and preserves a meaningful causal limitation. See id.; see also Exxon Corp. v. Dep’t of Conservation & Natural Resources, 859 P.2d 1112, 1118 (Alaska 1993) (recognizing OCSLA’s strong federal interest in regulating offshore operations).

Here, there was more than enough nexus. Juan Valladolid worked primarily on offshore platforms operated by Pacific Operations, and he was killed while performing maintenance duties at the onshore facility that processed crude slurry from those platforms. Valladolid, 668 F.3d at 1140-41. His fatal injury was not an independent land-based accident unrelated to shelf operations; it arose directly from work integral to Pacific Operations’s extraction and processing enterprise. Under § 1333(b), that is sufficient. See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b); Valladolid, 668 F.3d at 1148-49.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
