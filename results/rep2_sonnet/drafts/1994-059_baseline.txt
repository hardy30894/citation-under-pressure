# ARGUMENT

## I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF ARTICLE I ESTABLISH EXCLUSIVE AND FIXED CRITERIA FOR CONGRESSIONAL SERVICE THAT STATES MAY NOT SUPPLEMENT

The Constitution specifies, in Article I, § 2, that a Representative must be twenty-five years of age, seven years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the State in which chosen; Article I, § 3 sets parallel qualifications for Senators. These enumerations are not illustrative floors but complete ceilings. As Justice Story explained, and as this Court has recognized, the Framers deliberately rejected proposals to allow adjustment of these qualifications precisely because uniformity across the several states was essential to the character of the national legislature.

Amendment 73 imposes an additional qualification—prior electoral service—that appears nowhere in the constitutional text. Whether accomplished by a formal bar or, as Arkansas insists here, through mere ballot-access restriction, the practical effect is identical: a citizen otherwise fully qualified under Article I is prevented from becoming a Member of Congress solely because of a state-created criterion. This Court's reasoning in *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), forecloses any such maneuver. There, the Court held that the House could not add to the qualifications the Constitution specifies for its own membership, reasoning that the Qualifications Clauses were adopted as *exclusive* — the product of a considered constitutional choice to remove qualification-setting from majoritarian discretion. The Court emphasized that the Framers, having considered and rejected property and other qualifications, intended the enumerated criteria "were to be fixed, exclusive, and uniform," 395 U.S. at 550, and that neither Congress nor, by direct implication, the states may alter that constitutional judgment through their own devices.

If the House of Representatives itself cannot add qualifications, it follows *a fortiori* that individual states cannot do so unilaterally. The structural reasoning of *Powell* rests on the notion that no single political actor, however proximate to Congress, should have the power to exclude candidates the Constitution deems qualified. A state's role in this scheme is to hold elections, not to redefine eligibility for federal office. Permitting each state to impose its own supplemental qualifications—whether age limits, property requirements, or term limits—would recreate the exact patchwork of state-by-state variation in access to national office that the Framers sought to prevent by fixing qualifications in the Constitution itself.

## II. BALLOT-ACCESS RESTRICTIONS THAT FUNCTION AS DISQUALIFICATIONS VIOLATE THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES REGARDLESS OF FORM

Petitioners' principal defense—that Amendment 73 merely restricts *ballot access* rather than *eligibility*—elevates form over substance and cannot withstand analysis. This Court has consistlently examined the practical effect of state election regulations on access to federal office, not merely their formal characterization. In *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), the Court struck down Ohio's early filing deadline for independent presidential candidates, recognizing that state election laws which impose "burdens on a minority political party or its supporters that other parties or supporters do not suffer," 460 U.S. at 793, must be evaluated according to their actual effect on the constitutional right to stand for office and the corresponding right of voters to choose among candidates.

Amendment 73's Section 3 operates with unmistakable clarity: any person who has served three terms in the House, or two terms in the Senate, "shall not be certified as a candidate and shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot." The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that this is a *permanent* bar to a fully qualified candidate's name appearing before the voters—the functional equivalent of disqualification. As the court below observed, "the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp," and this Court should decline to elevate that semantic distinction into a constitutional loophole. A state cannot achieve through the ballot-access door what the Qualifications Clauses forbid at the front door.

## III. PERMITTING STATE-IMPOSED TERM LIMITS ON CONGRESS THROUGH ANY MECHANISM UNDERMINES THE UNIFORM, NATIONAL CHARACTER OF THAT BODY

The Arkansas Supreme Court's own reasoning below is correct and should guide this Court: "Congressional officeholders partake of the same national character as the President of the United States. . . . Uniformity of qualifications is paramount, and individual states are not free to engraft variations. The terms for members of Congress can be limited only by amending the United States Constitution."

This principle finds firm grounding in *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), where Chief Justice Marshall articulated the foundational structural premise that the federal government, though enumerated in its powers, is supreme within its sphere and not subject to piecemeal alteration by the states that compose the Union. Members of Congress serve the entire nation, not merely their home states; permitting Arkansas to impose eligibility restrictions unavailable in other states would produce exactly the sort of disuniformity in the national legislature that the structure of the Constitution as a whole—and the Qualifications Clauses in particular—was designed to prevent.

## IV. RESPONDENT'S STANDING AND THE JUSTICIABILITY OF THIS CONTROVERSY ARE WELL ESTABLISHED

Petitioners' suggestion that this dispute is premature cannot survive scrutiny. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly applied *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), in recognizing that the amendment's mere existence "brings into play impairment of speech and association rights under the First and Fourteenth Amendments" for voters and supporters of affected candidates. Respondent Thornton, having already served three terms, faces certain exclusion from the ballot in the next election absent this Court's intervention—an injury as concrete and imminent as any this Court has recognized as sufficient for Article III standing.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court holding Section 3 of Amendment 73 unconstitutional should be affirmed.
