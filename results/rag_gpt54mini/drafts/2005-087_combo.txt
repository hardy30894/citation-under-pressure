The judgment should be affirmed because this Court has never recognized a free-standing constitutional right to introduce psychiatric evidence to negate mens rea apart from an insanity defense, and Arizona’s rule is consistent with due process.

This Court has repeatedly held that a State may require a defendant who seeks to avoid criminal punishment on mental-health grounds to proceed through the insanity defense and to bear the burdens associated with that defense. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court sustained a state law that required the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, while recognizing that the prosecution still had to prove every element of the offense. 343 U.S. at 797-99. The Court explained that even where insanity is pleaded, the jury may consider insanity evidence on issues such as intent only if state law so permits; Leland approved the trial court’s instruction that insanity evidence be considered “in regard to the ability of the defendant to premeditate, form a purpose, to deliberate, act wilfully, and act maliciously.” Id. at 799. That holding defeats respondent’s claim that the Constitution independently entitles him to use expert mental-state evidence however he wishes.

Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), likewise reflects that insanity is a distinct defense, historically governed by separate procedural rules. The Court there addressed the burden of proof for insanity and explained that sanity is an essential fact only once insanity is properly raised. Id. at 484-86. Nothing in Davis suggests a constitutional right to bifurcate psychiatric evidence into a standalone mens rea defense; to the contrary, it confirms that the manner in which insanity evidence is used has always been a matter of settled criminal procedure.

Later cases continue the same theme. In Davis v. United States, 165 U.S. 373 (1897), the Court approved instructions treating insanity as a special defense and reaffirmed that the jury may be told to decide criminal responsibility through the insanity doctrine. Id. at 378-79. Hotema v. United States, 186 U.S. 413 (1902), similarly upheld instructions that focused the jury on whether the defendant’s “insane delusion” controlled his will and whether proof established that condition beyond a reasonable doubt. Id. at 415-16. These decisions show that mental-condition evidence is constitutionally adequate when routed through the insanity framework.

Blackburn v. Alabama, 361 U.S. 199 (1960), and Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), are no help to petitioner. Blackburn held that the admission of a confession from a defendant whose “long history of pronounced irrational behavior” raised serious voluntariness concerns violated due process. 361 U.S. at 207-10. Pate held that due process required a competency hearing where the evidence raised doubt about competence to stand trial. 383 U.S. at 385-86. Those cases protect the reliability of the trial process and the accused’s present ability to defend, not a substantive right to present psychiatric evidence on mens rea in defiance of state evidentiary rules.

Nor do cases concerning expert testimony establish petitioner’s position. In Massey v. Moore, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), the Court required a hearing on whether the petitioner had been insane at the time of trial. Id. at 105-06. In United States Ex Rel. Smith v. Baldi, 344 U.S. 561 (1953), the Court rejected a due process attack on Pennsylvania’s handling of insanity issues, emphasizing that the defendant could have pursued an insanity plea. Id. at 568-69. And Lynch v. Overholser, 369 U.S. 705 (1962), recognized that a defendant who “chooses to claim that he was mentally irresponsible when his offense was committed” may be required to accept the consequences of that chosen route. Id. at 714-15. That is precisely what Arizona did here.

Finally, Panico v. United States, 375 U.S. 29 (1963), is inapposite. There the Court required a hearing on criminal responsibility in a federal contempt proceeding because of the “fair administration of federal criminal justice.” Id. at 29-30. Panico rested on federal supervisory principles, not a Fourteenth Amendment command binding the States. It therefore provides no basis to invalidate Arizona’s allocation of mental-state evidence to the insanity defense.

Due process guarantees a fair trial, not a constitutional entitlement to present psychiatric evidence in whatever form the defendant prefers. Because Arizona allowed petitioner to present expert evidence on insanity, while limiting its use to that defense, the state court’s judgment should be affirmed.
