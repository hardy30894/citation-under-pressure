**Argument**

The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction. Removal is proper only if the action could have been filed originally in federal court, and the removing party bears the burden of establishing jurisdiction. *Mulcahey v. Columbia Organic Chems. Co., Inc.*, 29 F.3d 148, 151 (4th Cir. 1994). Any doubt must be resolved against removal. *Id.* The complaint here is a state-law declaratory judgment action concerning the scope of a private flowage easement and the parties’ rights under Virginia property law. That dispute does not arise under federal law, and it is not converted into a federal question merely because APCO operates a licensed hydroelectric project. The district court therefore should have remanded. *See* *Moor v. Alameda County*, 411 U.S. 693, 702-03 (1973).

Under the well-pleaded complaint rule, federal jurisdiction exists only when a federal question is presented on the face of the plaintiff’s properly pleaded complaint. *Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams*, 482 U.S. 386, 392 (1987). A plaintiff is the “master of the claim,” and may avoid federal jurisdiction by relying exclusively on state law. *Id.* The Pressls did exactly that. Their complaint seeks declarations about the meaning and effect of a 1960 easement, APCO’s asserted rights under that easement, and the Pressls’ own property rights in land in Franklin County, Virginia. Those are classic state-law issues. Virginia property law governs construction of easements, and state courts routinely adjudicate such disputes. *See* *Mitchum v. Foster*, 407 U.S. 225, 242 (1972) (recognizing that property and tort disputes ordinarily arise under state law absent a substantial federal issue).

APCO relied on 28 U.S.C. § 1331, but federal-question jurisdiction cannot be created by a federal defense, even a defense that the federal defendant expects to prevail. *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908); *Franchise Tax Bd. v. Constr. Laborers Vacation Tr.*, 463 U.S. 1, 12-13 (1983). APCO’s theory below was that FERC’s licensing scheme and project boundary somehow displaced the Pressls’ claims. But that is, at most, a defense or background regulatory context. It does not appear on the face of the complaint, and it does not supply original federal jurisdiction. *Caterpillar*, 482 U.S. at 393; *Franchise Tax Bd.*, 463 U.S. at 14.

Nor does the “substantial federal question” doctrine apply. State-law claims may arise under federal law only in the rare case where they necessarily raise a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, that a federal forum may entertain without disturbing the federal-state balance approved by Congress. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005); *Gunn v. Minton*, 568 U.S. 251, 258 (2013). This case fails that test. The Pressls can obtain complete relief by a declaration construing the easement under Virginia law. Whether APCO also has regulatory obligations under FERC or whether the parcel lies within a FERC project boundary is not an essential element of the Pressls’ claim. A federal issue is not “necessarily raised” if the plaintiff can prevail on a state-law theory without resolving it. *Gunn*, 568 U.S. at 259.

The district court’s reliance on 16 U.S.C. § 825p was also mistaken. Section 825p does not create general federal-question jurisdiction over all disputes involving FERC licensees. Courts construing the Federal Power Act have held that § 825p confers jurisdiction only over actions to enforce the Act, orders issued under it, or duties specifically imposed by the Act. *See* *Alabama Power Co. v. Fed. Energy Regulatory Comm’n*, 993 F.2d 1557, 1560-61 (D.C. Cir. 1993); *City of Tacoma v. Taxpayers of Tacoma*, 357 U.S. 320, 331-32 (1958). The Pressls do not seek to enforce or challenge any FERC order, nor do they ask the court to interpret a federal license. Their suit seeks a declaration about private rights under a recorded easement. Section 825p therefore provides no jurisdictional footing. *See also* *J.E.M. Ag Supply, Inc. v. Pioneer Hi-Bred Int’l, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 124, 144 (2001) (federal jurisdiction depends on Congress’s actual grant, not judicial enlargement).

Because removal jurisdiction was lacking, the district court also lacked authority to reach the merits. A court without subject matter jurisdiction “cannot proceed at all in any cause.” *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 94 (1998). The proper course is to vacate the dismissal order and remand with instructions to return the case to state court. *See* *International Primate Prot. League v. Administrators of Tulane Educ. Fund*, 500 U.S. 72, 89 (1991).

For these reasons, the Court should vacate the judgment and remand with instructions to remand the case to the Circuit Court of Franklin County, Virginia.
