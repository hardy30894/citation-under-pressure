**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 318 (1945). This requirement ensures that a defendant has fair notice that its conduct may subject it to litigation in a particular jurisdiction and that the exercise of jurisdiction is consistent with “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *Id.* at 316. Here, Ford Motor Company has no contacts with Montana that gave rise to or are related to the claims brought by the estate of Markkaya Jean Gullett. The vehicle at issue was never sold, serviced, or even operated in Montana. It was assembled in Kentucky, first sold in Washington, and later transferred through Oregon before being brought into Montana by a private purchaser. Ford did not advertise, market, or sell vehicles in Montana, nor did it maintain any sales or service presence in the state. The only alleged connection is Ford’s general, passive distribution of parts through national channels, which is insufficient to establish specific jurisdiction.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed itself of the privilege of doing business” in Montana by selling parts within the state is legally unsound. *See* *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 141 S. Ct. 1038, 1043 (2021) (per curiam). The mere fact that Ford sells parts nationwide does not mean it has purposefully directed its activities toward Montana. *See* *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985) (specific jurisdiction requires “purposeful availment” of forum benefits). A corporation cannot be deemed to have purposefully availed itself of a state’s laws simply by selling products through a nationwide distribution network. *See* *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987) (no jurisdiction where defendant’s contacts were “random, fortuitous, or attenuated”). The Montana Supreme Court’s reasoning would allow any state to assert jurisdiction over any national manufacturer based on the mere possibility that its products may eventually reach that state—regardless of whether the defendant had any meaningful connection to the forum. Such a rule would violate the constitutional limits on personal jurisdiction and render the “arise out of or relate to” standard meaningless.

Moreover, the claim at issue—alleging a design defect in a tire that failed during operation—has no causal nexus to any activity Ford conducted in Montana. The tire failure occurred on a Montana highway, but the tire was manufactured and sold outside the state. Ford did not design, test, or inspect the tire in Montana. No Ford employee ever interacted with the plaintiff or her vehicle in Montana. The injury and the claim are entirely disconnected from any conduct by Ford within the state. *See* *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (jurisdiction improper where “the injury was inflicted by an event that occurred entirely outside the forum state”). The Montana Supreme Court’s decision effectively treats the mere presence of a product in a state as sufficient to establish jurisdiction, which is precisely the kind of “sliding scale” approach the Supreme Court has rejected. *See* *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 924 (2011) (specific jurisdiction requires “some act by which the defendant purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws”).

The dissent’s reliance on *Newbury*, *Conway*, and *Harris* is misplaced. Those cases involved insurance contract disputes and the application of Montana’s collateral source rule in the context of insurance payments, not personal jurisdiction. *See* *Newbury v. State Farm Fire & Cas. Ins.*, 2008 MT 156, 184 P.3d 1021; *Conway v. Benefis Health Sys.*, 2013 MT 73, 297 P.3d 1200. They do not address the constitutional limits on jurisdiction. The dissent’s argument that allowing evidence of medical bills is a “windfall” is irrelevant to the jurisdictional question. The issue before this Court is not whether the plaintiff may recover damages, but whether the state court may exercise jurisdiction over Ford at all. *See* *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117, 137 (2014) (jurisdiction must be “based on the defendant’s relationship to the forum”).

The Montana Supreme Court’s decision undermines the predictability and fairness of the legal system. It invites forum shopping and exposes national corporations to litigation in any state where their products may eventually be used. This is inconsistent with the Due Process Clause, which requires that jurisdiction be grounded in the defendant’s own conduct. *See* *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475. The Court should reverse the Montana Supreme Court’s judgment and hold that a state may not exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when none of the defendant’s contacts with the state caused the plaintiff’s claims.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be reversed.
