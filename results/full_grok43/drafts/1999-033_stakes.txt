The Equal Protection Clause secures to all persons within a State’s jurisdiction “the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has repeatedly described the Clause as a prohibition on differential treatment “whether occasioned by express terms of a statute or by its improper execution through duly constituted agents.” Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County, 260 U.S. 441, 445 (1923). That prohibition, however, has always been understood to require identification of a class or group whose members are treated differently from others similarly situated. See, e.g., Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) (segregation by race denies equal protection because it treats one racial group differently from another).

A plaintiff who alleges only that government action singled her out for arbitrary or irrational reasons, without alleging membership in any class or group, states no claim under the Clause. The requirement of class-based differentiation is not a pleading technicality; it is the substantive distinction between equal-protection violations and ordinary torts or due-process claims. See Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) (“The unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination.”). Purposeful discrimination, in turn, has been defined by reference to protected classes or suspect classifications. See Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976).

Recognizing a “class of one” action would convert the Clause into “a font of tort law,” a result this Court has consistently rejected. See Paul v. Davis, 424 U.S. 693, 701 (1976). Every discretionary decision by a local official—whether to grant a permit, connect a water line, or enforce an ordinance—could then be relitigated in federal court simply by alleging that similarly situated individuals received more favorable treatment. Such a rule would federalize routine land-use and municipal disputes without any textual or historical warrant in the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary holding—that a plaintiff may prevail by showing only that state action was “motivated solely by a spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective”—eliminates the class element altogether. That formulation finds no support in this Court’s precedents and would render the Equal Protection Clause indistinguishable from substantive due process or state-law tort claims. Because respondent Olech alleged neither membership in a protected class nor any class-based animus, the complaint fails to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause.
