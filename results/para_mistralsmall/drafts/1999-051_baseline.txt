**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that Carpenter’s procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could excuse the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. This Court has repeatedly emphasized that “[a]n ineffective-assistance claim may constitute cause for a procedural default, but only if the underlying constitutional claim is itself meritorious.” *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 623 (1998). The Court has never suggested that an ineffective-assistance claim—even if meritorious—can excuse a procedural default when the ineffective-assistance claim itself is procedurally defaulted. To the contrary, the Court has made clear that “[t]he cause requirement is not satisfied where the claim of cause is itself procedurally defaulted.” *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 492 (1986).

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary rule would eviscerate the procedural-default doctrine by allowing prisoners to bootstrap a defaulted claim into cause for another default. This Court has rejected similar attempts to circumvent procedural rules. See *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991) (“[I]f a State’s procedural framework is adequate to protect a federal right, a state prisoner may not obtain federal habeas corpus review of a constitutional claim that the state court declined to hear because the prisoner failed to comply with that framework.”). The Sixth Circuit’s approach would permit prisoners to “manufacture” cause by defaulting their ineffective-assistance claims, thereby undermining the finality of state court judgments. See *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 623.

**II. Even if an ineffective-assistance claim were not defaulted, it cannot excuse the default of a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.**

The Sixth Circuit further erred in holding that an ineffective-assistance claim could excuse the default of a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, regardless of whether the ineffective-assistance claim was defaulted. This Court has never recognized such a broad rule. To the contrary, the Court has limited the availability of cause to situations where the ineffective-assistance claim is “‘so novel’ that the prisoner could not reasonably have been expected to raise it in the state courts.” *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 623 (quoting *Reed v. Ross*, 468 U.S. 1, 16 (1984)). A sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim is neither novel nor unforeseeable; it is a garden-variety constitutional claim that prisoners routinely raise in state and federal habeas proceedings. See *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 130 (1982) (noting that “most constitutional claims” are not so novel as to excuse procedural default).

Moreover, the Court has cautioned that “[t]he mere fact that a claim was procedurally defaulted does not mean that it is without merit.” *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 622. A sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim is not inherently meritorious; it must survive the deferential review applied to such claims. See *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979) (“[T]he relevant question is whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.”). The Sixth Circuit’s rule would allow prisoners to bypass procedural rules simply by alleging ineffective assistance, even where the underlying claim is frivolous or meritless.

**III. The Sixth Circuit’s holding conflicts with this Court’s precedents governing procedural default and ineffective assistance.**

The Sixth Circuit’s decision is irreconcilable with this Court’s precedents governing procedural default and ineffective assistance. In *Carrier*, the Court held that “[t]he cause requirement is not satisfied where the claim of cause is itself procedurally defaulted.” 477 U.S. at 492. The Court has also made clear that “[a]n ineffective-assistance claim may constitute cause for a procedural default, but only if the underlying constitutional claim is itself meritorious.” *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 623. The Sixth Circuit’s rule—allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse the default of another claim—directly contravenes these holdings.

The Sixth Circuit’s approach also conflicts with this Court’s admonition that “[t]he doctrine of procedural default ensures that federal habeas review is not used as a substitute for appeal.” *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 730. By permitting prisoners to bootstrap defaulted claims into cause for other defaults, the Sixth Circuit’s rule would transform habeas corpus into an end-run around state procedural rules. This Court has repeatedly rejected such attempts to circumvent procedural default. See *id.* at 731 (“[A] federal habeas court may not address a constitutional claim that the state court refused to hear because the petitioner failed to comply with a state procedural rule.”).

**Conclusion**

The Sixth Circuit’s holding is contrary to this Court’s precedents governing procedural default and ineffective assistance. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim, and even if it were not defaulted, it cannot excuse the default of a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. This Court should reverse the judgment below.
