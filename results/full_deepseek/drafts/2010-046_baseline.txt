## ARGUMENT

The three-judge court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population to 137.5% of design capacity fully complies with the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA), 18 U.S.C. § 3626. The PLRA permits prisoner release orders when, as here, the court finds by clear and convincing evidence that overcrowding is the primary cause of constitutional violations and that no other relief will remedy them. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The district court made both findings after extensive proceedings, and those findings are entitled to substantial deference.

### I. The Order Satisfies the PLRA’s Requirements for Prisoner Release Orders

**A. The court found by clear and convincing evidence that overcrowding is the primary cause of Eighth Amendment violations.**

The three-judge court conducted a 14-day trial, heard testimony from dozens of witnesses, and reviewed thousands of pages of evidence. It found that “overcrowding is the primary cause of the unconstitutional conditions” in California’s prisons. *Coleman v. Schwarzenegger*, No. CIV S-90-0520 LKK JFM P, 2009 WL 2430820, at *1 (E.D. Cal. Aug. 4, 2009). This finding was supported by expert testimony and undisputed evidence that California’s prisons operated at nearly 200% of design capacity, leading to inadequate medical and mental health care. The PLRA requires only that the court “find[] by clear and convincing evidence that crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i). The court made that finding, and it is not clearly erroneous. *See Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985) (findings of fact reviewed for clear error).

**B. The court found that no other relief would remedy the violations.**

The PLRA also requires a finding that “no other relief will remedy the violation of the Federal right.” § 3626(a)(3)(E)(ii). The three-judge court found that “no other relief” short of a population cap would remedy the systemic deficiencies. *Coleman*, 2009 WL 2430820, at *1. This finding was based on years of failed remedial efforts, including consent decrees, court-appointed receivers, and numerous orders that California failed to implement. The State’s own experts conceded that without population reduction, constitutional care was impossible. The PLRA does not require courts to exhaust every conceivable alternative before ordering a population cap; it requires only a finding that “no other relief” will work. That finding was made and is supported by the record.

**C. The order is narrowly drawn and the least intrusive means.**

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be “narrowly drawn, extend[] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and [be] the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order satisfies this standard. The 137.5% cap is a modest reduction from the current overcrowding and was chosen because it is the level at which the State’s own experts testified constitutional care could be provided. The order gives the State two years to comply, allows for exceptions, and does not require the release of any particular inmate. It is far less intrusive than the alternatives, such as a complete shutdown of the prison system or a blanket release order. *See Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978) (approving population limits as a remedy for unconstitutional conditions).

### II. The PLRA Does Not Prohibit Necessary Remedial Orders

The State argues that the PLRA bars any order that requires the release of prisoners, but the statute expressly contemplates such orders. Section 3626(a)(3) provides a specific mechanism for prisoner release orders, subject to the findings described above. The PLRA was enacted to limit frivolous prison litigation, not to prevent courts from remedying ongoing constitutional violations. *See Miller v. French*, 530 U.S. 327, 340 (2000) (PLRA “does not disturb the court’s authority to order prospective relief” for constitutional violations). The State’s interpretation would render the PLRA’s release-order provision a nullity and would leave federal courts powerless to address the most egregious prison conditions. That cannot be what Congress intended.

### III. The State’s Interpretation Would Allow Ongoing Constitutional Violations

The Eighth Amendment requires that prisons provide adequate medical care. *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 103 (1976). When a state fails to do so, federal courts have both the authority and the duty to order effective relief. *See Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 511 (2011) (“Courts may not allow constitutional violations to continue simply because a remedy would involve intrusion into the realm of prison administration.”). The State’s argument that the PLRA prohibits any order that reduces the prison population would effectively immunize California from judicial enforcement of the Eighth Amendment. The PLRA does not strip courts of their core equitable powers. *See id.* at 538 (Kennedy, J., concurring) (“The PLRA does not, however, displace the courts’ traditional equitable authority to remedy constitutional violations.”).

The three-judge court acted within its authority under the PLRA and the Constitution. Its order is supported by clear and convincing evidence, is narrowly drawn, and is the least intrusive means to remedy the ongoing constitutional violations. This Court should affirm.
