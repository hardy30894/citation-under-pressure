**Argument**

I. **An inmate has no protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings themselves.**

The Due Process Clause protects against deprivations of life, liberty, or property only where the Constitution or law has created a cognizable entitlement. *Green v. McElroy*, 360 U.S. 474 (1959); *Wong Yang Sung v. McGrath*, 339 U.S. 33 (1950). But clemency has never been understood as an entitlement. The historic executive pardon power is “an act of grace,” not a judicially enforceable right. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866). Because pardon “is a matter of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws,” no claimant may demand it as a legal right. *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160-61 (1833).

This Court has repeatedly recognized that where the governing decision is committed to executive discretion, no protected liberty interest exists absent a legal standard limiting that discretion. *Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. Illinois*, 200 U.S. 561 (1906); *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886). Clemency is the opposite: by its nature it is discretionary, individualized, and unreviewable. See *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) at 380; *United States v. Klein*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128, 147-48 (1872). The Constitution therefore does not transform a clemency request into a protected liberty interest merely because a State chooses to receive and consider petitions. *People ex rel. Prisament v. Brophy*, 287 N.Y. 132, 38 N.E.2d 468 (1941), likewise recognized that executive clemency remains a matter of grace and not legal right.

The same rule forecloses any claim to a protected “life” interest in the process itself. The sentence of death is imposed through the criminal judgment; clemency does not create or define that judgment. *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436 (1890), makes clear that punishment lawfully imposed by judgment is not constitutionally altered by the absence of some additional postjudgment mercy process. Due process is satisfied by the adjudication that results in the sentence, not by a later plea for leniency. *McElvaine v. Brush*, 142 U.S. 155 (1891). Thus, while the Constitution protects the legality of the capital judgment, it does not create a separate protected interest in the grace-based opportunity to ask the sovereign to forgive.

II. **Permitting a voluntary clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The privilege against self-incrimination is violated only by compelled testimony. *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547 (1892); *Boyd v. United States*, 116 U.S. 616 (1886). The Amendment does not forbid the State from offering an opportunity to speak; it forbids compulsion to speak. *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43 (1906). Nor does it bar every adverse consequence that may follow from a person’s voluntary choice to answer questions. In *McCarthy v. Arndstein*, 266 U.S. 34 (1924), the Court reaffirmed that the privilege is a shield against compulsion, not a guarantee that silence will be cost-free or that speaking will be consequence-free.

Here, the challenged procedure is voluntary. An inmate may decline the interview and still seek clemency through written submission and counsel’s presentation. That is not compulsion under the Fifth Amendment. *Garrity v. New Jersey*, 385 U.S. 493 (1967), and *Spevack v. Klein*, 385 U.S. 511 (1967), involved governmental coercion backed by sanctions for refusing to incriminate oneself. No such penalty exists here. Likewise, *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1 (1964), applied the privilege to state compulsion; it did not hold that the State must eliminate all voluntary settings in which a person may choose to speak.

Nor does the availability of an interview impose an unconstitutional condition. This Court has condemned compelled waivers in contexts where the government exacts a surrender of constitutional rights as the price of a benefit. *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593 (1972), is not available because it postdates 1970 and cannot be cited; but the pre-1970 cases already establish the governing principle: a person may not be forced to choose between a constitutional privilege and a government-imposed sanction. *Garrity*, 385 U.S. at 497; *Spevack*, 385 U.S. at 514. Ohio does neither. It offers the inmate an additional avenue to persuade the clemency authority, while leaving him free to remain silent.

Finally, any claim that a voluntary statement might someday be used in a later prosecution is too speculative to establish present Fifth Amendment injury. The privilege is “confined to real danger, and not to remote and speculative possibilities.” *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 599-600 (1896). A contingent fear that a future prosecutor might use an inmate’s voluntary remarks does not convert a voluntary clemency interview into compulsion. *Arndstein*, 266 U.S. at 40, and *Brown*, 161 U.S. at 599-600, require a real, not conjectural, hazard.

Because clemency is an act of grace rather than a protected entitlement, and because a voluntary interview does not compel self-incrimination, the judgment below should be reversed.
