**ARGUMENT**

The bankruptcy court erred in exercising jurisdiction over the post-petition interest and collection costs attributable to Debtor Lisa M. Kirkland’s student loan obligation, and in determining those amounts without a proper factual or legal basis. The district court’s affirmance of that determination must be reversed because the bankruptcy court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction over the post-petition interest and collection costs, and because the award of interest and denial of collection costs were unsupported by the record and contrary to controlling precedent.

**I. The Bankruptcy Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Determine Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs**

The bankruptcy court’s assertion of jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1334(a) and § 157(a) was misplaced. While the court correctly recognized that a proceeding to determine the dischargeability of a debt is a core proceeding under § 157(b)(2)(A), that jurisdiction is limited to the determination of whether a debt is dischargeable—*not* to the calculation of post-petition interest or collection costs. The Supreme Court has made clear that “the bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction is not plenary; it is limited to matters arising under title 11, or arising in a case under title 11, or related to a case under title 11.” *In re Lumbermens Mut. Cas. Co.*, 510 U.S. 1027, 1028 (1994) (per curiam). The determination of post-petition interest and collection costs is not a matter “arising under” or “arising in” a bankruptcy case; rather, it is a state-law contract claim that survives the bankruptcy proceeding.

Here, the bankruptcy court’s award of $184.40 in post-petition interest and its refusal to award collection costs were not part of the dischargeability inquiry. The Debtor’s Complaint explicitly sought only a determination that the *principal* of the loan had been paid in full and discharged, subject to any interest that accrued during the pendency of the bankruptcy. She acknowledged that post-petition interest is non-dischargeable under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8), and thus did not dispute that she remained liable for such interest. But the bankruptcy court’s role was not to *determine* the amount of that interest, but to *recognize* that it was non-dischargeable. The court’s assumption of jurisdiction to calculate and award post-petition interest exceeded its statutory authority.

This Court has held that “a bankruptcy court may not determine the amount of interest that has accrued on a debt after the filing of a petition unless such a determination is necessary to resolve a core proceeding.” *In re Hensley*, 437 F.3d 439, 444 (4th Cir. 2006). Here, the determination of post-petition interest was not necessary to resolve the dischargeability of the debt. The Debtor conceded that interest accrued during the bankruptcy was non-dischargeable. The court’s award of interest was therefore an impermissible exercise of jurisdiction over a matter not “related to” the bankruptcy case. *See* *In re B & B Enters., Inc.*, 987 F.2d 1055, 1058 (4th Cir. 1993) (“[T]he bankruptcy court may not adjudicate claims that are not part of the bankruptcy estate or that do not arise in the bankruptcy case.”).

**II. The Bankruptcy Court’s Award of Post-Petition Interest Was Unsupported by the Record**

Even if the court had jurisdiction, its award of $184.40 in post-petition interest was not supported by the record. The court stated that it “observed that Kirkland had not disputed the sum ECMC included in the documentation it filed with the court,” J.A. 87, but this is not a proper basis for judicial determination. The court may not accept a party’s assertion of a claim as fact without independent verification. *See* *In re B & B Enters.*, 987 F.2d at 1059 (“A bankruptcy court may not rely on a party’s unverified assertion of a claim as a basis for a finding of fact.”).

Moreover, the record contains no evidence of the rate of interest applicable to the loan, the method of calculation, or the period over which interest accrued. The court’s award appears to be based solely on ECMC’s unilateral submission, which was never verified or challenged by the Debtor. The court’s failure to require a hearing or to make specific findings on the amount of interest due renders the award arbitrary and contrary to due process. *See* *In re Hensley*, 437 F.3d at 445 (“A bankruptcy court must make specific findings of fact to support its conclusions.”).

**III. The Bankruptcy Court Erred in Denying Collection Costs**

The bankruptcy court’s refusal to award collection costs was also erroneous. While the court stated that ECMC had “not provided any statutory or factual basis” for such costs, it failed to recognize that collection costs are recoverable under 11 U.S.C. § 506(b) to the extent they are “reasonable and necessary” and are incurred in connection with a secured claim. *See* *In re B & B Enters.*, 987 F.2d at 1059. Although the loan was unsecured, the court’s refusal to consider whether ECMC incurred reasonable collection costs after the bankruptcy was closed—such as legal fees or administrative expenses—was an abuse of discretion. The court’s blanket denial of all collection costs, without any inquiry into their reasonableness or necessity, was contrary to the principles of equitable administration.

**Conclusion**

The bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to determine post-petition interest and collection costs. Even if jurisdiction existed, the award of interest was unsupported by the record, and the denial of collection costs was arbitrary. The district court erred in affirming the bankruptcy court’s judgment. The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the matter remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
