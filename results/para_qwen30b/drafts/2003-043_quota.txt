**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government must assess individual partners for a tax debt in order to collect from them after the partnership has been assessed—is governed by clear statutory text, long-standing precedent, and the fundamental principle that no taxpayer may be subjected to enforcement without a valid assessment. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year window under § 6501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code bars collection against them, even if the partnership was timely assessed. This result is compelled by the plain language of the Code, binding circuit precedent, and the Supreme Court’s insistence on procedural due process in tax matters.

**I. The Statute of Limitations Is Not Extended to Individual Partners by an Assessment Against the Partnership**

Congress has made it unequivocally clear that the statute of limitations for assessing taxes applies separately to each “taxpayer.” Under § 6501(a), the IRS must assess a tax “within 3 years after the date the return was filed.” A “taxpayer” is defined as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes both individuals and partnerships. 26 U.S.C. §§ 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Because each partner is a distinct “person” and “taxpayer,” the statute of limitations runs independently for each. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (“[T]he assessment at issue named only the partnership and ‘an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers.’”); *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561, 562 (7th Cir. 1995) (assessments against both partnership and partners extended limitations period for both).

Here, the IRS assessed the partnership but failed to assess the individual partners within the three-year window. No such assessment was ever made. Thus, the three-year limitation period for each partner has expired. *See United States v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960) (affirming district court that partner could not recover refund because he had been assessed neither individually nor jointly). The IRS cannot circumvent this bar by relying on a partnership assessment alone. *See United States v. W. Prods., Ltd.*, 168 F.Supp.2d 84, 91 (S.D.N.Y. 2001) (government may collect withholding taxes from general partner only where there is either an individual assessment or judgment against the partner).

**II. An Assessment Must Identify the Taxpayer; It Cannot Be Constructive**

Section 6203 requires that an assessment be made “by recording the liability of the taxpayer in the office of the Secretary.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The regulation implementing this provision mandates that the assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. This requirement is not merely formalistic—it ensures that the taxpayer knows of the assessment and can defend against it. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (“[T]he assessment must identify the taxpayer.”). In *Coson*, the court held that a lien against a partner’s property was invalid because the assessment named only the partnership and “an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers.” Id. at 458. The court emphasized that “no lien exists without such an assessment” against the individual. Id. at 458.

This principle applies with equal force here. The IRS never identified the Gallettis or Briguglios as taxpayers in any assessment. Their names were not recorded in the Secretary’s office. Therefore, no valid assessment ever occurred as to them. *See United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562 (assessments against both partnership and partners were required to extend limitations period to partners). Without a valid assessment, the IRS lacks the legal basis to proceed against the partners.

**III. State Law Does Not Create a Right to Collect Without Judicial Action**

The IRS argues that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, so a claim against the partnership should suffice. But California law does not permit automatic collection from a partner’s personal assets. *Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)* provides: “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” *See also* 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001) (“[Although a partner need not be named individually in an action against a partnership, the partner must be individually named and served in the action or in a later suit, and judgment entered against that partner, in order to reach the partner’s personal assets.”).

Thus, even under state law, the IRS must obtain a separate judgment against each partner before it may levy on their personal property. *See United States v. W. Prods., Ltd.*, 168 F.Supp.2d at 91 (holding that government must obtain judgment against partner to collect from personal assets). The IRS did not do so. And because the three-year statute of limitations has run, it may no longer bring such an action. *See United States v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d at 910 (partner not entitled to refund because assessment did not name him, and no action was brought).

**IV. The Bankruptcy Court Properly Disallowed the Claims**

Under § 502(b)(1) of the Bankruptcy Code, a claim may be disallowed if it is “unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor, under applicable law.” The IRS’s claim is unenforceable because it rests on no valid assessment and no judgment against the partners. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (lien invalid absent individual assessment). The bankruptcy court did not err in disallowing the claims. *See United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562–63 (statute of limitations barred collection against partners despite timely assessment against partnership).
