**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court’s Arbitrary Denial of an Expert Fingerprint Witness and Continuance Violated Mr. Winbush’s Right to Due Process and a Fair Defense.**

A criminal defendant’s right to present a meaningful defense is a fundamental element of due process. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 68 (1932). When the government employs expert testimony to explain the absence of physical evidence, the defendant must have a fair opportunity to rebut that testimony. The district court’s refusal to allow Mr. Winbush to retain a fingerprint expert—and its concomitant denial of a short continuance—was an abuse of discretion that prejudiced his defense.

**A. The Denial Was Arbitrary and Failed to Consider the Merits of the Request.**

The magistrate judge denied the motion solely because it was filed “on the Friday before trial” in a case “pending for over two years.” This mechanical application of a scheduling deadline ignores the substance of the request. A court may not deny a continuance or expert assistance without weighing the movant’s need against the public interest in prompt trials. *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964) (no mechanical test for denying continuance; court must consider “the circumstances of the case”). Here, the government’s own expert notice (filed October 3, 2007) signaled that the government would call a fingerprint examiner to testify that no latent prints of value were found, and a second expert to explain *why* prints were absent. Mr. Winbush initially agreed to stipulate, but later realized that such stipulation would leave the government’s interpretive testimony unchallenged. The need for a defense expert arose only after the government’s trial strategy became clear. The district court made no inquiry into the reason for the delay, the availability of an expert on short notice, or the possibility of a brief continuance. This was arbitrary. *See Powell*, 287 U.S. at 71 (trial court must ensure “some regard for the decencies of justice”).

**B. The Expert Testimony Was Essential to Counter the Government’s Case.**

The government’s fingerprint expert would testify that the absence of latent prints on the jacket, gun, and drugs was unremarkable. A defense expert could have explained that the complete absence of any prints—including those of the defendant—might indicate that the items had been handled by others, were wiped clean, or were never touched by the defendant. This testimony was directly relevant to the possession charges, especially the firearm and drug counts. The government’s evidence of possession was circumstantial: the jacket was found near the flight path, and the gun and drugs were inside the jacket. But if Mr. Winbush’s fingerprints were absent, a jury could reasonably infer that he did not possess those items. Denying him the means to present that inference violated his right to “be heard in his own defense.” *Chandler v. Fretag*, 348 U.S. 3, 10 (1954) (defendant entitled to “reasonable opportunity to prepare and present his defense”).

**C. The Denial Was Prejudicial.**

The error was not harmless. The jury heard only the government’s expert explanation for the absence of fingerprints. Without a defense expert, the jury could not evaluate whether that absence cut in Mr. Winbush’s favor. The evidence against him was strong, but not airtight. The gun and drugs were found in a jacket he had discarded, but he was not wearing it at arrest. The absence of his prints could have supported a defense that the jacket and its contents belonged to another person. This case is analogous to *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963), where the Court held that the suppression of exculpatory evidence violates due process. Here, the *ability* to present exculpatory evidence was suppressed by the court’s refusal to permit a necessary expert. The prejudice is evident: the jury might have acquitted on the firearm possession charge (18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)) and the possession-with-intent counts, or at least reduced the sentence.

**D. The Denial of a Continuance Was an Abuse of Discretion.**

A trial court’s refusal to grant a continuance is reviewed for abuse of discretion, but discretion is not unlimited. *Ungar*, 376 U.S. at 589. The court must consider the “the diligence of the movant, the probable effect of the continuance, the inconvenience to the court and the parties, and the public interest.” *United States v. Moore*, 362 F.2d 341, 342 (7th Cir. 1966). Mr. Winbush moved for an expert only five days before trial, but the government had disclosed its expert witness strategy a month earlier. The defense initially believed a stipulation would suffice, but once it became clear that the government would offer interpretive testimony, the need for a defense expert became urgent. A short continuance of a few days would have allowed the defense to locate an expert—a task that the magistrate judge’s own comment (that the request was “woefully late”) suggests was not impossible. The inconvenience to the court was minimal, as the trial had not yet begun. The public interest in a fair trial outweighs the interest in rigid adherence to schedule. *See United States v. Taylor*, 338 F.2d 679, 682 (7th Cir. 1964) (reversing conviction where denial of continuance prevented defendant from obtaining needed expert). The district court’s refusal to consider any alternative was arbitrary and violated due process.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of conviction should be reversed, or in the alternative, the sentence vacated, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
