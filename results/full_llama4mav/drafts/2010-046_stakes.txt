ARGUMENT

I. The District Court's Order Requiring California to Reduce Prison Overcrowding Does Not Violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) does not prohibit a court from ordering a state to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions. In Brown v. Plata, the Supreme Court will consider whether the PLRA limits a court's authority to order prospective relief in the form of reducing a state's prison population. However, the Ninth Circuit has already established that a court-ordered reduction in prison population is permissible under the PLRA if it is narrowly drawn and the least intrusive means to remedy the constitutional violation. See Gilmore v. California, 220 F.3d 987, 999 (9th Cir. 2000) (holding that the PLRA "does not categorically prohibit population limits as a form of prospective relief").

The district court's order in this case is narrowly tailored to address the unconstitutional conditions caused by overcrowding in California's prisons. The court's order is based on a thorough examination of the evidence and a determination that overcrowding is the "primary cause" of the constitutional violations. As the Supreme Court has held, a court's order must be "narrowly drawn, extend no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and be the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A); see also Brown v. Plata, 131 S. Ct. 1910, 1944 (2011) (affirming a three-judge court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions).

II. The District Court's Order Is a Proper Exercise of Its Authority to Remedy Unconstitutional Conditions

The district court's order is a proper exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. The court's order is based on a finding that the conditions in the prisons violate the Eighth Amendment, and that overcrowding is the primary cause of those violations. As the Supreme Court has held, a court has the authority to order prospective relief to remedy unconstitutional conditions, including reducing a state's prison population. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978) (affirming a district court's order requiring a state to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions).

The Ninth Circuit has also recognized that a court has the authority to order a state to take specific actions to remedy unconstitutional conditions. See Armstrong v. Schwarzenegger, 622 F.3d 1058, 1070 (9th Cir. 2010) (holding that a district court's order requiring a state to provide adequate medical care to prisoners was a proper exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions).

In this case, the district court's order is a necessary and narrowly tailored response to the unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. The court's order is designed to remedy the constitutional violations caused by overcrowding, and is the least intrusive means of doing so. Therefore, the order does not violate the PLRA and is a proper exercise of the court's authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions.

In sum, the district court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population is a permissible exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions, and does not violate the PLRA. The order is narrowly tailored to address the constitutional violations caused by overcrowding, and is the least intrusive means of remedying those violations.
