# ARGUMENT

## The Ineffective Assistance of Appellate Counsel That Caused Respondent's Procedural Default of His Sufficiency Claim Need Not Independently Survive an Exhaustion or Default Analysis

### A. The Cause-and-Prejudice Standard Asks Only Whether an External Impediment Prevented Compliance With State Procedure

This Court has long held that a state prisoner who has procedurally defaulted a federal claim may nonetheless obtain habeas review by showing "cause" for the default and "prejudice" resulting therefrom. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). In *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), this Court explained that cause requires "some objective factor external to the defense" that "impeded counsel's efforts to comply with the State's procedural rule." *Id.* at 488. The Court further held that constitutionally ineffective assistance of counsel satisfies this standard because "[a]ttorney error that constitutes ineffective assistance of counsel is cause" under the *Sykes* framework. *Id.* at 488.

The inquiry *Carrier* prescribes is factual and historical: did counsel's performance fall below the *Strickland* standard, and did that failure cause the default? *See Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984). Nothing in *Carrier* suggests that this historical fact—counsel's ineffectiveness—evaporates because the state courts, applying an unrelated procedural time bar, declined to adjudicate a freestanding claim resting on the same facts. The ineffectiveness occurred when appellate counsel failed to raise the sufficiency claim on direct appeal; that objective, external impediment to Carpenter's compliance with state procedure does not depend on the timing of a subsequent Rule 26(B) application.

### B. Respondent Satisfied the Exhaustion Requirement by Fairly Presenting the Ineffective-Assistance Claim to the State Courts

Federal habeas relief ordinarily requires that a claim first be exhausted in state court. *Rose v. Lundy*, 455 U.S. 509, 515 (1982). Carpenter did exactly what this Court's exhaustion doctrine demands: represented by new counsel, he presented his ineffective-assistance-of-appellate-counsel claim to the Ohio Court of Appeals through an application to reopen his direct appeal, and then to the Ohio Supreme Court. The state courts had a full and fair opportunity to correct the constitutional violation; they declined to do so only on timeliness grounds. Exhaustion does not require that the state court reach the merits—only that the prisoner give the state courts "the opportunity to pass upon and correct alleged violations of its prisoners' federal rights." *Picard v. Connor*, 404 U.S. 270, 275 (1971) (internal quotation marks omitted). That opportunity was afforded here.

### C. Requiring an Independent Cause-and-Prejudice Showing for the Cause Claim Itself Would Create an Unworkable Regress Inconsistent With Comity Principles

Petitioner's position—that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim can never serve as cause unless it, too, survives a cause-and-prejudice analysis—would generate an infinite regress. If Carpenter must show cause for defaulting his ineffective-assistance claim, and that showing itself must overcome a further default, habeas review could recede indefinitely, contrary to this Court's admonition that procedural-default rules serve "comity" and orderly federal-state relations, not endless forfeiture. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 730-731 (1991) (explaining that the doctrine "is grounded in concerns of comity and federalism").

This Court has been careful to calibrate procedural bars so that they do not foreclose review of claims that a habeas petitioner, through no fault of his own, was unable to raise. In *Reed v. Ross*, 468 U.S. 1 (1984), the Court recognized that cause exists where "a constitutional claim is so novel that its legal basis is not reasonably available to counsel." *Id.* at 16. The same solicitude for petitioners disabled by circumstances beyond their control—here, the very ineffectiveness of the attorney tasked with raising claims—counsels against erecting a second procedural wall in front of the first.

Moreover, this Court in *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107 (1982), reaffirmed that the cause standard exists to accommodate "the important interest in finality" while still protecting against "a miscarriage of justice." *Id.* at 135. Treating attorney ineffectiveness as cause without demanding a second layer of exhaustion accommodates both interests: it respects Ohio's timeliness rule as the operative bar on the sufficiency claim while ensuring that a defendant is not made to suffer for an error he had no capacity to prevent. As the Court observed in *Murray v. Carrier*, "[s]o long as a defendant is represented by counsel whose performance is not constitutionally ineffective under the standard established in *Strickland*," the defendant bears the risk of attorney error; but where counsel's performance *is* constitutionally deficient, "the constitutional error of counsel taints the trial" and the resulting default. 477 U.S. at 488.

### D. Respondent Established Both Cause and Prejudice

The District Court found, and the Court of Appeals affirmed, that appellate counsel's failure to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence was constitutionally deficient under *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 687-88, and that but for this omission there is a reasonable probability the outcome of the appeal would have differed. That finding satisfies *Carrier*'s cause requirement and *Sykes*'s prejudice requirement together, entitling Carpenter to review of his sufficiency claim.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
