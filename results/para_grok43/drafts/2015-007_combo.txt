The Sixth Amendment secures the right to jury trial in criminal prosecutions, but this guarantee has never extended to require unanimous jury findings on every sentencing factor or an absolute bar to execution. As the Court explained in *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930), the constitutional provision preserves “the substance of the common-law right” without mandating procedural innovations unknown at common law. *Sparf v. United States*, 156 U.S. 51, 102 (1895), likewise confirms that the jury’s role is to decide guilt or innocence; questions of punishment remain for the court once that verdict is returned.

Historical practice reinforces this division. In *Thompson v. Utah*, 170 U.S. 343, 349 (1898), the Court traced the jury right to the common-law requirement of twelve jurors for conviction, yet observed that sentencing determinations were never part of that verdict. *Callan v. Wilson*, 127 U.S. 540, 549 (1888), similarly limited the guarantee to “the trial of the issue of guilt,” leaving post-verdict matters to judicial discretion. *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2, 123 (1866), underscored that the jury right attaches to the determination of criminal liability, not to every collateral factual inquiry that might affect the sentence.

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishments, incorporated against the States through the Due Process Clause, *see Pointer v. Texas*, 380 U.S. 400, 406 (1965); *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 342 (1963), likewise imposes no requirement of jury unanimity or jury resolution of mental-retardation claims. *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947), held that the Clause forbids only punishments that “involve torture or a lingering death,” a standard measured by longstanding usage rather than modern procedural mandates. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958), reiterated that the Clause draws its meaning from “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society,” yet those standards have never demanded jury unanimity for capital sentencing. *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962), confirmed that the Clause addresses the nature of the punishment itself, not the identity of the decision-maker.

Florida’s scheme, which permits a non-unanimous jury recommendation followed by independent judicial sentencing, therefore accords with the historical understanding preserved by the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The absence of a jury finding on mental retardation as an absolute bar raises no constitutional objection, for that determination has always been treated as a post-conviction inquiry committed to the court. *In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. 257, 273 (1948), recognized that certain factual questions arising after conviction fall outside the jury-trial guarantee. The same principle governs here.
