**ARGUMENT**

**I. CONGRESS LACKED CONSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY TO ENACT 18 U.S.C. § 1951**

Appellant challenges the constitutionality of § 1951 of Title 18, the Hobbs Act, arguing that Congress exceeded its enumerated powers in enacting the statute. The Hobbs Act criminalizes interference with interstate commerce by robbery or extortion. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 1951(a). Appellant contends that the statute impermissibly regulates purely intrastate activity that has no substantial effect on interstate commerce.

This Court has consistently upheld the Hobbs Act against constitutional challenges. In *United States v. Sullivan*, 505 F.3d 1170, 1175 (10th Cir. 2007), this Court reaffirmed that the Hobbs Act is a valid exercise of Congress’s commerce power under the Necessary and Proper Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3, and the Commerce Clause. The Supreme Court has likewise upheld the Hobbs Act’s constitutionality. *See, e.g., Stirone v. United States*, 361 U.S. 212, 215 (1960) (holding that the Hobbs Act “reaches any interference with interstate commerce, whatever the means”); *United States v. Culbert*, 435 U.S. 371, 373 (1978) (per curiam) (upholding the Hobbs Act as a valid exercise of congressional power).

Appellant’s argument that the robbery at issue had no substantial effect on interstate commerce is foreclosed by Supreme Court precedent. In *Perez v. United States*, 402 U.S. 146, 150 (1971), the Court held that Congress may regulate local activities that, in the aggregate, substantially affect interstate commerce. The Court explained that Congress need not prove that a particular instance of robbery had a substantial effect on interstate commerce; rather, it need only demonstrate that the class of activities regulated by the Hobbs Act substantially affects interstate commerce. *Id.* at 154-55.

Here, the government presented evidence that Mr. Goodcents, the victimized business, purchased goods from out-of-state suppliers and that the robbery caused economic harm to the business. *See* R. Vol. III at 120-21. This evidence is sufficient to establish that the robbery had a substantial effect on interstate commerce. *See United States v. Ramirez*, 710 F.2d 579, 583 (9th Cir. 1983) (holding that a robbery of a local business that purchased goods from out-of-state suppliers substantially affected interstate commerce).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that Congress may regulate local criminal activity under the Commerce Clause if the activity is economic in nature and substantially affects interstate commerce. *See Gonzales v. Raich*, 545 U.S. 1, 16-17 (2005). The Hobbs Act regulates robbery, an economic crime that, in the aggregate, substantially affects interstate commerce. *See Sullivan*, 505 F.3d at 1175. Accordingly, § 1951 is a valid exercise of Congress’s commerce power.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERROREOUSLY REFUSED TO ALLOW APPELLANT TO SUBMIT A TARDY ALIBI DEFENSE**

Appellant contends that the district court abused its discretion in precluding his tardy alibi defense. The district court excluded the alibi evidence after appellant failed to comply with the court’s scheduling order requiring disclosure of alibi witnesses at least ten days before trial. *See* Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1(a).

This Court has held that district courts have broad discretion to enforce scheduling orders and preclude untimely alibi disclosures. *See United States v. Cook*, 949 F.2d 1250, 1253 (10th Cir. 1991). The Supreme Court has likewise recognized that district courts may exclude untimely alibi evidence to prevent trial by ambush. *See Taylor v. Illinois*, 484 U.S. 400, 414-15 (1988). The Court explained that the Sixth Amendment does not guarantee a right to present untimely alibi evidence. *Id.* at 410.

Here, the district court did not abuse its discretion in excluding the alibi evidence. Appellant failed to comply with the court’s scheduling order, and the government would have been prejudiced by the late disclosure of the alibi witnesses. *See Cook*, 949 F.2d at 1253. Accordingly, the district court’s exclusion of the alibi evidence was proper.
