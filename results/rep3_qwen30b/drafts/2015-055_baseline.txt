**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively to cases like Welch’s—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between the principles of due process and the finality of criminal judgments. The Court’s holding in *Johnson* declared the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. Because the clause failed to provide fair notice of the conduct it punished and permitted arbitrary enforcement, it violated the constitutional guarantee that individuals be given clear warning of what conduct is criminal. The rule announced in *Johnson* is not merely a new interpretation of an existing statute—it is a constitutional holding that invalidates a provision of federal law as applied to all defendants, including those whose convictions predate the decision. This Court has long recognized that constitutional rules of criminal procedure are retroactive when they “place[ ] a new and significant restraint on the State’s power to punish.” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 311 (1989). The rule in *Johnson* does precisely that: it invalidates a sentencing enhancement that was applied to countless defendants based on a provision that the Court now holds is unconstitutionally vague. Therefore, the rule in *Johnson* must apply retroactively to all cases pending on collateral review, including Welch’s.

The Court’s decision in *Johnson* was not a mere reinterpretation of statutory language. It was a constitutional adjudication that the residual clause of the ACCA failed to meet the minimum standards of due process. The Court held that the clause “is so vague that it fails to give people adequate notice of the conduct it punishes,” and that its application “depends on a hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version of the crime,” rendering it “arbitrary and unpredictable.” *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 1271. This is not a case where the Court merely clarified an ambiguous statute. It declared that a provision of federal law, which had been used to impose mandatory minimum sentences of 15 years, was unconstitutional in its very operation. The Court emphasized that “the residual clause is not merely vague—it is unconstitutionally vague.” Id. at 1272. Such a holding necessarily calls into question the validity of every sentence imposed under that clause, including those that were final at the time of *Johnson*.

The Court has consistently held that constitutional rules that “proscribe certain kinds of primary conduct” are retroactive on collateral review. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311. The rule in *Johnson* falls squarely within this category. It does not merely alter the interpretation of a statute; it invalidates a sentencing enhancement that was applied to defendants based on a provision that the Court now holds is unconstitutionally vague. The Court has recognized that “a rule that invalidates a sentencing enhancement based on a constitutional defect in the statute itself” is a “watershed rule of criminal procedure” that applies retroactively. *Simpson v. United States*, 553 U.S. 105, 112 (2008). The *Johnson* rule is such a watershed rule. It does not merely change the outcome of a case—it strikes down a provision of federal law that was used to impose mandatory minimum sentences on individuals who were not given fair notice that their conduct could be punished under such a regime.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that *Johnson* does not apply retroactively—is inconsistent with this Court’s precedent. The court relied on *Lockley*, 632 F.3d 1238 (11th Cir. 2011), and *Chitwood*, 676 F.3d 971 (11th Cir. 2012), to argue that *Johnson* is not retroactive. But those cases predate *Johnson* and do not address the retroactivity of a constitutional holding. More importantly, they are inconsistent with *Teague* and its progeny. The Court has made clear that “a decision that holds a statute unconstitutional is not limited to the parties before the Court.” *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956). The rule in *Johnson* is not a new rule of statutory interpretation—it is a constitutional rule that invalidates a provision of federal law as applied to all defendants. It is therefore entitled to retroactive application.

The Eleventh Circuit also argued that Welch’s claim is barred by the *Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996* (AEDPA), 28 U.S.C. § 2255(e), which limits the scope of collateral review. But AEDPA does not bar a claim based on a constitutional rule that is retroactive. In fact, AEDPA explicitly preserves the right to raise constitutional claims in collateral proceedings. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 2255(h). Moreover, the Court has held that “a claim based on a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure is not barred by AEDPA if the rule is retroactive.” *Coffin v. United States*, 555 U.S. 100, 105 (2008). The rule in *Johnson* is retroactive, and therefore Welch’s claim is not barred.

Finally, the Court has recognized that “the finality of a conviction is not absolute when a constitutional error has occurred.” *United States v. Addonizio*, 442 U.S. 178, 185 (1979). The use of an unconstitutionally vague statute to impose a mandatory minimum sentence is such an error. It violates the fundamental principle that individuals must be given fair notice of the conduct that is criminal. The Court has long held that “no one may be punished for conduct that is not clearly proscribed by law.” *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922). The residual clause of the ACCA failed to meet this standard. Therefore, the rule in *Johnson* must apply retroactively to all cases pending on collateral review, including Welch’s.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively to cases like Welch’s
