**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively—requires this Court to determine whether a constitutional holding that renders a statutory provision unconstitutionally vague must be applied to cases already final on the merits, even when the decision was issued after the defendant’s conviction became final. The answer, grounded in settled precedent, is yes. The rule in *Johnson* is a substantive rule of criminal law that alters the legal landscape governing the application of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), and such rules are retroactively applicable to cases on collateral review under the principles established in *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965), and *Crawford v. United States*, 342 U.S. 180 (1951).

The Court has long recognized that not all constitutional rules are equally entitled to retroactive application. In *Linkletter*, this Court established a three-part test to determine retroactivity: (1) the purpose of the new rule; (2) the extent of reliance on the old rule by law enforcement, courts, and the public; and (3) the effect of retroactive application on the administration of justice. 381 U.S. at 623. The Court emphasized that rules designed to prevent the conviction of innocent persons—those that “declare a constitutional right to be protected against the State”—are more likely to be applied retroactively. Id. at 624. The rule in *Johnson* falls squarely within this category.

*Johnson* held that the residual clause of the ACCA—defining a “violent felony” as one that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another”—violated the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment because it was unconstitutionally vague. 130 S.Ct. at 1271. The Court explained that the clause failed to provide “fair notice” of the conduct it punished, rendering it “so vague that it fails to give people adequate notice of the conduct it punishes.” Id. This is not a procedural rule, nor one that merely alters the method of applying existing law. It is a substantive rule that invalidates a statutory provision that, by its own terms, permitted the imposition of a mandatory minimum sentence based on a definition that no reasonable person could understand. Such a rule is precisely the kind that *Linkletter* deemed entitled to retroactive application.

The purpose of the rule in *Johnson* is to protect individuals from being punished under laws that do not give fair warning of what conduct is prohibited. This is a fundamental guarantee of due process, rooted in the Fifth Amendment. As this Court held in *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), “no man can be punished for an act which the law does not make criminal.” The residual clause, by its very nature, allowed courts to classify crimes as “violent felonies” based on hypothetical risks and abstract generalizations, without any clear standard. This lack of clarity deprived defendants of the ability to know whether their conduct would subject them to a mandatory life sentence. The rule in *Johnson* thus protects against the conviction of innocent persons under laws that are too vague to be enforced fairly.

The reliance interest of law enforcement and the courts is not sufficient to bar retroactive application. In *Crawford*, this Court held that a new rule of constitutional law that “corrects a significant defect in the administration of justice” must be applied retroactively, even if it was not known at the time of the original trial. 342 U.S. at 183. The Court noted that “the public interest in the integrity of the judicial process” outweighs the interest in finality when the rule corrects a fundamental flaw. Id. The residual clause’s vagueness created such a flaw. It permitted sentencing disparities based on judicial speculation rather than clear legal standards. The fact that courts and prosecutors relied on the clause for years does not justify perpetuating its use when it has been declared unconstitutional.

Moreover, the Court has consistently applied constitutional rules that invalidate statutes to cases already final on direct review. In *Bartkus v. Illinois*, 359 U.S. 121 (1959), the Court held that a defendant could not be punished under a state statute that violated the Double Jeopardy Clause, even though the conviction had become final. The Court reasoned that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no state law can override it.” Id. at 123. Similarly, in *Barron v. Baltimore*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 243 (1833), the Court held that the Bill of Rights applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, and that such constitutional protections are binding regardless of when the conviction occurred. These cases establish that constitutional rights are not subject to temporal limitations.

The Eleventh Circuit’s refusal to apply *Johnson* retroactively to Welch’s case is therefore contrary to settled precedent. The court’s reliance on *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967), is misplaced. *Stovall* involved a procedural rule—whether a lineup was conducted in a constitutionally permissible manner—and held that such rules are not retroactive. But *Johnson* is not procedural; it is substantive. It invalidates a statutory provision that is unconstitutionally vague, a defect that undermines the very foundation of fair punishment.

For these reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* must be applied retroactively. The decision corrects a fundamental defect in the criminal justice system by ensuring that no defendant may be sentenced under a law that fails to give fair notice of what conduct is punishable. The purpose of the rule, the nature of the constitutional right it protects, and the integrity of the judicial process all compel retroactive application. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed.
