Parker Drilling Management Services, Ltd. respectfully submits that the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) adopts state law only when federal law leaves a true gap in the relevant subject matter, not whenever state law is merely “applicable” and “not inconsistent” in the abstract. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with the statute’s text, structure, or this Court’s OCSLA decisions.

This Court has repeatedly explained that OCSLA’s purpose was to create a body of law governing fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf by borrowing state law only as a surrogate where federal law does not already speak. In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court held that fixed platforms are to be treated like federal enclaves and that the Act was designed to fill the legal vacuum for such installations. Id. at 355, 366. Likewise, in Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473 (1981), the Court described OCSLA as making adjacent state law applicable on the Shelf as federal law. Id. at 480. But that incorporation is not limitless. The statute itself conditions assimilation on state law being “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “not inconsistent” does not eliminate the distinct requirement that the state law be needed because federal law is incomplete. Rather, both conditions operate together: state law is borrowed only to the extent it can function as surrogate federal law in an area not already occupied by federal law.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule improperly reads the federal-gap requirement out of the statute. In Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners’ Mutual Insurance Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), the Fifth Circuit recognized that state law on the OCS is assimilated only when there is a “significant void or gap” in federal law. Id. at 1036. That understanding reflects the basic structure of incorporation statutes: state law fills interstices; it does not displace a federal scheme that Congress has already supplied. This Court’s OCSLA cases follow the same logic. In Rodrigue, the Court emphasized that state law was available because the federal maritime remedy did not apply to fixed platforms. 395 U.S. at 366. In Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), the Court again treated adjacent state law as governing because federal law did not otherwise provide the rule of decision. Id. at 98-100. And in Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043 (5th Cir. 1990), the Fifth Circuit articulated the familiar three-part test requiring that federal law not apply of its own force before adjacent state law is adopted under OCSLA. Id. at 1047. That test has been widely followed because it preserves the Act’s function as a gap-filling statute.

Here, federal law does apply of its own force. Congress enacted the Fair Labor Standards Act as a comprehensive wage-and-hour code, and that statute contains its own minimum wage and overtime standards. 29 U.S.C. §§ 206, 207. The FLSA also includes a savings clause preserving state laws that establish higher wage protections in ordinary domestic settings. 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). But the presence of a savings clause does not convert OCSLA into a vehicle for importing every neighboring state rule onto fixed offshore platforms. The question under OCSLA is not whether California law is generally consistent with the FLSA; it is whether federal law leaves room for California law to be borrowed as surrogate federal law on the Shelf. Where Congress has already legislated comprehensively, there is no gap to fill.

Nor does Louisiana ex rel. Guste v. M/V Testbank, 752 F.2d 1019 (5th Cir. 1985), or other preemption doctrine support respondent. OCSLA incorporation is not ordinary preemption analysis. Congress created a unique choice-of-law regime for the Shelf, and this Court has instructed that fixed platforms are governed by federal law, with adjacent state law borrowed only as surrogate where federal law is silent. Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355, 366; Gulf Offshore, 453 U.S. at 480. The Ninth Circuit’s “applicable and not inconsistent” test collapses that regime into a broad state-law incorporation rule.

The better reading also avoids serious administrability problems. Under the Ninth Circuit’s approach, any state law touching wages, breaks, recordkeeping, penalties, or remedies would apply on the Shelf unless expressly preempted. That would produce a patchwork of potentially divergent offshore rules depending on the adjacent State, even though the Shelf is federal territory governed by federal law. OCSLA was enacted to prevent precisely that uncertainty. Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Department of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19, 26-27 (1988).

Because the FLSA supplies the governing federal rules for wage-and-hour claims, California’s meal-period and related wage provisions cannot be borrowed under OCSLA to create additional federal duties on a fixed platform. The Ninth Circuit’s judgment should therefore be reversed.
