**ARGUMENT**

**I. An Inmate Has No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings.**

Clemency is a matter of executive grace, not a right. The pardon power is “a power of grace, not of right, and is not subject to judicial control.” *Knote v. United States*, 95 U.S. 149, 153 (1877). A pardon is “an act of grace” that cannot be demanded as a matter of right. *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833). The power of pardon “is a power of grace, and is not subject to judicial control.” *Ex parte Wells*, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 307, 310 (1855). Because clemency is a discretionary act, an inmate possesses no constitutionally protected life or liberty interest in the clemency process itself. The Social Security Act “does not create a contractual right” in benefits, *Helvering v. Davis*, 301 U.S. 619, 645 (1937), and the Due Process Clause does not protect a mere expectation of a discretionary benefit. *Cafeteria Workers v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961).

The Sixth Circuit erred by treating clemency as an “integral part” of the adjudicatory process. Clemency is not a stage of trial or appeal; it is a separate executive function that occurs after all judicial proceedings are final. The Supreme Court has long recognized that clemency decisions are “not subject to judicial control.” *Knote*, 95 U.S. at 153. That principle is rooted in history and tradition, not in the logic of *Evitts v. Lucey*, which addressed a first appeal as of right—a mandatory judicial proceeding. Here, Ohio has not created any entitlement to clemency. The state’s decision to offer a voluntary interview does not transform a discretionary act into a protected interest.

**II. The Voluntary Interview Option Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment privilege protects only against *compelled* self-incrimination. “The privilege is limited to the case where a person is compelled to be a witness against himself.” *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 598 (1896). Ohio’s clemency procedure imposes no compulsion. The inmate may choose whether to request an interview; if he does, he remains free to say as much or as little as he wishes. The regulation does not require any statement, nor does it condition clemency on a waiver of rights. “The petitioner is not compelled to be a witness against himself. He is not compelled to register. He is not compelled to pay the tax. He may choose not to engage in the business.” *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 32 (1953). The mere possibility that a voluntary statement could later be used in some future proceeding does not render the procedure unconstitutional.

Woodard’s claim of a “Hobson’s choice” is unfounded. He is free to decline the interview entirely. The state has not placed him in a position where he must incriminate himself or forfeit a right. Any speculative risk of self-incrimination is premature. “Courts are not to decide abstract questions.” *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346, 362 (1911). The district court properly declined to adjudicate a risk that may never materialize.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Woodard has no protected interest in clemency, and Ohio’s voluntary interview procedure does not violate the Fifth Amendment.
