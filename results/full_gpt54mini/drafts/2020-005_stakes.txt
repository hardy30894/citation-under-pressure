**ARGUMENT**

I. **Specific jurisdiction exists when the defendant’s forum conduct is closely related to the plaintiff’s claims, even if the forum conduct did not itself cause the injury.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific jurisdiction when the defendant has “purposefully avail[ed]” itself of the forum and the litigation “arise[s] out of or relate[s] to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316-17 (1945); *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472, 475-76 (1985). The second requirement is disjunctive: claims may “arise out of or relate to” forum contacts, meaning relatedness is not limited to strict causal proof. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of California, San Francisco County*, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017); *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 923-24 (2011).

That understanding is consistent with *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court*, 592 U.S. 351, 358-59, 369-71 (2021), which held that specific jurisdiction was proper in Montana and Minnesota even though the particular vehicles at issue were not designed, manufactured, or first sold in those states. The Court rejected Ford’s argument that “but-for causation” is required, explaining that the plaintiff’s claims need only “relate to” the defendant’s forum activities. *Id.* at 362-64, 371-72. The Court emphasized that Ford had “systematically served a market” in the forum states for the very vehicles and parts involved in the suits. *Id.* at 357, 363-67.

That is the same framework that governs here. Ford extensively marketed, sold, and serviced the Explorer line and replacement parts in Montana, and it cultivated a market for the very model of vehicle involved in the accident. When a plaintiff sues over an allegedly defective Ford Explorer in the forum where Ford targeted that product, the suit plainly “relate[s] to” Ford’s Montana contacts. *Id.* at 357, 366-69. Due process does not require the plaintiff to prove that the forum contacts themselves caused the precise injury. *Id.* at 362-64.

II. **Ford’s contrary rule would improperly collapse “relate to” into “arise from,” contrary to Supreme Court precedent.**

Ford’s position would read the word “relate” out of the controlling standard. But the Supreme Court has repeatedly used the disjunctive formulation “arise out of or relate to.” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 472, 475; *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, 582 U.S. at 262; *Ford Motor Co.*, 592 U.S. at 359. The Court in *Ford Motor Co.* explained that “arise out of” and “relate to” are not synonymous, and that “relate to” “contemplates that some relationships will support jurisdiction without a strict causal showing.” *Ford Motor Co.*, 592 U.S. at 362.

That is decisive here. The plaintiff’s claims concern the safety and performance of a Ford Explorer—a product Ford extensively marketed and serviced in Montana. The suit therefore bears a close relationship to Ford’s Montana activity, even if the exact vehicle traveled through other states before reaching Montana. *Id.* at 366-69.

III. **The exercise of jurisdiction is also consistent with fair play and substantial justice.**

Once minimum contacts are established, a defendant must present a compelling case that jurisdiction would be unreasonable. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 476-77; *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 292, 297 (1980). Ford cannot make that showing. Ford is a global corporation that deliberately serves the Montana market for the very vehicles and parts implicated in this suit. Montana has a strong sovereign interest in providing a forum for its residents injured by allegedly defective products sold and serviced in the state. *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957); *Ford Motor Co.*, 592 U.S. at 373-74. The plaintiff’s interest in convenient and effective relief is substantial, and the interstate judicial system’s interest in efficient resolution is not undermined by litigating where the injury and the relevant market are both centered. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 477.

IV. **The Montana Supreme Court’s decision should be affirmed.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford’s purposeful Montana conduct—advertising, selling, and servicing the Explorer line and related parts—supported specific jurisdiction. That conclusion accords with *International Shoe*, *Burger King*, *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, and *Ford Motor Co.* Because Ford’s Montana contacts are closely related to the plaintiff’s claims, the Due Process Clause permits Montana courts to adjudicate them. *Ford Motor Co.*, 592 U.S. at 361-72.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
