The question presented is whether an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act. The plain language of the statute, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), requires only that the employer engage in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which introduced punitive damages to Title VII, indicates that Congress intended to adopt a standard similar to that under 42 U.S.C. § 1981. Under § 1981, courts have considered the state of mind required for punitive damages. In Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court discussed willful deprivation of rights, noting that a defendant's actions must be done "willfully" to be considered a deprivation under the statute. The Restatement of Torts, which reflects the common law as of 1939, provides guidance on the concept of punitive damages. Restatement of Torts § 908(2) (1939).

In the context of tort law, courts have long recognized that punitive damages serve to deter and punish egregious conduct. As the Supreme Court noted in Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), the purpose of exemplary damages is to punish the defendant. In Milwaukee & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489 (1875), the Court held that exemplary damages may be given in certain circumstances.

The Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, in Krodel v. Young, 748 F.2d 701 (D.C.Cir.1984), discussed the relevance of an employer's preselection of a job candidate to the question of discriminatory intent. The ADA's conduct, including preselecting Spangler for the promotion and ignoring Kolstad's efforts to discuss the position, demonstrates a level of malice or reckless indifference that warrants consideration of punitive damages. The District Court should have allowed the jury to consider this claim, as the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to support a finding of punitive damages under the standard established by Congress in § 1981a(b)(1).

In conclusion, the plain language of § 1981a(b)(1), combined with the legislative history and relevant case law, including Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), and Philadelphia, W. & B.R. Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202 (1858), supports the conclusion that an employer's conduct does not need to be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings on Kolstad's punitive damages claim.
