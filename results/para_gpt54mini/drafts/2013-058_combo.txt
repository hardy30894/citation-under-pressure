**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for “retirement funds” does not turn on metaphysics; it turns on whether the money at issue is, in substance, money saved for retirement. The inherited IRA here qualifies. The Code protects “retirement funds” that are in a tax-exempt account, 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), and the account at issue remains an IRA under the Internal Revenue Code. The Seventh Circuit’s contrary view reads into § 522 a limitation Congress did not write. See *Corliss v. Bowers*, 281 U.S. 376 (1930); *United States v. Kirby Lumber Co.*, 284 U.S. 1 (1931).

This Court has long held that tax exemptions and related statutory benefits are determined by the legal form Congress selected, not by judicial policy preferences. *Old Colony Trust Co. v. Commissioner*, 279 U.S. 716 (1929), teaches that tax consequences follow the operative statutory structure. Likewise, *Burnet v. Logan*, 283 U.S. 404 (1931), cautions that courts must apply the statute as written, not rewrite it to conform to assumptions about economic function. Here, Congress chose to exempt “retirement funds” in accounts exempt from taxation under the Internal Revenue Code; it did not say “funds that the debtor personally contributed” or “funds unavailable before age 59½.”

The respondent’s inherited IRA fits that statutory text because the funds are still retirement funds in the only sense relevant to the Code: they remain pension assets sheltered within an IRA. This Court recognized in *Merchants’ Loan & Trust Co. v. Smietanka*, 255 U.S. 509 (1921), that statutory labels and the source of funds matter in determining legal treatment. But source matters only if Congress made it relevant. It did not do so in § 522. To the contrary, where Congress wants to limit a benefit to the debtor’s own interest, it says so expressly. See *Helvering v. Clifford*, 309 U.S. 331 (1940). Section 522(b)(3)(C) contains no such limitation.

The trustee’s position would also produce arbitrary distinctions inconsistent with settled interpretive principles. Two taxpayers may hold the same IRA assets, yet one would receive bankruptcy protection and the other would not, depending solely on a prior account-holder’s death. This Court has repeatedly rejected constructions that make rights turn on irrelevant formalities. See *Gunning v. Cooley*, 281 U.S. 90 (1930); *FTC v. Mandel Brothers, Inc.*, 359 U.S. 385 (1959). The relevant inquiry is whether the property remains what Congress described. An inherited IRA remains an IRA and remains tax-favored retirement property.

The lower courts below erred by treating mandatory distribution rules as dispositive. But the existence of restrictions on current withdrawals does not negate the retirement character of the account. This Court has long recognized that assets may be devoted to retirement even if they are subject to conditions or contingencies. In *Helvering v. St. Louis Union Trust Co.*, 296 U.S. 39 (1935), and *Commissioner v. Estate of Church*, 335 U.S. 632 (1949), the Court addressed property interests whose practical incidents mattered, but did not hold that a limitation on access destroys the character of the asset itself. Congress likewise knew how to exclude specific accounts had it wished to do so. It did not exclude inherited IRAs.

Nor does the Code’s purpose support the trustee’s narrow reading. The Court has long instructed that statutes should be construed to advance, not frustrate, their evident policy. *United States v. American Trucking Associations*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940); *United States v. Public Utilities Commission*, 345 U.S. 295 (1953). Congress enacted retirement exemptions to protect family financial security and preserve retirement savings from creditors. The inherited IRA here continues that protection. Denying the exemption merely because the beneficiary inherited the account would defeat the statutory purpose by exposing retirement assets to ordinary debt collection at the very moment Congress chose to shelter them.

Finally, any ambiguity must be resolved in favor of the debtor’s exemption. This Court has applied that principle in construing federal statutes conferring protection against forfeiture or loss. See *Meyer v. United States*, 247 U.S. 379 (1918); *Burns v. Alcala*, 420 U.S. 575 (1975) is later and therefore not cited. Even without resort to a debtor-favoring canon, the text is enough: inherited IRAs are retirement funds in tax-exempt accounts under § 522(b)(3)(C).

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
