**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is governed by the clear text, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Third Circuit erred in holding that TPS confers admission, thereby rendering petitioners statutorily ineligible for adjustment of status under § 1255. This Court should reverse.

### I. THE TEXT OF THE INA CLEARLY DISTINGUISHES “ADMISSION” FROM “LAWFUL STATUS,” AND A GRANT OF TPS DOES NOT CONSTITUTE AN “ADMISSION”

Congress has defined “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely procedural—it is substantive. It refers to the *physical act* of entering the country lawfully, following inspection at a port of entry. As this Court has held, “admission” involves “physical entrance into the country,” which is distinct from the subsequent *status* an individual may hold. Hanif v. Att’y Gen., 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012); see also Taveras v. Att’y Gen., 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013) (“The words ‘entry’ and ‘into’ plainly indicate that ‘admission’ involves physical entrance into the country…”).

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that “[t]he alien shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” This language does not equate “lawful status” with “admission.” Indeed, Congress chose to use the phrase “being in, and maintaining, lawful status”—not “admitted”—to describe the effect of TPS. If Congress had intended TPS to constitute an admission, it would have said so. Instead, it used a different term, reflecting its intent to distinguish between the *act* of admission and the *status* conferred thereafter.

Petitioners argue that because TPS recipients are treated as being in “lawful status,” they must be deemed “admitted.” But this argument conflates two distinct concepts. The Government’s position—that “lawful status” and “admission” are separate and independent requirements—is consistent with long-standing precedent. See Gomez v. Lynch, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016) (“Admission and status are fundamentally distinct concepts.”); In re H-G-G-, 27 I. & N. Dec. 617, 635 (AAO 2019) (“A grant of asylum places the individual in valid immigration status but is not an ‘admission.’”).

Moreover, the INS General Counsel explicitly opined in 1991 that TPS “should not be construed as an admission into the United States.” Temporary Protected Status and Eligibility for Adjustment of Status under Section [1255], INS Gen. Counsel Op. No. 91-27, 1991 WL 1185138 (Mar. 4, 1991). That interpretation was consistent with the agency’s decision not to adopt a rule allowing TPS recipients to adjust status regardless of how they entered the country. In re H-G-G-, 27 I. & N. Dec. at 621. These contemporaneous interpretations confirm that Congress did not intend TPS to serve as a substitute for admission.

### II. THE STRUCTURE OF THE INA REINFORCES THAT TPS DOES NOT CONSTITUTE AN ADMISSION

Congress structured the INA to treat “admission” and “status” as separate prerequisites. Section 1255(a) requires that an alien “was inspected and admitted or paroled” before eligibility for adjustment of status can be considered. Section 1255(c)(2) bars adjustment if the alien “has failed… to maintain continuously a lawful status since entry.” And § 1255(k)(1) permits adjustment only if the alien “is present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission.”

These provisions demonstrate that Congress viewed “admission” and “lawful status” as distinct and cumulative requirements. If a grant of TPS were equivalent to admission, then every TPS recipient would automatically satisfy § 1255(a)’s threshold requirement. But Congress did not write the statute that way. Instead, it created exceptions for specific categories—such as special immigrants under § 1255(h) and certain visa holders under § 1255(i)—but *excluded* TPS recipients from such exceptions. Where Congress includes particular language in one section but omits it in another, we presume it acted intentionally. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. at 432.

Furthermore, § 1254a(h)(2) mandates a supermajority vote in the Senate to pass legislation adjusting the status of TPS recipients. This provision exists precisely because Congress recognized that TPS is temporary relief, not a path to permanent residence. To read § 1254a(f)(4) as satisfying § 1255(a)’s admission requirement would render § 1254a(h)(2) superfluous, undermining the statutory balance Congress struck. See Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (“A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions…”).

### III. THE PURPOSE OF TPS CONFIRMS THAT IT WAS NOT MEANT TO SERVE AS A PATH TO PERMANENT RESIDENCE

The purpose of TPS is to provide temporary protection from removal during extraordinary conditions abroad—such as natural disasters or armed conflict—not to confer permanent immigration benefits. Congress designed TPS as a “limited, temporary form of relief.” Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954, 963 (9th Cir. 2017). It allows individuals already present in the United States to remain without fear of deportation, but it does not alter their underlying immigration status.
