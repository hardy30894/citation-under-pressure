ARGUMENT

I. THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A SEPARATE, HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD FOR A DEFENDANT WHO PLEADS GUILTY OR WAIVES COUNSEL.

A. This Court Has Never Recognized Two Tiers of Mental Competence.

The Ninth Circuit held that a defendant may be competent to stand trial yet incompetent to plead guilty or discharge counsel unless he also satisfies a "reasoned choice" standard. That bifurcated approach has no footing in this Court's due process jurisprudence. Where this Court has confronted the mental capacity of a defendant to make fundamental decisions about his own case, it has applied a single, unitary inquiry into competence, not a hierarchy of competency standards keyed to which right is at stake.

In United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi, 344 U.S. 561 (1953), the state court's competency finding—affirmed as consistent with due process—asked simply whether "there was nothing in this record which convinces us that this relator was insane when he committed the murder charged or when he pleaded guilty or at the time he was sentenced to death." Id. at 561. That formulation makes no distinction between the mental capacity required to stand trial and the mental capacity required to plead guilty; sanity, not some elevated "reasoned choice" faculty, was the touchstone.

Carter v. Illinois, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), likewise treated the waiver of counsel as governed by ordinary capacity to choose: "The right to be represented by counsel is one which the defendant may waive or claim, as he shall determine." Id. at 174. Nothing in Carter suggests that determination requires proof of a superior mental faculty beyond what is necessary to stand trial.

And in Palmer v. Ashe, 342 U.S. 134 (1951), this Court framed the due process inquiry into a guilty plea entered without counsel around whether "special circumstances" existed "showing that without a lawyer a defendant could not have an adequate and a fair defense," id. at 134—again, a contextual fairness inquiry, not a freestanding heightened competency test.

B. The Constitutional Benchmark Is a Knowing and Voluntary Waiver, Not "Reasoned Choice."

This Court's settled test for the validity of a waiver of trial rights is whether it reflects an "intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." Brookhart v. Janis, 384 U.S. 1, 4 (1966) (quoting Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938)). In Brookhart, this Court examined the record to determine whether the petitioner had "intelligently and knowingly waived his right," id. at 4, not whether he possessed some enhanced decisional capacity beyond ordinary competence. The "knowing and voluntary" standard already accounts for the seriousness of what is waived; it does not require importing an additional, undefined mental-capacity threshold.

McCarthy v. United States, 394 U.S. 459 (1969), confirms that the constitutional and procedural safeguards surrounding a guilty plea are satisfied when the trial court ascertains that the defendant understands the "consequences" of his plea and the rights he is relinquishing. Id. at 463–64. The District Court's colloquy there—asking whether the defendant understood that his plea waived a jury trial and exposed him to a specific range of punishment—illustrates that the inquiry is properly focused on understanding and voluntariness, precisely the components already embedded in ordinary competency and waiver analysis.

Arsenault v. Massachusetts, 393 U.S. 5 (1968), likewise addressed the validity of an uncounseled guilty plea by reference to whether the proceeding at which it was entered was itself constitutionally infirm, not by asking whether the defendant possessed a distinct competency to make "reasoned" decisions. See id. at 5–6.

C. A Novel "Reasoned Choice" Standard Would Disserve the Values Underlying Habeas Review of State Convictions.

Federal courts sitting in habeas review of state criminal judgments have long been cautioned against layering new substantive constitutional standards onto proceedings that were otherwise regularly conducted. In Matter of Moran, 203 U.S. 96 (1906), this Court examined a habeas petition premised on claimed defects in the trial court's jurisdiction and organization, recognizing that such petitions properly reach only fundamental defects "alleged to go to the jurisdiction of the trial court," id. at 97—not freestanding revisions of the applicable substantive standard. The same restraint counsels against the Ninth Circuit's invention of a "reasoned choice" gloss that appears nowhere in this Court's competency decisions.

Rees v. Peyton, 384 U.S. 312 (1966), is instructive by contrast: when doubt arose concerning a habeas petitioner's capacity to withdraw his own petition, this Court did not invent a special standard; it directed further proceedings "because evidence cast doubt on Rees' mental competency," id. at 313–14, thereby employing the ordinary competency framework applicable to any determination of a defendant's capacity to make decisions affecting his own legal proceedings. That approach—one competency standard, applied faithfully to the facts—is the approach due process requires.

* * *

The competency standard this Court articulated for standing trial, see Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), together with the knowing-and-voluntary waiver requirement of Brookhart v. Janis and Johnson v. Zerbst, fully protects a defendant's rights when he chooses to plead guilty or proceed without counsel. Respondent Moran was examined by two psychiatrists and found to understand the charges against him and to be capable of assisting in his defense—precisely the inquiry due process demands. No additional "reasoned choice" standard is required by the Constitution, and the Ninth Circuit erred in imposing one.
