ARGUMENT

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts only “retirement funds” in accounts qualifying under specified Internal Revenue Code provisions. The text requires that the funds be held for retirement. “Retirement funds” is undefined, so its ordinary meaning controls. United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc., 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). That meaning is funds “set aside for retirement,” not assets that must be distributed before the owner retires. Rousey v. Jacoway, 544 U.S. 320, 326 (2005).

An inherited IRA lacks this attribute. Payout must begin within one year of the original owner’s death and cannot be rolled over. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). The funds therefore represent an opportunity for current consumption, not savings reserved for the beneficiary’s post-employment years. This Court has repeatedly held that exemptions turn on the debtor’s use of the asset at the time of filing. See Schwab v. Reilly, 560 U.S. 770, 782 (2010); Patterson v. Shumate, 504 U.S. 753, 759 (1992).

The Seventh Circuit correctly rejected the view that prior ownership by anyone suffices. In re Barker, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985), illustrates the point: a homestead exemption requires the debtor’s domicile for the statutory period; inheritance of a former family home does not qualify. The same temporal and functional limit applies here. Allowing exemption whenever funds were once someone’s retirement savings would convert a specific statutory category into an open-ended inheritance shield, contrary to the principle that “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987).

Statutory structure confirms the distinction. Other exemptions in § 522 expressly reference “the debtor’s” interest; the retirement-fund provisions do not, yet still require that the assets constitute retirement funds during the bankruptcy case. Lamie v. United States Trustee, 540 U.S. 526, 534 (2004) (“[W]hen the statute’s language is plain, the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms.”). An inherited IRA satisfies the tax-deferral prong but not the retirement-funds prong.

Policy arguments for broader protection are unavailing. Dodd v. United States, 545 U.S. 353, 359 (2005), instructs that courts may not “rewrite the statute” to advance unexpressed objectives. Congress could have exempted all inherited tax-deferred accounts; it did not. Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co., 534 U.S. 438, 462 (2002) (“[C]ourts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there.”).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
