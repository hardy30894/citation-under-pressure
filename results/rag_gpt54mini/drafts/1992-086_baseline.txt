**Argument**

The First Amendment forbids a public school district from denying access to a forum it has opened to outside speakers because the proposed speech is religious. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), and *Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schools (Dist. 66) v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), control this case.

The District here allowed community groups to use school facilities after hours, but refused Lamb’s Chapel’s requests solely because the films were “church related” and would be shown “from a Christian perspective.” That is content-based exclusion of speech. In *Widmar*, the Court held that a university could not exclude a student religious group from rooms otherwise open for meetings, because the policy “prohibits the use of University buildings or grounds ‘for purposes of religious worship or religious teaching,’” 454 U.S. at 265, and that discrimination against religious speech was content based. 454 U.S. at 270-75. The Court explained that once the government creates a forum generally open for expressive activity, it may not exclude speech because of its religious content absent a compelling justification. 454 U.S. at 267-70.

The same principle applies with even greater force here. Public schools do not become immune from the First Amendment simply because the speech is religious or because the speakers are outsiders. The Court in *Mergens* reaffirmed that a school may not “discriminat[e], based on the content of the students’ speech, against students who wish to meet on school premises during noninstructional time.” 496 U.S. at 247. Although *Mergens* involved students and the Equal Access Act, the Court’s reasoning rested on the constitutional significance of a limited open forum and the impermissibility of excluding religious speech from such a forum. 496 U.S. at 248-53. The school there had created a forum by allowing noncurriculum-related student groups to meet after school. Id. at 232-37. If a school may not discriminate against a student Christian club in a forum it has opened, it certainly may not exclude a community church from speaking in a forum open to community groups generally.

The Second Circuit erred in treating the District’s rule as viewpoint neutral. The District did not exclude all discussion of family values, child rearing, or moral issues; it excluded Lamb’s Chapel because the same subject matter would be presented “from a Christian perspective.” That is not neutral administration of a forum. A restriction is not neutral where it singles out religious speech for disfavored treatment. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 274-75; *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 247-48. The government may limit a nonpublic forum by subject matter or speaker identity, but once it opens a limited public forum, exclusions must be consistent with the forum’s purpose and cannot rest on hostility to a particular perspective. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802-06 (1985).

The District’s invocation of the Establishment Clause does not justify the exclusion. *Widmar* squarely rejected the argument that equal access for religious speech amounts to establishment of religion, observing that the university’s exclusion was itself the unconstitutional act. 454 U.S. at 271-75. Likewise, *Mergens* held that allowing a religious student group to meet in a public school after hours would not violate the Establishment Clause. 496 U.S. at 248-53. This Court has long recognized that government may accommodate religious activity without establishing religion. *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313-14 (1952). Indeed, the Constitution does not permit a school to suppress religious expression to avoid a mistaken appearance of endorsement. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 274.

Nor does the District’s reliance on the schoolhouse setting alter the analysis. Public schools may restrict access to preserve educational function, but if they open their facilities to community expression after hours, they may not then exclude religious speech because of its religious viewpoint. The Court has repeatedly held that when the government opens property for expressive use, the First Amendment limits its ability to discriminate among speakers. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46; *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 802-03. That principle governs here.

The judgment should be reversed. Lamb’s Chapel sought only equal access to speak on family values and child rearing in a religious perspective, after hours, in a forum the District had opened to community use. The First Amendment does not allow the District to say yes to secular views on those subjects and no to the same views when expressed by a church.
