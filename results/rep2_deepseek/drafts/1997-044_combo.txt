**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge “any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The plain language limits nondischargeability to the value of the property actually obtained through fraud. Treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs are not “obtained by” fraud; they are statutory penalties imposed after the fact. This Court has long held that exceptions to discharge must be strictly construed in favor of the debtor. *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877) (“the act is to be liberally construed in favor of the debtor”); *Strang v. Bradner*, 114 U.S. 555, 561 (1885) (exceptions “must be confined to the plain meaning of the words used”). The phrase “to the extent obtained by” cannot be read to encompass punitive awards that the debtor never received.

The fraud exception under the Bankruptcy Act of 1898, § 17a(2), which excepted “liabilities for obtaining property by false pretenses or false representations,” was similarly limited. In *Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915), this Court held that the debt must be “for obtaining money by false pretenses”—i.e., the money actually obtained. Punitive damages, which are not “obtained” by the debtor, fall outside the exception. *Crawford v. Burke*, 195 U.S. 176, 193 (1904) (discharge provisions “should be construed liberally in favor of the bankrupt”). The lower court’s contrary reading would expand the exception beyond its text.

The “fresh start” policy of the Bankruptcy Code reinforces this conclusion. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (the purpose is to “relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness and permit him to start afresh”). Discharging punitive damages does not reward fraud; it prevents the bankruptcy process from becoming a vehicle for state-imposed penalties that bear no relation to the property obtained. The debtor’s fraud is already addressed by the nondischargeability of the actual overcharges—here, $31,382.50. Trebling that amount serves a punitive, not compensatory, function.

The lower courts that have addressed this issue under the 1898 Act uniformly held that only compensatory damages are nondischargeable. *In re Dunfee*, 219 F. 630, 632 (2d Cir. 1915) (“the debt … is the money obtained by the false pretenses, not the penalty imposed by statute”); *In re Harr*, 143 F. 421, 423 (7th Cir. 1906) (same); *In re Taylor*, 187 F. 873, 875 (2d Cir. 1911) (“the debt is the money obtained, not the punitive damages”); *In re Garcelon*, 104 F. 330, 332 (1st Cir. 1900) (“the exception applies only to the property obtained”). These decisions reflect the consistent principle that the fraud exception is tied to the debtor’s ill-gotten gain, not to subsequent statutory enhancements.

The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act’s treble-damages provision is a penalty, not a debt “obtained by” fraud. The debtor never obtained those trebled amounts; they are imposed by law as punishment. *In re Weisberg*, 255 F. 847, 849 (2d Cir. 1919) (statutory penalties are dischargeable because they are not “for obtaining property”). The lower court’s reliance on the foreseeability of the penalty is irrelevant; the statutory text controls. *Neal*, 95 U.S. at 709.

Petitioner does not seek to discharge the actual overcharges. He concedes that the $31,382.50 obtained by fraud is nondischargeable. But the treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs—amounting to over $94,000—are not “obtained by” fraud. They are punitive and must be discharged to give effect to the Code’s fresh start policy. *Local Loan*, 292 U.S. at 244.

The judgment below should be reversed insofar as it holds the treble damages and fees nondischargeable.
