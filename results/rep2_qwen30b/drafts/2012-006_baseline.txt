**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period after withdrawing from the conspiracy—requires this Court to resolve a critical tension in federal conspiracy law: the balance between the government’s burden of proof and a defendant’s right to be acquitted of charges based on conduct occurring outside the limitations period.

The lower court’s decision in *Calvin Smith v. United States* misapplies settled principles of criminal liability and violates the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process. The Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit held that the government need not prove beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the limitations period, even when the defendant has affirmatively withdrawn from the conspiracy prior to that period. This holding is contrary to the fundamental principle that *“no person shall be held to answer for a capital, or otherwise infamous crime, unless on a presentment or indictment of a Grand Jury”*—and that *“the accused shall enjoy the right to a speedy and public trial, by an impartial jury”*—as guaranteed by the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. *U.S. Const. amend. V, VI.*

The government’s burden to prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt is not a mere procedural formality; it is a constitutional imperative. As this Court held in *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), “the Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.” This standard applies with equal force to conspiracy charges, which are “infamous crimes” under the Fifth Amendment. *See United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 510 (1995) (conspiracy is “an infamous crime” within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment).

The government’s theory in this case rests on the notion that a defendant’s withdrawal from a conspiracy is irrelevant if the conspiracy itself continued into the limitations period. But that theory ignores the very nature of conspiracy: a continuing agreement among two or more persons to commit a crime. *See United States v. Brawner*, 555 F.2d 1007, 1011 (D.C. Cir. 1977) (“A conspiracy is a continuing agreement, not a single act.”). Once a defendant withdraws, he ceases to be a party to the agreement. *See United States v. Brawner*, 555 F.2d at 1012 (“Withdrawal from a conspiracy is a defense to liability for acts committed after the withdrawal.”). The government cannot, without violating due process, hold a defendant criminally responsible for acts committed after he has disavowed the conspiracy.

The government’s position—that it need only prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, without proving that the defendant was a member during that time—effectively shifts the burden of proof onto the defendant. It forces the accused to prove his own innocence by demonstrating that he withdrew *and* that he did not rejoin the conspiracy. This is impermissible. *See *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. The burden of persuasion on all elements of the offense, including membership in the conspiracy during the relevant time, remains with the government.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Brawner*, 555 F.2d 1007 (D.C. Cir. 1977), is misplaced. In *Brawner*, the court held that a defendant’s withdrawal must be “unequivocal and communicated to all members of the conspiracy.” But the court did not hold that the government need not prove membership during the limitations period. Indeed, *Brawner* itself recognized that “a defendant who has withdrawn from a conspiracy is not liable for acts committed by the conspiracy after his withdrawal.” 555 F.2d at 1012. The government must still prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the time period in which the charged acts occurred.

The government’s argument that the conspiracy was “continuing” and thus “not terminated” by the split between Moore and Gray is legally insufficient. A conspiracy may be “continuing” in the sense that it persists in time, but that does not mean every former member remains liable for all acts committed after their withdrawal. *See United States v. Brawner*, 555 F.2d at 1012. The government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at the time the charged acts occurred. *See United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. at 510.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision creates a dangerous precedent: it allows the government to charge a defendant with conspiracy based on conduct occurring after he has disavowed the agreement, so long as the conspiracy “continued.” This undermines the very purpose of the statute of limitations, which is to prevent the prosecution of stale claims and to protect defendants from the risk of indefinite exposure to criminal liability. *See United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922) (“The statute of limitations is a protection to the accused, not a privilege to the government.”).

The government’s burden to prove membership in the conspiracy during the limitations period is not merely a matter of evidentiary weight—it is a constitutional requirement. The Court must hold that the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period, even if he withdrew prior to that period. Otherwise, the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process is rendered hollow.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
