**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s failure to advise petitioner of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not warrant habeas relief because petitioner was aware of that right and chose not to appeal. The error was harmless, and habeas relief requires a showing of actual prejudice that is absent here.

**I. The Rule 32 Violation Was Harmless Because Petitioner Knew of His Right to Appeal**

The harmless-error standard applies to nonconstitutional procedural errors. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) (error is harmless unless it “affect[s] the substantial rights of the parties”). Rule 32’s advice requirement is a procedural safeguard, not a constitutional command. Its purpose is to ensure that defendants know of their appeal right. When a defendant already knows of that right, the rule’s purpose is served, and any omission is harmless.

In *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967), this Court held that even constitutional errors may be harmless if the State proves beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. Here, the district court found that petitioner knew of his appeal right at sentencing. That finding is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous. Because petitioner’s knowledge satisfied the rule’s objective, the failure to advise could not have affected his decision not to appeal. The error was therefore harmless.

**II. Habeas Relief Requires a Showing of Prejudice, Which Petitioner Cannot Make**

A federal prisoner seeking relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 must demonstrate that the alleged error resulted in a “complete miscarriage of justice” or was “inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure.” *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). In *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954), this Court emphasized that § 2255 is not a substitute for direct appeal and that relief is available only for “fundamental” defects. A mere failure to recite a procedural formality, when the defendant already possesses the information, is not fundamental.

Petitioner’s claim fails because he cannot show any prejudice. He knew of his right to appeal, understood it, and elected not to appeal. His decision was voluntary and intelligent. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver is “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right”). Because petitioner had actual knowledge, the omission did not deprive him of any opportunity. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 398-99 (1963) (distinguishing between forfeiture and waiver of federal rights). Here, no deprivation occurred.

**III. The Purpose of Rule 32 Was Satisfied**

Rule 32(a)(2) requires the court to “advise the defendant of his right to appeal.” The rule’s purpose is to ensure that defendants are informed of that right. When a defendant already knows of the right, the rule’s objective is met. Procedural rules are “designed in the interest of fairness” and not to create technical traps. *Hark v. United States*, 320 U.S. 531, 536 (1944). Petitioner admitted at the evidentiary hearing that he knew he could appeal. The district court’s failure to repeat that advice did not undermine the rule’s purpose.

Moreover, petitioner’s failure to appeal was a deliberate choice. *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947) (noting that “the normal and customary method of correcting errors of the trial is by appeal”). Petitioner could have appealed but chose not to. He cannot now use a procedural omission to collaterally attack his sentence. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 444 (1944) (failure to raise an issue on appeal waives it).

**IV. The Courts Below Correctly Applied Harmless-Error Review**

The Third Circuit held that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmless-error review and that the error was harmless because petitioner knew of his right. This approach comports with *Kotteakos* and *Chapman*. The district court’s factual finding that petitioner had actual knowledge is dispositive. *United States v. Hayman*, 342 U.S. 205, 219 (1952) (habeas petitioner must show that the error “resulted in a complete miscarriage of justice”). No miscarriage occurred here.

Because petitioner suffered no prejudice, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
