**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Re-Entered Before Its Effective Date**

The plain text of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) (the “reinstatement statute”) authorizes the Attorney General to reinstate a prior removal order only against an alien who “has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed.” The statute does not specify whether it applies to re-entries that occurred before its enactment. However, this Court has repeatedly held that “[s]tatutes are not to be given retroactive effect absent clear congressional intent.” Landgraf v. USI Film Prods., 511 U.S. 244, 264 (1994). The reinstatement statute contains no such clear intent to apply retroactively. To the contrary, the statute’s structure and legislative history demonstrate that Congress intended it to apply only prospectively.

In *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), this Court cautioned against interpreting immigration statutes to apply retroactively where doing so would attach new legal consequences to past conduct. The reinstatement statute operates precisely in this manner: it imposes a new bar to relief—automatic reinstatement of a prior removal order and denial of all forms of discretionary relief—on aliens who re-entered illegally before the statute’s enactment. Such an application is impermissibly retroactive. *See id.* at 320 (holding that a statute’s retroactive effect must be “sufficiently clear” before it will be applied to pre-enactment conduct).

The Tenth Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 438 F.3d 1196 (10th Cir. 2006), is unpersuasive. The court relied on *United States v. Heth*, 26 U.S. 399 (1828), for the proposition that a statute may apply to pre-enactment conduct if it does not “create[] a new obligation” but merely “enforces[] an obligation previously existing in morality or in equity.” *Fernandez-Vargas*, 438 F.3d at 1202. However, this reasoning conflates the reinstatement statute’s enforcement mechanism with its substantive effect. The statute does not merely enforce an existing obligation; it creates a new legal disability—denial of all forms of relief—that did not exist at the time of Fernandez-Vargas’s re-entry. *See Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 274 (explaining that a statute is retroactive if it “attaches a new disability, in respect to transactions or considerations already past”).

**II. The Reinstatement Statute’s Application to Pre-Enactment Re-Entries Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

Even if the reinstatement statute could be read to apply to pre-enactment re-entries, such an application would violate the Constitution’s prohibition on retroactive legislation. In *Landgraf*, the Court held that a statute is retroactive if it “impair[s] rights a party possessed when he acted, increase[s] a party’s liability for past conduct, or impose[s] new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” 511 U.S. at 280. The reinstatement statute does all three.

First, it impairs the right to seek discretionary relief that Fernandez-Vargas possessed at the time of his re-entry. Before the statute’s enactment, an alien who re-entered illegally could apply for adjustment of status, cancellation of removal, or other forms of relief. *See, e.g., INS v. Yang*, 519 U.S. 26, 30 (1996) (noting that pre-IIRIRA law allowed discretionary relief for certain deportable aliens). The reinstatement statute strips aliens of this right retroactively.

Second, it increases liability for past conduct by subjecting aliens to mandatory removal without a hearing. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) (providing for automatic reinstatement of prior removal orders). This is a stark departure from pre-enactment law, which allowed aliens to contest removal in removal proceedings. *See Yang*, 519 U.S. at 30.

Third, it imposes new duties with respect to completed transactions—namely, the act of re-entering the United States. Before the statute’s enactment, re-entry was not a predicate for automatic removal. *See id.* The reinstatement statute retroactively criminalizes this conduct by making it the basis for mandatory removal.

The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on *Fernandez-Vargas* for the proposition that aliens have no “protectable expectation” of being able to adjust their status is misplaced. *Fernandez-Vargas*, 438 F.3d at 1204. This Court has rejected the argument that retroactive effect is permissible merely because a party lacks a “vested right” in a particular legal regime. *See Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 272-73 (holding that retroactive effect is impermissible even where a party has no vested right in a prior legal regime). The proper inquiry is whether the statute attaches new legal consequences to past conduct, not whether the party had a “protectable expectation” of avoiding those consequences. *See id.*

**III. The Reinstatement Statute Violates Due Process**

The reinstatement statute also violates the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process. In *United States v. Security Industrial Bank*, 459 U.S. 70 (1982), the Court held that retroactive application of a statute may violate due process if it “is so harsh and oppressive as to transgress the constitutional limitation.” *Id.* at 78. The reinstatement statute’s automatic denial of all forms of relief—including adjustment of status, cancellation of removal, and asylum—is undeniably harsh and oppressive. It leaves aliens with no opportunity to present their claims to an immigration judge, effectively foreclosing any possibility of relief. *See Yang*, 519 U.S. at 30 (noting that pre-IIRIRA law allowed aliens to contest removal in removal proceedings).

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that Fernandez-Vargas had no due process right to a hearing because he was “apprehended” is unsupported by precedent. *Fernandez-Vargas*, 438 F.3d at 1204. This Court has repeatedly held that aliens in removal proceedings are entitled to due process, including the right to a hearing before an immigration judge. *See Reno v. Flores*, 507 U.S. 292, 306 (1993) (holding that due process requires a hearing before an immigration judge for detained aliens). The reinstatement statute deprives aliens of this right retroactively, in violation of the Fifth Amendment.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the reinstatement statute cannot be applied to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date. Even if it could, such an application would violate the Constitution’s prohibition on retroactive legislation and the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
