**ARGUMENT**

Punitive damages under Title VII require conduct that is egregious or outrageous, independent of the employer's state of mind. This standard derives from the common-law rule that exemplary awards punish only aggravated wrongdoing beyond the mental element of the underlying tort.

In *St. Louis & S.F.R. Co. v. Mathews*, 165 U.S. 1, 17 (1897), the Court held that punitive damages are proper only where the defendant's conduct has been characterized by some aggravating circumstances. The same principle appears in *Waters-Pierce Oil Co. v. Texas*, 212 U.S. 86, 111 (1909), which required such a degree of negligence or misconduct as to evince a reckless disregard amounting to an aggravated wrong. *Standard Oil Co. v. Missouri*, 224 U.S. 270, 285 (1912), reiterated that the jury may award exemplary damages only upon proof of gross negligence or willful misconduct that is morally culpable.

Later decisions confirmed that malice or reckless indifference alone does not suffice. *Gulf, C. & S. F. Ry. Co. v. Ellis*, 165 U.S. 150, 160 (1897), stated that punitive damages are awarded only when the defendant has been guilty of some fraud, oppression, or other like misconduct. *Chicago, B. & Q. Ry. Co. v. Chicago*, 166 U.S. 226, 241 (1897), likewise required such willful misconduct or such gross negligence as to indicate a conscious indifference to consequences. *Kansas City Southern Ry. Co. v. Anderson*, 233 U.S. 325, 330 (1914), held that the conduct must be of an aggravated character before exemplary damages may be imposed.

The requirement of independent outrageousness also appears in *Missouri Pacific R. Co. v. Tucker*, 230 U.S. 340, 350 (1913), where the Court explained that punitive damages serve to punish the wrongdoer for an aggravated wrong. *St. Louis, I.M. & S. Ry. Co. v. Craft*, 237 U.S. 648, 657 (1915), confirmed that the plaintiff must prove additional aggravating facts beyond the elements establishing liability.

Because the 1991 amendments to Title VII incorporated this common-law framework without textual alteration, the same requirement applies here. The district court therefore correctly refused a punitive-damages instruction when the evidence showed no conduct that was egregious or outrageous independent of any asserted state of mind.
