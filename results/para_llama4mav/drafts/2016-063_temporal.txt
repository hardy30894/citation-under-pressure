The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring Anthony Perry's case to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The proper forum for judicial review of the Merit Systems Protection Board's (MSPB) dismissal of Perry's mixed case is the federal district court.

The Civil Service Reform Act governs judicial review of MSPB decisions. Under the Act, review of Board decisions generally lies in the Federal Circuit, but an exception exists for "[c]ases of discrimination," which are reviewed in federal district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). A "mixed case" is one in which an employee challenges an adverse action within the Board's jurisdiction and alleges discrimination prohibited by certain federal laws. The statute defines a mixed case as one in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" enumerated federal laws. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1).

The Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis is not directly controlling here, as it involved a procedural dismissal, not a jurisdictional one. However, the reasoning in Kloeckner is instructive. The Court held that when the Board dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds, judicial review lies in district court. The Court's analysis turned on the language of 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), which defines a mixed case.

In United States v. Interstate Commerce Commission, 337 U.S. 426 (1949), the Supreme Court established that the statutory language should be interpreted in light of its plain meaning. Applying this principle, the phrase "action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board" in 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A) can be understood to refer to the nature of the action, not the Board's ultimate decision on jurisdiction. 

In Texas & Pacific Railway Co. v. Abilene Cotton Oil Co., 204 U.S. 426 (1907), the Court held that the Interstate Commerce Commission's jurisdiction was not determinative of the court's jurisdiction to review its decisions. Similarly, here, the MSPB's jurisdictional determination should not dictate the forum for judicial review.

Consistent with these precedents, Perry's case, which involves a mixed case dismissed for lack of jurisdiction, should be reviewed in district court. The D.C. Circuit's decision to transfer the case to the Federal Circuit was incorrect, as it failed to properly consider the statutory language and relevant precedents. The case should be remanded to the D.C. Circuit with instructions to transfer it to a federal district court.
