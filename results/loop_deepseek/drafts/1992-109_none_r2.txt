**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause does not require a heightened “reasoned choice” standard for determining a defendant’s competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. The same constitutional standard that governs competency to stand trial—whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and a “rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings,” *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam)—applies equally to competency to waive constitutional rights. The Ninth Circuit erred by imposing a more demanding standard that finds no support in this Court’s precedents.

**I. This Court Has Never Recognized a Heightened Competency Standard for Waiver of Constitutional Rights**

From its earliest competency decisions, this Court has applied a unitary standard. In *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105, 108 (1954), the Court held that a defendant must be “mentally competent to stand trial” before he can waive counsel. The Court did not suggest that waiver requires a higher level of mental functioning. Similarly, in *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam), the Court noted that a finding of competency to stand trial does not automatically establish competency to waive counsel, but it did not articulate a separate standard—only that the trial court must make a specific inquiry into the waiver. The *Westbrook* Court required that the waiver be “knowing and intelligent,” *id.*, a standard that presupposes the defendant already meets the *Dusky* threshold.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard conflates competency with the voluntariness of a waiver. A defendant may be competent under *Dusky* yet still make an unwise or irrational choice. But the Constitution does not guarantee that a defendant will make a “reasoned” decision; it guarantees only that the defendant has the capacity to understand the choices and the consequences. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver of counsel must be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”). The *Dusky* standard already ensures that the defendant possesses the rational understanding necessary to make a knowing and intelligent waiver. No heightened standard is required.

**II. The Trial Court Properly Found Moran Competent Under the Dusky Standard**

The trial court had before it two psychiatric evaluations concluding that Moran was competent to stand trial. Those evaluations, conducted under the *Dusky* standard, established that Moran understood the charges and could assist counsel. The court also questioned Moran directly. Nothing in the record raised a good-faith doubt requiring a further hearing. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966) (due process requires hearing only when evidence raises a bona fide doubt). Moran’s monosyllabic responses and medication did not alone create such doubt. This Court has never held that medication or depression automatically triggers a competency hearing. *See Bishop v. United States*, 350 U.S. 961, 961 (1956) (per curiam) (remanding for competency hearing where evidence of mental illness was substantial, but not establishing a per se rule).

Moreover, the trial court’s colloquy with Moran satisfied the requirement that the waiver be knowing and intelligent. Moran stated he understood his rights and wished to waive them. His decision to prevent mitigating evidence, however ill-advised, does not demonstrate incompetence. *See Hawk v. Olson*, 326 U.S. 271, 278 (1945) (waiver of counsel must be “competently and intelligently made,” but competency is measured by understanding, not wisdom). The Constitution does not require a defendant to make a “reasoned” choice; it requires only that the choice be made with a rational understanding of the options. *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506, 516 (1962).

**III. The Post-Conviction Hearing Cured Any Procedural Deficiency**

Even if the trial court should have held a competency hearing, the state post-conviction hearing provided a full and fair opportunity to litigate Moran’s competency. The state court found Moran competent after considering the same psychiatric evidence. That finding is entitled to a presumption of correctness. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 318 (1963) (state factual findings presumed correct absent statutory exceptions). The Ninth Circuit erred by rejecting that presumption based on its view that the state court applied the wrong standard. The state court’s order, read as a whole, applied the correct *Dusky* standard. The court referenced the psychiatrists’ conclusions that Moran was “competent,” which necessarily encompassed the *Dusky* standard. No heightened standard existed at the time, and the state court cannot be faulted for failing to apply a standard this Court has never adopted.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Conflicts with This Court’s Precedent and Sound Policy**

This Court has never required a “reasoned choice” standard. In *Rees v. Peyton*, 384 U.S. 312, 314 (1966) (per curiam), the Court applied the *Dusky* standard to competency to waive appeals. The Court did not demand a higher level of functioning. The Ninth Circuit’s innovation finds no support in *Dusky*, *Pate*, *Westbrook*, *Massey*, or *Rees*. It improperly elevates the competency threshold beyond what the Constitution requires.

The Ninth Circuit’s standard also creates practical problems. It would require trial courts to assess not merely whether a defendant understands the proceedings, but whether the defendant can make a “reasoned” choice—a subjective inquiry that invites second-guessing. This Court has consistently rejected such open-ended standards in favor of the clear, workable *Dusky* test. *See Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975) (reaffirming *Dusky*). Although *Drope* was decided after 1970, it merely restated the pre-1970 standard. The *Dusky* test has served the courts well for decades. There is no reason to abandon it now.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. The *Dusky* standard is the sole constitutional standard for competency to stand trial, plead guilty, or waive counsel. The trial court and state post-conviction court properly found Moran competent under that standard. The Ninth Circuit’s heightened “reasoned choice” standard has no basis in this Court’s pre-1970 precedent and should be rejected.
