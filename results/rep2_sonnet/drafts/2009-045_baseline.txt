# ARGUMENT

## THE LODESTAR MAY BE ENHANCED IN RARE AND EXCEPTIONAL CASES SUPPORTED BY SPECIFIC EVIDENCE OF SUPERIOR PERFORMANCE AND EXCEPTIONAL RESULTS

This Court has never held that fee enhancements based on quality of representation and results obtained are categorically forbidden. To the contrary, in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), and *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986) (*"Delaware Valley I"*), this Court expressly preserved the availability of such enhancements while cabining them to exceptional circumstances supported by specific evidence. The district court's award here fits squarely within that narrow but real category.

### A. Blum and Delaware Valley I Preserve Enhancements in Exceptional Cases

In *Blum*, this Court rejected an enhancement not because such enhancements are never permissible, but because the fee applicants there "offered no evidence" that the case was exceptional or that counsel's performance exceeded what the hourly rate already compensated. 465 U.S. at 899. The Court explained that the "novelty and complexity of the litigation" and "the quality of representation" are "reflected in the reasonable hourly rate," and therefore cannot serve as "independent basis for increasing the basic fee award" *absent* such evidence. *Id.* Critically, the Court did not stop there—it identified the circumstance under which enhancement would be proper: "the rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged." *Id.*

This Court reaffirmed that framework two years later in *Delaware Valley I*. There, the Court held that "the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar," 478 U.S. at 566 (emphasis added)—language that necessarily contemplates exceptions to the ordinary rule. The Court further confirmed that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible... in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." *Id.* at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901). Had this Court intended to categorically foreclose enhancement based on performance and results, it would not have repeatedly invoked the "rare" and "exceptional" case exception while citing *Blum* with approval throughout its opinion. *See id.* at 564, 568.

Nor did *Delaware Valley I*'s companion holding regarding the risk-multiplier issue disturb this framework. The Court there addressed only whether enhancement was available for the risk of nonpayment in a fee-shifting case—a distinct question from whether enhancement is available for demonstrably superior performance yielding exceptional results.

### B. This Case Presents the Rare and Exceptional Circumstance Contemplated by Blum and Delaware Valley I

The district court did not rely on generalized praise or unsupported assertions. It made detailed, record-based findings that counsel's performance was superior to what the lodestar rate already captured and that the results—a comprehensive consent decree reforming Georgia's entire foster care system—were exceptional. The court explained that "the superb quality of counsel's representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee." This is precisely the "specific evidence" *Blum* requires, 465 U.S. at 899, and the "detailed findings" *Delaware Valley I* demands, 478 U.S. at 565.

The district judge, moreover, possessed exactly the kind of first-hand institutional knowledge this Court has recognized as relevant to fee determinations. In *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), this Court emphasized that "the district court has firsthand knowledge... the district court's findings that this attorney is entitled to the fee award... [are] entitled to substantial deference." Consistent with that deference, courts of appeals reviewing enhancements apply an abuse-of-discretion standard precisely because trial judges possess unique competence to assess litigation performance day by day.

### C. Every Circuit to Address This Question Agrees That Enhancement Is Permissible in Exceptional Cases

The absence of any circuit conflict confirms that the courts of appeals have correctly read *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* as permitting—not foreclosing—enhancement in rare cases. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits have all held that enhancement is available upon a showing of specific evidence establishing superior performance and exceptional results. *See, e.g., Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999) (affirming enhancement "on account of the exceptional results obtained"); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993) (upward adjustments proper where "supported by specific evidence on record and detailed findings by lower courts"); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000) (adjustment "justified only in the rare case where there is specific evidence" of superior quality and exceptional success). This uniform consensus across eight circuits, developed over more than two decades of applying this Court's precedent, warrants substantial weight.

### D. Petitioners' Categorical Rule Would Undermine the Purpose of Fee-Shifting Statutes

Congress enacted 42 U.S.C. § 1988 to ensure that civil rights plaintiffs could secure competent counsel. A categorical bar on enhancement—regardless of how exceptional the performance or result—would sever the connection between compensation and the quality of advocacy that fee-shifting statutes are designed to reward. Nothing in *Blum* or *Delaware Valley I* compels so rigid a rule, and this Court should decline to adopt one now.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
