**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE TRUE-THREAT EXCEPTION TO FIRST AMENDMENT PROTECTION REQUIRES PROOF OF SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN.**

The First Amendment does not protect “true threats.” *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). But the Court has never defined what constitutes a “true threat” beyond noting that it involves “serious expressions” of intent to commit unlawful violence. *Id.* at 359–60. This Court should clarify that a “true threat” requires proof that the speaker *subjectively intended* his statements to be understood as threats. Absent such proof, the government cannot constitutionally criminalize speech.

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—that a statement is a “true threat” if a reasonable person would foresee that it would be interpreted as a threat—is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Black*, the Court rejected Virginia’s argument that the First Amendment permits a State to “prohibit only those threats that are directed at producing ‘a fear of violence’ in the recipient.” *Id.* at 360. The Court explained that “[t]he speaker need not actually intend the recipient to hear the threat” because “[i]ntimidation in the arena of political speech would have obvious chilling effects.” *Id.* at 360. But the Court did not hold that the First Amendment permits conviction based solely on an objective foreseeability standard. Rather, it emphasized that the speaker’s *subjective intent* is constitutionally significant. *Id.* at 365 (“The speaker’s beliefs about the audience’s likely reaction to his words are not irrelevant.”).

The Third Circuit’s approach also conflicts with *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam). In *Watts*, the Court reversed a conviction under a federal statute prohibiting threats against the President, holding that the defendant’s statement—“If they ever make me carry a rifle, the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J.”—was constitutionally protected hyperbole. *Id.* at 706–07. The Court explained that the statute required proof of a *specific intent* to threaten. *Id.* at 708. The Third Circuit’s objective standard would have permitted the conviction in *Watts*, despite the Court’s conclusion that the statement was protected speech.

Finally, the Third Circuit’s rule is inconsistent with the Court’s holding in *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723 (2015), that *negligence* is insufficient to support a conviction under § 875(c). The Court reasoned that “negligence is not sufficient to support a conviction” because “[t]he mental state requirement in § 875(c) is satisfied by proof of the defendant’s *knowing* and *willful* commission of the proscribed act.” *Id.* at 737 (emphasis added). If negligence is insufficient, then a fortiori, an objective standard that does not require any mental state is unconstitutional.

II. **THE THIRD CIRCUIT’S OBJECTIVE STANDARD FAILS TO PROTECT CORE FIRST AMENDMENT VALUES.**

The Third Circuit’s rule risks criminalizing protected speech—including political hyperbole, artistic expression, and social commentary—that a reasonable person might construe as threatening. This chills speech protected by the First Amendment. *See Black*, 538 U.S. at 365 (“The First Amendment does not permit [States] to impose criminal sanctions on the basis of a prediction of how a person’s words will be received by others.”).

The government’s contrary argument—that an objective standard is necessary to protect victims from fear—is unavailing. The Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that the First Amendment permits restrictions on speech based solely on the *effect* it has on listeners. *See, e.g., Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (“[A] function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute. It may indeed best serve its high purpose when it induces a condition of unrest, creates dissatisfaction with conditions as they are, or even stirs people to anger.”). The government’s fear-based rationale would justify criminalizing vast amounts of protected speech.

III. **THE COURT SHOULD HOLD THAT § 875(C) REQUIRES PROOF OF SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN.**

Section 875(c) criminalizes the *transmission* of threats in interstate commerce. The statute does not define “threat,” but this Court has held that statutory silence on mens rea does not dispense with a culpable mental state requirement. *See Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 731–32. The Court should construe § 875(c) to require proof that the defendant *subjectively intended* his statements to be understood as threats.

This construction is consistent with the Court’s precedents and avoids serious constitutional concerns. It ensures that only those who *intend* to threaten are criminally liable, while protecting core First Amendment speech. The government’s objective standard, by contrast, would impermissibly chill protected expression and permit convictions based on mere carelessness or misinterpretation.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment below and remand with instructions to vacate Petitioner’s conviction.
