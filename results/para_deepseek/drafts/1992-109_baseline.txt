## ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause requires that a criminal defendant be competent to stand trial. The standard for competency to stand trial is well established: a defendant must have “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and a “rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). This standard, as refined in *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 171-72 (1975), and *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 386 (1966), is the constitutional floor for all competency determinations.

The Ninth Circuit erred by imposing a heightened “reasoned choice” standard for competency to waive counsel or plead guilty. This Court has never held that the *Dusky* standard is insufficient for such waivers. To the contrary, the competency inquiry is distinct from the separate question whether a waiver of rights is knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver must be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”). A defendant who meets the *Dusky* standard is capable of understanding the proceedings and consulting with counsel; that capacity necessarily includes the ability to make a reasoned choice among alternatives. The Ninth Circuit’s requirement of a higher level of mental functioning conflates competency with the voluntariness of the waiver, which is a distinct inquiry.

The Court has consistently applied the same competency standard to all phases of a criminal proceeding. In *Drope*, the Court noted that the test for competency to stand trial “must be applied to assure that the defendant has the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense.” 420 U.S. at 171. Nothing in *Drope* or *Pate* suggests that a different, more demanding standard applies when a defendant chooses to waive counsel or plead guilty. Indeed, the *Dusky* standard already requires a “rational understanding” of the proceedings, which encompasses the ability to evaluate options and make decisions.

The Ninth Circuit relied on *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and other circuit cases to create a “reasoned choice” standard. But those decisions are not grounded in this Court’s precedents. This Court has never adopted a heightened competency standard for waivers of constitutional rights. In *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975), the Court held that a defendant who is competent to stand trial may waive counsel, provided the waiver is knowing and intelligent. The Court did not require a separate, higher competency standard. Similarly, in *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242-43 (1969), the Court required that a guilty plea be knowing and voluntary, but did not alter the competency standard.

The *Dusky* standard is sufficient to protect a defendant’s rights. A defendant who meets that standard has the capacity to understand the charges, the consequences of a plea, and the nature of the rights being waived. If the defendant also makes a knowing and voluntary waiver, due process is satisfied. The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard improperly requires a trial court to assess the quality of the defendant’s decision-making, rather than simply his capacity. This Court has never required such an inquiry.

Moreover, the state court’s post-conviction finding that Moran was competent to waive counsel and plead guilty is entitled to deference under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). The state court applied the correct legal standard—the *Dusky* standard—and its factual findings are supported by the record. The Ninth Circuit erred by conducting a de novo review and substituting its own judgment.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
