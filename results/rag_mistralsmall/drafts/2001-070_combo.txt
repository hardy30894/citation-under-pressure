**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” must be interpreted by reference to the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society. Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion).  In assessing those standards, this Court accords substantial deference to the legislative judgments of the several States, but it remains the ultimate arbiter of the Amendment’s meaning. Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 175–76 (1976) (opinion of Stewart, Powell, and Stevens, JJ.).  The Commonwealth submits that the execution of a mentally retarded offender does not offend the Constitution’s core prohibitions, and that the judgment below should be affirmed.

A.  The Court has long recognized that the Eighth Amendment forbids punishments that are “barbaric” or “inconsistent with evolving standards of decency.” Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 135–36 (1879).  In Wilkerson, the Court upheld a sentence of public shooting for first-degree murder, but cautioned that “[d]ifficulty would attend the effort to define with exactness the extent of the constitutional provision which provides that cruel and unusual punishments shall not be inflicted.” Id. at 135.  That caution reflects the Amendment’s textual and structural limits: it does not prohibit all punishments that may be severe, but only those that are “cruel and unusual” in light of contemporary moral standards. See id. at 136.

B.  The Court has repeatedly deferred to legislative judgments about the severity of punishments, including capital punishment, so long as those judgments are not “grossly disproportionate” to the offense. Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740, 752 (1948) (upholding mandatory death penalty for first-degree murder); McElvaine v. Brush, 142 U.S. 155, 167 (1891) (upholding death sentence for first-degree murder).  In Andres, the Court explained that “[t]he Constitution does not require that legislative judgments be based on evidence adduced in court,” id. at 753, and that deference is especially appropriate where the legislature has considered the characteristics of particular offenders. See id. at 752–53.

C.  The Commonwealth’s position is consistent with this Court’s precedents recognizing that mental retardation diminishes culpability but does not eliminate it.  In Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), the Court upheld the execution of a mentally retarded offender, concluding that “neither the text nor the history of the Eighth Amendment” supported a categorical bar. Id. at 335 (plurality opinion).  Although Penry was decided after the Virginia Supreme Court’s decision in this case, the Court’s reasoning in Penry is consistent with its earlier precedents. See, e.g., Andres, 333 U.S. at 752–53 (upholding mandatory death penalty for first-degree murder despite offender’s youth and lack of prior criminal record).

D.  The Court has also recognized that the Eighth Amendment does not require individualized sentencing in all capital cases.  In Andres, the Court upheld a mandatory death sentence for first-degree murder, rejecting the argument that the Constitution requires consideration of mitigating evidence. 333 U.S. at 752–53.  Similarly, in Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605 (1967), the Court upheld a sentencing scheme that permitted consideration of prior convictions not introduced at trial, explaining that “[t]he modern probation report draws on information concerning every aspect of a defendant’s life,” id. at 608, and that “[t]he type and extent of this information make totally impractical if not impossible open court testimony with cross-examination,” id. at 609.  These precedents demonstrate that the Eighth Amendment does not require individualized sentencing in all cases, and certainly not where the legislature has determined that certain categories of offenders remain eligible for the death penalty.

E.  The Commonwealth further submits that the execution of a mentally retarded offender does not violate the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments.”  The Court has long recognized that the Amendment’s reach is limited to punishments that are “barbaric” or “inconsistent with evolving standards of decency.” Wilkerson, 99 U.S. at 135–36.  Mental retardation, while relevant to culpability, does not render an offender categorically ineligible for the death penalty.  The Court’s precedents in this area are consistent with the Commonwealth’s position, and the judgment below should be affirmed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
