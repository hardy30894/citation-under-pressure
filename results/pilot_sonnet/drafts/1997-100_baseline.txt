# ARGUMENT

## THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE BARS RETRIAL OF A PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION IN A NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDING WHERE THE FACTFINDER HAS ALREADY DETERMINED THE EVIDENCE INSUFFICIENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has long recognized that the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That protection does not evaporate merely because the "offense" at issue is a sentencing enhancement rather than the underlying substantive crime. Where, as here, a sentencing proceeding shares the "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence," the Double Jeopardy Clause applies with full force. *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981).

### A. Bullington and Rumsey Establish That Double Jeopardy Protection Turns on the Character of the Proceeding, Not the Label "Capital"

In *Bullington*, this Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause barred the State from seeking the death penalty on retrial after a jury had already imposed a life sentence in the first proceeding. 451 U.S. at 445-46. The Court's reasoning did not rest on the fact that a human life was at stake; it rested on the *procedural character* of the sentencing hearing itself. The Missouri capital sentencing proceeding required the prosecution to prove aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, and the jury's decision "was made in a proceeding that was itself a trial on the issue of punishment." *Id.* at 438. Because that proceeding had the "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence," id. at 439, the Court concluded that the jury's decision amounted to an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes—one that could not be undone by a second attempt to prove the same aggravating facts.

Three years later, in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), the Court reaffirmed and clarified this functional approach. The relevant inquiry, the Court explained, is whether the sentencing proceeding involves characteristics "that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause." *Id.* at 209-10. Nothing in either *Bullington* or *Rumsey* confined this analysis to death-penalty cases as a categorical matter; rather, the capital context in those cases supplied the trial-like procedural safeguards—a burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, the right to present evidence, and a discrete factfinding proceeding separate from the trial on guilt—that triggered double jeopardy protection. The same safeguards are present here.

### B. California's Prior-Conviction Proceeding Bears Every Hallmark of a Trial

California law required the prosecution to prove the truth of Monge's prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt. *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993). California law further guaranteed Monge the right to a jury trial on that allegation, Cal. Penal Code § 1025, the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses, *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal. 3d 857, 863 n.5 (1974), and the application of ordinary rules of evidence, *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 224 (1996). These are precisely the "hallmarks" that led this Court in *Bullington* to treat the capital sentencing hearing as the functional equivalent of a trial. 451 U.S. at 439. A proceeding bounded by a reasonable-doubt standard, a jury right, confrontation, cross-examination, and the rules of evidence is not the sort of open-ended, discretionary sentencing determination this Court described in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), where the sentencing judge was free to consider information "obtained outside the courtroom from persons whom a defendant has not been permitted to confront or cross-examine." *Id.* at 245. *Williams* concerned a due process challenge to informal, discretionary sentencing procedure; it says nothing about the double jeopardy consequences of a state's voluntary choice to erect trial-like procedures for finding sentencing facts. Once a State chooses to try an issue with the full panoply of trial safeguards, the resulting adjudication must carry the same finality that attaches to any other verdict.

### C. Caspari Does Not Foreclose Petitioner's Claim

The Court of Appeal below was not required to disregard *Bullington* on the theory that this Court has confined it to capital cases. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), this Court did not hold that *Bullington*'s reasoning is inapplicable to noncapital proceedings; it held only that, on habeas review, "a reasonable jurist reviewing our precedents" could not have considered such an extension "dictated by our precedents" as of the relevant date. *Id.* at 393. That threshold, non-merits observation, made in the *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), retroactivity context, does not answer the question presented here on direct review: whether a noncapital sentencing proceeding possessing all of *Bullington*'s trial-like hallmarks triggers double jeopardy protection. Similarly, *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28 (1985) (per curiam), addressed a materially different procedural posture and did not purport to limit *Bullington* to capital sentencing as a categorical matter.

### D. The Values Underlying Double Jeopardy Apply With Equal Force Here

The finality interests protected by the Double Jeopardy Clause—shielding a defendant from the "personal strain, public embarrassment, and expense" of repeated proceedings, and from the risk that the State, having failed once, may strengthen its case on retrial—are fully implicated when the State seeks a second opportunity to prove a prior conviction after failing to do so the first time. *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717. The Court of Appeal correctly held that the evidence was constitutionally insufficient; that determination is the functional equivalent of an acquittal, and the Double Jeopardy Clause forbids the State a second trial to cure its own evidentiary failure. This Court should reverse.
