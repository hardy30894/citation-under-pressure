**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE DOES NOT APPLY TO ALIENS WHO RE-ENTERED BEFORE ITS ENACTMENT BECAUSE CONGRESS DID NOT CLEARLY EXPRESS SUCH RETROACTIVE INTENT.**

The settled rule, “sacred” in our jurisprudence, is that statutes are not to be construed retrospectively unless the language “plainly and clearly requires it.” *United States v. Heth*, 3 Cranch 399, 413 (1806). This presumption against retroactivity applies with full force to immigration statutes. *Chew Heong v. United States*, 112 U.S. 536, 559 (1884) (holding that “a statute ought not to be construed to operate retrospectively unless its language clearly indicates that it is intended so to operate”). The reinstatement statute contains no express statement that it applies to aliens who re-entered before its effective date. It speaks only of “aliens who have reentered the United States illegally” in the present tense—a “prospective” construction that respects the “well-settled principle that statutes are not to be given retroactive effect unless such intention is clearly expressed.” *Claridge v. Evans*, 137 U.S. 62, 69 (1890).

The Tenth Circuit erred by inferring retroactive intent from the statute’s broad language. That approach ignores the repeated admonition that “the presumption against retroactivity has consistently been applied to statutes affecting substantive rights.” *United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926). Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 re-entry was a completed act; attaching new legal consequences to that past conduct—including barring any form of relief—alters the legal significance of his prior entry. Nothing in the statute’s text “clearly indicates” such a result. *United States v. The Schooner Peggy*, 1 Cranch 103, 109 (1801) (Story, J.). The Court should therefore hold that § 241(a)(5) applies only to aliens who re-enter after the statute’s effective date.

**II. EVEN IF THE STATUTE APPLIES, SUCH APPLICATION IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE.**

**A. The Statute Impairs Vested Rights.**

By 1997, Fernandez-Vargas had lived in the United States for fifteen years, married a citizen, and applied to adjust his status. The right to seek adjustment of status is a significant benefit that accrues over time—a “vested” interest that Congress cannot retroactively destroy. *Lynch v. United States*, 292 U.S. 571, 577 (1934) (“The Congress of the United States, like the legislatures of the states, cannot take away rights that have been vested.”). The reinstatement statute does not merely impose a new disability; it retroactively extinguishes a right that arose under pre-existing law. *See Miller v. United States*, 294 U.S. 435, 439 (1935) (“Retrospective operation of a statute is not to be inferred.”).

**B. The Statute Attaches New Legal Consequences to Past Conduct.**

The statute retroactively alters the legal effect of Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 re-entry. At the time he re-entered, he was subject only to deportation and could later seek discretionary relief. The new law eliminates that possibility entirely. This is precisely the kind of retroactive effect that the presumption against retroactivity forbids. *Harisiades v. Shaughnessy*, 342 U.S. 580, 594 (1952) (upholding retroactive deportation only because the alien’s continued Communist Party membership after the statute’s enactment constituted new conduct). Here, the conduct—re-entry—was complete long before the statute’s passage. There is no “continuing” act that justifies applying the new law.

**C. The Tenth Circuit Erred in Finding No Protectable Expectation.**

The court below held that Fernandez-Vargas had no “protectable expectation” because he was always subject to deportation. That reasoning conflates the continued possibility of deportation (which existed all along) with the specific remedy of adjustment of status (which Congress later eliminated). The right to adjust status is a substantive protection that the law had provided for years. Retroactively removing that protection violates fundamental fairness and the principle that “retrospective laws are generally unjust.” *Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler*, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.). A noncitizen who lawfully marries a citizen and applies for adjustment relies on the settled legal regime. To retroactively destroy that reliance is “contrary to the natural sense of justice.” *Calder v. Bull*, 3 Dall. 386, 391 (1798) (Chase, J., seriatim) (discussing ex post facto principle, which applies equally to civil retroactivity).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the petition for review should be granted. Congress did not clearly express an intent to apply the reinstatement statute to pre-existing re-entries, and applying it retroactively violates long-standing constitutional norms.
