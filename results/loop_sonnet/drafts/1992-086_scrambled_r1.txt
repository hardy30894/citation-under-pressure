# ARGUMENT

## I. Because Lamb's Chapel Sought to Use School Property for Constitutionally Protected Expression, the District's Exclusion Based on the Religious Content of That Expression Violates the First Amendment.

Government property need not be a street or park to trigger First Amendment scrutiny of content-based exclusions. Once a governmental body opens its property to expressive use by outside groups, it may not selectively bar categories of speech because of the ideas they convey. This principle has deep roots in this Court's recognition that places entrusted for communicative use must remain open on equal terms to those who wish to speak there. See *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147 (1939) (invalidating restrictions on the distribution of literature in public places); *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501 (1946) (recognizing that property held open for public use carries with it First Amendment obligations). The Center Moriches School District, by regularly permitting outside organizations—civic groups, garden clubs, and cultural performers—to use its auditorium after hours, created precisely such a forum for community expression. Having done so, it could not then draw the line at religious speech alone.

That the expression here took the form of a film series does not diminish its constitutional protection. This Court held decades ago that motion pictures are "a significant medium for the communication of ideas" entitled to the same First Amendment protection as the printed or spoken word. *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495, 501 (1952). The District cannot avoid its constitutional obligations by treating a film about family values differently than it would treat a lecture or a live presentation on the identical subject, merely because the film happened to be produced from a religious perspective. See *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940) (recognizing that the liberties secured by the First Amendment protect religious expression from discriminatory suppression by the State). A "function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute," *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), and the District's effort to silence Lamb's Chapel because its message on child-rearing was religiously informed strikes at the very core of that function.

## II. Excluding Religious Speech While Permitting Comparable Secular Speech Is Impermissible Viewpoint Discrimination.

This Court has repeatedly condemned government action denying access to public facilities specifically because the excluded speaker's message was religious, while similar secular uses were tolerated. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court struck down the denial of a park permit to Jehovah's Witnesses where other groups, religious and secular alike, had been permitted to use the same park, and the denial rested on disagreement with the group's religious views rather than any neutral, generally applicable standard. The Court reached the same result in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), invalidating the discriminatory application of a park ordinance against a Jehovah's Witness religious meeting where other groups conducting similar gatherings were left undisturbed.

This Court's decisions likewise condemn licensing schemes that vest officials with discretion to suppress disfavored speech. In *Staub v. City of Baxley*, 355 U.S. 313, 322 (1958), the Court explained that "an ordinance which ... makes the peaceful enjoyment of freedoms which the Constitution guarantees contingent upon the uncontrolled will of an official ... is an unconstitutional censorship or prior restraint upon the enjoyment of those freedoms." The District's unwritten practice of approving secular applications while categorically rejecting anything "church related" reflects exactly the sort of standardless, discriminatory discretion this Court has consistently invalidated.

The record here establishes the same infirmity condemned in *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, and *Staub*. The District permitted the Southern Harmonizers to perform, allowed a library-sponsored lecture series touching on "spiritual" and "metaphysical" topics, and opened its facilities to numerous civic organizations—yet drew the line categorically at anything with religious content. That the District's own clerk repeatedly characterized Lamb's Chapel's film application as being refused because it "does appear to be church related" is itself an admission of the viewpoint discrimination these decisions forbid.

## III. Public School Property Is Not an Enclave Where Officials May Suppress Disfavored Viewpoints.

This Court has long insisted that publicly funded educational institutions remain subject to the constraints of the First Amendment when they regulate expression on their premises. As this Court explained, "state-operated schools may not be enclaves of totalitarianism," and "it can hardly be argued that either students or teachers shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate." *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503, 506, 511 (1969). If students and teachers retain their expressive rights on school premises, the District cannot claim greater license to exclude community speakers from a forum it has voluntarily opened to outside groups. Nor does the associational and expressive character of the group seeking access lose protection simply because it is a religious congregation rather than a civic club; the right to advocate one's views collectively is itself protected activity. See *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 430 (1963).

## CONCLUSION

The District's blanket exclusion of religious speech from a forum otherwise open to comparable secular expression cannot be reconciled with *Schneider*, *Marsh*, *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, *Staub*, *Burstyn*, *Terminiello*, or *Tinker*. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
