# ARGUMENT

## I. Florida's Capital Sentencing Scheme Satisfies Ring v. Arizona Because a Unanimous Jury Found the Aggravating Circumstance Exposing Hurst to Death.

*Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), held only that "the Sixth Amendment requires that the aggravating factors necessary for imposition of the death penalty be found by a jury." *Id.* at 609. Arizona's scheme violated that rule because a judge alone, "sitting without a jury," found the aggravator that made the defendant eligible for death. *Id.* Florida's scheme presents no such defect on these facts. The jury unanimously convicted Hurst of first-degree murder committed during a robbery—the very felony that constitutes the statutory aggravating circumstance under Fla. Stat. § 921.141(5)(d). That guilty verdict necessarily found, beyond a reasonable doubt, the fact that exposed Hurst to a sentence greater than life imprisonment. *Cf. Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 494 (2000) (Sixth Amendment concern arises only when a sentence exceeds "the maximum he would receive if punished according to the facts reflected in the jury verdict alone"). Because the jury's own verdict supplied the constitutionally required aggravator, no judicial factfinding "expose[d] the defendant to a greater punishment than that authorized by the jury's guilty verdict." *Id.* at 494.

## II. Ring Did Not Overrule Spaziano and Hildwin, Which Control Florida's Advisory-Jury Structure.

This Court has twice sustained Florida's hybrid jury-recommendation/judicial-sentencing structure against Sixth Amendment attack. *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 465 (1984), held that "the Sixth Amendment does not require that the specific findings authorizing the imposition of the sentence of death be made by the jury." *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640-41 (1989), reaffirmed that "the Sixth Amendment does not require that the specific findings authorizing the imposition of a sentence of death be made by a jury," because "the additional findings required for the imposition of the death penalty may be made by the court." *Ring* expressly overruled only *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), "to the extent that it allows a sentencing judge, sitting without a jury, to find an aggravating circumstance necessary for imposition of the death penalty." *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609. *Ring* did not purport to disturb *Spaziano* or *Hildwin*, because Arizona's scheme, unlike Florida's, afforded no jury role whatsoever in sentencing. The continued vitality of *Spaziano* and *Hildwin* forecloses Hurst's Sixth Amendment claim.

## III. Neither the Sixth Amendment Nor Ring Requires Jury Unanimity in a Capital Sentencing Recommendation.

The Sixth Amendment's jury-trial guarantee, applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, does not itself require unanimous verdicts. *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404, 406 (1972) (plurality) (concluding that "unanimity is not requisite" under the Sixth Amendment as incorporated against the States); *Johnson v. Louisiana*, 406 U.S. 356, 362 (1972) (upholding non-unanimous verdicts against due process and equal protection challenge). If unanimity is not constitutionally compelled for a jury's verdict of guilt on the substantive offense, it cannot be compelled, a fortiori, for an advisory sentencing recommendation that the judge alone is empowered to weigh under Fla. Stat. § 921.141. Nothing in *Ring* addressed jury unanimity; *Ring* concerned only who—judge or jury—may find an aggravator, not by what vote a jury must recommend a sentence.

## IV. The Eighth Amendment Permits Florida to Assign the Mental-Retardation Determination to the Trial Court.

*Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), held that the Eighth Amendment bars execution of the mentally retarded, but this Court expressly "le[ft] to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction upon [their] execution of sentences." *Id.* at 317. Florida has done so through Fla. Stat. § 921.137 and Fla. R. Crim. P. 3.203, which channel the *Atkins* determination to the trial judge under a clear-and-convincing-evidence standard. This Court confirmed that States retain broad latitude in selecting the factfinder and procedure for *Atkins* claims when it summarily reversed a court of appeals for imposing a jury-trial requirement on Arizona's *Atkins* procedure. *Schriro v. Smith*, 546 U.S. 6, 7 (2005) (per curiam) ("we have made clear that the Constitution allows States to take steps to ensure reliable jury findings of mental retardation," but "the Ninth Circuit had no authority" to dictate the mechanism). Nothing in *Atkins* or *Ring* extends the Sixth Amendment's jury-trial guarantee to this Eighth Amendment eligibility question, which is conceptually distinct from the aggravating-circumstance findings *Ring* addressed—mental retardation operates as a categorical bar to execution, not as a fact increasing the available punishment. Florida's assignment of that determination to the court, applying a heightened clear-and-convincing standard, more than satisfies the "heightened reliability" the Eighth Amendment demands. *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987).

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, Florida's capital sentencing scheme, as applied to Hurst, violates neither the Sixth nor the Eighth Amendment. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Florida should be affirmed.
