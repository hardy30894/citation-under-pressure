**ARGUMENT**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment or the Eighth Amendment. The jury’s role is advisory, and the judge makes the ultimate findings of aggravating factors and the sentence. This Court has repeatedly upheld such schemes, and *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), does not compel a different result.

**I. Florida’s Advisory Jury Verdict Satisfies the Sixth Amendment**

The Sixth Amendment requires that any fact increasing the maximum penalty be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000). In *Ring*, this Court applied *Apprendi* to Arizona’s capital scheme, holding that aggravating factors must be found by a jury because they are “the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense.” 536 U.S. at 609. But Florida’s scheme is materially different. In Florida, the jury renders an advisory recommendation; the judge independently finds aggravating and mitigating circumstances and imposes sentence. *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 464 (1984) (upholding Florida’s advisory jury system). This Court has consistently affirmed that “the Sixth Amendment does not require that the jury specify the aggravating factors” or that its verdict be unanimous. *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640-41 (1989) (per curiam); *Harris v. Alabama*, 513 U.S. 504, 511-12 (1995) (rejecting claim that advisory jury verdict must be unanimous). *Ring* did not overrule these precedents. *Ring* distinguished Florida’s scheme, noting that in Arizona the judge alone found aggravators, whereas in Florida the jury participates. 536 U.S. at 608 n.6. Because the jury’s recommendation is not binding, the judge’s independent fact-finding does not violate the Sixth Amendment. *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 648-49 (1990) (approving judge-finding of aggravators), *overruled on other grounds by Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Unanimity or Jury Determination of Mental Retardation**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment but does not mandate specific procedural requirements for capital sentencing. *Kansas v. Marsh*, 548 U.S. 163, 173-74 (2006) (states have “substantial discretion” in capital procedures). This Court has never held that jury unanimity is constitutionally required for a death sentence. *See Harris*, 513 U.S. at 511-12. Florida’s non-unanimous advisory verdict does not offend the heightened reliability demanded by the Eighth Amendment because the judge independently reviews the evidence and must find aggravators beyond a reasonable doubt. *See Buchanan v. Angelone*, 522 U.S. 269, 275-76 (1998) (Eighth Amendment requires individualized sentencing, not specific jury procedures).

Regarding mental retardation, *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002), held that executing the mentally retarded violates the Eighth Amendment but left to the states “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” Florida has done so by requiring the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence in a post-sentencing hearing. *See Schriro v. Smith*, 546 U.S. 6, 7 (2005) (per curiam) (states may choose procedures for Atkins claims). The jury need not make that determination; the judge’s finding suffices. *Oregon v. Guzek*, 546 U.S. 517, 525-26 (2006) (states may limit evidence at sentencing). Hurst received a full evidentiary hearing, and the trial court found he was not mentally retarded. That comports with the Eighth Amendment.

**III. Florida’s Scheme Is Not an Outlier in Any Constitutionally Relevant Sense**

Petitioner argues Florida is an outlier, but this Court has never held that outlier status alone violates the Constitution. *Marsh*, 548 U.S. at 178-79. The Sixth and Eighth Amendments set a floor, not a ceiling. Florida’s scheme provides robust protections: the jury must find the defendant guilty of first-degree murder, the judge must find at least one aggravator beyond a reasonable doubt, and the sentence is subject to proportionality review. *See Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 460-61. The absence of jury unanimity on aggravators does not render the scheme unconstitutional.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
