# ARGUMENT

## AMENDMENT 73's BALLOT-ACCESS RESTRICTIONS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY ADD TO THE QUALIFICATIONS FOR CONGRESS FIXED BY THE FRAMERS

### I. The Constitution Establishes Fixed, Exclusive Qualifications for Membership in Congress

The Constitution specifies that Representatives must be twenty-five years old, seven years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the state; Senators must be thirty years old, nine years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the state. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, 3. This Court has long recognized that the offices of Senator and Representative are creatures of the Constitution itself, not of state law. As this Court explained in *Wiley v. Sinkler*, 179 U.S. 58 (1900), "the right to vote for a member of Congress does not depend on the Constitution" of any State, because "[t]he office . . . is created by that Constitution and by that alone." *Id.* The Constitution "declares how it shall be filled, namely, by election," and while States may prescribe voter qualifications by reference to their own legislative electorate, they "cannot prescribe the qualification for voters for those *eo nomine*." *Id.* The same principle applies with even greater force to the qualifications of candidates themselves: because Congress is a national institution "created by" the federal Constitution alone, the qualifications for its membership cannot be supplemented by fifty different sovereigns.

This structural understanding finds further support in *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), which held that the House could not add qualifications beyond those enumerated in Article I when judging the qualifications of its own members. If Congress itself lacks the power to add qualifications to the constitutional list, it follows *a fortiori* that individual States lack that power. The Framers, at the Constitutional Convention, considered and rejected proposals for durational limits on congressional service. As the Arkansas Supreme Court itself recognized below, "the action finally taken by the framers of the constitution, following exhaustive debates, is strong evidence that term limits for senators and representatives was rejected." That deliberate constitutional choice cannot be undone by a single State's initiative process.

### II. Permitting States to Impose Term-Limit Ballot Bars Would Destroy the Uniformity the Framers Demanded

The structure of Article I contemplates a single, national qualification standard, precisely because Members of Congress serve national, not merely local, interests. As the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly observed, "[c]ongressional officeholders partake of the same national character as the President of the United States. Members of Congress pass laws which affect not only their own state, but all the states." Because "[t]he rules which govern their qualifications are contained in the Constitution of the United States," "[u]niformity of qualifications is paramount, and individual states are not free to engraft variations."

This Court's decision in *Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham*, 279 U.S. 597 (1929), underscores that the Constitution itself allocates to the Senate — not to the States — the authority to judge qualifications and elections of its Members. The Court there emphasized that the Senate's authority to exclude persons "who either had not been elected or . . . had been elected by resort to fraud, bribery, corruption, or other sinister methods" was rooted in constitutional text, not state law. *Id.* If the Senate's own power to police membership must be traced to specific constitutional text, then a State's purported power to add disqualifying criteria — criteria nowhere mentioned in the Constitution — has no textual foundation whatsoever.

Nor can Amendment 73 be salvaged by its ballot-access framing. Arkansas's proponents attempted to characterize § 3 as a mere restriction on ballot access rather than an outright qualification, but as the Arkansas Supreme Court recognized, "the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp" — and, this Court should hold, too fine a point to escape the constitutional prohibition. A state provision that renders a twice-elected Senator or thrice-elected Representative permanently ineligible to appear on the ballot functions, in every practical sense, as an additional qualification for office. Denial of ballot access is denial of the practical ability to serve; substance, not form, must control.

### III. The Amendment Process Set Forth in Article V Is the Exclusive Mechanism for Altering Congressional Qualifications

This Court's decision in *Hawke v. Smith (No. 1)*, 253 U.S. 221 (1920), confirms that where the Constitution assigns a function to a specifically designated process, the States may not substitute a different mechanism of their own devising. There, Ohio attempted to subject ratification of a federal constitutional amendment to a state referendum. This Court held that Article V's assignment of the ratification function to state "legislatures" was exclusive, and that Ohio's attempt to graft an additional procedural requirement onto that constitutionally specified process was invalid. The same logic controls here: Article V provides the exclusive method for altering the qualifications the Framers wrote into Article I, §§ 2 and 3. A state constitutional amendment — however popular, however well-intentioned — is not a permissible substitute for the Article V amendment process when the subject is the qualifications for federal office.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court insofar as it held Section 3 of Amendment 73 unconstitutional as an impermissible addition to the qualifications for United States Senator and Representative fixed exclusively by the United States Constitution.
