# ARGUMENT

## I. A PRIVATE INDIVIDUAL LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE A FEDERAL CRIMINAL STATUTE ON TENTH AMENDMENT GROUNDS

### A. This Court's Precedent Forecloses Bond's Claim

The Tenth Amendment protects the sovereign prerogatives of the States, not the personal liberty interests of private citizens. This Court settled the question of who may invoke that protection in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). There, private utility companies argued that a federal agency's sale of electric power invaded a domain reserved to the States. This Court rejected the challenge, holding that the companies, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." *Id.* at 144. That holding directly controls here. Bond, like the petitioners in *Tennessee Electric*, is a private party attempting to assert a structural constitutional limitation that belongs to the States as sovereign entities, not to individuals.

This Court's decision in *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), reinforces the principle that federalism-based objections to federal action are the province of the States themselves. This Court there refused to entertain a taxpayer's generalized grievance about the allocation of authority between the state and federal governments, explaining that such disputes are appropriately resolved through the political relationship between sovereigns rather than through private litigation. The logic of *Mellon* applies with equal force to Bond's Tenth Amendment claim: the alleged injury to state sovereignty is the State's injury to raise or waive, not an individual's.

### B. Ordinary Principles of Prudential and Third-Party Standing Confirm This Result

Even apart from *Tennessee Electric*, general standing doctrine bars Bond's claim. This Court has long held that a litigant "generally must assert his own legal rights and interests, and cannot rest his claim to relief on the legal rights or interests of third parties." *Warth v. Seldin*, 422 U.S. 490, 499 (1975). The right Bond seeks to vindicate—the States' retained sovereignty under the Tenth Amendment—belongs to the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, not to her. Pennsylvania has never objected to the federal prosecution of Bond's conduct, nor has it suggested that § 229 encroaches upon its sovereign authority. Bond cannot manufacture standing to litigate a right that the actual rights-holder has declined to assert.

This Court has also made clear that jurisdictional standing questions must be resolved before reaching the merits of any constitutional claim. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998) ("The requirement that jurisdiction be established as a threshold matter . . . is inflexible and without exception."). Because Bond cannot establish that she, rather than the State, suffered the sovereignty-based injury the Tenth Amendment addresses, her claim fails at the threshold.

### C. The Weight of Circuit Authority Supports Denying Standing to Private Litigants

The substantial majority of the courts of appeals to consider this question have followed *Tennessee Electric* and denied private parties standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims. The Tenth Circuit held that a criminal defendant could not challenge a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds because "the Tenth Amendment protects state sovereignty rather than individual rights." *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284 (10th Cir. 2004). The Eighth Circuit reached the identical conclusion in *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525–27 (8th Cir. 2009), as did the Ninth Circuit in *Oregon v. Legal Services Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971–72 (9th Cir. 2009), and the Second Circuit in *Brooklyn Legal Services Corp. v. Legal Services Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006). The First Circuit likewise held in *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33–36 (1st Cir. 2005), that a private plaintiff lacked standing, explaining that recognizing such claims would allow individuals to interject themselves into disputes over the federal-state balance of power that properly belong to the sovereigns themselves.

This consensus reflects sound structural reasoning. Permitting any criminal defendant to relitigate the scope of federal power under the guise of a Tenth Amendment defense would transform every federal prosecution into an occasion for freestanding constitutional adjudication untethered to any personal right of the defendant. That is precisely the outcome *Tennessee Electric* forecloses.

### D. Denying Bond Standing Does Not Leave Federalism Values Unprotected

Denying individual standing does not render the Tenth Amendment a dead letter. As the First Circuit observed, "the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state's policy of acquiescence." *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35. Pennsylvania remains free to challenge § 229 directly, to decline to cooperate with its enforcement, or to lobby for its repeal through ordinary political channels. Nothing about denying Bond standing forecloses the vindication of federalism values through the proper institutional actor—the State itself, acting through its officers.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
