# ARGUMENT

**Judicial Review of a Board Decision Dismissing a Mixed Case for Lack of Jurisdiction Lies in District Court**

## I. The Plain Text of the Civil Service Reform Act Defines a "Mixed Case" by the Nature of the Employee's Allegations, Not by the Board's Ultimate Ruling

The statute directs district-court review whenever an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the [Board]" and "alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination." 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). Nothing in that text conditions the forum on whether the Board *agrees* it possesses jurisdiction. This Court has long instructed that "the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Perry alleged discrimination arising from an action—forced retirement and suspension—of the very type Congress made appealable to the Board. That the Board ultimately found the action voluntary does not change what Perry "alleges"; it resolves a contested question the statute commits, in the first instance, to a coordinated scheme of review.

## II. Congress's Evident Purpose Requires a Coordinated, Not Bifurcated, Scheme of Review

Statutory words are read in light of legislative purpose. "There is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, Inc., 310 U.S. 534, 542 (1940). Congress divided review between the Federal Circuit and the district courts precisely so that discrimination claims—claims requiring fact-finding, credibility determinations, and the tools of trial courts—would be heard by tribunals equipped for that task, while routine civil-service questions would receive uniform appellate treatment. Splitting review based on the fortuity of *how* the Board disposed of the case frustrates that considered division of labor. This Court has cautioned that administrative and judicial review provisions must be read so that "the whole scheme" Congress enacted is not rendered unworkable by a construction Congress could not have intended. Federal Communications Comm'n v. Pottsville Broadcasting Co., 309 U.S. 134, 143–46 (1940). A rule sending discrimination claims to the Federal Circuit whenever the Board mislabels its disposition "jurisdictional" produces exactly the kind of unintended, incoherent scheme Pottsville forbids.

## III. Remedial Statutes Protecting Employees from Discrimination Must Be Liberally Construed in Favor of Coverage

Where a statute exists to protect employees from discriminatory treatment, this Court has consistently construed it generously toward the class it was designed to protect, resisting narrow or technical readings that would defeat coverage. See Tennessee Coal, Iron & R. Co. v. Muscoda Local No. 123, 321 U.S. 590, 597 (1944). The same principle animates decisions construing "employee" broadly under remedial labor legislation to effectuate Congress's protective purpose rather than to exclude claimants on formalistic grounds. See NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 129–31 (1944). Perry's discrimination claim is no less a discrimination claim because the Board, before ever reaching it, decided that his retirement was "voluntary." A jurisdictional label should not be permitted to strip a discrimination claimant of the forum Congress built for discrimination claims.

## IV. The Board's Characterization of Its Own Jurisdiction Is Not Controlling on the Question of Forum

An agency's self-serving characterization of the case before it is entitled only to so much weight as its reasoning independently commands. As this Court explained, the weight due an agency's judgment "will depend upon the thoroughness evident in its consideration, the validity of its reasoning, its consistency with earlier and later pronouncements, and all those factors which give it power to persuade, if lacking power to control." Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944). The Board's decision to call its own dismissal "jurisdictional" rather than "procedural" or "on the merits" cannot, by that label alone, dictate which Article III court reviews the very ruling being challenged.

## V. The Substance of the Claim, Not the Tribunal's Label, Must Control the Forum Inquiry

This Court has long distinguished the substance of a claim from the label a tribunal affixes to its disposition of that claim. "Jurisdiction . . . is not defeated . . . by the possibility that the averments might fail to state a cause of action on which [the claimant] could actually recover." Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678, 682 (1946). So too here: the voluntariness question the Board resolved against Perry is inseparable from his discrimination and coercion allegations—the very allegations that, in substance, make this a discrimination case. Treating the Board's jurisdictional ruling as if it transformed the case's substance elevates form over the underlying claim that this Court's precedents forbid.

## VI. A Single, Coordinated Forum Avoids the Costs of Piecemeal, Fragmented Litigation

Finally, sound judicial administration disfavors fragmenting a single controversy into successive proceedings in different courts applying different standards of review. This Court has recognized the practical costs and confusion that piecemeal, bifurcated review imposes on litigants and courts alike. Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). Because the voluntariness of Perry's retirement is bound up with his discrimination allegations, sending the case to the Federal Circuit—which lacks authority to adjudicate the discrimination claim on the merits—guarantees a second proceeding in district court if the Federal Circuit rejects the Board's jurisdictional ruling. Unified review in district court avoids that waste.

For these reasons, the decision below should be reversed.
