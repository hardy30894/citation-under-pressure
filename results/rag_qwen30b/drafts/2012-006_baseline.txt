**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period when the defendant has withdrawn from the conspiracy prior to that period—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between the constitutional right to a fair trial and the government’s burden of proof. The lower court’s decision, which affirmed the conviction despite the defendants’ withdrawal from the conspiracy before the limitations period began, rests on a flawed understanding of the government’s burden in conspiracy prosecutions. The government may not shift the burden of proof to the defendant simply because the conspiracy allegedly continued beyond the point of withdrawal. The Constitution demands that the government prove every element of the offense, including membership in the conspiracy during the relevant time, beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (“[T]he Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.”).

The government’s argument in the lower court—that the burden shifts to the defendant to prove withdrawal—contradicts settled precedent. In *Emich Motors Corp. v. General Motors Corp.*, 340 U.S. 558, 565 (1951), this Court held that “the judgment [of a criminal conviction] is prima facie evidence of the conspiracy and also of the performance of such acts in accomplishing it as the jury in the criminal case, in rendering a verdict of guilty, necessarily found to have occurred.” This principle underscores that the government bears the burden of proving the existence and scope of a conspiracy, including the defendant’s participation during the relevant period. The jury’s verdict, which is based on the government’s evidence, cannot be used to shift the burden to the defendant to disprove participation. To hold otherwise would violate the *Winship* standard and permit the government to rely on a defendant’s silence or failure to rebut a presumption of continued membership, which is impermissible.

Moreover, the government’s position is inconsistent with the nature of conspiracy as a continuing offense. A conspiracy is not a single act but a series of agreements and actions taken over time. *McDaniel v. Traylor*, 212 U.S. 428, 435 (1909), recognized that “the essence of the suit is the alleged fraudulent combination and conspiracy” to achieve a common goal. But the government cannot prove a conspiracy that allegedly continued into the limitations period unless it proves that the defendant was still a party to that agreement during that time. If a defendant withdraws, the government must prove that the withdrawal was ineffective or that the defendant re-entered the conspiracy. *See* *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 401 U.S. 321, 330 (1971) (holding that the statute of limitations may be tolled only if the defendant’s conduct “continues” to affect the plaintiff’s rights). The government cannot assume that withdrawal is ineffective merely because the conspiracy allegedly continued.

The lower court’s reliance on the absence of evidence of a split in the conspiracy is misplaced. The government’s burden is not to prove that the conspiracy continued, but to prove that the defendant was a member of it during the relevant period. *Price v. Henkel*, 216 U.S. 488, 495 (1910), affirmed that “the probative weight of certified copies of the New York indictments is necessarily limited to such counts as are identical in the two sets of indictments.” Similarly, the government’s evidence of a continuing conspiracy cannot be used to infer continued membership unless it specifically connects the defendant to that period. The jury was not instructed that the government had to prove the defendant’s membership during the limitations period, nor was it given a standard for evaluating whether withdrawal was effective. This failure deprived the defendants of a fair trial.

Finally, the government’s argument that the burden shifts to the defendant to prove withdrawal is contrary to the principle that “the burden of proof is on the prosecution to prove every element of the offense.” *Howard v. Fleming*, 191 U.S. 126, 132 (1903). The defendant’s mere presence at the beginning of a conspiracy does not establish continued membership. The government must prove that the defendant remained a participant. *Partmar Corp. v. Paramount Pictures Theatres Corp.*, 347 U.S. 89, 93 (1954), held that “the judgment in the case of United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc., 334 U.S. 131, which, it argues, would provide, under § 5 of the Clayton Act, prima facie evidence of the conspiracy on which the counterclaims were based.” But that evidence was not conclusive; it was merely prima facie. The same applies here: the government’s evidence of a continuing conspiracy is prima facie, not conclusive, and the burden remains on it to prove the defendant’s continued participation beyond a reasonable doubt.

For these reasons, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. The lower court erred in affirming the conviction without requiring the government to meet this burden. The judgment should be reversed.
