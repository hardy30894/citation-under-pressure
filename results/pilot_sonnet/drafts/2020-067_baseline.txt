# ARGUMENT

## I. Rule 52(b)'s Plain-Error Standard Permits Reviewing Courts To Consult the Entire Record, Not Merely the Evidence Presented to the Jury

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) permits appellate courts to correct forfeited errors only where the defendant demonstrates: (1) an error, (2) that is plain, (3) that "affect[s] substantial rights," and (4) that "seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). This Court has repeatedly confirmed that a defendant bears the burden on the third and fourth prongs, and that in carrying that burden, "there is no reason to warp the intended effect of Rule 52(b) by holding a court of appeals to a truncated version of the record." *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 74 (2002).

*Vonn* is directly controlling. There, this Court held that when a defendant fails to object to a Rule 11 colloquy error, "a silent defendant has the burden to satisfy the plain-error rule and ... a reviewing court may consult the whole record when considering the effect of any error on substantial rights." *Id.* The Court reasoned that Rule 52(b) contains no textual limitation confining review to the evidence adduced before the factfinder, and that artificially blinding appellate courts to readily available record evidence would frustrate the "practical" purpose of plain-error review—conserving the extraordinary remedy of reversal for cases where errors genuinely undermined the proceeding's reliability. *Id.* at 59, 74. That logic applies with full force to a Rehaif error. Just as the *Vonn* defendant's plea-colloquy record contained information bearing on prejudice beyond the four corners of the colloquy itself, Greer's record—including his prior undisputed felony convictions, sentences served, and his own representations during plea negotiations—illuminates whether the omitted knowledge-of-status element could plausibly have been contested.

## II. This Court's Precedents Confirm That Overwhelming, Uncontested Record Evidence Defeats a Showing That an Omitted Element Affected the Outcome

This Court has consistently looked past the four corners of the jury's evidentiary universe to assess whether an unobjected-to instructional error affected the outcome. In *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461 (1997), the district court failed to submit the element of materiality to the jury in a perjury prosecution. This Court held that because the evidence of materiality was "overwhelming" and "essentially uncontroverted," the error did not satisfy the fourth prong of plain-error review, explaining that "it is the rare case in which an improper instruction will justify reversal of a criminal conviction when no objection has been made in the trial court." *Id.* at 470 (quoting *Henderson v. Kibbe*, 431 U.S. 145, 154 (1977)). The Court did not require that the omitted element have been proven through evidence formally presented to the jury on that issue; it asked whether the record as a whole demonstrated the element was not genuinely in dispute.

*United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625 (2002), reinforces this approach. There, a drug-quantity element was omitted from the indictment—an error analogous in structure to the omission here, since both concern an element never presented for the jury's determination. This Court held that because "the evidence that the defendants were involved in a vast drug conspiracy ... was 'overwhelming' and 'essentially uncontroverted,'" the omission did not seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. *Id.* at 633 (quoting *Johnson*, 520 U.S. at 470). Critically, the Court reached this conclusion notwithstanding that the quantity finding, like the knowledge finding here, was never tested by adversarial submission to the jury—precisely because plain-error review is not a vehicle for relitigating facts that the defendant has never disputed and could not plausibly dispute.

The standard governing the third prong—effect on "substantial rights"—likewise requires a probabilistic assessment informed by the whole record. In *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74 (2004), this Court held that a defendant "must show a reasonable probability that, but for the error, he would not have entered the plea." *Id.* at 83. That "reasonable probability" inquiry, by its nature, requires courts to examine everything bearing on what the defendant knew and would have argued—not an artificially constricted trial record.

## III. Confining Review to the Trial Record Would Convert Plain-Error Review Into Automatic Reversal, Contrary to Olano's Design

Rule 52(b) review exists precisely to prevent forfeited errors from producing windfall reversals where the error could not plausibly have changed the result. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 732, cautions that relief under the Rule's fourth prong is reserved for circumstances amounting to a "miscarriage of justice," and that appellate discretion to notice forfeited error "is to be used sparingly." A rule barring courts from consulting undisputed evidence of a defendant's own criminal history—evidence he himself stipulated to at sentencing—would guarantee reversal in every Rehaif case regardless of whether the defendant could genuinely contest knowledge of his felon status. That result cannot be squared with *Vonn*'s command to consult "the whole record," 535 U.S. at 74, nor with *Johnson* and *Cotton*'s insistence that plain-error relief track genuine, not hypothetical, prejudice. The Eleventh Circuit's approach—examining Greer's undisputed record of multiple felony convictions and years of imprisonment served—was consistent with this settled framework and should be affirmed.
