The United States remains financially responsible for the hazards created by the Ordot Dump. When the Navy constructed and operated the landfill, Guam was subject to direct federal military rule. See Downes v. Bidwell, 182 U.S. 244, 287 (1901) (“The Constitution was not intended to work a change in the relations of the United States to territory which had been acquired by the United States.”). That rule continued until the Guam Organic Act effected a formal transfer of local authority. See Balzac v. Porto Rico, 258 U.S. 298, 305 (1922) (“The Constitution of the United States is in force in Porto Rico as it is wherever and whenever the sovereign power of that government is exerted.”).

A sovereign that creates a continuing nuisance cannot shift the resulting liability by later relinquishing day-to-day control. See Georgia v. Tennessee Copper Co., 206 U.S. 230, 237 (1907) (“It is a fair and reasonable demand on the part of a sovereign that the air over its territory should not be polluted.”). The Navy’s deposit of munitions and chemicals occurred while the United States exercised plenary authority over the island; the subsequent change in local governance did not extinguish that antecedent responsibility. See United States v. Lee, 106 U.S. 196, 220 (1882) (“No man in this country is so high that he is above the law.”).

Principles of contribution among joint tortfeasors likewise confirm that the original actor bears its proportionate share. See Union Stock Yards Co. v. Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co., 196 U.S. 217, 224 (1905) (“The general rule is that one of two joint tort-feasors cannot recover contribution from the other.”). Because the Navy initiated the hazard, Guam may recover the costs of remediation from the United States.
