**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The National Labor Relations Act does not expressly address employer polling of incumbent union support. The Board’s current standard—permitting a poll only when the employer already possesses objective evidence sufficient to justify withdrawing recognition—defeats the very purpose of polling. An employer who already has enough evidence to withdraw recognition has no need for a poll; polling serves only to confirm a doubt that the employer already holds. *See Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989) (Board acknowledged that polling “can then act with confidence and certainty”). Yet the Board insists on the same evidentiary threshold for both actions. This is irrational.

Three circuits have correctly rejected this standard. The Fifth Circuit held that an employer may poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support (even if that evidence is not sufficient by itself to justify withdrawal).” *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981) (citing *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)). The Ninth Circuit agreed, holding that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for an employer “in sincere doubt,” and that the proper standard is “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient by itself to justify withdrawal of recognition.” *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). The Sixth Circuit likewise recognized that an employer faces a dilemma: refusing to bargain with a union that lacks majority support is an unfair labor practice, but bargaining with a minority union also violates § 8(a)(2). *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). Polling allows the employer to resolve that dilemma without risking either violation. *Id.*

The Board’s own reasoning in *Texas Petrochemicals* is internally inconsistent. The Board stated that RM elections are the “preferred method” for testing union support, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061, yet it applies the same evidentiary standard to polling—a less protective procedure—as to RM elections. If the Board truly favors elections, it should require a *higher* showing for polling, not an identical one. Alternatively, if the same standard is appropriate for polling and RM elections, then the Board’s standard for withdrawal of recognition—a unilateral employer action with no safeguards—should be *more stringent*, not equivalent. The Board’s failure to reconcile these anomalies undermines its claim to reasoned decisionmaking.

This Court has recognized that the Board’s primary responsibility for developing national labor policy does not immunize its rules from review. *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990). When the Board’s rule is arbitrary and defeats the statutory purpose of stable collective bargaining, deference is not warranted. *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 42 (1987) (deference is appropriate only when the Board’s rule is “rational and consistent with the Act”). The Board’s polling standard fails that test.

**II. The Board’s Factual Finding That Petitioner Lacked Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

Even under the Board’s own standard, the record compels a finding that Allentown Mack had a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status. The evidence must be upheld only if supported by “substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole.” *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Here, the evidence overwhelmingly demonstrates that the employer had objective reasons to doubt majority support.

At least seven employees made unambiguous statements repudiating the union. The Board discounted others, but its reasoning was flawed. For example, the Board refused to credit statements made during job interviews because the employer had indicated it would be non-union. Yet the Board’s own precedent, *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), enforced, 590 F.2d 4 (5th Cir. 1978), does not require automatic disregard of such statements. And the Board’s rejection of employee statements of dissatisfaction with union representation as insufficient to show opposition to representation, see *Destileria Serralles, Inc.*, 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988), enforced, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir. 1989), ignores common sense: an employee who feels he is “not getting his money’s worth” from the union is likely to vote against it.

The Board also improperly dismissed secondhand reports from employees about the views of others. In *Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 868, the Sixth Circuit reversed a similar Board finding where the employer had evidence that approximately one-fourth of the unit employees had expressed negative views. Here, the proportion is even higher. Moreover, the union had never been certified for the new unit of 32 employees; the presumption of majority support is weaker when the unit is a successor unit drawn from a larger group. *See Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 36-41.

The overwhelming evidence of employee disaffection, combined with the result of the secret-ballot poll supervised by a neutral third party in which 19 of 32 employees voted against the union, leaves no doubt that the employer’s doubt was reasonable. The Board’s contrary conclusion is arbitrary and capricious.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Board’s order should be reversed.
