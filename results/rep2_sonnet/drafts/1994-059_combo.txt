**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Qualifications Clauses of Article I Are Exclusive, and No State May Add to Them.**

The Constitution fixes, with precision, the qualifications for membership in the House of Representatives and the Senate: age, citizenship, and inhabitancy. Art. I, §2, cl. 2; Art. I, §3, cl. 3. This Court has already confirmed that these enumerated qualifications are exclusive even as against Congress itself. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that the House could not exclude a duly elected member for reasons beyond the three qualifications listed in the Constitution, explaining that "the Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution." *Id.* at 522. If Congress—the very body whose membership is at issue—lacks power to supplement the constitutional qualifications, a fortiori the States, which are strangers to the internal governance of the federal legislature, lack that power as well.

Amendment 73's Section 3 does precisely what *Powell* forbids: it adds a qualification—freedom from prior multi-term service—that nowhere appears in Article I. That the Amendment achieves this result through ballot-access mechanics rather than an outright bar changes nothing. A restriction that forecloses an otherwise-qualified candidate from appearing on the ballot is, in substance, an additional qualification for office, not a procedural regulation of the mechanics of the election.

**II. Congress Is a National Institution Whose Qualifications Must Be Uniform and Fixed by the National Charter, Not by Fifty Separate State Enactments.**

The Framers designed the House and Senate as instruments of a single national government, not as fifty distinct state institutions. In *Wesberry v. Sanders*, 376 U.S. 1 (1964), this Court traced the constitutional debates establishing that Representatives were to be chosen "by the people" directly, stressing the national character of the chamber and the imperative of equal treatment of voters regardless of state lines. *Id.* at 7–8, 14. That same imperative forecloses a regime in which a citizen's eligibility to serve—and a voter's ability to choose a particular candidate—turns on which state that candidate happens to represent. Uniformity in the qualifications for national office is not an incidental feature of the constitutional design; it is the design.

This Court's federalism precedents confirm that the States may not use their reserved powers to reach into, and reshape, institutions of the national government. In *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), the Court held that the States possess no authority "to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control" the operations of the constitutional government established by the people of the whole Union. *Id.* at 436. Amendment 73's Section 3 is exactly such an impediment: it controls, on a state-by-state basis, who may serve in a national legislature whose members "are not the representatives of a State, but of the people of the United States." *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651, 663 (1884). Yarbrough held that the right to choose members of Congress "has its foundation in the Constitution of the United States" rather than in state law, id. at 663–64, a principle reaffirmed in *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), where the Court recognized that the right to select congressional representatives is a right "secured by the Constitution," id. at 314, and therefore not subject to unilateral alteration by the States that happen to administer the ballot.

**III. Because Qualifications for Congress Are Fixed by the Constitution Itself, They May Be Changed Only Through Article V, Not by State Initiative.**

Petitioners' theory—that a state electorate may, through an ordinary initiative, alter the eligibility requirements for federal office—cannot be squared with this Court's settled understanding of how the Constitution may be changed. In *Hawke v. Smith*, 253 U.S. 221 (1920), the Court held that the function of ratifying amendments to the federal Constitution is a federal function granted by the Constitution itself, and that a State could not superimpose its own referendum requirement onto that federal process because "the framers of the Constitution intended it to be a . . . convention or legislature ratifying." *Id.* at 227. The Court applied the same principle in *Leser v. Garnett*, 258 U.S. 130 (1922), rejecting the argument that a State could impose additional conditions on the effectiveness of a constitutional amendment within its borders. These decisions establish a single, controlling proposition: matters committed to the federal constitutional structure are not subject to piecemeal state revision. Term limits for Congress, like the qualifications clauses generally, may be added only by amending the Constitution under Article V—not by forty-nine other means. See *Dillon v. Gloss*, 256 U.S. 368, 374–75 (1921) (Article V process governs the manner in which the Constitution may be altered).

**IV. Section 3 Is Not Severable, and the Entire Congressional Term-Limits Scheme Must Fall.**

Even apart from the federal constitutional defect, Arkansas law confirms that Section 3 cannot be excised while leaving the remainder intact. This Court's own severability test asks "whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion." *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750, 375 S.W.2d 809, 812 (1964). Sections 1 through 3 were submitted to voters as a single, undifferentiated term-limits package; no record evidence shows the electorate would have adopted state-officer limits alone. Respondents accordingly urge that, having found Section 3 unconstitutional, the Court should hold the entire Amendment void.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below invalidating Section 3 of Amendment 73 should be affirmed.
