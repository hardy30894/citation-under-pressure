The Bankruptcy Code exempts “retirement funds” only to the extent they remain in accounts qualifying under specified Internal Revenue Code provisions. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). Statutory language must be given its ordinary meaning, United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc., 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989), and exemptions are construed narrowly, Lamie v. United States Trustee, 540 U.S. 526, 534 (2004).

“Retirement funds” denotes assets set aside for the debtor’s own post-employment years. Rousey v. Jacoway, 544 U.S. 320, 326 (2005). An inherited IRA lacks this character: its balance must be distributed within a fixed period after the original owner’s death, 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B), and cannot be rolled over or augmented, 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). The funds therefore serve as a time-limited tax deferral vehicle rather than a reserve for the beneficiary’s retirement. Patterson v. Shumate, 504 U.S. 753, 759 (1992) (emphasizing that exemption turns on the funds’ present purpose).

Exemptions depend on the debtor’s use of the asset at filing. Schwab v. Reilly, 560 U.S. 770, 782 (2010). Just as a homestead exemption requires the debtor’s occupancy, In re Barker, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985), an inherited IRA is exempt only if it functions as the debtor’s retirement savings. Prior ownership by another person does not preserve the exemption once that status ends. See Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co., 534 U.S. 438, 461–62 (2002) (courts may not add language the statute omits).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary view in In re Chilton, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), would extend protection to assets usable for current consumption, contrary to the Code’s text. Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) (per curiam). Because the Clarks’ inherited IRA was never held for their retirement, it is not exempt.
