 ARGUMENT

I.   The OCSLA Extends LHWCA Coverage Only to Workers Injured on the Outer Continental Shelf Itself

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends workers’ compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) to employees injured "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources" of the Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain language of this provision requires a causal link between the operations on the Shelf and the injury. However, it does not explicitly state whether the injury must occur on the Shelf itself.

The Fifth Circuit has held that the OCSLA requires a "situs-of-injury" test, meaning that the injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it. Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). This interpretation is supported by the Supreme Court's dictum in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 n.2 (1986), which suggests that the OCSLA has a "situs requirement." The Ninth Circuit erred in rejecting this interpretation and adopting a "substantial nexus" test, which is too broad and not supported by the statutory text.

Furthermore, the "situs-of-injury" test is consistent with the purpose of the OCSLA, which is to provide a uniform system of compensation for workers on the outer Continental Shelf. See Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414, 419-20 (1985). Extending coverage to workers injured on land would create inconsistent and overlapping coverage with state workers' compensation laws.

II.  The BRB Correctly Denied Benefits Under the LHWCA

The LHWCA provides coverage for maritime employees injured on navigable waters or on an "adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel." 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Ninth Circuit has applied a "functional relationship" test to determine whether a facility is an "adjoining area." Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978).

In this case, the La Conchita facility is not a maritime situs because it is not used as an integral part of longshoring operations. Although it is located near the ocean, it is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks, and has no direct access to any pier or dock. The facility's primary purpose is to process crude oil slurry, a non-maritime activity. The occasional dumping of scrap metal from the offshore platforms does not convert the facility into a maritime situs.

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied the Herron factors and found that La Conchita is not a maritime situs. Therefore, the BRB's denial of benefits under the LHWCA should be affirmed.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed, and the BRB's denial of benefits under the OCSLA should be reinstated. The denial of benefits under the LHWCA should be affirmed.
