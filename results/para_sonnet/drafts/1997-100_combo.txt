**ARGUMENT**

**THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE DOES NOT BAR RETRIAL OF A PRIOR-CONVICTION SENTENCING ALLEGATION IN A NONCAPITAL CASE**

The Fifth Amendment's Double Jeopardy Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That guarantee, made binding on the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), safeguards a defendant from repeated *prosecutions* and multiple *punishments* for the same *offense*. Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. 163, 168 (1874) (no person shall "be twice punished for the same offence"). A proceeding to determine whether a defendant suffered a prior conviction is neither. It adjudicates no offense; it determines a sentencing fact bearing on the punishment for the offense of which the defendant already stands convicted. Respondent respectfully submits that retrial of such an allegation, after reversal for evidentiary insufficiency, does not implicate the Double Jeopardy Clause at all.

**A. Sentencing Proceedings Are Not Trials for Double Jeopardy Purposes.**

This Court has long recognized that the formal protections attending a trial on guilt do not attach with equal force to sentencing determinations. In Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court upheld a death sentence imposed by a judge who relied on out-of-court information regarding uncharged burglaries never proven at trial, holding that "the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure." Id. at 251. If due process itself does not compel trial-like procedures at sentencing, there is little basis to import the Double Jeopardy Clause—a guarantee tied to "prosecution" and "offence"—into that same setting. A prior-conviction enhancement hearing determines a status fact relevant to punishment, not guilt of a new "offense" within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment.

**B. This Court Has Repeatedly Permitted Resentencing—Including Harsher Punishment—Without Double Jeopardy Difficulty.**

The settled practice of this Court confirms that sentencing determinations lack the finality that double jeopardy protects. In Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), a jury convicted the defendant of first-degree murder "without capital punishment." Id. at 17–18. After reversal and retrial, a second jury convicted him without that qualification, and the trial court imposed a death sentence. This Court held the increased sentence valid, reasoning that the original verdict did not foreclose a harsher punishment on retrial. Id. at 18. Pearce likewise confirms that a defendant who obtains reversal of a conviction may, upon retrial, receive a more severe sentence than was first imposed, because "the guarantee against double jeopardy... imposes no restrictions upon the length of a sentence imposed upon reconviction." 395 U.S. at 719.

The principle is not limited to full retrials of guilt. In Bozza v. United States, 330 U.S. 160 (1947), the trial court had imposed an unlawful sentence omitting a mandatory fine; when the court corrected the sentence to include the fine, this Court rejected a double jeopardy challenge, explaining that "the Constitution does not require that sentencing should be a game in which a wrong move by the judge means immunity for the prisoner." Id. at 166–67. If a court may correct and even increase a sentence after it has already been pronounced and partially served, a fortiori the State may retry a discrete sentencing allegation that was never validly resolved at all.

**C. The Established Rules Governing Retrial After Reversal Confirm That No Double Jeopardy Bar Applies Here.**

This Court has consistently held that reversal of a conviction for trial error, including evidentiary deficiencies not going to ultimate innocence, permits retrial without offending the Double Jeopardy Clause. United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662, 671–72 (1896) (retrial permitted where first conviction was set aside on the defendant's own appeal). Similarly, in Trono v. United States, 199 U.S. 521 (1905), the Court held that a defendant who successfully appeals a conviction may, on retrial, be convicted of a greater offense than that for which he was originally found guilty, because he himself set the proceedings anew in motion. Id. at 533–34. And in Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184 (1957), the Court reaffirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause's central concern is preventing the State from twice placing a defendant in jeopardy for the same *offense*—not from litigating ancillary sentencing facts. Id. at 187–88.

These principles apply with full force here. The Court of Appeal's reversal rested on insufficiency of proof that petitioner personally used a deadly weapon—an evidentiary defect in the sentencing allegation, not an acquittal of any offense. Under Ball, Trono, and Stroud, such a defect does not trigger the finality that double jeopardy protects; it simply returns the parties to the position they occupied before the flawed determination, permitting a new adjudication of the sentencing fact.

**CONCLUSION**

Because a prior-conviction sentencing allegation is not itself an "offence" within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment, and because this Court's decisions in Williams, Stroud, Pearce, Bozza, Ball, Trono, and Green establish that sentencing determinations do not carry double jeopardy finality, the Double Jeopardy Clause does not preclude retrial of the prior-conviction allegation in this noncapital case. The judgment of the Supreme Court of California should be affirmed.
