**ARGUMENT**

I. **JOHNSON’S RULE APPLIES RETROACTIVELY TO CASES ON COLLATERAL REVIEW**

The Supreme Court has long held that a new constitutional rule announced in a criminal case applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it places certain categories of personal conduct beyond the reach of the criminal law. *See Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351 (2004) (citing *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 307 (1989) (plurality opinion)). In *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), the Court held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), was unconstitutionally vague in violation of the Due Process Clause. *Id.* at 2556-57. The Court’s decision in *Johnson* announced a new substantive rule of constitutional law that must be applied retroactively to cases on collateral review. *See Welch v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 1257, 1265 (2016) (holding that *Johnson* applies retroactively to cases on collateral review).

The Eleventh Circuit erred by failing to apply *Johnson* retroactively to Mr. Welch’s collateral challenge. The Supreme Court has made clear that a new constitutional rule applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it “places a class of private conduct beyond the reach of the criminal law” or “requires the observance of those procedures that are implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 307 (plurality opinion). *Johnson* falls squarely within this framework because it invalidated the residual clause of the ACCA, thereby placing certain conduct beyond the reach of the criminal law. *See Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. The Eleventh Circuit’s failure to apply *Johnson* retroactively was error.

II. **MR. WELCH’S PRIOR CONVICTION DOES NOT QUALIFY AS A “VIOLENT FELONY” UNDER THE ACCA**

Mr. Welch’s 1996 Florida conviction for strong-arm robbery does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA. The ACCA defines a “violent felony” as any crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year that:

> (i) has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another; or
> (ii) is burglary, arson, or extortion, involves use of explosives, or otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.

18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B). The Supreme Court has held that the ACCA’s residual clause must be interpreted in light of the categorical approach. *See Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 600 (1990). Under the categorical approach, courts look only to the statutory elements of the prior offense, not the particular facts underlying the conviction. *Id.* at 602.

Florida’s robbery statute at the time of Mr. Welch’s conviction criminalized the taking of property from a person with “force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.” Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1) (1996). The Florida courts have consistently held that this statute criminalizes conduct that does not involve the use of violent force. *See, e.g., Goldsmith v. State*, 573 So. 2d 445, 445 (Fla. 2d Dist. Ct. App. 1991) (holding that the “slight force used ... to remove the bill from [the victim’s] hand” was “insufficient to constitute the crime of robbery”); *A.J. v. State*, 561 So. 2d 1198, 1198 (Fla. 3d Dist. Ct. App. 1990) (holding that “the degree of force used to [grab a camera hanging from the victim’s shoulder] was insufficient to constitute robbery”). Because Florida’s robbery statute criminalizes conduct that does not involve violent force, Mr. Welch’s prior conviction does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA’s elements clause. *See Descamps v. United States*, 570 U.S. 254, 260 (2013) (holding that courts may not look to the particular facts underlying a conviction to determine whether it qualifies as a violent felony).

Moreover, Mr. Welch’s prior conviction does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA’s residual clause. The Supreme Court has held that the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. Because the residual clause is unconstitutional, it cannot serve as a basis for enhancing Mr. Welch’s sentence under the ACCA. *See id.* at 2563.

III. **MR. WELCH’S SENTENCE WAS IMPOSED IN VIOLATION OF THE SIXTH AMENDMENT**

Mr. Welch’s sentence was imposed in violation of the Sixth Amendment because it was based on a prior conviction that did not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA. The Supreme Court has held that the Sixth Amendment requires that any fact that increases a defendant’s sentence beyond the statutory maximum must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000). Because Mr. Welch’s sentence was enhanced based on a prior conviction that did not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA, his sentence was imposed in violation of the Sixth Amendment. *See Alleyne v. United States*, 570 U.S. 99, 103 (2013) (holding that any fact that increases a defendant’s mandatory minimum sentence must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit and remand the case for resentencing consistent with *Johnson* and the Sixth Amendment.
