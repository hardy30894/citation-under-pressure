ARGUMENT

The district court deprived Winbush of a fair opportunity to defend by denying his eleventh-hour request for a fingerprint expert and continuance. In Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932), the Court recognized that due process demands “a fair opportunity to defend against the charge.” The government’s late-noticed experts on fingerprint recovery and narcotics practices placed Winbush at a decisive disadvantage once he sought matching assistance to test the meaning of absent latent prints on the recovered firearm, jacket, and narcotics packaging.

Denial of that assistance left him unable to meet the government’s proof on equal terms. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 462-63 (1938) (due process requires “the guiding hand of counsel” at every critical stage so that the accused may “have his rights protected”). The magistrate’s refusal to grant even a brief continuance compounded the error. In Isaacs v. United States, 159 U.S. 487, 489 (1895), the Court observed that a continuance rests in the sound discretion of the trial court, yet that discretion is abused when the denial prevents the defendant from obtaining evidence essential to his defense.

The resulting trial therefore failed to satisfy the fundamental requirement of fairness that the Due Process Clause imposes. See Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935) (a conviction obtained through proceedings that deny the accused “the opportunity to present his defense” cannot stand). Winbush’s convictions and sentence should be vacated.
