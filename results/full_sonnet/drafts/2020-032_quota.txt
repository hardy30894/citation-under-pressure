# ARGUMENT

## I. A CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES ALONE CANNOT KEEP ALIVE AN OTHERWISE MOOT CONTROVERSY

Article III confines the judicial power to "Cases" and "Controversies," and this requirement "subsists through all stages of federal judicial proceedings." *Spencer v. Kemna*, 523 U.S. 1, 7 (1998). When "the issues presented are no longer 'live,'" a case becomes moot and the federal courts lack power to adjudicate it. *Los Angeles County v. Davis*, 440 U.S. 625, 631 (1979). Once GGC rescinded the challenged policies, there was no live controversy over their constitutionality: petitioners no longer faced any threat of enforcement, and the declaratory and injunctive relief they sought became irrelevant to any ongoing dispute. Petitioners do not contest this conclusion. Their sole argument is that a request for nominal damages—one dollar, unconnected to any actual injury—preserves federal jurisdiction indefinitely. That argument cannot be squared with this Court's settled mootness doctrine.

This Court has repeatedly held that "a plaintiff must demonstrate standing separately for each form of relief sought." *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 185 (2000). A live claim for compensatory damages can preserve a case because it redresses actual injury that persists regardless of subsequent policy change. *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 307 (1986) (compensatory damages "are designed to provide" a plaintiff "with fair and appropriate compensation for the injury caused"). Nominal damages serve a fundamentally different function. They exist not to compensate but "to vindicate deprivations of certain 'absolute' rights," *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978), and this Court has instructed that "the amount awarded as nominal damages" is a purely "symbolic" recognition of a past violation, *id.* at 267 n.24. A request for a symbolic dollar, untethered to any ongoing or future injury, does not present the kind of concrete stake in the outcome that Article III requires.

The Eleventh Circuit's decision in *Flanigan's Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), correctly applied these principles to the mootness context. There, as here, the plaintiffs challenged the constitutionality of a law that the government later repealed, and sought only nominal damages in addition to injunctive and declaratory relief. *Id.* at 1256–57. The en banc court held that "a claim for nominal damages, by itself, cannot save a case from mootness" once the underlying policy has been repealed and no other concrete relief remains available. *Id.* at 1263. That holding reflects the settled principle that federal courts do not sit to resolve abstract disputes about the constitutionality of policies no longer in effect. *See Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013) (a case becomes moot when "it is impossible for a court to grant any effectual relief whatever to the prevailing party").

## II. PETITIONERS' CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES CANNOT PROVIDE MEANINGFUL RELIEF ONCE THE CHALLENGED POLICY IS ABANDONED

This Court has long recognized that mootness turns on whether "a court can still order relief that would benefit the plaintiff." *Chafin v. Chafin*, 568 U.S. 165, 172 (2013). Nominal damages awarded after the fact of policy revision do not "affect the behavior of the defendant toward the plaintiff," *Farrar v. Hobby*, 506 U.S. 103, 113 (1992), a factor this Court has treated as central to the practical significance of relief awarded to a civil-rights plaintiff. In *Farrar*, the Court observed that a party who receives only nominal damages, without more, "has failed to prove an essential element of his claim for monetary relief" in any meaningful sense and typically obtains "no consequence" other than "the moral satisfaction of knowing that a federal court concluded that his rights had been violated." *Id.* at 114 (Souter, J., concurring); *id.* at 113 (majority op.). While *Farrar* concerned attorney's fees rather than mootness, its reasoning confirms what *Flanigan's* held explicitly: a bare request for a token sum does not carry independent weight sufficient to sustain a live controversy once all other requested relief has become moot.

This conclusion follows directly from the voluntary-cessation doctrine as properly limited. This Court has held that a defendant's voluntary cessation of challenged conduct moots a case unless there remains a reasonable expectation that the conduct will recur, *see Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 189, or unless some other form of relief—such as damages for completed injury—remains available to remedy harm already suffered, *see City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982). Petitioners' amended complaint, however, alleged no compensable injury: no lost wages, no medical or travel expenses, no specific reputational harm supported by factual allegation. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that "compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit [must] be based on actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the 'abstract value' of the constitutional rights that may have been violated." *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000). Because petitioners pleaded no actual injury, and because GGC's revised policy eliminated any prospective threat, the only relief theoretically available—nominal damages—could not "benefit the plaintiff" in the sense *Chafin* requires. 568 U.S. at 172.

## III. PETITIONERS' RULE WOULD ELIMINATE MOOTNESS AS A MEANINGFUL LIMIT ON FEDERAL JURISDICTION

Permitting a token nominal-damages claim to preserve federal jurisdiction indefinitely would allow parties to sidestep the mootness doctrine simply by including boilerplate language in a complaint, contravening this Court's repeated admonition that mootness principles exist "to prevent the federal courts from resolving disputes that no longer present concrete and adverse interests." *DeFunis v. Odegaard*, 416 U.S. 312, 316 (1974) (per curiam). If a single dollar of nominal damages could keep any repealed-policy case alive in perpetuity, defendants would have no incentive to correct unconstitutional policies through the ordinary legislative or administrative process, since litigation would never actually end. This would frustrate the salutary policy, recognized in *City of Mesquite*, 455 U.S. at 289, that voluntary compliance ordinarily moots a controversy and encourages exactly the kind of corrective action GGC undertook here.

For these reasons, the Eleventh Circuit's judgment affirming dismissal on mootness grounds should be affirmed.
