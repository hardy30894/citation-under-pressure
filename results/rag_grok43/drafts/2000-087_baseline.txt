**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention of a removable alien beyond the 90-day removal period. The provision must be read to permit detention only for a period reasonably necessary to effect removal.

Section 1231(a)(6) states that certain aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period.” Its text supplies no explicit temporal limit. Yet this Court has long required that deportation statutes be construed to avoid serious constitutional doubts. In *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948), the Court explained that “deportation is a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile,” and therefore “ambiguities” must be resolved in favor of the alien. The same principle governs here: an interpretation permitting detention without foreseeable end would raise grave due-process concerns.

Indefinite civil detention without criminal adjudication offends the Fifth Amendment. In *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896), the Court held that “all persons within the territory of the United States are entitled to the protection guaranteed by” the Due Process Clause, and that imprisonment “at hard labor” for an unauthorized alien could be imposed only after judicial trial. Although *Wong Wing* distinguished deportation from punishment, it recognized that prolonged restraint of liberty requires justification tied to a legitimate governmental purpose. Detention under § 1231(a)(6) serves only the purpose of securing removal; once removal ceases to be reasonably foreseeable, that purpose evaporates and continued confinement becomes punitive in effect.

This Court has repeatedly rejected constructions that would authorize open-ended preventive detention outside narrow, specially justified contexts. *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542 (1952), upheld pre-hearing detention of alien Communists only because the Attorney General retained discretion to grant bail and the detention was “not excessive in relation to the purpose of the statute.” *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194, 198 (1957), construed a post-order supervision provision narrowly to avoid “serious constitutional questions” that would arise from unlimited governmental inquiry. The same narrowing construction is required here.

The statute’s structure reinforces the limitation. Section 1231(a)(1)–(2) mandates removal within 90 days; subsection (a)(3) then shifts the alien to supervised release. Subsection (a)(6) merely authorizes continued detention of specified classes “pending removal.” The phrase “pending removal” presupposes that removal remains a realistic prospect. When no country will accept the alien, removal is no longer pending; detention therefore exceeds the statute’s authorization.

Petitioner’s case illustrates the point. After more than three years of post-removal confinement, Germany and Lithuania had both refused repatriation, and no other nation had been identified. Continued detention under these circumstances cannot be justified as “pending removal.” The statute therefore does not permit the Attorney General to hold petitioner indefinitely.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
