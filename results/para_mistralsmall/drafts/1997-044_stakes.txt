**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Debt Arising from Fraud Is Dischargeable Under the Bankruptcy Code.**

The Bankruptcy Code permits discharge of all debts except those specifically excepted by statute. 11 U.S.C. § 727(b). Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only those debts "for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." The Third Circuit correctly recognized that this provision requires a showing that the debtor obtained money or property "through a material misrepresentation" that he "knew ... was false or made with gross recklessness as to its truth," with "intent to deceive," and that the creditor "reasonably relied" on the misrepresentation and sustained damages as a result. *In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1992). However, the court erred in finding that Cohen’s conduct satisfied these elements.

A. **Cohen Did Not Make a Material Misrepresentation.**

The Bankruptcy Court and the Third Circuit erroneously concluded that Cohen’s silence in charging rents above the legal limit constituted an implied misrepresentation that the rents were lawful. *See* 185 B.R. at 177; 94 F.3d at 1118. This reasoning conflates a failure to comply with a regulatory obligation with the making of a false statement. The Supreme Court has long held that a misrepresentation under § 523(a)(2)(A) must be an "affirmative misstatement," not merely a breach of duty or a failure to disclose. *Field v. Mans*, 516 U.S. 59, 70 (1995) ("[T]he common law of fraud makes no general duty of disclosure, and the Bankruptcy Code does not expand it."); *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 25 (1999) (false representation requires "a statement that is false or fraudulent" and does not encompass omissions absent a duty to speak).

Cohen never affirmatively stated that the rents he charged were lawful. He did not represent to the tenants that he had complied with rent control laws, nor did he provide any statement about the legality of the rents. His mere failure to refund overcharges after being ordered to do so by the Rent Control Board does not constitute a false representation. *See In re Feinstein*, 218 B.R. 267, 273 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y. 1998) ("Silence alone, without a duty to disclose, does not constitute fraud under § 523(a)(2)(A).").

B. **Cohen Did Not Act with Intent to Deceive or Reckless Indifference to the Truth.**

Even assuming arguendo that Cohen’s conduct could be construed as a misrepresentation, the record does not support a finding of intent to deceive or reckless indifference to the truth. The Third Circuit relied on *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108 (3d Cir. 1995), for the proposition that intent may be inferred from reckless disregard of the truth. *Id.* at 1118-19. However, *Cohn* addressed § 523(a)(2)(B), which requires a "false written statement respecting the debtor’s financial condition." The scienter standard in § 523(a)(2)(A) is not identical. *See In re Geller*, 213 B.R. 608, 613 (Bankr. D. Md. 1997) (distinguishing the intent requirements of § 523(a)(2)(A) and (B)).

Moreover, reckless indifference requires more than negligence or a misunderstanding of the law. *In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr. E.D. Va. 1991). Cohen’s belief that he could charge market-rate rents to new tenants, while mistaken, was not reckless. He consulted no one about rent control laws, but his failure to educate himself does not evince a "conscious indifference" to the truth. *See In re Van Horne*, 823 F.2d 1285, 1287 (8th Cir. 1987) ("Recklessness is usually determined by a pattern of conduct."). There was no such pattern here.

C. **The Treble Damages Award Is Dischargeable.**

The Third Circuit held that the treble damages award under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act ("CFA") was nondischargeable because it was "obtained by" fraud. 94 F.3d at 1120. This conclusion misreads § 523(a)(2)(A). The statute excepts only the debt "to the extent obtained by" fraud. The Supreme Court has clarified that punitive or statutory damages are not "obtained by" fraud unless they are compensatory in nature or directly tied to the fraudulent transaction. *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991); *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir. 1991) ("[O]nly the compensatory portion of a judgment is excepted from discharge under § 523(a)(2)(A).").

The CFA’s treble damages are punitive in nature and not "obtained by" Cohen’s conduct in the sense required by § 523(a)(2)(A). *See In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 678 (11th Cir. 1993) (holding that punitive damages are dischargeable unless they are compensatory). The Third Circuit’s contrary view is erroneous and should be rejected.

**II. The Bankruptcy Court’s Findings Were Not Clearly Erroneous.**

The Third Circuit reviewed the Bankruptcy Court’s findings de novo, treating them as "ultimate facts." 94 F.3d at 1117. This was error. The Bankruptcy Court’s determination that Cohen’s conduct constituted "actual fraud" under § 523(a)(2)(A) was a mixed question of law and fact, but its underlying findings—such as Cohen’s knowledge of rent control laws—were basic facts subject to review for clear error. *Universal Minerals, Inc. v. C.A. Hughes & Co.*, 669 F.2d 98, 102 (3d Cir. 1981). The Bankruptcy Court’s findings were not clearly erroneous, but even if they were, the legal error in applying § 523(a)(2)(A) warrants reversal.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed. The debt arising from Cohen’s conduct is dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code, and the treble damages award is dischargeable as well.
