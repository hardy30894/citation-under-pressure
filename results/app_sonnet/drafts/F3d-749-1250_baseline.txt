**ARGUMENT**

**The District Court Plainly Erred in Admitting Agent Jones's Testimony That Stanley Was Lying, and Reversal Is Required.**

**A. Standard of Review**

Because Stanley did not object at trial, this Court reviews for plain error. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). Under that standard, Stanley must show "(1) error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affect[s] substantial rights," and, if those three are met, this Court "may then exercise its discretion to notice a forfeited error" if it "seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Id.* at 732, 736 (quoting *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936)). Each element is satisfied here.

**B. Admission of Agent Jones's Testimony Was Error**

Federal Rule of Evidence 608(a) confines opinion testimony about a witness's character to the narrow question of truthfulness or untruthfulness in general, and this Court has repeatedly held that the credibility of a particular witness on a particular occasion "is generally not an appropriate subject for expert testimony" because that determination "is within the exclusive province of the jury." *United States v. Toledo*, 985 F.2d 1462, 1467 (10th Cir. 1993). An expert who tells the jury that a defendant's statements are deceptive does not assist the jury—he replaces it. As this Court explained in rejecting similar testimony, an expert opinion that another witness is or is not truthful "encroach[es] upon the jury's vital and exclusive function to make credibility determinations." *United States v. Charley*, 189 F.3d 1251, 1267 (10th Cir. 1999).

Agent Jones did exactly what *Toledo* and *Charley* forbid. He did not merely describe Stanley's demeanor or recount observations for the jury to weigh; he told the jury, in the guise of expertise in "special tactics and ways to identify deception," that Stanley's answers were unworthy of belief because they "[did] not make sense," that Stanley's evasiveness was "common among the criminal element to keep law enforcement at bay," that he had "[n]ever . . . seen that with an innocent person," and that invoking God to support one's honesty is itself proof of deception. Each statement is a direct verdict on Stanley's veracity and, by extension, his guilt, dressed up as expert opinion. That is precisely the harm Rule 608(a) and this Court's precedent guard against—an expert's imprimatur substituting for the jury's own assessment of a witness who testified, or whose statements were placed, before it. *See Toledo*, 985 F.2d at 1467; *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1267.

**C. The Error Was Plain**

An error is plain if it is "clear" or "obvious" under current law. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. Given the settled rule that credibility determinations belong to the jury alone, *Toledo*, 985 F.2d at 1467, and that expert "vouching" testimony invades that function, *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1267, no reasonable reading of the record could excuse Agent Jones's repeated, unqualified assertions that Stanley was lying. The prosecutor's questions were designed to elicit, and did elicit, precisely the sort of credibility conclusions that the law forbids. The error was not close or debatable; it was obvious on the face of the transcript.

**D. The Error Affected Stanley's Substantial Rights**

To satisfy the third prong, Stanley must show "a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different." *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 76 (2004). That showing is met here. The government's case depended heavily on circumstantial evidence—proximity to the tracking device, presence at the East Pine residence, and Stanley's initial false identification to police. There was no eyewitness identification of Stanley as a participant in the robbery itself; indeed, the government's own theory of the case changed between the first and second trials, and the first jury deadlocked on identical charges without the benefit of Agent Jones's credibility testimony. Where the case turns so substantially on whether the jury believed Stanley's explanations for his conduct, an FBI agent's testimony that Stanley's demeanor and statements bore the hallmarks of guilt—that "[n]ever" had he "seen that with an innocent person"—cannot be deemed inconsequential. The testimony went to the heart of the only genuinely contested issue: whether Stanley was believable when he denied criminal involvement. There is a reasonable probability that, absent Agent Jones's improper opinion testimony, at least one juror would have reached a different verdict, just as the first jury did on the same underlying facts.

**E. The Error Seriously Affected the Fairness of the Proceedings**

Finally, this Court should exercise its discretion to correct the error because permitting a federal agent to tell a jury, under the mantle of expertise, that a defendant is lying "seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, [and] public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 732 (quoting *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160). Allowing such testimony to stand invites juries to defer to law-enforcement credibility assessments rather than perform their own, undermining the very function Rule 608(a) and this Court's precedent are designed to protect. *See Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1267; *Toledo*, 985 F.2d at 1467.

For these reasons, Stanley's conviction should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
