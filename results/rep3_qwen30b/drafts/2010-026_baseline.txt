**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. That holding misapplies the statutory standard, misreads Supreme Court precedent, and expands Exemption 2 beyond its narrow, textually grounded limits. The ESQD documents at issue are not “internal personnel rules and practices” within the meaning of 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2), nor do they meet the threshold for exemption under the “risk of circumvention” prong. The court’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard—without clear statutory or constitutional support—undermines the foundational principle that FOIA exemptions must be “narrowly construed” and “explicitly exclusive.” *Rose v. Dep’t of Air Force*, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976); *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989).

**I. The ESQD Documents Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices”**

Exemption 2 applies only to information “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has made clear that this exemption is not a catch-all for any sensitive government information. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 363. Rather, it is limited to matters such as “parking facilities, lunch hours, and sick leave,” which are “not of genuine and significant public interest.” Id. at 363. The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the “predominantly internal” standard—borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981)—transforms this narrow exemption into a broad, amorphous category that threatens to swallow the rule of law.

The ESQD arc maps are not internal personnel rules. They are technical safety calculations used to determine the safe storage and handling of explosive ordnance. As the Navy itself acknowledges, these maps are “designed to be a long term planning tool for the Navy” and are used to “design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities.” The documents are not about how Navy personnel are to be managed, trained, or disciplined. They are not internal directives governing employee conduct. They are engineering data—mathematical formulas and spatial representations—used to ensure public safety and prevent catastrophic sympathetic detonations. To classify such information as “internal personnel rules and practices” is to stretch the statutory language beyond recognition.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that FOIA exemptions must be interpreted narrowly. *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which treats technical safety data as “internal” simply because it is used by Navy personnel, contradicts this principle. The Court in *Rose* rejected the idea that any information used by an agency could be exempt under Exemption 2. It held that “the exemption is not intended to cover all information that is used internally by an agency.” 425 U.S. at 363. The Navy’s own manual, the OP-5, confirms this: it states that the purpose of the ESQD system is to “acquaint personnel engaged in operations involving ammunition, explosives, and other hazardous materials” and to “prescribe standardized safety regulations.” This is not about internal personnel practices—it is about public safety and operational risk mitigation.

**II. The Risk of Circumvention Is Not Established**

Even if the documents were arguably “internal,” Exemption 2 requires a showing that disclosure would “present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation.” *Morley v. CIA*, 508 F.3d 1108, 1124 (D.C. Cir. 2007). The Ninth Circuit found such a risk based on the Navy’s assertion that the maps could be used to “plan an attack or disrupt operations” on NMII. But this is speculative and unsupported by evidence.

The Navy has not classified the ESQD maps. If they posed a genuine threat to national security, the Navy would have classified them under Exemption 1, which specifically protects “classified matters kept secret in the interest of national defense or foreign policy.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1). The fact that the Navy has not classified the maps—despite Commander Whitbred’s claim that they could be reverse-engineered to reveal “particular ammunition, explosive[s and] weapons systems”—undermines any assertion of national security risk. *See* *Church of Scientology v. IRS*, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (9th Cir. 1979) (holding that information not classified cannot be withheld under Exemption 1). The Navy’s failure to classify the maps suggests that the risk is not one of national security, but of political sensitivity.

Moreover, the Navy has voluntarily released identical ESQD maps for the Bangor Naval Base—located just four to five miles from the nearest town—without incident. The contrast is telling: the Navy is willing to disclose similar safety data for a base with comparable risk profiles, but refuses to do so for NMII, which is only two miles from Port Townsend and one mile from Port Hadlock. This inconsistency suggests that the Navy’s refusal is not based on genuine security concerns, but on political pressure. The dissent correctly notes this disparity, and the majority’s failure to address it undermines its credibility.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s “Predominantly Internal” Standard Is Unlawful**

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the “predominantly internal” standard is not only unsupported by the text of Exemption 2, but also inconsistent with Supreme Court precedent. The Court has never endorsed a standard that allows agencies to withhold information simply because it is “mostly” used internally. *Rose* made clear that the exemption applies only to information “related solely” to internal personnel rules and practices.
