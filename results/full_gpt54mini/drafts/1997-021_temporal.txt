The Board’s polling rule cannot stand because it is neither rational nor consistent with the Act. The National Labor Relations Act does not regulate employee polling at all, and this Court has long held that the Board may fill statutory gaps only by reasoned policy choices that fit the Act’s structure. NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 130-31 (1944); NLRB v. Seven-Up Bottling Co., 344 U.S. 344, 346-47 (1953). Here, the Board imposed a rule that makes polling available only when the employer already has objective facts sufficient to justify withdrawing recognition. That standard collapses the poll into the withdrawal decision and strips polling of any independent function. A Board rule that renders a statutorily unmentioned practice practically useless is not a rational accommodation of the Act’s competing policies. See NLRB v. Express Publishing Co., 312 U.S. 426, 437-38 (1941).

The Act’s core objective is stable collective bargaining, but that objective presupposes majority support. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33-34 (1937). An employer who faces objective signs that a union has lost support is caught between two statutory risks: bargaining with a union that may no longer represent a majority, or refusing to bargain and later being found in violation. The Board’s rule worsens that dilemma by forbidding the employer to use a careful, noncoercive poll unless it has already amassed enough evidence to justify the very action the poll is meant to test. That makes no sense as a matter of administration or policy. See Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 798-99 (1945) (Board must accommodate Act’s policies through reasoned line-drawing).

The lower court correctly recognized that the Board may not make a poll available only after the employer has already crossed the threshold for withdrawing recognition, but the Board’s own factual finding is also unsound. The standard for Board factfinding under the Act is substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488-91 (1951). That standard does not permit the Board to disregard unrebutted evidence of employee disaffection and then announce, without adequate explanation, that an employer lacked good-faith doubt. See Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229-30 (1938) (evidence must be substantial in light of the record).

On this record, Allentown had ample objective grounds to doubt the union’s continued majority status. Employees made repeated statements indicating that the union had lost support. The company received comments from employees suggesting dissatisfaction with representation and indicating that a vote would go against the union. The employer did not rely on rumor alone; it conducted a secret-ballot poll supervised by a priest, and the result was 19 to 13 against union representation. The Board nonetheless discounted several of the statements and treated the remaining evidence as insufficient. But where the record contains direct employee expressions of antiunion sentiment and a fair poll corroborates that concern, a conclusion that the employer lacked reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. See NLRB v. Tennessee Packers, Inc., 379 U.S. 343, 346-47 (1964) (Courts must set aside Board action unsupported by substantial evidence).

The Board’s treatment of the evidence was especially arbitrary because it demanded a level of proof approaching certainty rather than reasonable doubt. The Act does not require an employer to prove majority disaffection before taking a noncoercive poll designed to test that very question. “Reasonable doubt” is not synonymous with certainty, and administrative insistence on proof sufficient to resolve the issue before permitting a poll reverses the inquiry. Cf. Eastern Associated Coal Corp. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229-30 (1938). The Board’s approach also ignores the practical reality that employee sentiment can be gauged only imperfectly; that is why polling exists. A rule that allows the Board to infer certainty from silence or equivocal comments, while discounting direct expressions of dissatisfaction and a fair poll result, cannot survive substantial-evidence review. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488-91 (1951).

Finally, the Court should not defer to a construction that disserves the Act’s basic balance. Deference is appropriate only where the Board’s policy choice is reasonable and consistent with the statute. NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 130-31 (1944). Here, the Board’s polling rule is neither. And its application of that rule to this record ignores the substantial evidence that Allentown had an objective, good-faith basis to doubt Local 724’s majority support. The order should therefore be denied enforcement.
