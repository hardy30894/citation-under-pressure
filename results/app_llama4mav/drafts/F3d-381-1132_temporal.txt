ARGUMENT

I. THE COURT OF FEDERAL CLAIMS ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT TO THE GOVERNMENT BECAUSE THE BPA'S INSTALLATION OF EXCESS FIBER OPTIC CAPACITY AND LEASING IT TO THIRD PARTIES CONSTITUTES A PHYSICAL TAKING OF TUTHILL'S PROPERTY.

The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits the government from taking private property without just compensation. U.S. Const. amend. V. A physical taking occurs when the government physically occupies or invades private property. United States v. Causby, 328 U.S. 256, 261-62 (1946). The government's actions in this case constitute a physical taking because the BPA's installation of excess fiber optic capacity and leasing it to third parties exceeds the scope of the easement granted in 1953.

The scope of the easement is crucial in determining whether the government's actions constitute a taking. As the Supreme Court held in United States v. Welch, 217 U.S. 333 (1910), "the right to take private property for public use is limited to the particular use for which it is taken." Id. at 339. The easement granted to the BPA in 1953 allowed for the installation of "signal lines... necessary in connection therewith." The BPA's decision to install excess fiber optic capacity far beyond its current needs and lease it to third parties is not "necessary in connection" with the transmission of electrical power.

The Court of Federal Claims erred in holding that the scope of the easement is irrelevant to the takings analysis. As the Supreme Court held in Portsmouth Harbor Land & Hotel Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 327 (1922), a taking can occur when the government exceeds the scope of its easement. Id. at 329-30. In this case, the BPA's actions exceed the scope of the easement, and therefore, constitute a physical taking.

Furthermore, the government's argument that the leasing of excess capacity to third parties does not constitute a physical invasion is unpersuasive. As the Supreme Court held in Causby, 328 U.S. at 266, a physical invasion can occur even if the government's actions do not result in a direct physical occupation of the property. The BPA's leasing of excess capacity to third parties is a use of Tuthill's property that is not authorized by the easement, and therefore, constitutes a physical taking.

In conclusion, the Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government. The BPA's installation of excess fiber optic capacity and leasing it to third parties constitutes a physical taking of Tuthill's property, and Tuthill is entitled to just compensation.

II. THE COURT OF FEDERAL CLAIMS ERRED IN HOLDING THAT TUTHILL MAY HAVE AVENUES OF RELIEF IN FEDERAL COURT, BUT NOT ON A TAKINGS THEORY.

The Court of Federal Claims held that Tuthill may have other avenues of relief, such as a trespass claim, but not on a takings theory. However, as the Supreme Court held in United States v. Lynah, 188 U.S. 445 (1903), a taking can occur even if the government's actions are considered a trespass. Id. at 470. The fact that Tuthill may have other causes of action does not preclude its takings claim.

In conclusion, the Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government, and Tuthill is entitled to just compensation for the physical taking of its property.
