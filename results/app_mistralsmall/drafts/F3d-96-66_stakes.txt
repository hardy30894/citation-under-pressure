**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT TO BORA CORP. BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT EXIST REGARDING WHETHER THE SHIP’S CREW BREACHED ITS “ACTIVE OPERATIONS” DUTY UNDER *SCINDIA STEAM NAVIGATION CO. v. DE LOS SANTOS*, 451 U.S. 156 (1981).

Under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”), 33 U.S.C. § 905(b), a vessel owner owes a duty of reasonable care to longshoremen when it is engaged in “active operations.” *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 167. This duty arises in two distinct contexts: (1) when the vessel owner is actively controlling or supervising the cargo operations (*“active operations” duty*), and (2) when the vessel owner has actual or constructive notice of a dangerous condition (*“turnover” duty*). *Id.* at 167–68. The district court erred by conflating these duties and concluding that Serbin’s actions precluded any vessel liability as a matter of law.

A. The Ship’s Crew Breached Its Active Operations Duty by Failing to Ensure the Snatch Block Was Properly Secured.

The Supreme Court in *Scindia* held that a vessel owner’s active operations duty requires it to exercise reasonable care to prevent injury to longshoremen from hazards “which are foreseeable and which are caused by the vessel’s own active involvement in the cargo operations.” *Id.* at 167. This duty is not absolute; rather, it is limited to hazards that are “foreseeable and preventable” by the vessel’s crew. *Id.* at 167–68.

Here, the record shows that the snatch block was improperly left in the down position, obstructing the cargo unit and creating a foreseeable hazard. The block’s position prevented the removal of the underlying cargo and posed a risk to workers in the wings. Serbin testified that he moved the block because it was unsafe and would delay operations. The ship’s crew, as the party responsible for operating the block and pulley system, had a duty to ensure the block was properly secured after use. *See Guttieres v. Ashcroft, Inc.*, 923 F.2d 1093, 1097 (1st Cir. 1991) (holding that a vessel owner breached its active operations duty when it failed to secure equipment properly during cargo operations).

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because reasonable jurors could differ on whether the crew’s failure to secure the block constituted a breach of its active operations duty. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) (summary judgment is inappropriate if reasonable minds could differ on the issue of negligence).

B. The District Court Improperly Relied on *Canal Barge Co. v. Scudero*, 727 F.2d 1147 (5th Cir. 1984), to Conclude That Serbin’s Actions Were the Sole Cause of His Injury.

The district court cited *Canal Barge* for the proposition that a longshoreman’s decision to move equipment himself severs any causal connection between the vessel’s negligence and the injury. However, *Canal Barge* does not stand for such a broad rule. In that case, the Fifth Circuit held that the longshoreman’s voluntary assumption of risk did not preclude liability where the vessel’s negligence was a substantial factor in causing the injury. *Id.* at 1154. The court emphasized that the vessel’s duty is not extinguished merely because the longshoreman acted to mitigate a hazard created by the vessel. *Id.*

Here, Serbin’s decision to move the block does not absolve the ship of liability if the crew’s failure to secure the block was a foreseeable cause of his injury. *See Berthelot v. Marine Transport Lines, Inc.*, 663 F.2d 369, 375 (5th Cir. 1981) (holding that a longshoreman’s actions do not necessarily break the causal chain where the vessel’s negligence remains a substantial factor in the injury).

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT EXIST REGARDING WHETHER THE SHIP’S CREW HAD ACTUAL OR CONSTRUCTIVE NOTICE OF THE DANGEROUS CONDITION.

Even if the court concludes that the active operations duty does not apply, the vessel owner may still be liable under its turnover duty if it had actual or constructive notice of the dangerous condition. *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 168. The turnover duty requires the vessel owner to warn of or eliminate hazards that are known or should have been known through the exercise of reasonable care. *Id.*

Here, the record shows that the snatch block was improperly left in the down position for an extended period, obstructing cargo and creating a hazard. A reasonable jury could infer that the crew should have known of the block’s improper position, as it was responsible for operating the block and pulley system. *See Johnson v. John F. Beasley Constr. Co.*, 822 F. Supp. 1460, 1465 (E.D. La. 1993) (holding that constructive notice may be inferred from the length of time a dangerous condition existed).

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because reasonable jurors could differ on whether the crew had constructive notice of the hazardous condition. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and this matter should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
