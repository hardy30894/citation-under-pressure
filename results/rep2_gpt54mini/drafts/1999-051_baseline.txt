### ARGUMENT

Petitioner’s proposed rule would dissolve the state procedural-default doctrine. This Court has repeatedly held that a federal habeas court may not reach a defaulted claim unless the prisoner shows “cause” and “prejudice.” Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977); Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). And when the asserted “cause” is itself a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, that claim must independently satisfy the exhaustion and non-default rules before it may excuse another default. Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488-89 (1986); Edwards v. Carpenter, 529 U.S. 446, 452-53 (2000).

That rule follows from the structure of habeas review. Cause must be an external obstacle that prevented compliance with state procedure, not a claim that has itself been forfeited. Murray, 477 U.S. at 488. Ineffective assistance can qualify, but only where the Sixth Amendment claim was properly presented to the state courts or where its default is itself excused. Id. at 489; Coleman, 501 U.S. at 753. Otherwise, a prisoner could “resurrect” any barred substantive claim simply by labeling counsel ineffective in a later proceeding. Edwards, 529 U.S. at 452.

This Court’s decision in Edwards v. Carpenter squarely controls. There, the Court held that “an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim asserted as cause for the procedural default of another claim can itself be procedurally defaulted.” 529 U.S. at 453. The Court explained that “a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim may not serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another claim” unless the prisoner demonstrates cause and prejudice for the ineffective-assistance default as well. Id. at 453-54. The Court also rejected the notion that federal habeas courts may disregard the default of the “cause” claim merely because the underlying substantive claim is substantial. Id. at 454-55.

The reasoning of Coleman is dispositive. There, the Court held that because there is no constitutional right to counsel in state collateral proceedings, attorney error in those proceedings cannot constitute cause to excuse a default. 501 U.S. at 752-57. The Court emphasized that federal habeas review respects state procedural rules unless the petitioner can show a valid excuse for each default. Id. at 750-51. Petitioner's rule would invert that principle by allowing an unexcused default in one claim to excuse another default, even though the “cause” claim was never properly preserved. That would be inconsistent with Coleman and Murray.

Nor does Martinez v. Ryan, 566 U.S. 1 (2012), help petitioner. Martinez created only a “narrow exception” for initial-review collateral proceedings where state law requires ineffective-assistance claims to be raised for the first time in collateral review. Id. at 9, 17. The Court later made clear that Martinez does not apply beyond that limited setting. Davila v. Davis, 582 U.S. 521, 529-30 (2017). The present case concerns ineffective assistance offered as cause for a separate defaulted claim, not the initial presentation of trial-counsel ineffectiveness in a first collateral review. Edwards remains the governing rule. 529 U.S. at 453-54.

Petitioner may argue that requiring exhaustion of the “cause” claim is formalistic. But the exhaustion doctrine exists to give state courts the first opportunity to correct constitutional error. O’Sullivan v. Boerckel, 526 U.S. 838, 844-45 (1999). Allowing a defaulted ineffectiveness claim to excuse another default would deprive state courts of that opportunity and would undermine comity and finality. Coleman, 501 U.S. at 731-32; Wainwright, 433 U.S. at 88-89.

Finally, petitioner cannot avoid Edwards by invoking miscarriage of justice. That exception applies only when the petitioner shows that “a constitutional violation has probably resulted in the conviction of one who is actually innocent.” Murray, 477 U.S. at 496. Nothing in the record remotely approaches that demanding standard.

Because petitioner’s ineffective-assistance theory was itself procedurally defaulted and not excused, it cannot serve as cause for the default of his underlying habeas claim. Edwards v. Carpenter, 529 U.S. 446 (2000), requires affirmance.
