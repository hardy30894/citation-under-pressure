**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—must be answered in the negative. The FDCPA, enacted in 1977, was designed to regulate the conduct of third-party collectors, not creditors collecting their own debts. The statutory definition of “debt collector” in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) is clear and unambiguous: it applies only to persons who regularly collect debts owed to another. When a company purchases a defaulted debt and thereafter collects it on its own behalf, it acts as a creditor, not a debt collector. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander, having acquired the debts from CitiFinancial Auto and then seeking to collect them for its own account, was not a “debt collector” under the Act.

The foundational principle governing the FDCPA’s scope is that the Act regulates only those who collect debts on behalf of others. This distinction is rooted in the Act’s legislative purpose: to eliminate abusive practices by third-party collectors, not to regulate the ordinary business activities of creditors. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, the law does not extend to entities acting in their own interest. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Court emphasized that “the scope of a statute must be determined by its language and the context in which it is used.” Here, the language of § 1692a(6) is plain: a “debt collector” includes any person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” The key phrase is “owed or due another”—a term that refers to the relationship between the collector and the original creditor at the time of collection, not at the time of origination.

The plaintiffs’ argument—that Santander becomes a “debt collector” simply because it purchased a defaulted debt—rests on a fundamental misreading of the statute. They contend that because the debt was in default when Santander acquired it, it must be considered “owed or due another” at the time of collection. But this interpretation ignores the plain meaning of the statutory language. The phrase “owed or due another” cannot be read to refer to debts that were originally owed to a third party, regardless of whether the current holder is now the owner. To hold otherwise would render the statutory definition meaningless. As the Court stated in *United States v. American Trucking Associations*, 310 U.S. at 543, “a statute must be construed so as to give effect to all its provisions.” If Congress had intended to include every purchaser of a defaulted debt within the definition of “debt collector,” it would have said so. Instead, it chose to define the term narrowly, focusing on the *relationship* between the collector and the creditor at the time of collection.

Moreover, the legislative history of the FDCPA confirms that Congress intended to exclude creditors collecting their own debts. The Senate Report accompanying the Act explicitly states that the definition of “debt collector” was not meant to cover “the activities of ... mortgage service companies and others who service outstanding debts for others, so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing.” S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. This language reflects a clear intent to distinguish between those who collect debts for others and those who collect their own. The fact that a debt was in default when acquired does not transform a creditor into a collector. The Act’s structure confirms this: the definition of “debt collector” is divided into two parts—those who are included and those who are excluded. The exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) applies only to persons collecting debts that were not in default when obtained. But this exclusion does not create a new category of “debt collector”; it merely clarifies that certain activities are not covered. The threshold inquiry remains whether the person is collecting debts owed to another.

The plaintiffs’ argument that Santander was a “debt collector” because it had previously collected on these loans for CitiFinancial Auto is equally untenable. The fact that an entity once acted as a collector for another does not make it a collector when it later purchases the debt and collects it for itself. As the Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Associations*, 310 U.S. at 543, “the meaning of a statute is not to be determined by the past conduct of the parties, but by the language and purpose of the law.” Santander’s status changed when it purchased the debt. It no longer collected on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto; it collected on its own behalf. The Act does not require that a person’s past conduct determine its current status. To hold otherwise would create a perpetual liability for any entity that once collected debts for another, regardless of whether it now acts as a creditor.

Finally, the plaintiffs’ claim that the Act would be rendered ineffective if Santander were not subject to it is a policy argument, not a legal one. The FDCPA is not a general consumer protection statute; it is a targeted regulation of abusive collection practices by third parties. Congress did not intend to subject every creditor who purchases defaulted debts to its provisions. The Act’s exclusions are not loopholes but deliberate limitations. As the Court stated in *United States v. American Trucking Associations*, 310 U.S. at 543, “the purpose of a statute is to be found in its language and in the context in which it is used.” Here, the context is clear: the FDCPA regulates debt collectors, not creditors. Santander, having purchased the debts and collecting them for its own account, is a creditor, not a debt collector.

For these reasons, the Fourth Circuit correctly concluded that the complaint failed to allege facts showing that Santander was a “debt collector” under § 1692a(6). The judgment must be affirmed.
