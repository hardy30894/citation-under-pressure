**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service’s regulation prohibiting the operation of hovercrafts within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve—on navigable waters that lie within the boundaries of the National Park System—does not violate the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (“ANILCA”), and the district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of the federal appellees. The plain text of ANILCA, the settled principles of federal regulatory authority over navigable waters, and binding precedent from this Court and the Supreme Court compel this conclusion. Sturgeon’s claim fails as a matter of law, and Alaska lacks standing to challenge the regulation at all.

**I. ANILCA’s Plain Text Does Not Prohibit the NPS from Regulating Activities on State-Owned Lands and Waters Within National Park Boundaries**

Sturgeon’s argument rests on a fundamental misreading of § 103(c) of ANILCA, which provides that “the Secretary of the Interior shall not have authority to regulate the use of lands or waters within the boundaries of a unit of the National Park System in Alaska that are not owned by the United States.” 16 U.S.C. § 3203(c). This provision is not a blanket exemption from federal regulation; rather, it is a narrow exception that applies only to *federal regulatory authority over nonfederal lands and waters*. It does not, and cannot, strip the federal government of its inherent authority to regulate activities on navigable waters—even when those waters lie beneath state-owned submerged lands—under the Commerce Clause, the navigational servitude, or the federal government’s general regulatory power over federally protected areas.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the federal government retains authority over navigable waters regardless of state ownership of the underlying bed. In *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 402 (1940), the Court held that “the power of Congress to regulate commerce includes the power to regulate the use of navigable waters,” and that “the right of the United States to regulate the use of navigable waters is not affected by the ownership of the bed.” This principle remains binding: the federal government may regulate activities on navigable waters within national parks even when the submerged lands are owned by a state. See also *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915) (“The navigable waters of the United States are subject to the exclusive control of the federal government.”).

Moreover, the NPS regulation at issue—36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), which prohibits the operation of hovercrafts in NPS-administered lands and waters—does not regulate the *ownership* of land or water, nor does it purport to govern state property as such. Rather, it regulates *conduct* on navigable waters within the boundaries of a federally protected area. Such regulation is well within the NPS’s statutory authority under the Organic Act of 1916, 16 U.S.C. § 1 et seq., which authorizes the Secretary of the Interior to “conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life therein” and to “provide for the enjoyment of the same in such manner and by such means as will leave them unimpaired for the enjoyment of future generations.” This authority includes the power to regulate activities that threaten the integrity of the park system, including the use of motorized vehicles on navigable waters.

Sturgeon’s interpretation would render the NPS powerless to regulate any activity on navigable waters within a national park—even if it poses a threat to wildlife, public safety, or the natural environment—so long as the submerged land is owned by a state. That result is inconsistent with the purpose of ANILCA, which was not to fragment federal regulatory authority, but to balance federal and state interests in Alaska’s public lands. See *State of Alaska v. Babbitt*, 72 F.3d 698, 705 (9th Cir. 1995) (“ANILCA was designed to preserve federal lands and waters while recognizing Alaska’s interests in its own resources.”). The Court has repeatedly rejected interpretations that would eviscerate federal regulatory authority in national parks. See *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 102 (2000) (“Congress may regulate activities on state-owned land within a national park if the regulation is necessary to protect the park’s resources.”).

**II. The NPS Regulation Is Not a “Regulation of Lands or Waters” Within the Meaning of ANILCA § 103(c)**

Sturgeon’s claim hinges on the idea that the hovercraft ban is a “regulation of lands or waters” under § 103(c). But the regulation does not regulate the *land* or *water* itself; it regulates *use* of a specific type of vehicle on navigable waters. The Supreme Court has made clear that not every federal regulation of conduct on state-owned land constitutes a “regulation of lands or waters” within the meaning of a statute like ANILCA. In *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28, 34 (1913), the Court held that “the power to regulate the use of navigable waters is not dependent upon ownership of the bed.” Similarly, in *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 126 (1967), the Court emphasized that “the federal government’s authority to regulate activities on navigable waters is not diminished by state ownership of the submerged lands.”

The Ninth Circuit has also recognized that ANILCA’s exception applies only to regulations that directly govern the *use of nonfederal land or water*, not to regulations that govern *activities* on navigable waters. See *John v. United States (Katie John II)*, 247 F.3d 1032, 1040 (9th Cir. 2001) (en banc) (“ANILCA’s limitation on federal regulatory authority applies only to the use of nonfederal lands and waters, not to the regulation of activities on navigable waters within park boundaries.”). The regulation here is a safety and conservation measure, not a land-use regulation. It is no different from the NPS’s authority to prohibit motorized boats, drones, or commercial fishing in national parks—regulations that have long been upheld as valid exercises of federal authority.
