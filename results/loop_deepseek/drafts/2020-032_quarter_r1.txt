**ARGUMENT**

The case is moot. Article III’s case-or-controversy requirement demands that a live dispute persist throughout litigation. *United States v. Hamburg-Amerikanische Packetfahrt-Actien Gesellschaft*, 239 U.S. 466, 475 (1916). When the challenged policy is repealed and no plaintiff remains subject to it, federal courts lack jurisdiction. *Atherton Mills v. Johnston*, 259 U.S. 13, 16 (1922); *Brownlow v. Schwartz*, 261 U.S. 216, 218 (1923). Here, Georgia Gwinnett College revised its “Freedom of Expression Policy” and “Student Code of Conduct” while the suit was pending, and Uzuegbunam graduated. The only remaining claim is for nominal damages. That is insufficient to avoid mootness.

**I. The Policy Change Extinguished All Ongoing Controversy**

A plaintiff must show a “substantial controversy, between parties having adverse legal interests, of sufficient immediacy and reality to warrant the issuance of a declaratory judgment.” *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240–41 (1937). Once the law that allegedly caused injury has been repealed, no controversy remains. *United States v. Munsingwear*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950) (moot when “the underlying controversey has ceased to exist”). The new policy permits expressive activity anywhere on campus without a permit, and the challenged code-of-conduct provision has been removed. There is no reasonable expectation that the college will reimpose the prior restrictions. *See United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632–33 (1953) (voluntary cessation moots case if “there is no reasonable expectation that the wrong will be repeated”). The college’s action was “unambiguous” and “permanent.” *Commercial Cable Co. v. Burleson*, 250 U.S. 360, 366 (1919) (moot when order revoked and no indication of reimposition). Because the policy is gone, both the facial and as-applied challenges are moot.

**II. Nominal Damages Do Not Revive a Moot Case**

A plaintiff cannot “maintain a suit after the right to relief has been extinguished by the act of the defendant” simply by requesting nominal damages. *Hamburg-Amerikanische*, 239 U.S. at 475. Nominal damages are a “technical” remedy that presupposes a live controversy; they cannot create one where none exists. *Meek v. Centre County Banking Co.*, 268 U.S. 426, 431 (1925) (moot when “no actual controversey” remains). The Court has consistently held that once the underlying dispute ends, the case must be dismissed even if nominal damages are sought. *Atherton Mills*, 259 U.S. at 16 (moot when child labor law expired, despite request for damages). *See also Brownlow*, 261 U.S. at 218 (moot when building demolished, despite claim for damages). The only exception is when the plaintiff has suffered a concrete, continuing injury. *Fiswick v. United States*, 329 U.S. 211, 222 (1946) (collateral consequences exception). Here, the complaint alleged no actual injury—only an abstract violation. Abstract injury, without more, cannot sustain a case after the policy is gone. *Brownlow*, 261 U.S. at 218.

**III. The Voluntary Cessation Doctrine Does Not Apply**

The voluntary cessation doctrine can avoid mootness only if the defendant is “free to return to its old ways.” *W.T. Grant*, 345 U.S. at 632. But the college’s policy change was a formal, permanent revision, not a temporary retreat. *Commercial Cable*, 250 U.S. at 366 (moot where “there is no probability that the acts complained of will be repeated”). The new policy superseded the old one, and the college has no incentive to reinstate a policy already held unconstitutional. There is no “reasonable expectation” of recurrence. *Id.*

**Conclusion**

Because the challenged policies were repealed and no plaintiff remains subject to them, the case is moot. A prayer for nominal damages cannot supply the ongoing controversy required by Article III. The district court properly dismissed the complaint.
