**Argument**

The judgment below should be affirmed because the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act does not extend Longshore Act coverage to every land-based injury with some connection to offshore drilling. Section 1333(b) extends benefits only for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That text requires more than a but-for chain; it requires an injury that is the result of shelf operations in the statutory sense, not merely an injury that would not have happened absent offshore work.

Textual limitations must be enforced as written. In **Lake County v. Rollins, 130 U.S. 662, 670 (1889)**, the Court held that “[w]here the words of a law are plain, and the meaning clear, we have no right to speculate upon the intentions of the legislature.” Likewise, **Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917)**, instructs that courts must apply statutory language according to its terms, and **United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820)**, warns that a statute “is not to be extended by construction.” Those settled principles foreclose respondent’s effort to read § 1333(b) as a general causal provision untethered to the Outer Continental Shelf itself. See also **United States v. Goldenberg, 168 U.S. 95, 102-03 (1897)**.

The Court has long treated compensatory statutes as bounded by the conditions Congress imposed. In **Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 54-55 (1932)**, the Court recognized that the Longshore Act turns on jurisdictional facts fixed by Congress, not on broad equitable notions of coverage. In **South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 260-61 (1940)**, the Court likewise enforced the statute’s limits rather than enlarging them. Those cases matter here because OCSLA incorporates Longshore coverage only for a defined class of offshore injuries; it does not authorize courts to replace Congress’s geographic limitation with a generalized nexus test.

Respondent’s position also collides with the principle that even remedial statutes may not be rewritten under the guise of liberal construction. **United States v. Merriam, 263 U.S. 179, 187-88 (1923)**, rejected the idea that courts may depart from statutory text because a different result seems more consistent with the statute’s purpose. And **Baltimore & Philadelphia Steamboat Co. v. Norton, 284 U.S. 408, 416 (1932)**, confirms that courts must enforce the limits Congress chose, not the limits a claimant wishes Congress had chosen. The same point appears in **Ex parte McNiel, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 236, 242 (1871)**, which holds that statutory conditions are not dispensed with by implication.

The respondent’s reading would also collapse the distinction between location-based coverage and mere causal antecedence. The statutory phrase is not “any injury related to offshore operations”; it is “any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That language contains both an injury component and an operations component. The injury here occurred at an onshore oil-processing facility, not on the Shelf, and the facility’s role in processing crude oil after it left the platforms does not transform a land-based forklift accident into an offshore injury. To hold otherwise would make the statutory reference to the Outer Continental Shelf largely surplusage. Courts do not read statutes that way. **Brown v. Duchesne, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 183, 194 (1856)**; **Hebert v. Louisiana, 272 U.S. 312, 316-17 (1926)**.

The Court’s compensation cases likewise counsel restraint. In **Ocean Accident & Guarantee Corp. v. Washington, 281 U.S. 574, 580-81 (1930)**, the Court emphasized that compensation coverage depends on the legislature’s chosen reach. And in **Baltimore Mail S.S. Co. v. Fawcett, 269 U.S. 247, 251-52 (1925)**, the Court declined to expand a maritime remedial scheme beyond the statutory design. These decisions confirm that, even in a remedial setting, courts must honor the line Congress drew.

For those reasons, the Ninth Circuit correctly rejected the Benefits Review Board’s situs-of-injury approach as a categorical requirement, but respondent’s broader theory still fails: § 1333(b) does not reach an onshore accident at a processing facility merely because the worker’s duties were connected to offshore extraction. The injury must arise from operations on the Shelf itself in the statutory sense, and this one did not. The judgment should be affirmed.
