**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine disputes of material fact exist regarding whether the vessel breached its duty under § 905(b) to intervene in the stevedore's operations when confronted with a foreseeable hazard. The vessel’s duty, as established by this Court and the Supreme Court, extends beyond mere turnover duties to encompass a continuing obligation to act when the vessel knows of an unreasonable risk of harm.

**I. The Vessel Had a Duty to Intervene Because It Knowingly Left a Snatch Block in a Dangerous Position**

The vessel’s crew had exclusive control over the snatch blocks and was solely responsible for moving them. *See, e.g., Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85, 95 (1946) (recognizing vessel’s nondelegable duty to provide safe working conditions). When crew members left the one-hundred-pound block in the down position atop a cargo unit, they created an obvious hazard that rendered the work area unsafe. *Cf. Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96, 104 (1944) (vessel liable for failing to repair or warn of defective gear that stevedore would be required to use). The block’s position blocked removal of cargo and threatened longshoremen working in the wing. The vessel knew or should have known that leaving this hazard would foreseeably cause injury.

**II. The Vessel’s Failure to Move the Block Was Active Negligence, Not a Mere Omission**

The vessel’s crew had the duty to secure the block in the vertical position after opening the hatch covers. Their failure to do so was not passive inaction but affirmative negligence creating a dangerous condition. *See, e.g., The T.J. Hooper*, 60 F.2d 737, 740 (2d Cir. 1932) (L. Hand, J.) (vessel owes duty of reasonable care to those lawfully aboard). Unlike cases where a vessel merely fails to discover a latent defect, here the crew *created* the hazard by leaving the block down. *Compare Alaska S.S. Co. v. Petterson*, 347 U.S. 396, 399-401 (1954) (vessel not liable for open and obvious stevedoring risks) with *Sieracki*, 328 U.S. at 95 (vessel liable for defective equipment). The ship cannot escape liability by arguing that the danger was open and obvious when the crew’s own conduct produced that danger.

**III. Serbin’s Decision to Move the Block Was Reasonable and Did Not Constitute Assumption of Risk**

Serbin moved the block to expedite unloading, a normal and expected act by an industrious longshoreman. *Cf. The Arizona v. Anelich*, 298 U.S. 110, 123 (1936) (seaman’s contributory negligence not a bar to recovery under maritime law). He had moved similar blocks before without injury and reasonably believed he could do so safely with a coworker’s help. The record shows no evidence that Serbin knew the block would throw him from the crates. Even assuming some risk, assumption of risk does not apply where, as here, the vessel’s negligence created the peril. *See, e.g., Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424, 431 (1939) (contributory negligence not a defense in admiralty when based on act done in course of employment).

**IV. Summary Judgment Was Inappropriate Because Material Facts Remain Disputed**

The district court improperly resolved disputed facts on summary judgment. *See, e.g., Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (summary judgment disfavored in negligence cases). Genuine issues exist as to: (1) whether the vessel’s crew knew the block was left down; (2) whether the block’s position created an unreasonable risk; and (3) whether Serbin’s actions were reasonable under the circumstances. These questions must be resolved by a jury.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
