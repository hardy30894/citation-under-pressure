# ARGUMENT

## The Use of Bryant's Valid Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment

### A. Tribal Court Convictions That Comply With the Indian Civil Rights Act Are Valid When Rendered and Remain Valid for All Purposes

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply to tribal court proceedings. As this Court has repeatedly held, the Bill of Rights "does not apply to Indian tribes." *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). Tribal courts derive their authority not from the Constitution but from the "continued exercise of retained tribal sovereignty." *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323–24 (1978). Tribal criminal proceedings are instead governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1303, which affords a right to counsel narrower than the Sixth Amendment's guarantee. Bryant's tribal court convictions fully complied with ICRA. They were valid when entered, and nothing in the Sixth Amendment retroactively invalidates them.

This distinction is decisive. A conviction obtained in full compliance with the applicable constitutional and statutory framework is not rendered "constitutionally infirm" merely because a different framework—one that never applied to the proceeding in the first place—would have required more. Just as Congress's plenary authority permits it to "expand[]" or contract the tribal courts' jurisdictional and procedural framework, *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004), that framework, not the Sixth Amendment, is the correct metric for assessing the validity of a tribal conviction.

### B. Nichols Establishes That a Valid, Uncounseled Prior Conviction May Be Used in a Subsequent Proceeding

This Court's decision in *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), controls. There, the Court held that a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction—valid because no sentence of imprisonment was imposed, see *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 373–74 (1979)—could be used to enhance a subsequent federal sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines. The Court reasoned that "an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid under *Scott* because no prison term was imposed, is also valid when used to enhance punishment at a subsequent conviction." *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746–47. The touchstone of the inquiry was not whether the defendant had counsel, but whether the underlying conviction was valid when rendered.

The same logic governs here. Bryant's tribal convictions were valid under the only body of law that governed them—ICRA—just as Nichols's conviction was valid under *Scott*. Under *Nichols*, that is the end of the constitutional inquiry. There is no principled basis for treating a conviction valid under ICRA differently from a conviction valid under *Scott* simply because the enhancement in this case establishes an element of a substantive offense rather than a sentencing factor. In both instances, the recidivist statute punishes only the defendant's *last* offense; the prior convictions establish status, not independent criminal liability. As this Court has explained, "[w]hen a defendant is given a higher sentence under a recidivism statute . . . 100% of the punishment is for the offense of conviction." *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008); *see also Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747 (recidivist statutes "penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant"). Section 117(a) punishes Bryant for his 2011 assaults; the prior tribal convictions merely establish the predicate status that triggers felony-level punishment for a third domestic-violence offense.

### C. Principles of Comity and Respect for Tribal Sovereignty Reinforce This Conclusion

Treating valid tribal convictions as categorically unusable disregards the comity owed to tribal courts. This Court has long recognized that federal courts must extend tribal judgments the same respect afforded to judgments of sovereign systems operating under different procedural rules. *Iowa Mutual Insurance Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987) (extending comity to tribal courts). As the Ninth Circuit itself has recognized in another context, "[c]omity does not require that a tribe utilize judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts," and extending deference to tribal judgments "is not an invitation for the federal courts to exercise unnecessary judicial paternalism in derogation of tribal self-governance." *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810–11 (9th Cir. 1997) (citing *Hilton v. Guyot*, 159 U.S. 113, 205 (1895)). Requiring tribal courts to mirror Sixth Amendment procedures as a precondition to the recognition of their judgments in a federal prosecution "intrude[s] needlessly on tribal self-government," a result this Court has rejected. *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978).

### D. Section 117(a) Reflects a Considered Congressional Judgment That Should Not Be Nullified by a Novel Sixth Amendment Rule

Congress enacted § 117(a) to address the well-documented epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country by ensuring that habitual abusers face felony consequences regardless of the forum in which their prior convictions arose. The statute expressly authorizes reliance on tribal court convictions as predicates. 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Reading the Sixth Amendment to bar their use—despite their validity under ICRA—would defeat this purpose and create the very jurisdictional anomaly the Ninth Circuit's rule now produces: identical recidivist conduct punished differently depending solely on the state or federal circuit in which the tribal defendant resides.

### Conclusion

Because Bryant's tribal court convictions were valid when rendered, and because *Nichols* permits the use of valid, uncounseled prior convictions in subsequent proceedings, the use of those convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) did not violate the Sixth Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
