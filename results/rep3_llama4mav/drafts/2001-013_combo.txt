ARGUMENT

I. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration Has Jurisdiction to Issue Citations to Uninspected Vessels

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) of 1970 grants OSHA broad authority to regulate workplace safety and health. Section 4(a) of the Act applies to "employment performed in a workplace in a State." The term "workplace" is not limited to land-based workplaces, and the Act's coverage extends to maritime employment. (See Marine Cook & Steward v. Smith, 78 F. Supp. 643, 646 (D. Md. 1948), citing The Herdis, 22 F. Supp. 891 (D. Md. 1938); see also G. Gilmore & C. Black, The Law of Admiralty § 1-11, at 32 (1st ed. 1957)). The MR. BELDON, a drilling barge, constitutes a "workplace" under the Act.

II. The Coast Guard's Authority Does Not Pre-empt OSHA's Jurisdiction Under Section 4(b)(1)

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to working conditions of employees with respect to which other federal agencies exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health. However, the Coast Guard's authority is not so comprehensive as to pre-empt OSHA's jurisdiction.

The Coast Guard's authority is limited to specific areas, such as vessel inspection, navigation, and safety equipment. (See 46 U.S.C. § 3301 et seq. (formerly 46 U.S.C. § 390a); H.R. Rep. No. 85-1387 (1958)). In contrast, OSHA's authority is broader, encompassing all aspects of workplace safety and health. (See 29 U.S.C. § 651 et seq.).

Historically, the Coast Guard's authority has been understood to be limited to specific areas, and not to be exclusive. (See United States v. Mead, 236 F. 167, 170 (D. Mass. 1916); The Pennsylvanian, 13 F.2d 455, 457 (E.D. Pa. 1926)). The Supreme Court has recognized that multiple federal agencies may have overlapping authority in certain areas. (See United States v. Borden Co., 308 U.S. 188, 198-199 (1939); United States v. C. I. O., 335 U.S. 106, 129-130 (1948)).

The cases relied upon by the Court of Appeals, Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), are distinguishable. Those cases did not involve the specific issue of pre-emption under Section 4(b)(1) and did not consider the full scope of OSHA's authority.

In fact, pre-1970 case law suggests that OSHA's predecessor agencies had jurisdiction over maritime employment. (See The Linseed King, 285 F. 975, 977 (E.D.N.Y. 1922); The Admiral Peoples, 295 U.S. 649, 653-654 (1935); O'Donnell v. Great Lakes Dredge & Dock Co., 318 U.S. 36, 40-41 (1943)). Moreover, courts have long recognized that state workers' compensation laws may apply to maritime employment, even where federal maritime law also applies. (See Davis v. Department of Labor and Industries, 317 U.S. 249, 256-257 (1942); Hahn v. Ross Island Sand & Gravel Co., 358 U.S. 272, 273 (1959)).

Furthermore, the legislative history of the Act indicates that Congress intended to maintain the existing jurisdictional boundaries between OSHA and other federal agencies. (See S. Rep. No. 91-1282 (1970); H.R. Rep. No. 91-1291 (1970)). Pre-1970 case law supports the conclusion that OSHA's jurisdiction is not pre-empted by the Coast Guard's authority. (See e.g., United States v. Republic Steel Corp., 362 U.S. 482, 489-490 (1960); United States v. Standard Oil Co., 384 U.S. 224, 226-227 (1966)).

In conclusion, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration has jurisdiction to issue citations to uninspected vessels like the MR. BELDON. The Coast Guard's authority does not pre-empt OSHA's jurisdiction under Section 4(b)(1) of the Act.
