## ARGUMENT

The constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this test, and its judgment should be affirmed.

### I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Grants States Title to Riverbeds That Were Navigable at Statehood

Under the equal footing doctrine, a State admitted to the Union acquires title to the beds of waters that were navigable at the time of statehood. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 224 (1845); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894). This title vests as of the date of admission and is not affected by subsequent changes in the river’s condition. *Packer v. Bird*, 137 U.S. 661, 669 (1891). The critical inquiry is whether the river was “navigable in fact” at the moment of statehood—that is, whether it was then “used, or susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce.” *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870); *The Montello*, 87 U.S. 430, 441 (1874).

### II. Navigability for Title Is Determined by the River’s Condition at Statehood, Not by Later Improvements or Changes

This Court has consistently held that the navigability of a river for title purposes is fixed at the time of statehood. In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931), the Court explained that “the question of navigability must be determined as of the time of the admission of the State into the Union.” The Court further stated that “the navigability of a river is not destroyed because of occasional portages or falls,” and that “the true test is the capacity of the river in its natural state for use as a highway for commerce.” *Id.* at 76. Similarly, in *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 591 (1922), the Court held that “the title to the beds of navigable rivers passed to the State upon its admission into the Union, and that the navigability of the river at that time is the controlling fact.”

The trial court properly applied this test. It examined historical evidence—including government surveys, newspaper accounts, and military records—to determine whether the specific stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable in 1889 when Montana became a state. The court found that each stretch was then used for commercial transport of goods and passengers. This fact-based determination is precisely what the constitutional test requires. *See Barney v. Keokuk*, 94 U.S. 324, 338 (1876) (navigability is a question of fact to be determined by evidence of the river’s condition at statehood).

### III. The Trial Court Correctly Focused on the Relevant Stretches at the Time of Statehood

PPL argues that the trial court should have considered only the specific segments where its dams are located. But the constitutional test does not require a segment-by-segment analysis divorced from the river’s overall character at statehood. In *United States v. Utah*, the Court examined the navigability of specific reaches of the Colorado and San Juan rivers, but it did so by looking at their condition at statehood, not by ignoring the river’s natural state. 283 U.S. at 82-87. The Court stated that “the navigability of a river is not to be determined by a mere consideration of the part of the river which lies within the State, but by the whole river.” *Id.* at 87. The trial court here followed that principle: it considered the historical evidence of each stretch’s use in 1889, including the presence of rapids, falls, and portages, and concluded that the stretches were nonetheless navigable in fact. That is the correct legal standard.

### IV. The Montana Supreme Court Properly Affirmed the Trial Court’s Application of the Constitutional Test

The Montana Supreme Court reviewed the trial court’s findings and concluded that they were supported by substantial evidence. The court correctly held that the constitutional test requires navigability at statehood and that the trial court had applied that test. The court also properly rejected PPL’s evidentiary objections, noting that historical documents are admissible to prove navigability at statehood. *See The Montello*, 87 U.S. at 441 (relying on historical accounts of river use). The court’s decision is consistent with this Court’s precedents and should be affirmed.

### CONCLUSION

The constitutional test for navigability for title requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of a river was navigable at the time of statehood. The Montana courts did exactly that. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
