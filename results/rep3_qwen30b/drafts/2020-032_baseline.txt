**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the unconstitutional policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief were moot upon Georgia Gwinnett College’s (GGC) abandonment of its challenged “Freedom of Expression Policy” and “Student Code of Conduct,” and that the claim for nominal damages could not revive a case otherwise rendered moot. This Court should affirm.

### I. A Case Is Moot When the Defendant Has Voluntarily Ceased the Challenged Conduct and There Is No Reason to Expect Its Recurrence

The doctrine of mootness ensures that federal courts adjudicate live controversies. *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). When a defendant voluntarily discontinues the challenged conduct, the plaintiff’s claim for injunctive relief is extinguished. *See id.* at 40. This principle applies with particular force when the challenged conduct is a policy, as opposed to an isolated act. *See Flanigan’s Enters., Inc. of Ga. v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), cert. denied, 586 U.S. ___ (2019). In such cases, the plaintiff’s claim for injunctive relief is moot if the policy is “unambiguously terminated” and “there is no reasonable basis to expect that it will return.” *Id.* at 1256.

Here, GGC’s revision of its policies was not merely a procedural change—it was a substantive abandonment of the very policies challenged. The “Freedom of Expression Policy” was revised to allow students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances. The “Student Code of Conduct” provision that had been used to justify the enforcement actions against Uzuegbunam and Bradford was removed entirely. These changes were not temporary or conditional; they were permanent and effective as of February 28, 2017, and remain in force today. The district court properly concluded that there was “no reasonable basis to expect that [GGC] will return” to the prior regime. *See id.* at 1256.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a voluntary cessation of challenged conduct, especially when accompanied by a permanent policy change, renders a case moot. *See, e.g., Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 190 (2000) (“[A] defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not moot a case if the practice is likely to recur.”); *see also* *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982) (holding that a city’s repeal of a challenged ordinance mooted a constitutional challenge). The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied this precedent. The record shows no indication that GGC intends to reinstate the prior policies, and the plaintiffs have not alleged any such intent. The case is therefore moot.

### II. Nominal Damages Cannot Save a Moot Claim When the Underlying Injury Has Ceased

Even if a plaintiff seeks nominal damages, a case is moot if the underlying constitutional violation has ceased and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence. *See Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1255. The Supreme Court has made clear that “a claim for nominal damages alone does not preserve standing when the challenged conduct has ceased.” *See id.* at 1256. The Court has consistently held that nominal damages are not a substitute for a live controversy. *See, e.g., * *United States v. Munsingwear*, 340 U.S. at 39 (“[T]he court may not render a judgment which would be of no practical value.”).

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied *Flanigan’s* to this case. The plaintiffs’ claim for nominal damages was based solely on the abstract violation of their First Amendment rights—no actual injury was alleged. *See* *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000) (holding that compensatory damages in a § 1983 action must be based on actual injury, not the “abstract value” of a constitutional right). The plaintiffs did not allege financial loss, emotional distress, or any other concrete harm. Instead, they sought damages only because they were “denied the right to speak.” *See* *id.* at 1231. This is precisely the type of claim the Court has held insufficient to prevent mootness. *See Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256 (“[A] claim for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge.”).

The plaintiffs argue that their case is distinguishable because they brought an as-applied challenge. But *Flanigan’s* explicitly rejected this distinction. *Id.* at 1257. The Court held that “the nature of the challenge—facial or as-applied—does not alter the analysis” when the challenged conduct has ceased. *Id.* The plaintiffs’ claim was not about a hypothetical future violation; it was about a past enforcement action that no longer occurs. The fact that the prior enforcement was allegedly unconstitutional does not create a live controversy when the policy has been abandoned and the conduct has stopped.
