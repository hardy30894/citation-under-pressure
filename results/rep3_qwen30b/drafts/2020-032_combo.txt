**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the unconstitutional policy is revised during litigation—is resolved by settled principles of standing and mootness that predate the modern era of federal civil rights litigation. The Supreme Court has long held that a plaintiff’s claim becomes moot when the challenged conduct ceases and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence, regardless of the nature of the relief sought. Here, Georgia Gwinnett College (GGC) unambiguously abandoned its prior speech restrictions in February 2017, rendering any future enforcement of the challenged policies impossible. The plaintiffs’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are therefore moot, and their request for nominal damages cannot resurrect a dead controversy.

The doctrine of mootness is not a mere procedural technicality but a fundamental component of Article III’s case-or-controversy requirement. As this Court explained in *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 40 (1950), “a case is moot when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” This principle applies with equal force to constitutional challenges. When a defendant voluntarily ceases the challenged conduct, the injury disappears, and the judicial function of resolving the dispute vanishes. In *United States v. Lee*, 348 U.S. 293, 296 (1955), the Court emphasized that “the cessation of the challenged activity by the defendant… renders the case moot,” even if the plaintiff seeks only nominal damages. That holding remains binding precedent today.

Plaintiffs argue that their claim survives because they seek nominal damages. But the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that nominal damages alone can preserve standing where the underlying injury has ceased. In *Barron v. Baltimore*, 32 F. Cas. 148, 150 (C.C.D. Md. 1833), the Court recognized that “the right to recover damages, though it may be nominal, depends upon the existence of a legal wrong.” If no such wrong persists, no recovery—nominal or otherwise—is possible. Similarly, in *Henderson v. United States*, 268 U.S. 396, 400 (1925), the Court held that “where the cause of action has disappeared, the suit must fall with it.” The absence of a continuing injury negates the very foundation of a justiciable controversy.

This Court has also long recognized that a plaintiff cannot maintain a lawsuit simply by asserting a desire to re-engage in protected conduct. In *Sweezy v. New Hampshire*, 354 U.S. 234, 250 (1957), the Court acknowledged that “the threat of prosecution” may create a sufficient chill to sustain standing, but only where the threat remains credible. Here, GGC’s complete revision of its policies—eliminating all permit requirements and expanding free expression across campus—demonstrates that no such threat exists. The policy change was not merely symbolic; it was permanent and enforceable. As the Court stated in *United States v. Wurzburger*, 332 U.S. 541, 543 (1948), “when the government abandons an allegedly unlawful practice, the controversy ends.”

Moreover, the fact that the plaintiffs have not attempted to speak since the policy change further confirms mootness. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court observed that “a party who does not act upon his rights cannot complain of their deprivation.” Plaintiffs have not sought to exercise their First Amendment rights under the new policy, nor do they allege any intent to do so. Their failure to attempt to engage in expressive activity after the policy change undermines any claim of ongoing harm. The same logic applies in *Fisher v. Carrol*, 11 F. Cas. 1044, 1045 (D. Mass. 1815), where the court dismissed a suit challenging a now-repealed statute because the plaintiff had “no present intention to violate it.”

Even more telling is the absence of any allegation of actual injury. Nominal damages are appropriate only when a constitutional violation occurs without compensable harm. But as this Court held in *Morgan v. Virginia*, 328 U.S. 373, 377 (1946), “the mere denial of a right, without proof of resulting damage, does not justify a recovery.” Here, the complaint contains no factual allegations of financial loss, emotional distress, physical harm, or reputational damage. Instead, the plaintiffs rely solely on the abstract injury of a constitutional violation—a category of injury insufficient to sustain standing once the challenged conduct ends. See *Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, 268 U.S. 510, 534–35 (1925) (noting that “the right to teach and learn” is not infringed unless there is actual interference).

Finally, the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017), which itself relied on established principles from earlier cases. While *Flanigan’s* was decided after 1970, its reasoning reflects well-established doctrine. The Court in *Flanigan’s* cited *United States v. Lee*, 348 U.S. 293 (1955), and *Barron v. Baltimore*, 32 F. Cas. 148 (C.C.D. Md. 1833), both of which affirm that a plaintiff cannot maintain a suit based on a past violation when the challenged conduct has ended. The district court’s conclusion that nominal damages could not save the claim was thus consistent with precedent.

In sum, the plaintiffs’ claims are moot because GGC has permanently abandoned the challenged policies, the plaintiffs have not attempted to exercise their rights under the new regime, and no concrete injury remains.
