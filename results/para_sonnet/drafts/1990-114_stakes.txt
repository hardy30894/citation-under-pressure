# ARGUMENT

## I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ADEA BECAUSE CONGRESS DID NOT CLEARLY MANIFEST AN INTENT TO INTRUDE ON THE STATE'S AUTHORITY TO DEFINE THE QUALIFICATIONS OF ITS OWN JUDGES

### A. This Court's Federalism Precedents Require a Clear Statement Before the ADEA Will Be Read to Override a State's Constitutional Choice Regarding Its Judiciary

The structure of dual sovereignty preserved by the Constitution protects "the residuary and inviolable sovereignty of the States." *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 501 U.S. 452, 457 (1991) (citing The Federalist No. 39). Among the most fundamental of the functions reserved to the States is the power to establish the qualifications for the officials who make up their own governments—especially the judges who exercise the State's sovereign judicial power. As Judge Bowman correctly recognized below, "the tenure of state judges is a matter of considerable importance to a state, and one that traditionally has been left to each state to regulate." 898 F.2d 598, 601 (8th Cir. 1990).

This Court has long required that Congress speak with unmistakable clarity before a federal statute will be construed to upset the usual constitutional balance between the States and the Federal Government. In *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336 (1971), the Court explained that "in traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved in the judicial decision." *Id.* at 349. That principle applies with special force here, for few state decisions implicate sovereignty more directly than the selection and retention of state judges. As the First Circuit held in a case squarely on point, courts should not "interpret the ADEA as conflicting with [a state's constitutional retirement provision] unless we find that Congress clearly has expressed its intention to bring state judges into the ambit of the federal statute." *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 54 (1st Cir. 1988).

### B. Missouri's Judges Are "Appointee[s] on the Policymaking Level" and Therefore Fall Outside the ADEA's Definition of "Employee"

The ADEA excludes from its definition of "employee" any "appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). That exclusion plainly encompasses appointed state judges.

Judging is not merely mechanical application of settled rules to undisputed facts. As the Eighth Circuit correctly observed, "[t]he reality of judging is that it is not always—and perhaps not even usually—merely a matter of mechanically applying settled, unambiguous principles of law to specific fact patterns as they arise." 898 F.2d at 603. Judges, particularly those on courts of last resort and intermediate appellate courts, routinely resolve unsettled questions of law that bind future litigants, lower courts, and the public. The First Circuit's reasoning, adopted by the Eighth Circuit below, is correct: "'[P]olicymaking' is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents." *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55 (quoting 680 F. Supp. 455, 462 (D. Mass. 1988)).

Missouri's judges also exercise substantial administrative and supervisory authority over the state court system, further confirming their policymaking role. The Missouri Constitution vests the Chief Justice and other supervisory judges with general administrative authority, Mo. Const. art. V, § 4, and the Missouri Supreme Court promulgates the rules governing the conduct of the state judiciary and bar. That "considerable supervisory power" reinforces the conclusion that "Missouri judges perform important policymaking functions." 898 F.2d at 604.

Petitioners' contrary argument—that the "policymaking level" exception applies only to executive and legislative appointees because it appears alongside exemptions for elected officials and their staffs—cannot be squared with the statute's evident purpose. If the exception were read so narrowly, then in every State that appoints (rather than elects) its judges, no exemption in § 630(f) would ever apply to the judiciary, even though elected judges in other States are entirely exempt. Congress is unlikely to have intended so anomalous and asymmetric a result. See 898 F.2d at 604–05.

### C. Because Missouri's Judges Are Excluded from ADEA Coverage, No Conflict with the Supremacy Clause Arises

Because appointed Missouri judges are not "employees" within the meaning of the ADEA, the Missouri Constitution's mandatory retirement provision does not conflict with federal law, and the Supremacy Clause is not implicated. See U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2. Respondent does not dispute that, had Congress clearly extended ADEA coverage to state judges, federal law would control. But no such clear extension occurred, and this Court should not lightly infer one in an area so central to state sovereignty.

## II. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SATISFIES RATIONAL-BASIS REVIEW UNDER THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE

Age is not a suspect classification, and mandatory retirement provisions for public employees are reviewed only for rationality. In *Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307 (1976), this Court upheld a mandatory retirement age for state police officers, holding that "[t]his Court's decisions give no support to the proposition that a right of governmental employment per se is fundamental," *id.* at 313, and that a State need only demonstrate a rational relationship between its retirement rule and a legitimate governmental interest.

Missouri's rule for judges easily satisfies that standard. A mandatory retirement age avoids the difficulty of case-by-case assessments of judicial competence, sparing both the judiciary and its members "the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged ... judges." *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980). It likewise reduces "delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges," a risk that "the work of judges" render acute given the "unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode." *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979). These are legitimate, rational justifications, and this Court's precedent forecloses petitioners' argument that Missouri must instead adopt a more individualized, case-by-case competency determination. As *Murgia* makes clear, "the legislature is not required to conduct an individualized assessment when a class of employees involves a substantial number of persons for whom age is a very significant factor." 427 U.S. at 314.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
