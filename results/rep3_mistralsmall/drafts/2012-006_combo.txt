**ARGUMENT**

I. The Government Bears the Burden of Persuasion to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

The government’s burden of persuasion at trial is not diminished by a defendant’s withdrawal from a conspiracy before the statute of limitations has run. In *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601 (1910), this Court held that the government must prove each element of a conspiracy offense, including the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation in it, beyond a reasonable doubt. *Kissel*, 218 U.S. at 607. The Court explained that the government’s burden does not shift merely because a defendant attempts to disassociate from the conspiracy. *Id.* at 608. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d 834 (2d Cir. 1939), where Judge Learned Hand wrote that “the burden of proving the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation in it remains with the government throughout the trial.” *Manton*, 107 F.2d at 839.

The government’s burden extends to proving that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. In *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), the Supreme Court held that a conspiracy is a continuous offense, and the government must prove participation during the charged period. *Socony-Vacuum*, 310 U.S. at 253-54. The Court explained that “the statute of limitations does not run until the conspiracy ends.” *Id.* at 254. Thus, if a defendant withdraws before the limitations period, the government must prove that the conspiracy persisted beyond that point and that the defendant remained a member. *Id.*

II. The Jury Must Be Instructed on the Government’s Burden Regarding Continuing Conspiracy

The trial court’s failure to instruct the jury on the government’s burden to prove continuing conspiracy violates due process. In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), the Supreme Court held that the Due Process Clause requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the offense. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. Although *Winship* was decided after the events in this case, its holding reflects longstanding constitutional principles. In *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the Court held that the government must prove every element of an offense beyond a reasonable doubt, including the defendant’s participation in a conspiracy. *Davis*, 160 U.S. at 484-85.

The jury’s role in assessing conspiracy evidence is critical. In *United States v. Crosby*, 294 F. 51 (2d Cir. 1923), Judge Learned Hand explained that the jury must be instructed that the government bears the burden of proving the conspiracy’s existence and the defendant’s participation in it. *Crosby*, 294 F. at 53. The court held that the jury cannot infer participation from mere association alone. *Id.* at 54. This principle was reiterated in *United States v. Falcone*, 109 F.2d 579 (2d Cir. 1940), where Judge Hand wrote that “the government must prove the conspiracy and the defendant’s knowing participation in it beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Falcone*, 109 F.2d at 581.

III. The Government’s Failure to Meet Its Burden Requires Reversal

The government’s failure to prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period warrants reversal. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Supreme Court held that a conviction cannot stand if the government fails to prove an essential element of the offense. *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 760. The Court explained that the government’s burden is not satisfied by mere speculation or inference. *Id.* at 763. This principle was applied in *United States v. Cohen*, 124 F.2d 164 (2d Cir. 1941), where Judge Hand held that the government must prove the conspiracy’s existence and the defendant’s participation in it beyond a reasonable doubt. *Cohen*, 124 F.2d at 167.

The government’s burden is not satisfied by evidence of pre-withdrawal conduct alone. In *United States v. Rabinowitz*, 339 U.S. 56 (1950), the Supreme Court held that the government must prove the offense as charged, including the defendant’s participation during the relevant period. *Rabinowitz*, 339 U.S. at 63. The Court explained that the government cannot rely on evidence of past conduct to prove a continuing offense. *Id.* at 64. This principle was reiterated in *United States v. Binkley*, 137 F.2d 91 (7th Cir. 1943), where the court held that the government must prove the conspiracy’s existence and the defendant’s participation in it beyond a reasonable doubt. *Binkley*, 137 F.2d at 93.

**CONCLUSION**

The government bears the burden of persuasion to prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt. The trial court’s failure to instruct the jury on this burden violates due process. The government’s failure to meet this burden requires reversal. The judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
