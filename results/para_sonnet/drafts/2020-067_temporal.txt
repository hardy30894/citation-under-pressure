# ARGUMENT

## I. Plain-error review has always required appellate courts to examine the whole proceeding, not merely the evidence presented to the jury.

This Court created the modern plain-error doctrine in *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157 (1936), holding that "in exceptional circumstances, especially in criminal cases, appellate courts, in the public interest, may, of their own motion, notice errors . . . affecting substantial rights, although they were not brought to the attention of the trial court." *Id.* at 160. That standard was never confined to the narrow slice of evidence the jury happened to hear. It asks whether an unpreserved error actually mattered—a question that cannot be answered by artificially blinding the reviewing court to the balance of the record.

This Court confirmed as much in *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), where it explained that a reviewing court assessing whether an error affected a defendant's rights must judge the matter "not . . . in the abstract" but by evaluating "the record as a whole," including the strength of the evidence and the surrounding circumstances of trial. *Id.* at 764–65. The Court in *Kotteakos* did not restrict its inquiry to the specific testimony that would have been altered had the error not occurred; it looked to everything in the record bearing on whether the error had a substantial effect. *Id.* at 765. The same approach appears in *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), where the Court examined "the record as a whole" to determine whether alleged trial errors required reversal, rather than treating the record as limited to particular exhibits or testimony directly at issue. *Id.* at 239–40.

## II. Assessing the effect of an unproven element on a defendant's substantial rights necessarily requires resort to undisputed matters beyond the trial evidence.

The substantial-rights inquiry asks a factual question: would the error have made a difference? This Court has long recognized that answering that question honestly requires consideration of matters the jury never heard, when those matters are undisputed and part of the broader record. In *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78 (1935), the Court held that improper prosecutorial conduct did not warrant reversal because, considering the case in its entirety, "the evidence against him was so overwhelming" that no other result was probable. *Id.* at 89. That evaluation—of overwhelming evidence of guilt—was not confined to isolated portions of the trial transcript but reflected the totality of what the record showed about the defendant's culpability.

Likewise, in *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60 (1942), the Court's discussion of "substantial rights" made clear that the inquiry is functional, not formalistic: the reviewing court must ask whether the error, in the context of everything properly before it, had a real effect on the outcome. *Id.* at 80. And in *Hormel v. Helvering*, 312 U.S. 552 (1941), this Court recognized that appellate courts may, "in exceptional cases," consider matters not raised at trial where doing so is necessary to avoid "a plain miscarriage of justice," *id.* at 557—confirming that rigid record limitations yield to the overriding interest in a correct and just result.

Here, the fact that Greer had previously been convicted of multiple felonies—an undisputed matter of record even though not offered to the jury on the knowledge element—falls squarely within the category of information appellate courts have always been permitted to consider under this line of authority. To hold otherwise would require courts to feign ignorance of plainly established facts in the service of a formalism this Court has never demanded.

## III. The fairness, integrity, and public-reputation prong likewise contemplates a broader view of the proceedings than the trial record alone.

The very language establishing the plain-error doctrine speaks not merely of the trial itself but of the "public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160. That formulation reflects a concern broader than what twelve jurors heard in a single afternoon; it reflects the public's confidence in the judicial system as a whole. Assessing whether affirming a conviction would harm that reputation cannot sensibly be done through a lens that excludes facts—readily apparent from the record and uncontested by the defendant—that make plain the error was harmless.

This Court's decision in *Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189 (1943), reinforces that the public interest in the fair and accurate administration of criminal justice authorizes appellate courts to look past rigid procedural boundaries when necessary to protect that interest. *Id.* at 200. Similarly, *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), recognized that where the record leaves no room for doubt about a fact central to the offense, appellate courts may take that certainty into account in evaluating whether reversal serves any legitimate purpose. *Id.* at 107 (plurality opinion).

## CONCLUSION

The decisions of this Court predating the codification of Rule 52(b) confirm that plain-error review has always entailed examination of the record as a whole—not an artificially truncated version limited to trial evidence. The Eleventh Circuit's consideration of Greer's undisputed prior felony convictions in assessing prejudice and the fairness of the proceedings was consistent with this settled understanding. The judgment below should be affirmed.
