**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the removal period. This Court construes statutes to avoid serious constitutional doubts when an alternative interpretation is “fairly possible.” Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). Indefinite civil detention “would raise serious constitutional concerns,” United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 755 (1987), because “liberty is the norm, and detention prior to trial or without trial is the carefully limited exception.” Id.

Section 1231(a)(6) applies broadly to aliens ordered removed “for many and various reasons,” not merely to “a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals.” It therefore cannot be read to permit detention “until the alien shows that no country will accept him.” Such a construction would convert a removal statute into an indefinite punitive or preventive scheme lacking the narrow tailoring required by the Due Process Clause. Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992); see also Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 218-19 (1953) (Jackson, J., dissenting) (indefinite detention of an alien “on our soil” implicates the same liberty interest as that of a citizen).

The statute’s text supplies no clear statement authorizing perpetual custody. It provides only that certain aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period and, if released, shall be subject to the terms of supervision.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). Supervision, not detention, is the default once removal cannot be accomplished. The Fifth Circuit’s requirement that an alien prove deportation is literally “impossible” demands more than the statute can bear and would authorize detention “no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable” removal may be.

Properly construed, § 1231(a)(6) therefore limits post-removal detention to the period reasonably necessary to effect removal. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention exceeds the statute’s authority. The district court correctly found that Zadvydas’s removal was not reasonably foreseeable; the judgment ordering his release, subject to appropriate conditions of supervision, should be affirmed.
