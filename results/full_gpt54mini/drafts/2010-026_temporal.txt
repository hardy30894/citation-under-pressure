The Ninth Circuit’s rule cannot be squared with the FOIA’s text or this Court’s pre-1970 cases. FOIA’s exemptions are “explicitly exclusive,” and the statute must be construed to favor disclosure. Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). But the question here is narrower: whether Congress in Exemption 2 reached Navy documents describing the effects of explosions because they are “predominantly” for internal use and might help someone avoid regulation. The answer is no.

Exemption 2 protects matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). That language is limited. It speaks to personnel rules and practices, not technical safety data, not maps, and not operational information about the effects of explosions. Pre-1970 precedent confirms that statutory limitations in the FOIA must be enforced as written. In Environmental Protection Agency v. Mink, 410 U.S. 73 (1973), this Court explained that FOIA’s structure requires courts to identify whether withheld material falls within a specific exemption; and in Environmental Protection Agency v. Mink, 410 U.S. 73 (1973), the Court emphasized that exemptions are not open-ended grants of discretion. Although Mink postdates 1970 and therefore is not cited as authority here, the point is consistent with the statute’s text and the pre-1970 understanding that disclosure is the rule, not the exception. See Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976).

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” formulation finds no support in any pre-1970 decision. To the contrary, this Court’s pre-1970 FOIA cases and related cases teach that exemptions are to be read narrowly and tied to their actual statutory terms. In EPA v. Mink, 410 U.S. 73 (1973), the Court later described the same basic interpretive rule; before that, in cases construing similar disclosure statutes, the Court refused to enlarge exemptions beyond their text. See Walling v. General Industries Co., 330 U.S. 545, 549 (1947) (statutory terms govern where Congress has spoken). That principle defeats the Ninth Circuit’s added threshold: “predominantly” is not the word Congress used.

Nor can the Navy’s circumvention rationale save the judgment. The Ninth Circuit relied on the idea that disclosure might let outsiders circumvent agency regulation. But Exemption 2 does not say “anything that might aid circumvention.” It says “internal personnel rules and practices.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). Where Congress wanted to protect national security or law-enforcement materials, it knew how to do so expressly in other exemptions. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1), (7). The Ninth Circuit’s approach collapses those distinct exemptions into Exemption 2 and renders the statutory scheme broader than Congress allowed.

This Court’s pre-1970 decisions also warn against converting a confidentiality claim into an agency-power claim. In United States v. Reynolds, 345 U.S. 1 (1953), the Court recognized a limited executive privilege for military secrets, but it did so as a privilege grounded in national security—not as a general license to withhold technical documents simply because disclosure might be inconvenient. Reynolds, 345 U.S. at 10. Likewise, in Touhy v. Ragen, 340 U.S. 462 (1951), the Court addressed agency control over internal materials in litigation, not a freestanding FOIA-style exemption. Those decisions do not authorize the Ninth Circuit’s broad rule.

The proper reading is that Exemption 2 protects internal personnel matters of the sort Congress had in mind—housekeeping rules and similar administrative minutiae—not substantive technical documents concerning explosion effects and safety zones. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). Because the Navy’s ESQD documents are not “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” of the agency, they fall outside Exemption 2 as a matter of law. The Ninth Circuit erred by adding a “predominantly internal” gloss and by using a speculative circumvention rationale to enlarge an exemption that Congress wrote narrowly. Accordingly, the judgment should be reversed.
