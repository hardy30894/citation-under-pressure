**ARGUMENT**

**I. DHL VIOLATED THE WORKER ADJUSTMENT AND RETRAINING NOTIFICATION (WARN) ACT BY FAILING TO PROVIDE 60 DAYS’ NOTICE OF THE MASS LAYOFF.**

The WARN Act requires employers to provide 60 days’ written notice before a "mass layoff," defined as a reduction in force that results in an employment loss at a single site of employment of either (1) 50 or more employees (excluding part-time employees) in any 30-day period, or (2) 500 or more employees in any 30-day period. 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(3). A "plant closing" triggering WARN notice occurs when an employment site is shut down for more than six months, or when 50 or more employees lose their jobs at a single site within a 30-day period. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(2); *Halkias v. Verizon Commc’ns, Inc.*, 646 F.3d 633, 637 (7th Cir. 2011).

Here, DHL’s November 10, 2008 announcement that it would cease domestic shipping operations by January 30, 2009 constituted a "plant closing" under the WARN Act. The shutdown resulted in the loss of employment for 506 workers at DHL’s Chicagoland facilities—far exceeding the statutory threshold. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(2). DHL concedes that it provided no WARN notice whatsoever. *See* App. 45-46. This failure to comply with the WARN Act’s notice requirement is a per se violation of the statute. *See* *United Steelworkers of Am. v. North Star Steel Co.*, 5 F.3d 39, 42 (3d Cir. 1993) ("The WARN Act’s notice requirement is mandatory and does not permit equitable exceptions.").

DHL’s argument that the severance agreements mooted the WARN claim is unavailing. The WARN Act’s purpose is to provide workers with advance notice to facilitate job training and job searches—not to compensate them for lost wages after the fact. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2102(b); *Halkias*, 646 F.3d at 638. The Supreme Court has made clear that the WARN Act’s notice requirement is not subject to waiver or release. *See generally* *Litton Fin. Printing Div. v. N.L.R.B.*, 501 U.S. 190 (1991) (construing analogous notice provisions strictly). Thus, the waivers signed by Appellants do not bar their WARN claims. *See* *In re CFLC Inc.*, 89 F.3d 673, 680 (9th Cir. 1996) (holding that WARN Act claims are not waivable).

**II. THE SEVERANCE AGREEMENTS WERE PROCURED UNDER DURESS AND ARE VOIDABLE.**

Even if the WARN Act did not apply, the severance agreements were procured under economic duress and are voidable. Duress exists where a party’s free will is overcome by a wrongful threat that leaves no reasonable alternative. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 175(1). The Seventh Circuit has recognized that a threat to terminate employment without severance can constitute duress when the employee has no reasonable alternative but to accept the offer. *See* *Batsell v. Stone Constr. Co.*, 599 F.3d 742, 747 (7th Cir. 2010).

Here, DHL presented Appellants with a Hobson’s choice: accept a meager severance package or face immediate unemployment without any financial cushion. The agreements were negotiated and signed in a compressed timeframe—drivers had just two days to decide whether to accept the ten-week plan, while other workers had slightly more time but still faced an imminent loss of income. *See* App. 23-25. This pressure was compounded by the fact that DHL had already begun layoffs in August 2008, leaving workers with little bargaining power. *See* *Batsell*, 599 F.3d at 747 (finding duress where employer exploited employee’s financial vulnerability).

Moreover, the waivers were presented as a condition of receiving any severance at all. The Seventh Circuit has held that such conditions can render an agreement unenforceable where the employee had no meaningful choice. *See* *Bowen v. Amoco Oil Co.*, 182 F.3d 745, 750 (7th Cir. 1999). The fact that Appellants were given seven days to consider the waivers does not cure the duress, as the coercive environment persisted throughout the decision-making process. *See* *Batsell*, 599 F.3d at 748.

**III. THE WAIVERS DO NOT BAR APPELLANTS’ CLAIMS.**

The waivers signed by Appellants purport to release "any and all actions, causes of action, demands, claims or liabilities ... arising out of [their] employment or the termination of [their] employment." App. 32. However, the Seventh Circuit has consistently held that such broad releases do not encompass statutory claims that an employer has violated fundamental public policies. *See* *Bowen*, 182 F.3d at 751 (finding that a release did not bar an ADEA claim where the statute served important public purposes); *Batsell*, 599 F.3d at 749 (holding that a release did not bar a claim for unpaid wages under the FLSA).

The WARN Act is similarly designed to protect workers from sudden, unanticipated job losses and to provide them with time to seek alternative employment. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2102(b); *Halkias*, 646 F.3d at 638. Allowing employers to circumvent the WARN Act’s notice requirement through coercive severance agreements would eviscerate the statute’s protections. *See* *In re CFLC Inc.*, 89 F.3d at 680. Thus, the waivers do not bar Appellants’ WARN Act claims.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in dismissing Appellants’ WARN Act claims and in enforcing the severance agreements under duress. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
