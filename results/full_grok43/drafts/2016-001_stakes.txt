**ARGUMENT**

Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that an infringer of a design patent “shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit” on “the article of manufacture” to which the patented design is applied. 35 U.S.C. § 289. When the patented design applies only to a component of a larger product sold as a unit, the statute limits the award to profits attributable to that component.

The Federal Circuit held that the entire profit on Samsung’s smartphones could be awarded even though the asserted design patents covered only the front face or bezel. Apple Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co., 786 F.3d 983, 1002 (Fed. Cir. 2015). That construction cannot be reconciled with the text. The phrase “article of manufacture” has long been understood to include components. See, e.g., Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros., 222 F. 902, 903 (2d Cir. 1915) (profits limited to the case of a piano when the design patent covered only the case). The statute’s use of the definite article “the” before “article of manufacture” confirms that the award is tied to the specific article embodying the design, not to every article in which the component is incorporated. See, e.g., McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 414 (1819) (Marshall, C.J.) (“the power to tax involves the power to destroy”).

This Court has repeatedly required apportionment when a patent covers only part of a larger product. In Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641, 646 (1915), the Court held that damages for infringement of a patent on a drill shoe must be limited to the value added by that shoe, not the entire drill. The same principle governs design patents. See also Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 489 (1853) (profits must be apportioned to the patented improvement).

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule produces absurd results. It would allow a design patent on a single button to capture profits on an entire automobile. Nothing in § 289 or its history supports such a windfall. The statute was enacted to overrule the no-profit rule of Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885), not to eliminate apportionment when the design is applied only to a component. See Nike, Inc. v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 138 F.3d 1437, 1441 (Fed. Cir. 1998) (tracing the legislative history).

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a determination of profits properly attributable to the patented components.
