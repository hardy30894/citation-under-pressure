**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Text and History of Section 1981a Require Proof of Egregious Misconduct Before Punitive Damages May Be Awarded.**

Congress did not write on a blank slate when it authorized punitive damages for Title VII violations. It borrowed language—"malice" and "reckless indifference"—that has long carried a specialized common-law meaning reserving punitive damages for the most blameworthy conduct. See Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (punitive damages available under § 1983 only for conduct involving "reckless or callous disregard for the plaintiff's rights," a standard drawn from "the common law of torts"). The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) confirms that punitive damages are reserved for conduct that is "outrageous, because of the defendant's evil motive or his reckless indifference to the rights of others." That formulation does not describe garden-variety intentional discrimination; it describes conduct at the extreme end of the spectrum of wrongdoing. When Congress imports settled common-law terms into a statute, this Court presumes it also imports their settled common-law meaning. See id. at 34 (looking to "the common law of torts" to interpret the scope of punitive liability). Section 1981a's malice/reckless-indifference language should be read the same way—as codifying, not abandoning, the traditional requirement that punitive damages redress only egregious misconduct.

This reading also harmonizes with the structure Congress created. Section 1981a caps *combined* compensatory and punitive damages at amounts ranging from $50,000 to $300,000 depending on employer size. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3). Congress would not have imposed such modest ceilings, applicable to every employer regardless of the severity of the violation, if it intended punitive damages to follow automatically from ordinary proof of intentional discrimination—a showing plaintiffs must make in every disparate-treatment case under McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). If intent alone sufficed, punitive damages would be the rule rather than the exception, and the careful statutory caps would function as a damages multiplier rather than as a safety valve for the rare, truly reprehensible case. The far more sensible reading is that Congress meant punitive damages to remain what they have always been at common law: a remedy for the worst offenders, not a routine addition to every judgment of liability.

**II. Courts Construing the Analogous Standard Under Section 1981 Have Required a Heightened Showing Beyond Ordinary Intent.**

Because Congress used the same malice/reckless-indifference formulation that had already developed under 42 U.S.C. § 1981—a statute punitive damages have long attached to, see Section 1981a(b)(1)—decisions construing that provision are instructive. The Seventh Circuit has explained that punitive damages under § 1981a demand something "more" than the intent required for liability itself, describing this as a "higher hurdle" than simple proof of intentional discrimination. Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co., 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996). Similarly, in Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co., 817 F.2d 1290, 1296 (7th Cir. 1987), the court tied punitive liability to whether "the application of the law to the facts at hand was so clear at the time of the act that reasonably competent people would have agreed on its application"—a standard that necessarily screens out close or debatable cases from punitive exposure. These decisions reflect the sound instinct that punitive damages should track blameworthiness, not merely the bare elements of liability.

That instinct finds further support in this Court's punitive-damages jurisprudence generally. In BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559, 575 (1996), the Court identified "the degree of reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct" as "[p]erhaps the most important indicium of the reasonableness of a punitive damages award." An employer's mistaken, even incorrect, judgment about a promotion decision—reached after genuine (if flawed) deliberation, and not accompanied by threats, insults, or campaigns of harassment—does not exhibit the reprehensibility that has historically justified punitive sanctions.

**III. The Legislative History Confirms That Congress Intended Punitive Damages to Be Reserved for Extraordinarily Egregious Conduct.**

The interpretive memorandum accompanying the 1991 Act, offered by Senator Dole and his cosponsors, states directly that punitive damages under the amendments are available only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). This statement was not an isolated aside; it reflects the compromise nature of the 1991 Act, which for the first time extended damages remedies to Title VII plaintiffs while simultaneously capping those damages and limiting punitive exposure to the most flagrant violations. The House Report likewise indicates that Congress understood § 1981a to demand of a punitive-damages plaintiff "an even higher standard" than that required merely to prove intentional discrimination. Reading § 1981a's malice/reckless-indifference language against this backdrop, the natural conclusion is that Congress meant to preserve—not eliminate—the traditional, independent inquiry into the egregiousness of the employer's conduct.

**IV. Applying an Egregiousness Requirement Here, ADA's Conduct Cannot Sustain a Punitive Award.**

Even accepting that a jury could find intentional discrimination in ADA's promotion decision, nothing in this record suggests the kind of outrageous, malicious campaign that the common law, § 1981 precedent, and the statute's legislative history reserve for punitive treatment. ADA's decision-makers engaged in a flawed but good-faith personnel process; they did not act with the animus or callousness that Smith v. Wade and the Restatement require. Absent that showing, punitive damages are unavailable, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
