**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Calls 2 Through 5 Because No Evidence Establishes That a Prerecorded Voice Was Actually Played on Those Calls.**

*A. Standard of Review*

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, applying the same standard as the district court. *Turner v. Baylor Richardson Med. Ctr.*, 476 F.3d 337, 343 (5th Cir. 2007). Summary judgment is proper only where “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact.” *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). In making that determination, the court must view the evidence and draw all reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). Where the record “could not lead a rational trier of fact to find” for the party bearing the burden of proof, judgment must go the other way. *Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986). Here, the record could not lead a rational trier of fact to find that DISH violated the TCPA on Calls 2 through 5, and the district court accordingly erred in granting Ybarra summary judgment on those calls.

*B. The TCPA Prohibits Only Calls Actually Made Using a Prerecorded Voice.*

The TCPA makes it unlawful “to make any call … using any automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice” to a cellular telephone number absent consent. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). Because the statute does not define “call,” courts construe the term according to its ordinary meaning and require proof that the prohibited conduct—here, the use of a prerecorded voice—actually occurred in connection with the call at issue. *Satterfield v. Simon & Schuster, Inc.*, 569 F.3d 946, 954 (9th Cir. 2009) (holding that the TCPA’s terms must be given their “ordinary, contemporary, common meaning”). The statute does not impose liability for the mere existence of equipment capable of delivering a prerecorded message; it imposes liability for a call made “using” that voice. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). Liability therefore turns on what happened on each call, not on the general capabilities of DISH’s dialing platform.

The record establishes that DISH’s Cisco Dialer played a prerecorded message only when a call was met by a “positive voice”—that is, either a live human answer or a recorded voicemail greeting that the system’s software affirmatively detected. Calls 1, 6, and 7 were coded “Positive Voice,” and DISH does not dispute that those calls involved delivery of a prerecorded message; DISH has conceded liability on those three calls. But Calls 2, 3, and 4 were coded “Answering Machine”—a distinct result signifying that the system did not register a “positive voice” trigger—and Call 5 was coded “Data Error-No Value,” indicating that the call did not even complete in a manner the system could classify. Nothing in this record shows that the prerecorded message was ever played on any of those four calls.

*C. The District Court Improperly Treated DISH’s General Admission of Using a Prerecorded-Voice System as Conclusive Proof That Every Call Used That Voice.*

The district court reasoned that “[w]ithout needing to determine whether the CISCO Dialer is an ATDS, Defendant has admitted to using a prerecorded voice in calls from the 8047 number,” and that this admission was sufficient “regardless of whether Ybarra triggered it with a positive voice.” That reasoning collapses the distinction between a dialing system’s capacity to use a prerecorded voice and the statutory requirement that the call actually be “made … using” that voice. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). DISH’s admission that its Cisco Dialer was configured to play prerecorded messages upon a positive-voice trigger is not equivalent to an admission that the message played on calls where, by the system’s own data, no such trigger occurred. See *Satterfield*, 569 F.3d at 954 (courts must look to what the statute actually proscribes rather than to a defendant’s general practices). A genuine dispute—indeed, an absence of any evidence supporting Ybarra’s position—exists as to whether a prerecorded voice was used on Calls 2 through 5, and summary judgment for Ybarra on those calls was therefore improper. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255.

*D. Call 5 Independently Fails Because the Record Contains No Evidence a Call Was Even Completed.*

Call 5’s designation as “Data Error-No Value” reflects that the system could not even classify the outcome of the dialing attempt, let alone confirm delivery of any voice message. Because § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii) requires that DISH “make” a call “using” a prerecorded voice, an attempted contact that generated no data confirming connection or message delivery cannot support liability. See *Celotex*, 477 U.S. at 322 (the moving party is entitled to judgment where the opposing party fails to establish an essential element of its claim). Ybarra offered no evidence—apart from the dialer log itself—that any message was played during Call 5. That failure of proof independently entitled DISH to summary judgment on Call 5.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s grant of partial summary judgment as to Calls 2, 3, 4, and 5, and remand with instructions to enter judgment for DISH on those four calls.
