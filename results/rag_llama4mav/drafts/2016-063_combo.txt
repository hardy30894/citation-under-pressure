The question presented is whether a Merit Systems Protection Board decision disposing of an employment discrimination case on jurisdictional grounds is subject to judicial review in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The petitioner, Anthony Perry, argues that the proper forum for judicial review is the district court.

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, 5 U.S.C. § 1101 et seq., governs the review of MSPB decisions. As a general matter, review of Board decisions lies in the Federal Circuit, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A). However, there is an exception for "[c]ases of discrimination," as to which review lies in federal district court, id. § 7703(b)(2). The statute defines "cases of discrimination" as ones in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" enumerated federal laws, id. § 7702(a)(1).

The Supreme Court has addressed the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals in various contexts. In Elgin, Joliet & Eastern Railway Co. v. Burley, 325 U.S. 711 (1945), the Court emphasized the importance of understanding the factual context underlying a jurisdictional determination. Similarly, in Levers v. Anderson, 326 U.S. 219 (1945), the Court highlighted the need to exhaust administrative remedies before seeking judicial review. These cases suggest that jurisdictional dismissals, like procedural dismissals, involve complex factual and legal determinations that may be subject to review in a district court.

The Court has also addressed the relationship between jurisdictional determinations and the merits of a claim. In National Labor Relations Board v. MacKay Radio & Telegraph Co., 304 U.S. 333 (1938), the Court held that certain factual determinations underlying an agency's jurisdiction were subject to judicial review. Similarly, in Lerner v. Casey, 357 U.S. 468 (1958), the Court considered the implications of an employee's refusal to answer questions on the grounds of self-incrimination, highlighting the complex interplay between jurisdictional and merits-based determinations.

Furthermore, in Chessman v. Teets, 354 U.S. 156 (1957), the Court emphasized the importance of ensuring that a record is properly settled before an appeal is heard. This concern for procedural regularity is relevant to the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals. In Hussey v. United States, 222 U.S. 88 (1911), the Court addressed the jurisdictional requirements for a claim to be heard by the Court of Claims, highlighting the need for a clear understanding of the jurisdictional basis for a claim.

In Beadles v. Smyser, 209 U.S. 393 (1908), the Court considered the implications of a statute of limitations on the jurisdiction of a court. The Court held that the statute of limitations was a jurisdictional bar to the claim. Similarly, in Glaspell v. Northern Pacific Railroad, 144 U.S. 211 (1892), the Court addressed the jurisdictional implications of a removal petition, emphasizing the need for a clear understanding of the jurisdictional basis for the removal.

In Douglas v. Green, 363 U.S. 192 (1960), the Court considered the implications of a state's failure to provide an appeal as of right to an indigent defendant, highlighting the importance of ensuring equal protection under the law. While not directly on point, this case suggests that jurisdictional determinations can have significant implications for the rights of litigants.

In Local No. 207, International Ass'n of Bridge, Structural & Ornamental Iron Workers Union v. Perko, 373 U.S. 701 (1963), the Court addressed the jurisdictional implications of a union's actions on a supervisor's employment status. The Court held that the National Labor Relations Board had jurisdiction over the claim, highlighting the complex interplay between jurisdictional and merits-based determinations.

These cases collectively suggest that jurisdictional dismissals, like procedural dismissals, involve complex factual and legal determinations that may be subject to review in a district court. The petitioner, Anthony Perry, argues that the district court is the proper forum for judicial review of the MSPB's jurisdictional dismissal. The statutory language and relevant case law support this conclusion, and the Court should hold that the district court has jurisdiction to review the MSPB's decision.
