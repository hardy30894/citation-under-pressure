**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT PAKISTAN’S EXPROPRIATION OF BEG’S PROPERTY DID NOT FALL WITHIN THE COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION TO THE FSIA.

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (“FSIA”) provides the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in United States courts. Argentine Republic v. Amerada Hess Shipping Corp., 488 U.S. 428, 434 (1989). Under the FSIA, a foreign state is presumptively immune from suit unless one of the enumerated exceptions applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), removes immunity where the action is “based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state” or where the action “is based upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere” or where the action “is based upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States.” Id.

A. Pakistan’s Expropriation of Beg’s Property Constitutes Commercial Activity.

The FSIA defines “commercial activity” as “either a regular course of commercial conduct or a particular commercial transaction or act.” 28 U.S.C. § 1603(d). The Supreme Court has instructed that the “nature” rather than the “purpose” of the conduct controls. Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 611 (1992). In Weltover, the Court held that Argentina’s issuance of bonds—an act typically commercial in nature—fell within the exception even though the bonds were issued to address a national financial crisis. Id. at 614.

Here, Pakistan’s expropriation of Beg’s land was an act of a type traditionally performed by governments in their proprietary capacity—acquiring real property for public use. See Permanent Mission of India to U.N. v. City of New York, 551 U.S. 193, 200 (2007) (noting that property acquisition and management are quintessential commercial acts). The fact that the land was allegedly used for military housing does not transform the act into a sovereign one; the nature of the act remains the acquisition and disposition of real estate. See Saudi Arabia v. Nelson, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993) (distinguishing between sovereign acts and commercial acts based on the nature of the conduct).

B. The Expropriation Had a Direct Effect in the United States.

The FSIA’s third clause of § 1605(a)(2) applies where the act causing the claim occurs outside the United States but causes a “direct effect” in the United States. The Supreme Court has held that a “direct effect” is one that “follows as an immediate consequence of the defendant’s activity.” Weltover, 504 U.S. at 618. The Eleventh Circuit has further clarified that a direct effect exists where the effect is “immediate and substantial.” See Rubin v. Islamic Republic of Iran, 637 F.3d 1237, 1243 (11th Cir. 2011) (quoting Transaero, Inc. v. La Fuerza Aerea Boliviana, 30 F.3d 148, 152 (D.C. Cir. 1994)).

Beg’s complaint alleges that his property was expropriated and then transferred to members of the Pakistani military, and that he was later offered an alternative parcel in the United States. These allegations, if proven, demonstrate that the expropriation had an immediate and substantial effect in the United States—namely, the deprivation of Beg’s property and the consequential loss of its value, which he seeks to recover in U.S. courts. See Keller v. Cent. Bank of Nigeria, 277 F.3d 811, 816 (6th Cir. 2002) (holding that a foreign state’s expropriation of property causing the plaintiff to suffer financial harm in the U.S. constituted a direct effect).

II. THE DISTRICT COURT’S RULING CONFLICTS WITH THE FSIA’S LEGISLATIVE HISTORY AND PURPOSE.

Congress enacted the FSIA to provide a predictable framework for resolving disputes involving foreign sovereigns, replacing the judicially created doctrine of absolute immunity. H.R. Rep. No. 94-1487, at 7 (1976), reprinted in 1976 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6604, 6605. The commercial activity exception reflects Congress’s intent to subject foreign states to suit when they engage in conduct akin to private actors. See Weltover, 504 U.S. at 614. The district court’s narrow construction of the exception undermines this purpose by insulating foreign states from liability for conduct that is inherently commercial in nature.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO CONSIDER THE TOTALITY OF THE CIRCUMSTANCES.

The Eleventh Circuit has held that courts must examine the “totality of the circumstances” when determining whether the commercial activity exception applies. See Rubin, 637 F.3d at 1242. This includes not only the nature of the act but also its connection to the United States. Here, the district court improperly focused solely on the locus of the expropriation and ignored the fact that Beg seeks compensation for property located in the United States and that the alternative offer was made here. These factors weigh in favor of jurisdiction under § 1605(a)(2).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in dismissing Beg’s complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The expropriation of Beg’s property constitutes commercial activity with a direct effect in the United States, thereby falling within the FSIA’s commercial activity exception. The judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
