The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Bryant, which held that an uncounseled conviction in tribal court cannot be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), is erroneous and should be reversed. The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, and the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) governs the rights of defendants in those courts. See Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). The use of prior uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment, as established by this Court's decision in Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994).

In Nichols, this Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction can be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, as long as the conviction did not violate the Sixth Amendment. Id. at 746-47. The same logic applies to the use of prior uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a). The Ninth Circuit's attempt to distinguish Nichols on the grounds that it was a sentencing case, rather than a guilt-phase case, is unpersuasive. The Court's reasoning in Nichols was based on the reliability of the prior conviction, not the stage of the proceedings. See id. at 747-48.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit's decision creates a circuit split with the Eighth and Tenth Circuits, which have held that prior uncounseled tribal court convictions can be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a). See United States v. Shavanaux, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011); United States v. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011). The Ninth Circuit's decision also undermines the purpose of § 117(a), which is to provide felony-level punishment for serial domestic violence offenders. By holding that prior uncounseled tribal court convictions cannot be used as predicate offenses, the Ninth Circuit's decision effectively nullifies the statute in certain jurisdictions.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is misplaced. Ant was decided before Nichols, and its holding is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's subsequent decision in that case. Furthermore, Ant's reasoning is flawed because it fails to account for the fact that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts. The ICRA, not the Sixth Amendment, governs the rights of defendants in tribal courts, and Congress has taken steps to expand the right to counsel in tribal courts in certain circumstances. See 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)-(c).

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Bryant should be reversed. The use of prior uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment, and the Ninth Circuit's decision creates a circuit split and undermines the purpose of the statute. This Court should clarify that Nichols applies to the use of prior uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a), and that the Ninth Circuit's decision is erroneous.
