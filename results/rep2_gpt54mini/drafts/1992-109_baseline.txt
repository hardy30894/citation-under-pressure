**Argument**

The Constitution does not permit a trial court to demand a special, heightened “reasoned choice” competency standard before a defendant may plead guilty or waive counsel. The controlling inquiry under the Due Process Clause is whether the defendant possesses the capacity required to stand trial: “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and a “rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 95 S. Ct. 896, 904, 43 L. Ed. 2d 103 (1975). This Court has never held that the Constitution imposes a higher mental-capacity threshold for pleading guilty or waiving counsel. To the contrary, Godinez’s rule is that the same competency standard governs. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389, 396-402, 113 S. Ct. 2680, 2684-88, 125 L. Ed. 2d 321 (1993).

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” test cannot be squared with this Court’s competency decisions. In Dusky, the Court rejected formulations that turned on whether a defendant could make a “reasonable choice” among alternatives; the constitutional standard asks instead whether the defendant has the present ability to understand and assist. Dusky, 362 U.S. at 402. Drope likewise emphasized that competency concerns a defendant’s understanding and ability to consult, not a judge’s appraisal of whether the defendant’s decision is wise. Drope, 420 U.S. at 171-73, 95 S. Ct. at 903-04. And Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 386-87, 86 S. Ct. 836, 842-43, 15 L. Ed. 2d 815 (1966), confirms that due process requires a hearing when evidence raises a bona fide doubt about competency, but it does not authorize courts to invent a more demanding substantive competency test for particular decisions.

This Court’s waiver cases distinguish between competency and the separate question whether a waiver is knowing and voluntary. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464-65, 58 S. Ct. 1019, 1023, 82 L. Ed. 1461 (1938), requires courts to ensure that waiver is an “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 242-44, 89 S. Ct. 1709, 1711-12, 23 L. Ed. 2d 274 (1969), requires that guilty pleas be knowing and voluntary. Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806, 835, 95 S. Ct. 2525, 2541, 45 L. Ed. 2d 562 (1975), protects the right of self-representation. But none of those cases suggests that competency itself varies with the decision a defendant seeks to make. As Godinez explained, “[t]he competence that is required of a defendant seeking to waive his right to counsel is the competence to waive that right, not the competence to represent himself.” Godinez, 509 U.S. at 399, 113 S. Ct. at 2686. The Constitution therefore requires a valid waiver, not a heightened competency standard. Id. at 400-01, 113 S. Ct. at 2687-88.

Nor does Indiana v. Edwards, 554 U.S. 164, 128 S. Ct. 2379, 171 L. Ed. 2d 345 (2008), support respondent’s position. Edwards permits a State to insist upon counsel for a defendant who is competent to stand trial but not competent to conduct trial proceedings himself. Id. at 178, 128 S. Ct. at 2388. But Edwards expressly rests on a State’s discretion to require counsel; it does not constitutionalize a heightened standard, and it does not overrule Godinez. Id. at 173-78, 128 S. Ct. at 2385-88.

Petitioner’s rule would also confuse due process with paternalism. The Constitution presumes that a competent defendant may make even grave, unwise choices. See Faretta, 422 U.S. at 834; Godinez, 509 U.S. at 399-400, 113 S. Ct. at 2686-87. A trial judge’s role is to ensure competency and a knowing, voluntary waiver, not to substitute a judicial view of “reasoned” decisionmaking for the defendant’s autonomy.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary approach should be reversed. The Due Process Clause requires only the established competency inquiry under Dusky and Drope, coupled with the separate requirements that a guilty plea and waiver of counsel be knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. Godinez, 509 U.S. at 396-402, 113 S. Ct. at 2684-88. It does not require a heightened “reasoned choice” standard.
