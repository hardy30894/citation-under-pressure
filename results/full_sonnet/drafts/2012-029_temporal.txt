**ARGUMENT**

**I. Minton's Malpractice Claim Is Created by Texas Law, and an Embedded Patent Question Does Not Transform It Into a Suit "Arising Under" the Patent Laws.**

Justice Holmes's enduring formulation controls: "A suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action." *American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916). Minton's claim is legal malpractice—a cause of action created, defined, and remedied entirely by Texas common law. That the malpractice inquiry requires the fact-finder to consider what would have happened in the underlying patent suit does not convert the claim into one arising under federal patent law any more than a contract suit becomes a patent suit merely because a patent's scope must be discussed to resolve it. In *The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913), this Court held that whether a case "arises under" the patent laws must be determined from the plaintiff's own cause of action, not from questions "which may present themselves in the course of the litigation" as incidents of proof or defense. The same principle controlled in *Excelsior Wooden Pipe Co. v. Pacific Bridge Co.*, 185 U.S. 282 (1902), and in *Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co.*, 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897), where the Court held that a suit on a contract touching a patented invention does not "arise under" the patent laws simply because the patent's validity might be discussed along the way. And in *Luckett v. Delpark, Inc.*, 270 U.S. 496, 510–511 (1926), the Court distinguished suits that truly arise under the patent statute from those in which patent questions are merely incidental to the enforcement of contractual or state-created rights. Minton's malpractice claim falls squarely on the incidental side of that line: the '643 Patent's validity is not the right sued upon; it is, at most, one link in the causal chain the plaintiff must prove to establish a state tort.

**II. The Hypothetical, Backward-Looking Character of the "Case Within a Case" Deprives the Embedded Patent Question of the Substantiality Necessary for Federal Jurisdiction.**

Even where a federal issue must be resolved to decide a state claim, jurisdiction does not automatically follow. As Justice Cardozo explained in *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–118 (1936), "not every question of federal law emerging in a suit is proof that a federal law is the basis of the suit," and courts must undertake "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." The patent question here is not substantial in that sense. Minton must show only that, had the experimental-use exception been timely raised, *he* would have prevailed in *his* particular infringement suit. That is a purely retrospective and case-specific hypothesis. It will not alter the '643 Patent's status, bind the PTO, or affect any other litigant's rights—precisely the concern this Court flagged in *Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.*, 339 U.S. 667, 671–673 (1950), cautioning against reading "arising under" jurisdiction so broadly that any case in which federal law might be discussed becomes a federal case. Judge Friendly's analysis in *T.B. Harms Co. v. Eliscu*, 339 F.2d 823, 827 (2d Cir. 1964), is instructive by analogy: federal jurisdiction attaches only when "the complaint is for a remedy expressly granted by the Act," not whenever "the mere presence of a claimed right under a federal statute" appears as "an ingredient of a cause of action." No provision of the Patent Act supplies Minton's remedy; Texas tort law does. The patent issue is thus a hypothetical predicate fact, not a substantial federal question warranting the exercise of exclusive federal jurisdiction.

**III. Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction Over State Malpractice Claims Would Disturb the Traditional Allocation of Judicial Authority Between State and Federal Courts.**

The regulation of attorneys and the adjudication of malpractice liability have always been matters of state cognizance. Because grants of federal jurisdiction displace that traditional state authority, they must be construed with care. *Louisville & Nashville R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908) (limiting "arising under" jurisdiction to what appears from the plaintiff's own statement of his claim, not anticipated federal defenses). *Gully* likewise instructs that federal courts should not seize jurisdiction over matters "normally, if not exclusively," within state competence merely because a federal ingredient can be found somewhere in the litigation. 299 U.S. at 112. Sweeping malpractice actions like this one into federal court would mean that any Texas malpractice suit touching copyright, trademark, or patent advice—however incidental—must be litigated exclusively in federal court, stripping state courts of authority over claims sounding entirely in state tort and professional-responsibility law. Nothing in the patent statute's grant of jurisdiction, 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), or in this Court's precedents, contemplates so significant an intrusion upon the States' historic responsibility for governing the practice of law within their borders.

For these reasons, the state-law malpractice claim does not "arise under" the patent laws, and the Texas courts properly exercised jurisdiction over it.
