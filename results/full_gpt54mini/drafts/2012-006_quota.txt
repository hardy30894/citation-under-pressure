Petitioner Smith’s convictions should be affirmed. The question presented misstates the governing rule and would invert settled conspiracy law. The government is not required to disprove withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt as part of its prima facie case unless and until the defendant produces evidence of withdrawal sufficient to raise that defense. Once a conspiracy is established, participation is presumed to continue until the defendant affirmatively withdraws. Smith v. United States, 568 U.S. 106, 110-11 (2013); Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912).

This Court has long held that withdrawal is an affirmative defense. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70. In Hyde, the Court explained that one who joins a conspiracy “remains a party to it” unless he takes affirmative steps to disavow or defeat the conspiracy. Id. at 369. That rule makes practical sense: the conspiratorial agreement itself is the criminal act, and continued membership is presumed from the proven agreement absent evidence to the contrary. United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607-08 (1910). Smith seeks to transform that settled presumption into a constitutional requirement that the government prove ongoing membership beyond a reasonable doubt in every case, even when the defendant offers no proof of withdrawal. Nothing in conspiracy doctrine supports that inversion.

The burdens of proof in criminal cases do not forbid allocating to the defendant the burden of persuasion on an affirmative defense. Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 206-07 (1977). In Patterson, the Court upheld a rule requiring the defendant to prove extreme emotional disturbance because the State still bore the burden to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. Id. at 210-11. The same principle controls here. The government must prove the conspiracy and the defendant’s knowing participation. United States v. Jimenez Recio, 537 U.S. 270, 274-75 (2003); Iannelli v. United States, 420 U.S. 770, 777-78 (1975). But withdrawal is not an element of conspiracy; it is a separate exculpatory claim that excuses otherwise established liability. Smith, 568 U.S. at 111-12.

The Court’s precedent on statutes of limitations confirms that result. A conspiracy offense continues until its objectives are accomplished or the defendant withdraws. Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 216 (1946); Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396-97 (1957). Because withdrawal cuts off future liability, the defendant “bears the burden of proving withdrawal.” Smith, 568 U.S. at 113. That allocation is especially appropriate where the defendant is uniquely positioned to prove affirmative acts of disavowal, such as notifying confederates or authorities. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70; United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 438 U.S. 422, 464-65 (1978).

Smith’s contrary rule would also undermine the practical administration of conspiracy prosecutions. Conspiracy is often proved by circumstantial evidence because the agreement is secretive by nature. Blumenthal v. United States, 332 U.S. 539, 557-58 (1947). Requiring the government to negate withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt in every case would impose an impossible burden to prove a negative over an open-ended period, even though the defendant can readily produce evidence of withdrawal if it occurred. Smith, 568 U.S. at 113-14. The Court has repeatedly rejected rules that would impose such asymmetrical and unworkable burdens absent a clear constitutional command. Patterson, 432 U.S. at 206-07; Parke v. Raley, 506 U.S. 20, 28-34 (1992).

Nor does due process require the government to prove a defendant’s continued membership during the limitations period once the conspiracy and participation are shown. The limitations defense generally belongs to the defendant unless Congress provides otherwise, and the defendant ordinarily bears the burden of proving facts that bring him within a limitations bar. Cf. Dixon v. United States, 548 U.S. 1, 5-8 (2006). Smith identifies no constitutional principle requiring the prosecution to disprove withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt. To the contrary, this Court’s cases treat withdrawal as a “defense” that a defendant must establish. Smith, 568 U.S. at 110-13; Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70.

Because the D.C. Circuit correctly placed the burden of proving withdrawal on the defendant, its judgment should be affirmed.
