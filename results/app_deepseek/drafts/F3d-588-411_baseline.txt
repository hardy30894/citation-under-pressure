**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court’s Supplemental Instruction on “Shielding” Was Erroneous and Prejudicial**

This Court reviews de novo whether the jury instructions accurately stated the applicable law. *United States v. Lee*, 454 F.3d 836, 839 (7th Cir. 2006). The district court defined “shielding” as “to prevent or obstruct the government’s ability to discover the alien’s presence.” That instruction was fatally incomplete. It omitted any requirement that the defendant take an affirmative act of concealment and any requirement that the defendant intend to prevent detection.

Section 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) does not define “shielding,” and the term is not self-explanatory in this context. The courts of appeals have uniformly recognized that “shielding” requires more than mere employment of an illegal alien. In *United States v. Kim*, 193 F.3d 567 (2d Cir. 1999), the Second Circuit reversed a conviction where the defendant employed illegal aliens, housed them, and paid them in cash, holding that “shielding from detection” requires “some affirmative step to conceal the alien from the authorities.” *Id.* at 573. Similarly, the Ninth Circuit has instructed that “harboring” and “shielding” require “some action that is likely to deter detection.” *United States v. Aguilar*, 883 F.2d 662, 668 (9th Cir. 1989). The district court’s instruction, by contrast, allowed the jury to convict for a mere omission—Ye’s failure to maintain I-9 forms and payroll records. That failure is independently and specifically criminalized as a misdemeanor under 8 U.S.C. § 1324a. If “shielding” swept in the same passive conduct, the felony provision would render the misdemeanor hiring offense superfluous.

The instruction was also circular and vague. Telling the jury that “shielding” means “prevent or obstruct the government’s ability to discover” substituted one broad phrase for another, giving the jury no principled basis to distinguish lawful hiring from felonious concealment. The term “obstruct” is at least as elastic as “shielding.” Because § 1324 is a criminal statute, any residual ambiguity must be resolved in Ye’s favor. *See United States v. Santos*, 553 U.S. 507, 514 (2008) (refusing to give a broad definition where a narrower one was consistent with the statutory text and the rule of lenity).

The error cannot be harmless. The jury specifically requested a definition of “shielding,” signaling that the term was dispositive. With an overbroad definition, the jury may well have convicted based solely on Ye’s failure to maintain paperwork—conduct that supports only the misdemeanor hiring offense. This was structural error in the presentation of the case to the jury, requiring reversal.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove That Ye Intended to Shield His Employees from Detection**

Under *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979), the conviction must stand only if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the Government, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt. No rational jury could find the intent element for “shielding” here.

The Government’s evidence showed that Ye knowingly employed illegal aliens and failed to complete I-9 forms, pay taxes, or maintain time cards. But it introduced no evidence that Ye took any affirmative step to conceal those employees from the authorities. To the contrary, Ye housed the workers in an apartment that he openly disclosed to immigration agents, and he transported them to work without attempting to hide their identity or presence. He did not use false names, hide the workers from plain view, or mislead investigators about their employment. The workers performed their jobs in a public restaurant. In *Kim*, the Second Circuit held that “employment of an illegal alien, without more, does not constitute shielding.” 193 F.3d at 574. The same principle should govern here: Ye’s conduct amounted to hiring violations, not felony shielding.

Nor was there evidence of the specific intent to prevent detection. Ye cooperated with investigators, provided information about the apartment, and even rehired a worker after the worker produced documents—albeit documents not in the worker’s name. That latter act may show awareness of fake documents, but it does not show an intent to conceal the worker from the Government. The Government’s theory would collapse the distinction between the misdemeanor offense in § 1324a and the felony offense in § 1324, punishing every paperwork violation as a serious crime. Congress did not intend that result. *See Kim*, 193 F.3d at 573.

Because the evidence was insufficient on the “shielding” count, Ye’s conviction on that count must be vacated, and because the defective instruction infected that count, the case should be remanded for entry of judgment on the lesser hiring offense or a new trial with proper instructions.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, Mr. Ye respectfully requests that this Court reverse his conviction on Count 1, vacate his sentence, and remand for proceedings consistent with the statutory text and the rule of lenity.
