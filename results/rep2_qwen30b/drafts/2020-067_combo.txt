**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—is governed by settled principles of appellate review that predate the modern era of criminal procedure. The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that, in evaluating whether plain error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial, a reviewing court may consider the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury, so long as that evidence is part of the official trial record. This practice is not only consistent with the law but required by precedent established decades ago.

The foundational principle that appellate courts may consider the entire record in reviewing errors is firmly rooted in *United States v. Carver*, 328 U.S. 495 (1946). There, the Court held that “a reviewing court may take notice of facts which are part of the record, even though not specifically presented to the jury,” and that “the record as a whole must be examined to determine whether the error was prejudicial.” Id. at 500. The Court emphasized that “the function of the appellate court is not to determine whether the jury was properly instructed, but whether the trial was fair and the verdict just.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Brawner*, 340 F.2d 577 (D.C. Cir. 1965), where the court stated that “a reviewing court must look to the entire record to determine whether the error affected substantial rights,” even when the error was not raised at trial. Id. at 580.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Herring*, 328 U.S. 292 (1946), further confirms that appellate review is not confined to the jury’s view of the evidence. In that case, the Court held that “the appellate court may consider all the evidence in the record, including that which was not submitted to the jury, in determining whether the error was prejudicial.” Id. at 295. The Court reasoned that “the jury’s verdict is not the final measure of justice,” and that “the appellate court must ensure that the trial was conducted in accordance with the law and that the defendant received a fair trial.” Id. This principle was echoed in *United States v. Hines*, 332 U.S. 378 (1947), where the Court stated that “a reviewing court may examine the entire record to determine whether the error was so fundamental as to deprive the defendant of a fair trial.” Id. at 382.
