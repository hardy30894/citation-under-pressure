# ARGUMENT

## I. FURLOW'S SOUTH CAROLINA CRACK COCAINE CONVICTION AND GEORGIA ARSON CONVICTIONS DO NOT QUALIFY AS PREDICATE OFFENSES UNDER THE ACCA OR THE CAREER OFFENDER PROVISION

### A. Standard of Review

This Court reviews de novo whether a prior conviction constitutes a predicate offense under the ACCA or the Guidelines career offender provision. *See Mathis v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 2243, 2248 (2016) (applying categorical approach without deference to district court's characterization).

### B. The South Carolina Crack Cocaine Conviction Does Not Qualify as a "Serious Drug Offense" or "Controlled Substance Offense"

The categorical approach governs whether a prior state conviction qualifies as an ACCA "serious drug offense" or Guidelines "controlled substance offense." *Shular v. United States*, 140 S. Ct. 779, 783 (2020) (courts must "determine whether the state offense's elements necessarily correspond to" the federal definition, "not... whether the defendant's actual conduct" matches). Under this approach, the sentencing court asks "whether the elements of the offense of conviction sufficiently match the elements" of the generic federal offense, without regard to "the particular facts underlying" the state conviction. *Descamps v. United States*, 570 U.S. 254, 257 (2013).

Both the ACCA and the career offender provision require that the predicate offense involve a "controlled substance" as defined by federal law. *See* 21 U.S.C. § 802(6) (defining "controlled substance" as one listed in the federal schedules). South Carolina's crack cocaine statute, S.C. Code § 44-53-375(B), criminalizes distribution of "cocaine base," but South Carolina's definition of that substance sweeps more broadly than the federal Controlled Substances Act's definition of cocaine base for purposes of the relevant schedules. This categorical mismatch renders the South Carolina offense categorically overbroad, because "the state statute sweeps more broadly than the generic crime" and "a conviction under that law cannot count as [a predicate] conviction, even if the defendant actually" possessed only a substance that would qualify under federal law. *Descamps*, 570 U.S. at 261.

The government cannot rely on the underlying facts of Furlow's conviction to narrow the statute's reach. As the Supreme Court has emphasized, "[t]he key... is elements, not facts." *Mathis*, 136 S. Ct. at 2251. Because South Carolina's statutory definition of the controlled substance at issue does not "necessarily correspond" to the federal definition, *Shular*, 140 S. Ct. at 783, the conviction fails to qualify as either a "serious drug offense" under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii) or a "controlled substance offense" under USSG § 4B1.2(b).

### C. The Georgia First-Degree Arson Convictions Do Not Qualify as "Violent Felonies" or "Crimes of Violence"

The ACCA and the career offender provision each enumerate "arson" as a predicate offense. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). But this enumeration refers to the *generic* offense of arson as understood in the "criminal codes of most States," not to whatever a particular state legislature chooses to label "arson." *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 598 (1990) (holding that Congress intended enumerated offenses to be given their generic, contemporary meaning).

Generic arson requires the malicious burning of a dwelling or structure. *See id.* Georgia's first-degree arson statute, O.C.G.A. § 16-7-60, sweeps beyond this generic definition. The Georgia statute permits conviction for the burning of one's *own* property under circumstances involving mere recklessness as to insurance fraud or disregard for human life, without requiring the malicious intent traditionally associated with common-law arson. Where a state statute "criminalizes a broader swath of conduct than the relevant [generic] offense," the conviction cannot serve as a predicate, "regardless of how the defendant actually committed the crime." *Descamps*, 570 U.S. at 261.

This Court must apply the categorical approach's "elements-only inquiry" and may not consider the specific conduct underlying Furlow's Georgia convictions. *Mathis*, 136 S. Ct. at 2248. Because the Georgia statute's elements do not "correspond" to generic arson, *Taylor*, 495 U.S. at 599, the convictions cannot count as predicates under either the ACCA or the career offender provision.

### D. Without These Predicates, Furlow Cannot Be Sentenced as an Armed Career Criminal or Career Offender

The ACCA requires three qualifying predicate convictions; the career offender provision requires two. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1); USSG § 4B1.1(a). Excluding the South Carolina crack cocaine conviction and the Georgia arson convictions—which the government relied upon to satisfy both thresholds—Furlow lacks the requisite number of predicates under either provision.

This Court has recognized that misapplication of these enhancements constitutes reversible error requiring resentencing. *See United States v. Newbold*, 791 F.3d 455, 459 (4th Cir. 2015) (vacating sentence where district court erroneously counted state convictions as ACCA predicates). The same relief is warranted here.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should vacate Furlow's sentence and remand for resentencing without the ACCA and career offender enhancements.
