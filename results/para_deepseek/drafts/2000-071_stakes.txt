**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School’s Exclusion of the Good News Club Was Permissible Viewpoint-Neutral Subject-Matter Discrimination in a Limited Public Forum.**

Milford Central School created a limited public forum by opening its facilities for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” while expressly excluding uses “for religious purposes.” *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 507 (2d Cir. 2000). When the government creates a limited public forum, it may restrict speech to the designated subject matter, so long as the restrictions are viewpoint neutral and reasonable in light of the forum’s purpose. *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985).

The Club’s proposed activities—prayer, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as a personal savior—constitute religious instruction and worship, not the teaching of morals and character from a religious viewpoint. The record demonstrates that the Club’s lessons explicitly distinguish between “saved” and “unsaved” children, urge children to “believe on the Lord Jesus Christ,” and include a “Challenge and Invitation” segment designed to elicit professions of faith. *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 508-10. These activities fall squarely within the category of “religious purposes” that the school policy excludes. *Id.* at 510.

The Court of Appeals correctly held that the school’s exclusion was subject-matter discrimination, not viewpoint discrimination. *Id.* at 511. The school did not exclude the Club because of its religious perspective on an otherwise permissible subject; it excluded the Club because the Club sought to engage in religious worship and instruction—a subject matter the school had not opened for discussion. *Id.* at 512. As this Court recognized in *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384, 393-94 (1993), a school may exclude religious worship even when it permits other uses of its facilities. Here, the school permitted groups like the Boy Scouts and 4-H to discuss morals and character from secular perspectives, but it did not open its forum for religious instruction. *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 510-11.

**II. The Exclusion Was Reasonable in Light of the Forum’s Purpose and Did Not Violate the Establishment Clause.**

The school’s policy of excluding religious uses is reasonable because it serves the legitimate purpose of avoiding entanglement with religion and preventing the school from appearing to endorse religion. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 271 (1981) (recognizing that a university’s interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations may justify content-based restrictions). Permitting the Good News Club to conduct religious instruction and prayer on school premises would create a substantial risk that the school would be perceived as endorsing the Club’s religious message, particularly given the young age of the children involved. *See Edwards v. Aguillard*, 482 U.S. 578, 584 (1987) (noting the heightened sensitivity to Establishment Clause concerns in elementary and secondary schools).

Moreover, the school’s policy is viewpoint neutral because it excludes all religious uses, regardless of denomination or belief. *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 511. The Club’s argument that its activities constitute a “viewpoint” on morals and character conflates subject matter with viewpoint. As the Court of Appeals explained, “the Club’s activities are quintessentially religious,” and the school “has not opened its limited public forum for religious instruction or prayer.” *Id.* at 510, 512. The school’s decision to exclude the Club was therefore a permissible exercise of its authority to define the boundaries of its limited public forum.

**III. The School’s Policy Does Not Violate the First Amendment’s Free Speech Clause.**

The Club’s free speech rights are not absolute. In a limited public forum, the government may restrict speech to the purposes for which the forum was created, so long as the restrictions are reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806. The school’s policy is reasonable because it ensures that school facilities are used for community-oriented activities that do not involve religious worship or instruction. *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 511. The policy is viewpoint neutral because it excludes all religious uses, not just those of a particular faith. *Id.*

The Club’s reliance on *Rosenberger* is misplaced. In *Rosenberger*, the University of Virginia had created a forum for student publications and excluded a student newspaper solely because of its religious viewpoint. 515 U.S. at 828-29. Here, by contrast, the school excluded the Club because its activities—prayer, Bible lessons, and invitations to accept Christ—constitute religious worship and instruction, not because of the Club’s religious perspective on a secular subject. *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 511-12. The school’s policy does not discriminate against any viewpoint; it simply excludes a subject matter that the school has chosen not to include in its limited public forum.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
