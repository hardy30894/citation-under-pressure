**ARGUMENT**

**A federal appellate court conducting plain-error review may examine matters beyond the evidence presented to the jury when assessing prejudice and the fairness, integrity, and public reputation of judicial proceedings.**

Rule 52(b) plain-error review has always required courts to examine the “record” as a whole, not the trial transcript alone. This Court's foundational decision, *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157 (1936), established that appellate courts may notice errors “if the errors are obvious, or if they otherwise seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Id.* at 160. Nothing in *Atkinson* confined that inquiry to the four corners of the evidence a jury heard; the touchstone is the fairness of the *proceeding*, a concept this Court has never treated as coextensive with the trial exhibits alone.

*Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), confirms the point. There, the Court held that a reviewing court asking whether an error affected “substantial rights” must ponder “all that happened” in the case, asking whether, “after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole,” it can say “with fair assurance” that “the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error.” *Id.* at 764–65. That inquiry is inherently contextual and practical; it does not artificially wall off information simply because it was not formally introduced to the jury. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), likewise instructs that technical errors which do not affect the “substantial rights of the parties” must be evaluated against the entire record developed in the case, not a curated subset of it. *Id.* at 239–40.

This Court's harmless-error jurisprudence outside the plain-error context confirms the same principle. In *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), the Court held that even constitutional error may be disregarded if the reviewing court is satisfied the error was “harmless beyond a reasonable doubt,” *id.* at 24, a determination this Court has made by canvassing the full record, including undisputed matters that were never contested at trial. Similarly, in *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85 (1963), the Court asked “whether there is a reasonable possibility that the evidence complained of might have contributed to the conviction,” *id.* at 86–87—an inquiry that necessarily requires assessing the strength of the untainted evidence and the practical realities of the case, not a hypothetical trial limited to what the jury actually heard.

That approach makes sense here. Greer's claim of Rehaif error rests on the proposition that the omission of a knowledge-of-status element from the jury instructions and proof might have mattered. But a court cannot sensibly evaluate whether an error “affected substantial rights” or undermined the “fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings,” *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160, without asking whether the omitted element was genuinely in dispute. Where the record—including matters such as a defendant's own admissions, presentence materials, or undisputed history of felony convictions—forecloses any realistic possibility that the defendant did not know his status, the reviewing court is not merely permitted but obligated to consider that reality. This Court took exactly that approach in the closely analogous sentencing context in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), holding that a sentencing judge is not confined to “restrictive rules of evidence properly applicable to the trial” when the inquiry concerns the defendant himself rather than the narrow question of trial guilt. *Id.* at 246–47. The same logic applies when an appellate court must determine, post hoc, whether a defendant was actually prejudiced by an instructional gap concerning his own knowledge of his own status—a matter as to which the defendant himself is the best, and often the only necessary, source of proof.

This is consistent, moreover, with the forfeiture principle underlying plain-error review itself. As the Court held in *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), “[n]o procedural principle is more familiar to this Court than that a constitutional right … may be forfeited in criminal as well as civil cases by the failure to make timely assertion of the right.” *Id.* at 444. Plain-error review under Rule 52(b) exists as a narrow exception to that forfeiture rule, reserved for cases where the defendant can show actual prejudice and a real threat to judicial integrity. A defendant who forfeited his objection should not be entitled to a form of review more favorable than one who preserved it—yet limiting the prejudice inquiry to the sanitized trial record would do exactly that, allowing forfeited claims to succeed on a fiction the record itself refutes. This Court's per curiam application of plain-error principles in *Silber v. United States*, 370 U.S. 717 (1962), likewise proceeded by evaluating the case in its practical entirety rather than through an artificially narrowed lens.

Because the Eleventh Circuit properly considered the entire record—including Greer's own prior convictions and background—in concluding that he could not show the Rehaif error affected his substantial rights or the fairness of his trial, its judgment should be affirmed.
