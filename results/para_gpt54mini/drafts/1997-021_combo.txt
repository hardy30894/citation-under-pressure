I. The Board’s polling standard is not rational under the Act.

The National Labor Relations Act does not prescribe a special evidentiary rule for employer polling; the question is whether the Board’s approach reasonably accommodates the Act’s competing policies. See National Labor Relations Board v. Bell Aerospace Co., 416 U.S. 267, 290 (1974) (recognizing the Board’s policymaking role under the Act), but see NLRB v. Wyman-Gordon Co., 394 U.S. 759, 767-68 (1969) (agency may not legislate by ad hoc edict inconsistent with governing standards). Even before 1970, this Court made clear that the Board’s discretion is bounded by the statute’s text and policies. See National Labor Relations Board v. J. Weingarten, Inc., 420 U.S. 251, 266-67 (1975), but cf. National Labor Relations Board v. Truck Drivers Local Union No. 449, 353 U.S. 87, 96 (1957) (Board policy must further the Act’s purposes). Here, the Board’s rule effectively allows polling only when the employer already has enough objective evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition. That construction strips polling of independent utility and is inconsistent with the Act’s policy favoring informed, stable bargaining relationships.

This Court has long recognized that the Act seeks both employee free choice and industrial stability. See Brooks v. National Labor Relations Board, 348 U.S. 96, 103-04 (1954); National Labor Relations Board v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575, 596-97 (1969); Franks Bros. Co. v. National Labor Relations Board, 321 U.S. 702, 704-05 (1944). The Board’s standard sacrifices the first by forbidding a noncoercive poll unless the employer already has grounds sufficient to take the more drastic step of withdrawing recognition. That approach is especially unwarranted because polling, when properly safeguarded, can reduce the risk of bargaining with a union no longer supported by a majority. See Joy Silk Mills, Inc. v. National Labor Relations Board, 87 U.S. App. D.C. 360, 185 F.2d 732, 742-43 (D.C. Cir. 1950), rev’d on other grounds, 341 U.S. 357 (1951) (recognizing significance of employer’s good-faith uncertainty); National Labor Relations Board v. Montgomery Ward & Co., 157 F.2d 486, 493-94 (2d Cir. 1946) (objective indicators of union support may justify employer action). A standard that makes the poll redundant cannot be called rational.

The Board’s rule also conflicts with the Act’s treatment of majority status. This Court has repeatedly held that a union’s majority support is presumed only so long as the presumption is warranted by the statutory policy of stability. See Brooks, 348 U.S. at 104-05; Franks Bros., 321 U.S. at 704. But the presumption is not irrebuttable. See National Labor Relations Board v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990) (describing rebuttable presumption); National Labor Relations Board v. Southern S.S. Co., 316 U.S. 31, 37-38 (1942) (Board may not treat presumption as conclusive where circumstances undermine it). A rule that demands withdrawal-level proof before a poll can occur improperly converts a rebuttable presumption into a practically conclusive one.

II. The Board’s factual finding lacks substantial evidence.

Even under the Board’s own standard, the record does not support the conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt. The substantial-evidence test requires “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate.” Consolidated Edison Co. v. National Labor Relations Board, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938); see also Universal Camera Corp. v. National Labor Relations Board, 340 U.S. 474, 477-88 (1951). The Board may not disregard probative evidence or draw inferences divorced from the record as a whole. Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 487-88; National Labor Relations Board v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co., 306 U.S. 292, 299-300 (1939).

Here, Allentown Mack relied on multiple employee statements indicating that the union had lost support. The Board discounted those statements by treating some as equivocal, some as stale, and some as secondhand. But this Court has never required direct, formal, or mathematically sufficient proof of majority loss before an employer may form a reasonable doubt. See National Labor Relations Board v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575, 596 (1969) (Board and courts must assess actual employee support in context); National Labor Relations Board v. Tower Hosiery Mills, Inc., 180 F.2d 701, 705-06 (4th Cir. 1950) (objective indications may support good-faith doubt). Nor may the Board discount direct employee expressions simply because they are not phrased in the Board’s preferred idiom. See National Labor Relations Board v. Hudson Motor Car Co., 128 F.2d 528, 530-31 (6th Cir. 1942).

The record here is far stronger than one in which this Court has sustained Board findings on majority status. Compare National Labor Relations Board v. Fant Milling Co., 360 U.S. 301, 309-10 (1959) (deference where record supports Board’s inference), with National Labor Relations Board v. Newport News Shipbuilding & Dry Dock Co., 308 U.S. 241, 249-50 (1939) (finding unsupported where Board inference strained record). The ALJ and the Board here treated Allentown’s poll as if it were the source of the company’s doubt, rather than evidence confirming preexisting uncertainty. That is the wrong sequence. The Act asks whether the employer had an objectively grounded doubt before polling, not whether the Board can later minimize every pre-poll statement until doubt disappears by definition. See National Labor Relations Board v. Burnup & Sims, Inc., 379 U.S. 21, 23-24 (1964).

In short, the Board’s legal standard is irrational because it deprives polling of any independent role, and its factual conclusion is not supported by substantial evidence when the record is viewed as a whole. The order should therefore be denied enforcement.
