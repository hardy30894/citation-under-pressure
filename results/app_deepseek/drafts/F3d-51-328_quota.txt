ARGUMENT

The district court abused its discretion by granting a preliminary injunction that compels Appellants to provide Appellee with a parking space outside the cooperative’s established wait-list system. The injunction improperly imposes a duty of reasonable accommodation where Appellee was not otherwise qualified for the benefit sought, and where the requested relief would fundamentally alter Appellants’ legitimate, nondiscriminatory allocation policy.

I. The District Court Erred in Finding a Likelihood of Success on the Merits

The Fair Housing Amendments Act requires only “reasonable accommodations” that do not impose an undue hardship or require a fundamental alteration of the housing provider’s operations. *Alexander v. Choate*, 469 U.S. 287, 300 (1985); *Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, 442 U.S. 397, 410 (1979). A neutral first-come, first-served waiting list for parking spaces is a legitimate, nondiscriminatory rule. It does not discriminate against persons with disabilities; it treats all residents equally regardless of disability. *United States v. California Mobile Home Park Mgmt. Co.*, 29 F.3d 1413, 1416–17 (9th Cir. 1994) (holding that FHAA does not require a landlord to prioritize disabled tenants over others on a waiting list for parking). The accommodation sought—an immediate parking space without regard to the wait list—is not reasonable because it would require Appellants to disrupt the settled expectations of more than 300 other residents who have waited years for a space. *Bangerter v. Orem City Corp.*, 46 F.3d 1491, 1503 (10th Cir. 1995) (“[A] reasonable accommodation does not require a housing provider to ‘ignore reasonable, nondiscriminatory rules that are necessary to the orderly administration of a housing program.’”); *Hovsons, Inc. v. Township of Brick*, 89 F.3d 1096, 1102 (3d Cir. 1996) (same for waiting lists under the FHAA).

Appellee’s disability does not entitle her to skip the queue. The FHAA does not require housing providers to give disabled persons a preference over non-disabled persons who are similarly situated. *Oxford House-C v. City of St. Louis*, 843 F.2d 1116, 1121 (8th Cir. 1988) (reasonable accommodation must not impose “an undue financial or administrative burden” or “a fundamental alteration in the nature of the program”). Appellants have already accommodated other disabled residents without disrupting the wait list; no evidence shows the wait list operates to exclude persons with disabilities. Indeed, Appellee remains eligible for a parking space in the normal course, after which Appellants pledged to make reasonable accommodations for her specific needs (e.g., assignment of a space near the elevator). Because the neutral rule does not discriminate, Appellee cannot show a likelihood of success on the merits. *Jacobsen v. New York City Health & Hosps. Corp.*, 22 F.3d 327, 330 (2d Cir. 1994) (plaintiff must show clear likelihood of success on the merits for preliminary injunction).

II. The District Court Erred in Finding Irreparable Harm

Appellee’s claimed injuries—difficulty finding street parking and occasional urinary accidents—do not constitute irreparable harm. Such harm is speculative and can be redressed by money damages or alternative transportation. *Hoxworth v. Blinder, Robinson & Co.*, 903 F.2d 186, 198 (3d Cir. 1990) (preliminary injunction requires “actual and imminent” irreparable harm, not merely speculative). Appellee has used public transportation, car services, and street parking with a handicapped sticker for years; nothing prevented her from continuing those arrangements while awaiting a space on the wait list. Moreover, Appellants offered to provide reasonable accommodation once she obtained a space. The district court improperly equated inconvenience with irreparable injury. *Jacobsen*, 22 F.3d at 330 (no irreparable harm where plaintiff’s medical needs could be addressed through other means).

III. The Balance of Hardships and Public Interest Weigh Against the Injunction

Granting the injunction disrupts Appellants’ long-standing, fair allocation policy and injures the 66 residents already on the wait list, none of whom received notice or a hearing. *Salute v. Stratford Greens Garden Apartments*, 136 F.3d 293, 300 (2d Cir. 1998) (district court must consider harm to other residents). The public interest in enforcing neutral, nondiscriminatory housing rules and in avoiding involuntary priority for disabled individuals over others who have patiently waited counsels against the injunction. *Bangerter*, 46 F.3d at 1503.

Because Appellee failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success, irreparable harm, or that the accommodation requested is reasonable without fundamentally altering the cooperative’s operations, the preliminary injunction should be vacated.
