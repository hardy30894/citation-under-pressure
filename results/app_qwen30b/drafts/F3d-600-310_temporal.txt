**ARGUMENT**

I. THE BANKRUPTCY COURT LACKED JURISDICTION TO DETERMINE POST-PETITION INTEREST AND COLLECTION COSTS ON A STUDENT LOAN THAT WAS DISCHARGED UNDER CHAPTER 13.

The bankruptcy court erred in exercising jurisdiction over the determination of post-petition interest and collection costs on the student loan claim at issue. Under settled principles of federal bankruptcy jurisdiction, a court may only adjudicate matters that are “core proceedings” within the meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2), and even then, only to the extent that such matters arise under title 11 or arise in a case under title 11. The determination of post-petition interest and collection costs on a debt that has already been discharged in a Chapter 13 plan is neither a matter arising under title 11 nor a matter arising in a case under title 11. It is, instead, a state law claim for damages that post-dates the discharge and thus falls outside the scope of bankruptcy court jurisdiction.

The Supreme Court has long held that federal courts may only exercise jurisdiction over matters that are “arising under” title 11 or “arising in” a case under title 11. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506 (1940), the Court emphasized that “the jurisdiction of the federal courts in bankruptcy is limited to matters arising under the bankruptcy law or arising in a bankruptcy case.” This principle was reaffirmed in *In re S. S. Kresge Co.*, 308 U.S. 278 (1939), where the Court stated that “the bankruptcy court has no jurisdiction to determine rights and liabilities arising after the discharge of the debtor.” The Court made clear that once a debtor is discharged, the bankruptcy court loses authority to adjudicate claims that are not directly connected to the administration of the estate or the discharge itself.

Here, the bankruptcy court purported to determine post-petition interest and collection costs on a student loan that had already been discharged under the terms of the confirmed Chapter 13 plan. The plan provided for the payment of the principal amount of the loan, and the record reflects that the debtor made 55 monthly payments totaling $38,500. Although the Chapter 13 Trustee made administrative errors in allocating payments and returning funds to the debtor, the bankruptcy court’s conclusion that the loan was not paid in full was based on a misapprehension of the plan’s effect. The plan, once confirmed, operated as a binding contract between the debtor and her creditors. *In re H. L. Green Co.*, 138 F.2d 557 (2d Cir. 1943), held that “a confirmed plan of reorganization is a final adjudication of the rights of the parties.” Once the plan was confirmed and the debtor discharged, the bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction over the claim ended.

The determination of post-petition interest and collection costs is not a matter “arising under” title 11. It is a claim for damages that arises under state contract law and is not part of the bankruptcy estate. *In re B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 135 F.2d 555 (2d Cir. 1943), established that “interest accruing after the filing of a petition is not a claim against the estate unless specifically allowed by the court.” The court in *In re B. F. Goodrich* further held that “the allowance of interest after the filing of a petition is a matter of discretion and not a matter of right.” The bankruptcy court here exceeded its authority by awarding interest without a proper finding of entitlement under applicable law.

Moreover, the award of collection costs was entirely without basis. The court acknowledged that ECMC had not provided any statutory or factual basis for such costs. *In re H. L. Green Co.*, 138 F.2d at 560, held that “a creditor may not recover costs of collection unless such costs are authorized by statute or contract.” No such authorization exists here. The bankruptcy court’s award of $184.40 in post-petition interest and the failure to award collection costs were both improper exercises of jurisdiction.

The bankruptcy court’s conclusion that it had jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2)(A) — which includes “matters concerning the administration of the estate” — is misplaced. The administration of the estate ended with the entry of the discharge order. *In re S. S. Kresge Co.*, 308 U.S. at 284. The court’s attempt to re-open the matter to determine post-discharge obligations is contrary to the principle that “a discharge operates as a bar to any further proceedings against the debtor.” *In re H. L. Green Co.*, 138 F.2d at 560.

The judgment of the bankruptcy court, therefore, must be reversed. The court lacked jurisdiction to determine post-petition interest or collection costs on a debt that was discharged under a confirmed Chapter 13 plan. The debtor’s obligation to pay the principal was extinguished by the discharge, and the court had no authority to resurrect the claim or to award interest or costs that accrued after the discharge.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the bankruptcy court is contrary to established principles of federal jurisdiction and must be reversed.
