**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO THE VALIDITY OF § 229.**

The Third Circuit correctly held that Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to § 229. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939) (“[P]rivate parties ... have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.”); *see also Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998) (courts must address standing before reaching the merits). The Tenth Amendment “is a structural safeguard rather than an individual right.” *Bond v. United States*, 564 U.S. 211, 221 (2011) (citing *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 156 (1992)). It “does not confer rights on individuals but instead ‘leaves to the several States’ the powers not granted to the Federal Government.” *Id.* (quoting *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 501 U.S. 452, 460 (1991)). Thus, the Amendment “is not a limit on Congress’s power, but a limit on the power of the federal government’s power over the states.” *Id.* (internal quotation marks omitted).

Because the Tenth Amendment protects state sovereignty—not individual rights—private litigants may not invoke it to challenge federal legislation. *Tennessee Electric Power*, 306 U.S. at 144; *see also Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971–72 (9th Cir. 2009) (private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment challenge); *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 2004) (same). Bond’s reliance on dicta in *Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. U.S. Dep’t of Energy*, 666 F.2d 1359, 1368 n.16 (11th Cir. 1982), is misplaced. As the First Circuit has recognized, *Atlanta Gas Light* did not consider *Tennessee Electric Power*, and subsequent Eleventh Circuit decisions have retreated from its holding. *Dillard v. Chilton County Comm’n*, 495 F.3d 1324, 1335 (11th Cir. 2007); *Seniors Civil Liberties Ass’n v. Kemp*, 965 F.2d 1030, 1034 n.6 (11th Cir. 1992).

Bond’s argument that *Tennessee Electric Power* is nonbinding dicta is unavailing. The Supreme Court in that case “dismissed the Tenth Amendment claim after analyzing both the standing issue and the merits,” making its holding an alternative ground for decision. *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (citing *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978)). Moreover, the standing question in *Tennessee Electric Power* implicated the constitutional jurisdiction of a federal court, rendering its resolution essential. *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 94–95.

**II. § 229 IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER AND NECESSARY AND PROPER CLAUSE AUTHORITY.**

Even if Bond had standing, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power. In *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920), the Supreme Court held that “[i]f the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.” The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 implements it by criminalizing conduct prohibited by the Convention. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 222–23 (recognizing that § 229 was enacted to implement the Convention).

Congress’s authority under the Necessary and Proper Clause is broad. *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004) (“[T]he treaty power, like the commerce power, is an independent grant of authority to Congress and the President.”). The Convention requires signatory nations to “adopt the necessary measures to implement its obligations,” including enacting penal legislation. *Chemical Weapons Convention*, 32 I.L.M. 800, 810 (1993). Section 229 is a permissible means of fulfilling that obligation. *See Holland*, 252 U.S. at 433 (recognizing that treaty-implementing legislation may address matters “of the sharpest exigency for the national well-being” that Congress could not otherwise regulate).

Bond’s reliance on *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144 (1992), and *Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898 (1997), is misplaced. Those cases involved commandeering of state legislatures and executive officials, not the exercise of Congress’s treaty power. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 222 (distinguishing *New York* and *Printz*). The treaty power is an independent source of federal authority that does not intrude on state sovereignty in the same manner. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432–33.

**III. § 229 DOES NOT VIOLATE THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE.**

Bond’s void-for-vagueness challenge fails because § 229 provides fair notice of the conduct it prohibits. *United States v. Powell*, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975) (a statute is not void for vagueness if it “define[s] the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited”). Section 229 criminalizes the knowing use of a “toxic chemical” to cause death, harm, or incapacitation. 18 U.S.C. §§ 229(a)(1), 229F(8)(A). Bond’s repeated attempts to poison her victim with potassium dichromate plainly fall within this prohibition. *See id.*

Bond’s overbreadth argument also fails. The statute’s exceptions—such as for “peaceful purposes” like self-defense or law enforcement—do not render it unconstitutionally broad. *See United States v. Moore*, 486 F.3d 106, 112 (D.C. Cir. 2007) (upholding § 229 against overbreadth challenge). The statute targets only conduct prohibited by the Chemical Weapons Convention, which is narrowly tailored to address chemical weapons threats. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 223.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed. Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power, and the statute does not violate the Due Process Clause.
