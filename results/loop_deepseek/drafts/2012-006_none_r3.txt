**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether, when a defendant introduces evidence that a conspiracy dissolved before the statute-of-limitations period, the government retains the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during that period. The answer is yes. The government’s burden never shifts. The lower court erred by effectively requiring petitioners to disprove their membership, and that error requires reversal.

**I. The Government Must Prove That the Defendant Remained a Member of the Conspiracy During the Limitations Period**

A conspiracy is a continuing offense. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 607–08 (1910). Its continuity is an essential element of the crime. Accordingly, the government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and that the defendant was a party to it during that time. *United States v. Cohen*, 145 F.2d 82, 89 (2d Cir. 1944); *United States v. Liss*, 105 F.2d 144, 146 (2d Cir. 1939). This requirement flows from the fundamental rule that the prosecution must prove every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895); *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895). The presumption of innocence remains with the defendant throughout trial, and the burden never shifts to him to disprove any element. *Coffin*, 156 U.S. at 453; *United States v. Gooding*, 25 U.S. 460, 475 (1827).

**II. Withdrawal Before the Limitations Period Does Not Shift the Burden of Persuasion to the Defendant**

This Court has held that a defendant who withdraws from a conspiracy bears the burden of proving that withdrawal by affirmative evidence. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369–71 (1912). But *Hyde* applies only *after* the government has already proved the defendant’s membership within the limitations period. *Id.* at 369 (“[W]hen the conspiracy is proved … the burden is on the accused to show withdrawal.”) (emphasis added). *Hyde* does not license the government to presume membership during the limitations period from membership at an earlier time. To the contrary, the government must prove continuity. Where, as here, the defendant introduces evidence that the conspiracy’s leaders split up *before* the limitations period began, the government’s initial burden is not satisfied. The defendant is not required to prove withdrawal as an affirmative defense; rather, the evidence of dissolution simply creates a reasonable doubt as to whether the conspiracy continued into the limitations period at all. The government must then prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member during that period, notwithstanding the evidence of dissolution. *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943) (no presumption may relieve the government of proving every element); *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 274–75 (1952) (same).

The lower court’s approach impermissibly inverted the burden. The government’s evidence showed that the alleged leaders of the conspiracy separated before the limitations period. That evidence, at minimum, raised a reasonable doubt as to whether any single, ongoing conspiracy existed during the relevant time. The jury should have been instructed that the government bore the burden of proving membership within the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt, and that evidence of the leaders’ split—even if insufficient to constitute a complete withdrawal defense—could be considered in determining whether the government had carried that burden. *United States v. Mennuti*, 239 F.2d 747, 750 (2d Cir. 1957) (“The burden is on the Government to prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy within the period of the statute of limitations.”).

**III. The Error Was Not Harmless**

The error here was not harmless. The jury could have credited the evidence that the conspiracy’s leaders split up before the limitations period and still convicted on the mistaken belief that petitioners bore the burden of proving they were not members during that period. Because the government’s case on the single-conspiracy theory was weak—resting on the continuity of a conspiracy that had already dissolved—the erroneous burden allocation likely affected the verdict. *See Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614–15 (1946) (reversing where jury instructions may have misled the jury on an essential element). The convictions must be reversed and the cause remanded for a new trial with proper instructions placing the burden of persuasion squarely on the government.
