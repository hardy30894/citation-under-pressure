**ARGUMENT**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only “any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The statutory text limits nondischargeability to the value actually obtained by the fraud; it does not reach additional sums imposed as punishment. See Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. 59, 64-66 (1995) (construing the phrase “obtained by” according to its ordinary meaning).

This Court has long recognized that the Bankruptcy Code embodies a “fresh start” for the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991); Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934); Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 236 U.S. 549, 554-55 (1915). Exceptions to discharge are therefore “narrowly construed.” Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877). Treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are not compensation for money obtained by fraud; they are a statutory penalty. Because they exceed the amount “obtained by” the fraud, they fall outside the exception. See Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915) (limiting “property” under the predecessor statute to what was actually received).

The same textual limit appears in the Court’s early fraud-discharge decisions. Strang v. Bradner, 114 U.S. 555, 559 (1885), and McIntyre v. Kavanaugh, 242 U.S. 138, 141 (1916), held nondischargeable only the sums the debtor had procured through the fraudulent conduct. Neither case suggested that additional punitive awards survive discharge.

The Third Circuit’s contrary reading would permit a state statute to enlarge the federal discharge exception, contrary to the principle that the scope of discharge is a federal question. See Brown v. Felsen, 442 U.S. 127, 135-36 (1979). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that only compensatory amounts “obtained by” fraud are excepted. In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196, 198-99 (9th Cir. 1991). The Eleventh Circuit’s broader view, In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672, 679-80 (11th Cir. 1993), cannot be reconciled with the statutory language or with the narrow-construction rule of Neal and Grogan.

Because the $62,765 in treble damages was not money obtained by fraud, it is dischargeable. The judgment below should be reversed to that extent.
