# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF § 522(b)(3)(C) AND (d)(12) REQUIRES THAT EXEMPT FUNDS BE RETIREMENT FUNDS FOR THE DEBTOR

This Court's interpretive task begins and ends with statutory text where that text is unambiguous. *Hartford Underwriters Ins. Co. v. Union Planters Bank, N.A.*, 530 U.S. 1, 6 (2000). Sections 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation" under specified Internal Revenue Code provisions. The statute's structure demands that the funds function as retirement savings *at the time of bankruptcy*—not merely that they once bore that character in a predecessor's hands.

An inherited IRA fails this functional test. Once Ruth Heffron died, the funds in her IRA "became no one's retirement funds." Unlike a traditional IRA, an inherited IRA prohibits new contributions, 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C), forbids rollover into the holder's own retirement account, and—critically—compels distribution beginning within one year of the original owner's death, with completion required in as little as five years. 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B). The beneficiary may withdraw the entire balance the day after probate closes, for any purpose, without penalty. These are not the "economic attributes of a retirement vehicle." The statute's use of "retirement funds"—undefined but ordinarily understood—cannot be squared with an account structurally designed for pre-retirement liquidation.

This Court has cautioned against reading exemption statutes to extend beyond their functional purpose merely because a term appears somewhere in a compound phrase. *See Dewsnup v. Timm*, 502 U.S. 410, 417 (1992) (bankruptcy provisions must be construed against their established statutory purpose). The presence of the word "IRA"—or even "retirement"—in the label "inherited IRA" does not transform an instrument of current consumption into one of retirement savings. As with any statutory term, courts must look to substance over nomenclature. *See Comm'r v. Court Holding Co.*, 324 U.S. 331, 334 (1945) (tax consequences turn on substance, not form of transaction).

## II. THE STRUCTURE OF § 522 CONFIRMS THAT EXEMPTIONS ATTACH TO THE DEBTOR'S OWN USE OF PROPERTY, NOT A PREDECESSOR'S

Congress's exemption scheme in § 522 is textually and structurally *temporal*—it protects property based on the debtor's relationship to that property at the time of filing, not on the property's provenance. Section 522(b)(3)(A) exempts a homestead only if the debtor has resided there for the requisite statutory period; a debtor cannot claim a deceased parent's home as exempt merely because the parent lived there. Section 522(b)(3)(B)'s tenancy-by-the-entirety exemption likewise depends on the debtor's interest "immediately before the commencement of the case."

This Court has repeatedly instructed that identical statutory language should be construed consistently across a single enactment. *Ratzlaf v. United States*, 510 U.S. 135, 143 (1994) ("A term appearing in several places in a statutory text is generally read the same way each time it appears."); *see also Sullivan v. Stroop*, 496 U.S. 478, 484 (1990). The retirement-funds exemption, though it omits the word "debtor's," must be read against this consistent structural backdrop: Congress designed § 522 to exempt property serving a protected *function* for the debtor, not property that merely passed through a relative's estate. A parallel principle governs here: just as a rental property inherited from a parent's residence would not qualify as the debtor's homestead, an IRA inherited and immediately subject to mandatory drawdown does not qualify as the debtor's retirement fund.

## III. THE PURPOSE OF THE RETIREMENT-FUNDS EXEMPTION—ENSURING FUNDS SET ASIDE FOR RETIREMENT REMAIN AVAILABLE FOR RETIREMENT—IS NOT SERVED BY EXEMPTING FREELY SPENDABLE INHERITED ASSETS

Bankruptcy exemptions exist to serve identifiable congressional purposes, and this Court has refused to extend exemptions beyond the bounds that purpose supports. *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) ("[N]o legislation pursues its purposes at all costs," and it "frustrates rather than effectuates legislative intent simplistically to assume that whatever furthers the statute's primary objective must be the law."). The retirement-funds exemption serves a specific policy: ensuring that funds "set aside for retirement need not be used to pay pre-retirement debts." That policy has no application to inherited IRAs, which by statutory design *cannot* be held until the beneficiary's retirement.

Permitting the exemption here would not merely extend Congress's policy—it would convert a tax-deferral mechanism into a wealth-transfer shelter, allowing "capital held in IRAs [to] pass down through the generations without ever being subject to income tax," contrary to the income tax structure Congress erected for inherited IRAs precisely to prevent that result. This Court's admonition against judicial extension of tax-preferred vehicles beyond their statutory design is well established. *See Gitlitz v. Commissioner*, 531 U.S. 206 (2001) (tax benefits limited to their statutory terms, notwithstanding taxpayer-favorable results). Nothing in the Bankruptcy Code's exemption provisions suggests Congress intended a broader shelter for inherited assets than the Internal Revenue Code itself provides for the underlying tax treatment.

## IV. EXEMPTIONS IN BANKRUPTCY ARE CONSTRUED ACCORDING TO ESTABLISHED FEDERAL STANDARDS, NOT EXPANDED TO RESOLVE EVERY CLOSE CASE IN THE DEBTOR'S FAVOR

Petitioners may invoke a presumption favoring debtors in close exemption disputes. But this Court has never adopted a categorical thumb-on-the-scale rule requiring maximal debtor-favorable construction irrespective of text and structure. *Cf. In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985) (declining to resolve whether any interpretive presumption governs exemption disputes absent genuine ambiguity). Where the statutory text, structure, and purpose converge—as they do here—no resort to a tie-breaking presumption is necessary or appropriate. This is not a close case: an inherited IRA, structurally incapable of serving as a retirement reserve, falls outside the exemption Congress crafted.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
