ARGUMENT

The district court abused its discretion by certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that antitrust damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement and quantification. Class actions require that common questions predominate, a principle derived from the equitable foundations of representative proceedings. Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32, 41 (1940) (class relief available only when named parties “fairly represent” absent members); Supreme Tribe of Ben Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 366 (1921) (representative decree binds only when interests align uniformly); Smith v. Swormstedt, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 302 (1853) (representative decree binds only when interests are “joint”).

In antitrust actions, damages must rest on evidence that is neither speculative nor individualized. Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (plaintiff must prove “the fact and amount of damages” with “reasonable certainty”); Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931) (damages “not to be determined by mere speculation or guess”); Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co., 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927) (proof must rest on “facts from which the jury can make a reasonable estimate”).

Where the proposed class encompasses thousands of subscribers across multiple counties, common proof of injury and damages is indispensable. Thomsen v. Cayser, 243 U.S. 66, 88 (1917) (antitrust plaintiffs must show injury “common to all”); Continental Wall Paper Co. v. Louis Voight & Sons Co., 212 U.S. 227, 238 (1909) (recovery requires evidence of “the extent of the injury” applicable to the claimant). Standard Oil Co. v. United States, 221 U.S. 1, 75 (1911), confirms that antitrust remedies turn on concrete proof of harm, not abstract theories. See also United States v. Trenton Potteries Co., 273 U.S. 392, 401 (1927) (antitrust violations demand evidence of actual competitive effects).

The record contains no such common, admissible evidence. Petitioners’ expert models rest on assumptions that vary by subscriber location, contract terms, and viewing preferences—precisely the individualized inquiries that preclude predominance. Because the district court accepted these models without requiring proof that damages can be measured uniformly for every class member, its certification order rests on an erroneous legal standard and must be reversed.
