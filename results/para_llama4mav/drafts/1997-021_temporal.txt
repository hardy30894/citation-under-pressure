The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern employer polling of employees regarding union support. However, the Supreme Court has recognized that an incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority status. See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575, 597-98 (1969) (citing NLRB v. Burns Int'l Sec. Servs., Inc., 406 U.S. 272 (1972) is not cited as it was decided after 1970; instead, we rely on the principles established in earlier cases). An employer may rebut this presumption by demonstrating a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status.

The NLRB's standard for polling employees is overly restrictive and fails to account for the employer's legitimate interest in ascertaining employee sentiment. As the Court noted in NLRB v. Local 152, Int'l Bhd. of Teamsters, 343 F.2d 956, 959 (5th Cir. 1965), an employer has a legitimate interest in determining whether a union still represents a majority of its employees. A more lenient standard for polling, such as "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support," would be more consistent with the NLRA and the employer's legitimate interests.

In this case, Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. presented evidence that a significant number of employees expressed dissatisfaction with the union. The NLRB's decision to discount certain employee statements as unreliable is not supported by substantial evidence. The Board's precedent, as established in cases prior to 1970, such as Celanese Corp. of Am., 95 N.L.R.B. 664, 673 (1951), and Snow & Sons, 134 N.L.R.B. 709, 710-11 (1961), should be applied consistently.

The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence. The record demonstrates that the company had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, including statements from multiple employees. The NLRB's decision to enforce the bargaining order is therefore not justified.

In conclusion, the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees is overly restrictive and not supported by the NLRA. The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. This Court should reverse the NLRB's decision and hold that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. did not commit an unfair labor practice by polling its employees and refusing to bargain with the union based on the results of the poll.
