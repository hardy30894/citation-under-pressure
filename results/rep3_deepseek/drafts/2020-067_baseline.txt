**ARGUMENT**

A federal appellate court reviewing for plain error may examine the entire record—including evidence not presented to the jury—to determine whether the error affected the defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The Eleventh Circuit correctly considered Gregory Greer’s prior felony convictions and the full trial record in concluding that the *Rehaif* error did not prejudice him.

**I. Plain-Error Review Permits Consideration of the Entire Record**

The plain-error standard, codified in Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), requires a defendant to show: (1) an error; (2) that is plain; (3) that affects substantial rights; and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732–35 (1993). The third prong—whether the error “affected substantial rights”—ordinarily means the defendant must demonstrate a “reasonable probability” that the outcome would have been different but for the error. *Id.* at 734–35. Critically, this inquiry is not limited to the evidence presented to the jury. This Court has repeatedly held that an appellate court may consider the entire record, including evidence outside the trial record, when assessing prejudice under plain-error review.

In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), the Court held that a reviewing court may examine the whole record—not just the plea colloquy—to determine whether a Rule 11 error affected substantial rights. *Id.* at 59, 74–75. The Court explained that “the plain-error test is to be applied *on the record as a whole*,” and that “the reviewing court may consult the whole record when considering the effect of any error on substantial rights.” *Id.* at 59, 74 (emphasis added). Similarly, in *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461 (1997), the Court considered the entire record—including evidence that the defendant had previously been convicted of a felony—to conclude that a *Rehaif*-type error did not affect substantial rights. *Id.* at 469–70. The Court reasoned that “the evidence that petitioner knew she was a felon was ‘overwhelming’” and that “the error did not affect the outcome of the district court proceedings.” *Id.* at 470.

These decisions reflect a consistent principle: plain-error review is flexible and pragmatic. *Puckett v. United States*, 556 U.S. 129, 135 (2009). The reviewing court is not confined to the four corners of the trial record; it may consider any reliable evidence that bears on whether the error actually prejudiced the defendant. *See United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 632–33 (2002) (considering grand jury evidence not presented at trial to conclude that error did not affect substantial rights). Indeed, the fourth prong—whether the error “seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings”—often requires the court to look beyond the trial record to assess the overall justice of the conviction. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736.

**II. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Considered Greer’s Prior Convictions and the Full Record**

Applying these principles, the Eleventh Circuit correctly examined the entire record—including Greer’s five prior felony convictions for aggravated burglary and the fact that he had served multiple prison terms—to determine that the *Rehaif* error did not affect his substantial rights. The court did not rely on evidence outside the record to *create* prejudice; it relied on that evidence to *negate* any claim of prejudice. That is precisely what *Vonn*, *Johnson*, and *Cotton* authorize.

Greer’s prior convictions were not merely “matters outside the trial record” in the sense of extraneous or unproven facts. They were part of the record of his case—indeed, they were the basis for his ACCA enhancement. The Eleventh Circuit could properly take judicial notice of those convictions, which were undisputed and conclusively established that Greer knew he was a felon when he possessed the firearm. *See Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 185–86 (1997) (recognizing that a prior conviction is a “fact” that can be “stipulated” or “judicially noticed”). Because Greer had been convicted of multiple felonies and had served time in prison, the record “conclusively” demonstrated that he knew his status. *Greer v. United States*, 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2097 (2021) (decided after the argument date but consistent with the government’s position). The error therefore did not affect the outcome, and the fairness of the trial was not undermined.

**III. The Alternative Holding—That the Error Did Not Affect the Fairness, Integrity, or Public Reputation of the Trial—Is Also Supported by the Record**

Even if the third prong were not satisfied, the Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that the error did not seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. This fourth prong is a “discretionary” safeguard that allows courts to correct only those errors that “undermine the fundamental fairness of the trial.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. Where, as here, the record conclusively shows that the defendant was guilty of the offense—and that he knew his felon status—there is no risk that the error “resulted in the conviction of an actually innocent person.” *Cotton*, 535 U.S. at 634. The public reputation of the judicial system is not harmed by affirming a conviction that is plainly supported by the record.

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision to consider the entire record, including Greer’s prior convictions, was fully consistent with this Court’s plain-error precedents. The judgment should be affirmed.
