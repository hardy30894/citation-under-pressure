# ARGUMENT

## I. MILFORD'S EXCLUSION OF THE CLUB IS VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION FORBIDDEN BY THE FIRST AMENDMENT

Milford opened its facilities as a limited public forum, permitting district residents to use the school for "social, civic and recreational meetings" pertaining to "the welfare of the community." Once a State opens a limited forum, it "must respect the lawful boundaries it has itself set," but "the State is not required to and does not allow persons to engage in every type of speech." *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). Milford permitted discussion of morals and character development—precisely the subject matter the Good News Club wished to address. The exclusion here therefore cannot be sustained as reasonable subject-matter line-drawing; it is discrimination against a particular viewpoint on a permitted subject, and such discrimination is "presumed impermissible when directed against speech otherwise within the forum's limits." *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 830.

This Court's precedents establish that a school may not exclude religious speech merely because it is religious in character. In *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), the Court held that a school district violated the First Amendment by denying a church access to school premises to show a film addressing family values from a religious perspective, where the district permitted other groups to address similar topics from secular perspectives. The Court explained that denial of access "was denied solely because the film dealt with the subject from a religious standpoint," and that such discrimination "is plainly invalid." *Id.* at 393-94. Likewise, in *Rosenberger*, the Court held that a university could not deny funding to a student publication solely because it offered a religious perspective on topics otherwise eligible for funding, holding that the university's justification—that the perspective was "too pervasively sectarian"—was "simply an inaccurate description" of the analytical distinction the university needed to draw. 515 U.S. at 831. The Court emphasized that religious speech is not a separate, excludable subject matter but often constitutes "a specific premise, a perspective, a standpoint from which a variety of subjects may be discussed and considered." *Id.*

The Second Circuit majority's characterization of the Club's activities as "quintessentially religious" rather than the teaching of morals from a religious viewpoint cannot be squared with this line of authority. As Judge Jacobs recognized in dissent below, "[n]o one should be surprised if a religious viewpoint on morality looks very like religion itself." 202 F.3d at 512. The fact that the Club teaches morality through prayer, scripture, and religious instruction does not transform the subject matter from morals and character into something else; it simply reflects the Club's viewpoint that morality is best understood through a religious lens. As this Court observed in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), there is "no indication when 'singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles' cease to be 'singing, teaching, and reading'... and become unprotected 'worship.'" *Id.* at 269 n.6. Milford's own distinction—between "teaching of morals and values" and "religious instruction"—is the very distinction *Widmar* refused to countenance as a basis for exclusion.

That other groups such as the Boy Scouts and the 4-H Club were permitted to teach character development while the Good News Club was not permitted to do so from a religious standpoint is textbook viewpoint discrimination. This Court has repeatedly held that a State violates the Free Speech Clause when it excludes religious perspectives on subjects that are otherwise open to secular discussion. *See Lamb's Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394. Milford's Community Use Policy purports to prohibit only "religious purposes," but as applied, it prohibited a religious viewpoint on the permitted purpose of moral and character development.

## II. PERMITTING THE CLUB TO MEET DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE

Milford's asserted Establishment Clause concern cannot justify the exclusion. This Court has already rejected nearly identical Establishment Clause defenses in the limited-public-forum context. In *Lamb's Chapel*, the Court held that permitting the church's film to be shown on school property "would not have been incompatible with the Establishment Clause" because the film would be shown "after school hours, [was] not sponsored by the school, and [was] open to the public, not just to church members." 508 U.S. at 395. The same is true here: the Club met after hours, was not sponsored by Milford, and was open to any student who wished to attend with parental permission.

This Court's decision in *Widmar* similarly rejected the argument that mere access to a public forum by religious groups constitutes establishment. The Court held that an "equal access policy" does not confer any imprimatur of state approval, since any benefit is "no more than incidental" to the neutral policy of permitting access to a broad class of speakers. 454 U.S. at 273. So too here: allowing the Club to use the school after hours, on the same terms as the Boy Scouts and 4-H, does not signal school endorsement of religion.

Nor can Milford rely on *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577 (1992), or similar coercion-based precedents, because those cases involved school-sponsored activity during the school day directed at a captive audience of students. Here, by contrast, the Club's meetings occur after the school day ends, are not sponsored by Milford, and require affirmative parental permission for attendance—eliminating any realistic concern about perceived endorsement or coercion. Any risk of misperception can be, and was, addressed through neutral disclaimers, as this Court suggested in *Mergens*, where the Court held that a school could dispel any inference of endorsement by "disclaiming] official support" for student religious clubs meeting on equal terms with other groups. *Board of Educ. of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 251 (1990) (plurality opinion).

Because the Establishment Clause does not require—and, indeed, forbids—the exclusion of religious viewpoints from an open forum, Milford's denial of access to the Good News Club violated the Free Speech Clause of the First Amendment and cannot be justified by Establishment Clause concerns.
