**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard is a permissible, but not unbounded, accommodation of the Act's competing policies, and it must be applied so as to leave employers a genuine means of testing disputed claims of majority support.**

This Court has long recognized that a union's certified majority status generates only a *rebuttable* presumption of continued employee support. In *Brooks v. NLRB*, 348 U.S. 96 (1954), the Court held that while a certification controls the bargaining relationship for a reasonable period, that protection is not absolute and must yield when objective circumstances demonstrate that the union no longer speaks for the employees it purports to represent. The Act's central purpose — protecting employees' choice of bargaining representative — is disserved, not served, when an employer is compelled to bargain with an organization that a substantial number of its own workers have repudiated.

This Court reaffirmed the rebuttable character of the majority presumption in *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575 (1969), explaining that an employer confronted with a bargaining demand retains the right to test the union's claimed support through objective evidence rather than simply capitulating or simply refusing outright. The Board's own polling procedure, codified in *Struksnes Construction Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967), exists precisely because polling — conducted with secret ballots, advance notice, and assurances against reprisal — is a legitimate, non-coercive method for an employer to resolve doubt about a union's continuing support without either party guessing at the employees' actual sentiments. A standard for polling that is administered so rigidly that it becomes indistinguishable from the standard for outright withdrawal of recognition defeats the very purpose the Board's own procedural safeguards were designed to serve: it forces the employer to gamble on withdrawal without ever availing itself of the more cautious, employee-protective device of a secret poll.

Petitioner does not ask this Court to discard the Board's "good-faith reasonable doubt" formulation. Rather, petitioner submits that the standard is rational only if the Board applies it evenhandedly to the evidence employers actually possess — statements volunteered by employees in the ordinary course of business — rather than through a categorical presumption that such statements are unreliable. Nothing in *Brooks* or *Gissel* countenances a rule that empties the polling mechanism of practical effect.

**II. The Board's determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole.**

Even accepting the Board's standard, its finding here cannot stand. This Court has instructed that "substantial evidence is more than a mere scintilla," and means "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." *Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Substantiality "must take into account whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight," and a reviewing court does not fulfill its duty by canvassing only the evidence that supports the Board's result. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Substantial-evidence review requires examination of the record as a whole, including evidence a fact-finder chose to discount. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939).

Measured against this standard, the Board's finding fails. The Board discounted, categorically, every employee statement that arguably supported Allentown Mack's doubt: statements made during job interviews, statements expressing dissatisfaction with dues or representation, and one employee's report of the sentiments of his fellow workers on the night shift. In each instance, the Board did not weigh the evidence — it excluded it by rule, on the theory that such statements are inherently unreliable indicators of genuine anti-union sentiment. But a categorical rule of exclusion is the antithesis of the individualized, whole-record inquiry demanded by *Universal Camera* and *Columbian Enameling*. An employee's statement that he sees "no need for a union" is evidence of his views regardless of the setting in which it was uttered; the Board may weigh it lightly, but it may not disregard it as though it were not evidence at all. When the wrongly excluded statements are restored to the balance, the record shows that a clear majority of the employees whose views were known to Allentown Mack — and later confirmed by the poll itself, which favored decertification nineteen to thirteen — had expressed opposition to continued union representation.

The Board's remedial authority to order bargaining, recognized in *Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 702 (1944), presupposes a valid predicate finding that the employer lacked good-faith doubt. Where, as here, that predicate finding rests on the systematic exclusion of relevant, uncontradicted evidence, the bargaining order cannot be sustained. The Board's application of its own standard was neither rational as applied nor supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
