The respondent, United States, respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on grounds that the statute exceeds the federal government's enumerated powers and infringes upon the Tenth Amendment.

As the Third Circuit Court of Appeals correctly held, Bond's challenge to § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds is barred due to her lack of standing. Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118, 143-144 (1939), establishes that private parties, like Bond, do not have standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims. The Supreme Court's holding in Tennessee Electric Power Co. is not dicta, as Bond suggests, but rather an alternative ground for the Court's decision, which is binding on lower courts. Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005).

The majority of circuit courts have followed this reasoning, denying private parties standing to bring Tenth Amendment challenges. See United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-527 (8th Cir.2009); Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir.2009); Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir.2006); Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 33-36; United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir.2004). This Court should follow the same reasoning and hold that Bond lacks standing to challenge § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds.

Furthermore, even if Bond had standing, her challenge to § 229 would fail on the merits. The statute was enacted pursuant to the Necessary and Proper Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18, to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention, a valid treaty under the Treaty Power, U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2. Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920), establishes that if a treaty is valid, there can be no dispute about the validity of a statute enacted to implement it. The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.

In conclusion, Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. Even if she had standing, her challenge would fail on the merits, as § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress's authority under the Necessary and Proper Clause to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention. The judgment of the Third Circuit Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
