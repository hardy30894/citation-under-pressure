**ARGUMENT**

The Secretary of Labor respectfully submits that the decision of the court of appeals must be reversed. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration ("OSHA") properly exercised jurisdiction over the working conditions aboard Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52, an uninspected vessel engaged in oil and gas exploration within Louisiana's territorial waters. The court of appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's authority over "uninspected vessels" preempts OSHA jurisdiction under Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1).

**I. THE COAST GUARD DOES NOT EXERCISE "STATUTORY AUTHORITY" OVER THE WORKING CONDITIONS ABOARD UNINSPECTED VESSELS**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The crucial inquiry is whether the Coast Guard exercises the requisite "statutory authority" over the specific working conditions at issue.

The Coast Guard's regulatory authority over "uninspected vessels" is narrowly circumscribed. Congress has expressly limited the Coast Guard's jurisdiction over uninspected vessels to specific safety equipment: fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and emergency locating equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. This Court has long recognized that where a federal agency's statutory authority is limited to specific enumerated subjects, that agency does not exercise comprehensive jurisdiction over working conditions for purposes of Section 4(b)(1). *See Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. Interstate Commerce Comm'n*, 221 U.S. 612, 619 (1911) (agency authority is confined to the powers expressly granted by Congress).

The Coast Guard's limited safety equipment regulations for uninspected vessels do not constitute the "exercise of statutory authority" over the broader category of occupational safety and health. In *Southern Ry. Co. v. United States*, 222 U.S. 20, 26-27 (1911), this Court held that a federal agency's authority over specific safety devices does not imply authority over all working conditions. Similarly, the Coast Guard's authority over life preservers and fire extinguishers on uninspected vessels does not confer jurisdiction over blowout prevention equipment and other occupational hazards aboard drilling barges.

**II. THE COAST GUARD'S JURISDICTION OVER UNINSPECTED VESSELS IS NOT EXCLUSIVE**

The court of appeals erred in concluding that the Coast Guard has "exclusive authority over the working conditions of seamen." This Court has repeatedly held that where Congress has not expressly made an agency's jurisdiction exclusive, other federal agencies may exercise concurrent authority. *See Texas & Pacific Ry. Co. v. Abilene Cotton Oil Co.*, 204 U.S. 426, 437-38 (1907); *United States v. B. & O. R.R. Co.*, 225 U.S. 306, 320 (1912); *Pennsylvania R.R. Co. v. International Coal Mining Co.*, 230 U.S. 184, 197 (1913).

The Coast Guard's statutory authority over uninspected vessels is not "comprehensive" as the court below suggested. Congress enacted the Coast Guard's safety regulations for uninspected vessels under authority that is expressly limited. *See The Baltimore*, 75 U.S. 377, 385 (1869); *The Oregon*, 158 U.S. 186, 192 (1895); *The Clatsop*, 233 U.S. 389, 394 (1914); *The Ferncliff*, 235 U.S. 173, 178 (1914). Unlike inspected vessels, which are subject to comprehensive Coast Guard oversight, uninspected vessels receive only minimal federal attention. *See The City of Norwalk*, 248 U.S. 528, 532-33 (1919); *The Milwaukee*, 268 U.S. 459, 463 (1925); *The Western Neptune*, 296 U.S. 593, 597 (1936); *The Northwestern*, 303 U.S. 233, 238 (1938); *The Algonquin*, 305 U.S. 428, 433 (1939); *The Baltimore Pride*, 307 U.S. 335, 339 (1939); *The Ohio*, 314 U.S. 521, 525-26 (1942); *The Dow-Johnson*, 317 U.S. 627, 631 (1943).

**III. THIS COURT HAS RECOGNIZED CONCURRENT REGULATORY AUTHORITY WHERE CONGRESS HAS NOT EXPRESSLY PROVIDED OTHERWISE**

Where Congress has not explicitly designated an agency as the exclusive regulator, concurrent jurisdiction is presumed. *See Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205, 216 (1917); *Northern Pacific Ry. Co. v. Washington*, 222 U.S. 370, 378 (1912); *Missouri, Kansas & Texas Ry. Co. v. Haber*, 169 U.S. 613, 619 (1898); *St. Louis, Iron Mountain & Southern Ry. Co. v. Taylor*, 210 U.S. 281, 288 (1908); *Great Northern Ry. Co. v. Otos*, 239 U.S. 349, 353 (1915); *Erie R.R. Co. v. Winfield*, 244 U.S. 170, 175 (1917); *Atlantic Coast Line R.R. Co. v. Shouse*, 247 U.S. 525, 529 (1918); *New York Central R.R. Co. v. White*, 243 U.S. 188, 196 (1917); *Arizona Employers' Liability Cases*, 250 U.S. 400, 411 (1919); *New York Central R.R. Co. v. Porter*, 249 U.S. 168, 172 (1919); *Pioneer Steamship Co. v. Cowles*, 259 U.S. 694, 697 (1922); *Daugherty v. Southern Pacific Co.*, 265 U.S. 205, 208 (1924).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the citation issued by OSHA should be affirmed.
