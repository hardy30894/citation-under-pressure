The respondent respectfully submits that the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity plea.

The Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant. This decision reflects the principle that the insanity defense is a separate and distinct issue from the question of whether the defendant formed the requisite criminal intent.

The respondent argues that the trial court's limitation on the use of Clark's expert evidence did not deprive him of a fundamental right. The Court has recognized that states have significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure in their courts. As noted in Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278 (1936), the due process clause requires that the state not use methods that are "so brutal and so offensive to the conscience of mankind" as to violate the clause. The Arizona rule limiting the use of expert evidence to the insanity claim is a reasonable exercise of this latitude, and it does not involve such methods.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that states have the authority to regulate the presentation of evidence in their courts. In Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893), the Court held that the government has the power to prescribe the rules of evidence. Similarly, in Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the Court upheld a state law that limited the defendant's right to be present at certain stages of the trial. See also Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), where the Court noted that the states have significant discretion in the administration of their justice systems.

The respondent also notes that the Court has upheld state laws that restrict the use of certain types of evidence. In Shepard v. United States, 290 U.S. 96 (1933), the Court held that a state law excluding certain evidence did not violate the due process clause.

The respondent concludes that the Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent and does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court.
