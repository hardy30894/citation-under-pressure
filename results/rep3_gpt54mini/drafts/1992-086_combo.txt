### Argument

The School District violated the First Amendment when it denied Lamb’s Chapel access to school premises because its proposed films were religious. A public school that opens its facilities for after-hours use may not exclude a speaker because the speech is religious or religiously motivated when the exclusion turns on the viewpoint expressed. The First Amendment forbids that discrimination even when the government retains authority to impose reasonable, content-neutral limits on the time, place, and manner of use. See *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940); *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 109-10 (1943); *Prince v. Massachusetts*, 321 U.S. 158, 164-65 (1944).

First, the School District’s facilities were not a purely private space immune from First Amendment scrutiny. Once the District chose to permit community use of its buildings after hours, it could not reserve the premises for favored secular subjects while excluding religious speech on the basis of its religious character. This Court has long recognized that government may not condition access to public property on the suppression of disfavored ideas. *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501, 506-09 (1946), held that the First Amendment constrains a property owner who performs a public function and opens property for public use. Likewise, *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 560-61 (1948), invalidated discretionary suppression of speech in a public setting, and *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290, 293-95 (1951), rejected licensing authority that enabled prior restraint based on officials’ views. Those decisions confirm that once government creates a forum for expression, it may not close the door to one speaker because of disagreement with the content or perspective of the speech.

Second, the District’s exclusion here was viewpoint-based. Lamb’s Chapel sought to show films about family values and child rearing from a Christian perspective. The District did not deny access because the films involved a controversial subject, nor because of any neutral scheduling concern; it denied access because the presentation was “church related.” That is discrimination based on viewpoint. The Court has repeatedly held that the government may regulate speech only through neutral rules and may not suppress expression because officials disapprove of its ideas. See *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 160-62 (1939); *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 530-32 (1945); *Bates v. City of Little Rock*, 361 U.S. 516, 522-24 (1960). A rule that allows discussion of family life but forbids the same discussion when informed by a religious viewpoint is no neutral regulation at all.

Third, the District’s policy also burdens the free exercise of religion. The Court has long held that religious speech and religious exercise cannot be singled out for disability. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-07 (1940), the Court explained that the free exercise of religion includes the right to communicate religious belief. In *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 109-10 (1943), the Court held that religious evangelism enjoys constitutional protection and cannot be treated as a taxable or regulatory nuisance merely because it is religious. In *Follett v. Town of McCormick*, 321 U.S. 573, 577-80 (1944), the Court again rejected a burden imposed uniquely on religious activity. And in *Sherbert v. Verner*, 374 U.S. 398, 403-06 (1963), the Court held that government may not impose substantial burdens on religious exercise without a compelling justification. No such justification appears here.

Fourth, the District cannot defend its exclusion as necessary to avoid an Establishment Clause problem. The Court has never held that equal access to a public facility for religious speech amounts to establishment of religion. To the contrary, neutrality toward religion is the constitutional norm. *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 17-18 (1947), required government to be evenhanded rather than hostile. *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 209-12 (1948), invalidated sectarian use of public-school machinery, but that case involved direct use of the schools to advance religious instruction as part of the school program. *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 430-31 (1962), and *School District of Abington Township v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 222-23 (1963), likewise concerned state sponsorship of devotional exercises, not neutral access after hours for private speech. Permitting Lamb’s Chapel to show films for the community would not amount to government sponsorship; it would simply treat religious speech on equal terms with comparable secular speech.

Finally, the School District’s position is inconsistent with the Court’s repeated insistence that government may not favor secular over religious expression. *Torcaso v. Watkins*, 367 U.S. 488, 495-96 (1961), held that the State may not impose a religious test or penalize belief. *Sherbert*, 374 U.S. at 404-06, rejected indirect burdens on religious conduct. The same principle governs here. When the District opened its premises for after-hours expressive activity, it could not exclude Lamb’s Chapel because its films presented a Christian perspective on family life. The First Amendment required equal access.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
