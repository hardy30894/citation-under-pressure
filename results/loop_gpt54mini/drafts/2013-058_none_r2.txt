**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt a nonspousal inherited IRA from the debtor’s estate. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and § 522(d)(12) protect only “retirement funds” held in certain tax-favored accounts. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). That text requires more than a tax benefit; it requires funds reserved for retirement. Statutory words must be read in their ordinary meaning and in context. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress chose the phrase “retirement funds,” not the broader notion of any account once connected to retirement savings.

Heidi Clark’s inherited IRA does not fit that description. As the Seventh Circuit explained, a nonspouse beneficiary cannot add contributions, cannot roll the account into another IRA, and must begin required distributions immediately. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 560–61 (7th Cir. 2013). The account therefore cannot function as a fund for Heidi’s own retirement; it is a vehicle for mandatory present consumption. A statutory exemption should not be expanded beyond the thing Congress described. *Crooks v. Harrelson*, 282 U.S. 55, 61 (1930); *Sutherland v. United States*, 264 U.S. 87, 88–89 (1924).

The structure of the Code reinforces that conclusion. When Congress wants to tie an exemption to a debtor’s own present use or status, it says so expressly. See 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A). Here, however, Congress exempted only “retirement funds,” not “funds that were once retirement funds” or “funds in tax-deferred retirement accounts.” Courts are not at liberty to rewrite that choice. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. at 485; *Badaracco v. Commissioner*, 464 U.S. 386, 398 (1984). If Congress had intended inherited IRAs to be exempt, it could have said so directly.

Petitioners’ contrary reading would collapse the distinction between retirement savings and inherited wealth. The Court has repeatedly rejected constructions that enlarge a statute’s coverage on the ground that they might better serve a general policy. *Helvering v. Stockholms Enskilda Bank*, 293 U.S. 84, 93 (1934); *United States v. Pelzer*, 312 U.S. 399, 402–03 (1941). The Bankruptcy Code protects funds saved for retirement; it does not shield assets that are available for current use merely because they originated in an IRA. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 562–64. As the court below observed, inherited IRAs are “an opportunity for current consumption, not a fund of retirement savings.” *Id.* at 564.

The tax rules governing inherited IRAs confirm that distinction. Congress specifically limits such accounts by prohibiting new contributions and requiring rapid distributions. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 560–61. That legal regime is incompatible with treating the account as a fund held for the beneficiary’s retirement. Courts evaluate the actual legal operation of a property interest, not a label divorced from substance. *Old Colony R. Co. v. Commissioner*, 284 U.S. 552, 560 (1932); *Commissioner v. Tower*, 327 U.S. 280, 287–88 (1946). Because this inherited IRA cannot remain intact until retirement, it is not “retirement funds” within § 522.

The contrary approach should be rejected because it turns the exemption into a historical inquiry about whether money was ever once retirement money in someone else’s hands. That is not what the statute says. *Crooks v. Harrelson*, 282 U.S. at 61; *Sutherland v. United States*, 264 U.S. at 88–89. The better rule—and the one the text compels—is that an inherited IRA, though tax deferred, is not a retirement fund in the debtor’s hands. The judgment should be affirmed.
