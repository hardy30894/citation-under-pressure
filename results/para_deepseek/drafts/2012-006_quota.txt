**ARGUMENT**

The government must prove every element of a conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the defendant was a member during the statute-of-limitations period. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to that period, if properly established, severs the defendant’s liability for subsequent acts. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912). The question presented is whether, once a defendant produces evidence of withdrawal, the burden shifts to the government to prove continued membership beyond a reasonable doubt. This Court should answer yes.

**I. The Government Bears the Ultimate Burden of Proving Every Element, Including Membership During the Limitations Period**

A conspiracy conviction requires proof that the defendant knowingly joined the agreement and that the conspiracy existed within the applicable limitations period. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 463 n.21 (1978). The statute of limitations is an affirmative defense, but the government must prove the conspiracy’s existence within that period. *Smith v. United States*, 568 U.S. 106, 111 (2013). Once a defendant presents evidence of withdrawal, the government’s burden includes disproving that withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt. *United States v. Lacey*, 661 F.3d 1012, 1017 (9th Cir. 2011). This comports with the principle that the prosecution must prove every fact necessary to constitute the crime. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364.

**II. Withdrawal Is an Affirmative Defense That, When Raised, Requires the Government to Disprove It Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

Withdrawal is not a mere defense to be proven by the defendant; it negates an element of the offense—the defendant’s intentional participation during the limitations period. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1232 (7th Cir. 1981). The government must therefore prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant did not withdraw. *United States v. Brown*, 578 F.2d 1280, 1284 (9th Cir. 1978). This rule aligns with *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 703–04 (1975), which held that the government must disprove a defense that negates a required mental state. Here, continued membership is a mental-state element; withdrawal negates it.

The lower court’s contrary approach—requiring defendants to prove withdrawal—impermissibly shifts the burden. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 215 (1977), allows states to place the burden of affirmative defenses on defendants, but only if the defense does not negate an element of the crime. Because withdrawal negates the element of membership during the limitations period, the government must bear the burden. *Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510, 524 (1979) (unconstitutional to shift burden of proof to defendant on an element).

**III. The Lower Court’s Decision Erroneously Placed the Burden on Petitioners**

The court below held that petitioners failed to prove withdrawal, effectively requiring them to negate the government’s case. That is error. *United States v. Lacey*, 661 F.3d at 1017, correctly holds that once a defendant “presents evidence of withdrawal, the burden shifts to the government to prove that the defendant did not withdraw.” Petitioners presented evidence that the conspiracy’s leaders split before the limitations period. That evidence raised a reasonable doubt as to petitioners’ continued membership. The government then bore the burden to prove otherwise beyond a reasonable doubt. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d at 1232. The court below failed to apply this standard.

**IV. The Government’s Burden Is Consistent with the Beyond-a-Reasonable-Doubt Standard**

The beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard is the bedrock of criminal procedure. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. Placing the burden on the government to disprove withdrawal ensures that no defendant is convicted for acts committed after he has effectively abandoned the conspiracy. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. at 464 (withdrawal requires “affirmative action” but does not shift burden). The mere fact that the leaders split does not automatically prove withdrawal, but it triggers the government’s duty to prove continued membership. *Hyde*, 225 U.S. at 369 (withdrawal requires “some act” to disavow conspiracy). The government failed to meet that duty here.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should hold that once a defendant presents evidence of withdrawal from a conspiracy before the statute-of-limitations period, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member during that period. The judgment below should be reversed.
