# ARGUMENT

## I. THE ACT IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME, PLACE, AND MANNER REGULATION THAT SURVIVES INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY

The Massachusetts Reproductive Health Care Facilities Act regulates location, not content. This Court has long held that a regulation is content-neutral if it "serves purposes unrelated to the content of expression," even where it may incidentally burden some speakers more than others. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). The Act's text does not single out any topic, viewpoint, or speaker category for restriction; it applies equally to counselors, protesters, and supporters alike within the buffer zone. The First Circuit correctly recognized the Act as "a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time-place-manner regulation." *McCullen v. Coakley*, 708 F.3d 1, 4 (1st Cir. 2013).

This case is squarely controlled by *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), in which this Court upheld a Colorado statute creating an eight-foot no-approach zone within a 100-foot radius of healthcare facility entrances. The Court there held that such restrictions are content-neutral because they "regulate[] the *places* where some speech may occur," not the speech itself. *Id.* at 719. The Massachusetts Act is, if anything, less restrictive than the Colorado statute—it creates a fixed buffer zone without any additional floating component, and petitioners remain free to speak, sing, pray, and distribute literature from just outside the thirty-five-foot line.

Under the intermediate scrutiny framework this Court established in *Ward*, a time-place-manner regulation is valid if it (1) is content-neutral, (2) is narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and (3) leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. 491 U.S. at 791. The Act satisfies each prong.

**A. Significant Governmental Interest.** Massachusetts has a compelling interest in "ensuring public safety and order, promoting the free flow of traffic on streets and sidewalks, protecting property rights, and protecting a woman's freedom to seek pregnancy-related services." *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715. This Court has repeatedly recognized that the government's interest in protecting access to healthcare facilities, particularly in light of the documented history of clinic violence and obstruction, is substantial. *See also Madsen v. Women's Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 767–68 (1994) (recognizing "significant governmental interest in ensuring the public safety and order" outside reproductive health facilities).

**B. Narrow Tailoring.** Narrow tailoring under intermediate scrutiny does not require that the regulation be the least restrictive means available; it requires only that the regulation "not burden substantially more speech than is necessary to further the government's legitimate interests." *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799. The buffer zone here is modest—thirty-five feet—and confined to the immediate area surrounding clinic entrances, exits, and driveways where congestion and confrontation are most likely to occur. As the First Circuit found on this extensive factual record, petitioners retain "oral speech of varying degrees of volume and amplification, distribution of literature, displays of signage and symbols, wearing of evocative garments and costumes, and prayer alone and in groups." *McCullen*, 708 F.3d at 14. Indeed, petitioner McCullen herself testified to having persuaded approximately eighty women to forgo abortions during the period the buffer zones were in effect—evidence that the Act does not foreclose effective communication.

**C. Ample Alternative Channels.** This Court has never required that speakers be afforded their *preferred* method of communication, only an adequate one. *See Heffron v. International Society for Krishna Consciousness, Inc.*, 452 U.S. 640, 647 (1981) (upholding fairground rule confining literature distribution to fixed locations because alternative channels remained available). The record here—developed through a full bench trial, unlike the truncated record in many facial challenges—establishes that sidewalk counselors continue to communicate with, and in some cases persuade, women approaching the clinics.

## II. HILL v. COLORADO WAS CORRECTLY DECIDED AND SHOULD NOT BE OVERRULED

Petitioners' invitation to overrule *Hill* runs headlong into this Court's settled respect for *stare decisis*, "the preferred course because it promotes the evenhanded, predictable, and consistent development of legal principles." *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827 (1991). Departure from precedent demands a "special justification," *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443 (2000), and petitioners offer none beyond disagreement with the result.

Petitioners' reliance on *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), and *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), is misplaced. Those cases addressed content-based and speaker-based restrictions in wholly different contexts—campaign finance, funeral picketing, and pharmaceutical marketing data. None purported to disturb the settled doctrine governing place restrictions around sensitive facilities. As the First Circuit correctly observed, these decisions recite only "conventional First Amendment principles" applied to "factual scenarios far different than the scenario at issue here." *McCullen*, 708 F.3d at 10. Nothing in this Court's subsequent jurisprudence undermines *Hill*'s core holding that a fixed buffer zone around healthcare facility entrances is a valid, content-neutral regulation of place.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
