The issue before this Court is whether the rule announced in Johnson v. United States regarding what constitutes a violent offense applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. Although Johnson was decided after Welch's conviction became final, the principle that the Supreme Court's decisions construing federal statutes apply retroactively supports the retroactive application of Johnson. See United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103, 110 (1801) (establishing that a court must apply the law as it exists at the time of its decision).

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) imposes a minimum sentence of 15 years for defendants with three prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). The ACCA defines a "violent felony" as a crime that has as an element the "use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another," or that is listed in a specific category or "otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another." The Supreme Court has held that a statute is void for vagueness if it fails to provide adequate notice of the conduct it prohibits, as seen in Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, where the Court stated that a penal statute must be "sufficiently explicit to inform those who are subject to it what conduct on their part will render them liable to its penalties."

In Johnson v. United States, the Court held that the residual clause of the ACCA was unconstitutionally vague because it failed to provide clear guidance on what constitutes a violent felony. The Court's decision in Johnson was based on the principle that a statute must provide fair notice of the conduct it prohibits, as established in Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). The Court has also held that a statute is unconstitutional if it is so vague that it allows for arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement, as discussed in Cline v. Frink Dairy Co., 274 U.S. 445, where the Court noted the importance of clear statutory language to avoid arbitrary enforcement.

The decision in Johnson applies retroactively to Welch's case because it is a substantive rule that narrows the scope of the ACCA. See Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1974) (holding that a new rule that narrows the scope of a criminal statute applies retroactively). The rule announced in Johnson is substantive because it changes the definition of a violent felony under the ACCA, and therefore, it applies retroactively to Welch's case.

Furthermore, the Court has held that a new rule applies retroactively if it is a substantive rule. Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643 (1961), established a new rule that was applied retroactively. The rule announced in Johnson is a substantive rule that alters the range of conduct punished by the ACCA, similar to the rule in Mapp.

In light of the above authorities, the rule announced in Johnson v. United States applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. The residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague, and Welch's prior conviction for strong arm robbery does not qualify as a predicate offense under the ACCA. Therefore, Welch's sentence should be vacated, and his case should be remanded for resentencing.

The relevant case authorities that support this argument are: United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103 (1801); Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451 (1939); Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385 (1926); Cline v. Frink Dairy Co., 274 U.S. 445 (1927); Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333 (1974); Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643 (1961); Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945); and United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81 (1921).
