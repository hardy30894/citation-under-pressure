I. The Reinstatement Statute Applies to Deportation Orders Issued Before its Enactment

The respondent argues that the reinstatement statute, Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), applies to prior deportation orders regardless of when they were issued. The Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals agreed, holding that Congress intended the statute to apply retroactively to prior deportation orders. Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales, 448 F.3d 1236, 1242 (10th Cir. 2006). However, this interpretation is not supported by the statutory text or relevant case law.

The Supreme Court has held that statutes should not be given retroactive effect unless Congress clearly indicates its intent to do so. Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994). In this case, the statutory text does not clearly indicate that Congress intended the reinstatement statute to apply to prior deportation orders. Therefore, the statute should not be applied retroactively.

Furthermore, the Court has also held that even if a statute is not explicitly retroactive, it may still be impermissibly retroactive if it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." Id. at 269-70. The reinstatement statute does just that by reviving prior deportation orders and denying relief to aliens who re-entered the country before the statute's enactment.

II. The Retroactive Application of the Reinstatement Statute is Impermissible

Even if the reinstatement statute is intended to apply to prior deportation orders, its retroactive application is impermissible. The statute denies relief to aliens who re-entered the country before its enactment, which is a new legal consequence attached to events completed before the statute's enactment.

The Court has identified several factors to consider when determining whether a statute has an impermissible retroactive effect, including "the nature and extent of the change in the law, the degree of connection between the operation of the new law and a relevant past event, and the extent to which the new law upsets settled expectations." St. Cyr v. INS, 229 F.3d 406, 418 (2d Cir. 2000), aff'd, 533 U.S. 289 (2001). In this case, the reinstatement statute significantly changes the law by denying relief to aliens who re-entered the country before its enactment. This change is connected to a relevant past event - Fernandez-Vargas' re-entry into the country in 1982. Moreover, the statute upsets Fernandez-Vargas' settled expectation of being able to adjust his status to permanent resident.

The respondent argues that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status." However, this argument is not supported by the record. Fernandez-Vargas had been living in the United States for over 15 years, had married a U.S. citizen, and had applied to adjust his status to permanent resident. He had a legitimate expectation that he would be able to adjust his status, which was upset by the retroactive application of the reinstatement statute.

In INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), the Court held that a statute that eliminated discretionary relief for certain aliens was not retroactive because it did not attach new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment. Id. at 321. However, the Court also noted that the statute would be retroactive if it were applied to aliens who had pleaded guilty to certain crimes before the statute's enactment, because they had relied on the availability of discretionary relief when making their plea. Id. at 323. Similarly, in this case, Fernandez-Vargas relied on the availability of relief under the INA when he re-entered the country and married a U.S. citizen.

The retroactive application of the reinstatement statute is impermissible because it attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment and upsets Fernandez-Vargas' settled expectations. Therefore, the statute should not be applied to Fernandez-Vargas' prior deportation order.

In conclusion, the reinstatement statute should not be applied to prior deportation orders issued before its enactment, and even if it is, its retroactive application is impermissible. The Court should reverse the Tenth Circuit's decision and hold that the reinstatement statute does not apply to Fernandez-Vargas' prior deportation order.
