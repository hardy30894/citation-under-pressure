**ARGUMENT**

The Federal Circuit erred in affirming an award of Samsung’s entire profits on the infringing smartphones. Section 289 permits recovery of “the total profit made by [the infringer] from the manufacture or sale … of the article of manufacture to which [the] patented design … has been applied.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. When the design is applied only to a component, the “article of manufacture” is that component, not the finished product. See Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros., 222 F. 902, 903 (2d Cir. 1915) (“The article to which the patented design was applied was the case, not the complete piano.”).

This Court has long recognized that a design patent covers only the “ornamental design” for an “article of manufacture.” 35 U.S.C. § 171; see Gorham Mfg. Co. v. White, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 511, 525 (1871). The profits remedy is correspondingly limited to the article embodying that design. See Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 445 (1885) (“The plaintiff … is entitled to recover … the profit which the defendant made by the use of the design.”). Allowing recovery of profits on unpatented components would convert the design patent into a patent on the entire product, contrary to the statutory text and the constitutional requirement that patents be limited to the invention disclosed. See Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 490 (1853).

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule—that the entire product is always the relevant article—finds no support in the statute or precedent. It rests instead on an atextual assumption that the finished article is invariably the unit of sale. That assumption collapses when, as here, the patented design is applied only to a component that is itself an article of manufacture. See Bush & Lane, 222 F. at 903. The jury therefore should have been instructed to determine the profits attributable to the infringing components alone.

Reversal is required.
