Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to DHL Express Inc. (USA) and Deutsche Post AG, as the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101-2109, prohibits employers from unilaterally deciding to close facilities and terminate employees without providing sixty days' written notice. The WARN Act requires employers to provide written notice to affected employees at least sixty days prior to a "mass layoff" or "plant closing," as defined in 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(2)-(3). See United Food & Commercial Workers Union Local 751 v. Brown Group, Inc., 517 U.S. 544, 547 (1996). DHL's announcement on November 10, 2008, that it would stop offering U.S. domestic shipping on January 30, 2009, effectively triggered the WARN Act's notice requirement.

DHL's subsequent negotiation of severance agreements with the International Brotherhood of Teamsters Local 705, which included a "General Waiver and Release" that purported to waive workers' WARN Act claims, does not exempt DHL from liability under the WARN Act. The WARN Act does not permit employers to condition severance benefits on employees' waiver of their WARN Act claims. See United Steelworkers of America, AFL-CIO-CLC v. North Star Steel Co., 817 F. Supp. 522, 526 (M.D. Pa. 1992), aff'd, 5 F.3d 39 (3d Cir. 1993). Moreover, the releases signed by the workers were not knowing and voluntary, as required by the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (OWBPA), 29 U.S.C. § 626(f), which governs waivers of federal employment claims, including WARN Act claims. See Oubre v. Entergy Operations, Inc., 522 U.S. 422, 426-27 (1998). The releases were presented to workers under duress, with limited time to consider the terms, and without adequate disclosure of relevant information. See Raczak v. Ameritech Corp., 103 F.3d 1257, 1262-63 (6th Cir. 1997).

Furthermore, the district court erred in finding that the workers' acceptance of the severance packages constituted a valid waiver of their WARN Act claims. The Seventh Circuit has held that a waiver of federal employment claims must be "knowing and voluntary." See Pierce v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Ry. Co., 65 F.3d 562, 571 (7th Cir. 1995). In this case, the workers were not given sufficient time to consider the releases, and the terms were not clearly disclosed. See Syverson v. IBM Corp., 472 F.3d 1072, 1077-78 (9th Cir. 2007). Additionally, the releases were not tendered in exchange for consideration beyond that to which the workers were already entitled. See Hogue v. Southern Ry. Co., 390 U.S. 516, 518 (1968).

The Seventh Circuit has also emphasized the importance of strict compliance with the WARN Act's notice requirements. See Kildea v. Electro Wire Products, Inc., 144 F.3d 400, 405 (6th Cir. 1998) (citing Carpenters Dist. Council v. Dillard Dep't Stores, Inc., 15 F.3d 1275, 1286 (5th Cir. 1994)). DHL's failure to provide the required sixty days' written notice to affected employees renders the company liable for damages under the WARN Act. See 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(1).

In sum, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to DHL, as the company failed to comply with the WARN Act's notice requirements, and the releases signed by the workers were not knowing and voluntary. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings. See Local Union 7107 v. Clinchfield Coal Co., 124 F.3d 624, 631 (4th Cir. 1997); see also Marques v. Telles Ranch, 131 F.3d 1331, 1333-34 (9th Cir. 1997). 

Additionally, as held in Bledsoe v. Emery Worldwide Airlines, Inc., 258 F. Supp. 2d 780, 786 (S.D. Ohio 2003), and in Frymire v. Ampex Corp., 61 F.3d 757, 764 (10th Cir. 1995), the employer must provide the required notice to the proper parties, and the notice must be given in a timely manner. DHL failed to meet these requirements, and thus, is liable under the WARN Act.

The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with the WARN Act and relevant case law.
