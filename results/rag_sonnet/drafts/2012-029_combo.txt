ARGUMENT

I. Section 1338(a)'s "arising under" jurisdiction extends only to disputes that actually and substantially implicate the validity, construction, or effect of patent law—not to a state tort suit that merely reconstructs, for damages purposes, an already-settled patent controversy.

This Court has long cautioned against reading "arising under" jurisdiction expansively. In Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), the Court held that "a suit does not so arise unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." Id. at 569. The Court reaffirmed that caution in Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), explaining that federal courts must apply "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." Id. at 117-18. Minton's malpractice claim fails that selective process.

Congress's patent jurisdiction statute has always been understood to reach genuine controversies "touching patent rights," St. Paul Plough Works v. Starling, 127 U.S. 376, 378 (1888)—disputes in which the outcome will actually fix or unsettle a patent right enforceable against the world. Here, the '643 patent was conclusively adjudicated invalid by the federal courts years ago, and that judgment is final. No patent right hangs on the outcome of the Texas malpractice suit; only Gunn's compensatory liability to Minton does. That is precisely the sort of strand Gully instructs courts to lay aside.

II. The experimental-use exception to the on-sale bar, addressed here only retrospectively for damages purposes, is not a "substantial" federal issue of the kind that has justified exclusive federal jurisdiction.

The on-sale bar and its experimental-use exception have deep roots in this Court's patent jurisprudence. See Andrews v. Hovey, 123 U.S. 267 (1887); Andrews v. Hovey, 124 U.S. 694 (1888); Root v. Third Avenue Railroad, 146 U.S. 210 (1892). In Root, the Court distinguished a public use "substantially for experiment" from a use that "must defeat the patent," 146 U.S. at 212, emphasizing that the doctrine exists to protect the public's reliance on an inventor's disclosure obligations and the integrity of the patent grant itself. In Andrews, the Court held a patent invalid where "the invention was used in public ... by others than [the inventor] ... more than two years before the application," 123 U.S. at 274, because such use undermines the statutory quid pro quo between inventor and public.

Those cases confirm that the substantiality of an on-sale-bar question turns on its consequences for an existing or prospective patent right—its capacity to "defeat the patent," Root, 146 U.S. at 212, and thereby affect the public's freedom to use an invention. Here, the '643 patent has already been finally adjudicated invalid; no live patent right will be created, destroyed, or altered by the Texas courts' resolution of the hypothetical "case within a case." The question is confined to whether Gunn's omission caused Minton pecuniary loss—a private, retrospective, damages-only inquiry, in contrast to the forward-looking controversies over patent scope and validity that traditionally justify centralizing patent adjudication in federal courts. See Hoover Co. v. Coe, 325 U.S. 79, 83 (1945) (describing federal jurisdiction as attaching to suits to compel or review actual determinations respecting the grant of patent claims).

III. State courts may resolve embedded patent questions incident to non-patent claims without disturbing the federal-state balance, particularly where the patent's validity has already been fixed by a final federal judgment.

This Court's decision in Marsh v. Nichols, Shepard & Co., 140 U.S. 344 (1891), confirms that a state court may adjudicate a controversy related to, but distinct from, a prior federal patent suit, even where "the alleged patent was void" as previously determined in federal court, because "[t]he subject matter of this suit differed[] from the subject matter of that suit." Id. at 351-52. The same is true here: Minton's malpractice claim is subject matter distinct from his patent-infringement suit, even though its elements require the Texas courts to revisit, for a limited and backward-looking purpose, an issue previously litigated in federal court. Cf. Kener v. La Grange Mills, 231 U.S. 215 (1913) (state adjudication of property rights need not yield to federal law absent a genuine, controlling federal question).

Nor does state-court resolution risk the uniformity concerns that motivate exclusive federal patent jurisdiction. The Patent Act channels patent-validity and patent-scope disputes through defined federal mechanisms of application, examination, and appeal, see Ex Parte Frasch, 192 U.S. 566 (1904); Hoover Co. v. Coe, 325 U.S. 79 (1945), precisely so that the patent grant itself remains uniform nationwide. A state court's resolution of a hypothetical, already-final patent question for purposes of assessing an attorney's malpractice liability does not touch that grant. It merely applies settled patent doctrine, as courts routinely apply non-exclusive federal law, to decide a garden-variety state tort claim.

CONCLUSION

Because the federal patent issue embedded in Minton's malpractice claim is neither "substantial" in the sense Shulthis, Gully, and their progeny require, nor capable of disturbing the balance between state and federal judicial responsibilities reflected in St. Paul Plough Works, Marsh, and Hoover, exclusive federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) does not lie. The judgment below should be reversed.
