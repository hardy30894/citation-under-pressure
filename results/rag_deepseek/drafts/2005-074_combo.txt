**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Re-entered Before Its Effective Date.**

Congress did not clearly express an intent to apply § 241(a)(5) to pre-enactment re-entries. This Court has long held that statutes are not to be given retroactive effect absent a clear congressional command. *Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685, 690 (1957) (savings clause preserved prior status where Act did not “otherwise specifically provide”). Here, the statute’s text—applying to aliens who “reenter” illegally—speaks in the present tense, not the past. The absence of language like “notwithstanding that such reentry occurred prior to enactment” is telling. *Cf. Mulcahey v. Catalanotte*, 353 U.S. 692, 696 (1957) (applying § 241(d) only because it expressly covered prior convictions). The Tenth Circuit erred by reading retroactive intent into silence.

**II. Applying the Statute to Pre-1997 Re-entries Is Impermissibly Retroactive.**

Even if Congress intended retroactive application, such application violates fundamental fairness by attaching new legal consequences to past conduct. *Mrvica v. Esperdy*, 376 U.S. 560, 563 (1964) (retroactive application of continuous-residence requirement denied where alien relied on prior law). Petitioner re-entered in 1982, when the law allowed him to later seek adjustment of status. The 1997 statute stripped that right, imposing a new disability—automatic reinstatement of a 1981 deportation order and a bar on all relief. This is precisely the kind of retroactive effect this Court has condemned. *Rogers v. Quan*, 357 U.S. 193, 197 (1958) (excluded aliens could not be deported under new statute where prior law gave them no status to lose). Petitioner had a settled expectation that his long residence and family ties would permit adjustment. *Foti v. INS*, 375 U.S. 217, 222 (1963) (suspension of deportation is a form of relief that creates reliance interests). The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that petitioner had no “protectable expectation” ignores that he married a U.S. citizen and applied for adjustment before the statute was invoked. *United States ex rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy*, 353 U.S. 72, 77 (1957) (suspension of deportation could not be denied retroactively where alien had relied on prior law).

The presumption against retroactivity is especially strong in immigration law, where aliens order their affairs based on existing rules. *INS v. Errico*, 385 U.S. 214, 220 (1967) (saving clause protected aliens who entered before enactment). Here, the statute retroactively eliminates all avenues of relief, including adjustment of status, which was available when petitioner re-entered. *Cheng Fan Kwok v. INS*, 392 U.S. 206, 212 (1968) (ancillary relief like adjustment is a substantive right, not a mere procedure). The Tenth Circuit’s holding that the statute is not retroactive because it “merely” reinstates a prior order ignores that the order itself was dormant and that the new bar on relief is a wholly new consequence. *Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro*, 349 U.S. 48, 51 (1955) (deportation is a drastic measure that must be applied prospectively). Finally, this Court has long recognized that deportation statutes should be construed to avoid retroactive hardship. *Low Wah Suey v. Backus*, 225 U.S. 460, 472 (1912) (deportation laws must be strictly construed in favor of the alien).

**Conclusion**

The reinstatement statute does not apply to pre-1997 re-entries, and even if it did, such application would be impermissibly retroactive. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
