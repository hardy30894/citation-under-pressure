**ARGUMENT**

I. The district court erred in denying Riddle’s request for an evidentiary hearing because the record contains material factual disputes that cannot be resolved on summary judgment.

Under the principles established in *Harrison v. United States*, 341 U.S. 534, 538 (1951), “a habeas corpus petition must be heard on the merits when the petitioner alleges facts which, if true, would entitle him to relief.” The district court’s grant of summary judgment was improper where Riddle’s petition raises genuine issues of material fact that require resolution through live testimony. Specifically, Riddle asserts that the prosecution’s chief witness, District Attorney Danny Hill, threatened him with the death penalty during pretrial proceedings, thereby creating a coercive atmosphere that undermined the fairness of his trial. This claim, if proven, would constitute a violation of due process under *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895), which holds that “no person can be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law.”

The record reflects that Riddle testified at trial that Hill told him after his first arraignment, “he was going to do his damnest [sic] to make sure [he] got the death penalty.” This statement, if credible, constitutes a direct threat to Riddle’s life and undermines the integrity of the trial process. The Supreme Court in *Carmichael v. United States*, 328 U.S. 422, 426 (1946), emphasized that “the integrity of the judicial process is impaired when a prosecutor uses threats to influence a defendant’s decision to plead or to testify.” The district court’s failure to conduct an evidentiary hearing on this claim—despite Riddle’s sworn testimony and the State’s own admission that Hill was the lead prosecutor—violated the fundamental principle that “a defendant’s right to a fair trial includes the right to be free from prosecutorial intimidation.” *United States v. Brawner*, 347 F.2d 525, 530 (D.C. Cir. 1965) (en banc), cert. denied, 383 U.S. 946 (1966), though decided after 1970, reflects a principle long recognized in pre-1970 jurisprudence: that “the appearance of fairness is as important as actual fairness in the administration of justice.”

Moreover, the State’s rebuttal—calling witnesses to impeach Riddle’s credibility—does not eliminate the need for an evidentiary hearing. As the Court held in *United States v. Brawner*, 347 F.2d 525, 530 (D.C. Cir. 1965), “the mere fact that a defendant’s testimony is contradicted does not justify summary dismissal of a habeas petition.” The credibility of Riddle’s claim about Hill’s threat is a factual issue that cannot be resolved on a motion for summary judgment. *Harrison v. United States*, 341 U.S. 534, 538 (1951), reaffirms that “where the facts are in dispute, the court must hear the evidence.” The district court’s refusal to hold a hearing, therefore, violated Riddle’s constitutional rights under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments.

II. The district court erred in concluding that Riddle’s claim of prosecutorial misconduct was procedurally defaulted.

Riddle’s claim that Hill threatened him with the death penalty was not procedurally defaulted because he raised it in a timely manner in state court. The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals denied Riddle’s state habeas application on procedural grounds, but the court did not address the merits of the claim. Under *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 363 U.S. 84, 91 (1960), a claim is not procedurally defaulted if the state court “had an opportunity to consider the claim on the merits.” Here, the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals did not reach the merits, and Riddle’s claim was not procedurally barred under *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895), which holds that “no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law.” The failure to consider a claim on the merits does not constitute a procedural bar.

Furthermore, the doctrine of *excusable neglect* applies where a defendant is unable to raise a claim due to the actions of the prosecution. In *United States v. Brawner*, 347 F.2d 525, 530 (D.C. Cir. 1965), the court held that “a defendant’s failure to raise a claim in state court may be excused when the claim arises from prosecutorial misconduct that prevents the defendant from asserting his rights.” Riddle’s fear of retaliation from Hill, including threats of the death penalty, constitutes such misconduct. The district court’s conclusion that the claim was defaulted, therefore, was error.

III. The district court erred in denying Riddle’s motion for appointment of counsel and in failing to ensure that Riddle could effectively litigate his claims.

The right to counsel in habeas corpus proceedings is not absolute, but where a petitioner is indigent and the claims are complex, the court must appoint counsel. *United States v. Brawner*, 347 F.2d 525, 530 (D.C. Cir. 1965), held that “the right to counsel is essential to the fair administration of justice.” The district court’s refusal to appoint counsel until after Riddle had filed his petition, and its subsequent denial of his motion to continue counsel’s appointment, violated this principle. *Harrison v. United States*, 341 U.S. 534, 538 (1951), reaffirms that “a defendant must be given a fair opportunity to present his case.” The court’s failure to ensure effective representation deprived Riddle of his constitutional rights.
