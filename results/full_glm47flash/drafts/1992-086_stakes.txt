**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School District’s Policy of Allowing Secular Community Use Created a Limited Public Forum Open to the General Public.**

The First Amendment prohibits the government from excluding speech from a forum it has opened to the general public based on the speech’s content. Here, the School District’s policy of allowing secular community groups to use its facilities while explicitly banning religious groups creates a limited public forum that is open to the public, not a closed nonpublic forum.

Under *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 44 (1983), the extent of permissible governmental regulation of expressive activity on publicly owned property is dependent upon the character of the public property. The Supreme Court has identified three categories of public property: traditional public forums, designated or limited public forums, and nonpublic forums. 460 U.S. at 44-45. A limited public forum is created when the government intentionally opens a particular property to the public for expressive activities. Id. at 45.

The School District’s policy of permitting secular community use of its facilities, such as the showing of movies and lectures, constitutes a policy that has opened the facilities to the general public. As the Supreme Court held in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 273 (1981), a state university may not deny access to university facilities to students who wish to conduct religious meetings on campus if it allows other student groups to use the facilities. The Court noted that "through its policy of accommodating their meetings, the University has created a forum generally open for use by student groups." Id. at 273. Similarly, the School District’s allowance of secular community groups to use its facilities creates a limited public forum open to the general public.

**II. The District’s Exclusion of Religious Speech Constitutes Content-Based Discrimination That Fails Strict Scrutiny.**

Once a limited public forum is established, the government may not exclude speech based on its content. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45. A regulation providing a content-based exclusion may be enforced only when "necessary to serve a compelling state interest" and must be "narrowly drawn" to serve that purpose. Id. The School District’s rule barring religious use of its facilities is a content-based exclusion. By allowing secular films on family values but refusing to allow religious films on the same topic, the District has discriminated against the religious content of Lamb’s Chapel’s speech.

The District has failed to demonstrate a compelling state interest in excluding religious speech from a forum it has opened to the general public. The mere fact that the facilities are school property does not justify a blanket ban on religious speech when secular speech is permitted. As the Supreme Court stated in *Widmar*, "the State may not, by its regulations, suppress or advance religious views." 454 U.S. at 273. The School District’s policy does exactly that.
