**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Trust's Mandatory Distribution of "All the Net Income" Satisfies Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(I).**

The Tax Court erred in holding that the Amendment fails to grant Mrs. Davis a "qualifying income interest for life." I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) requires only that the surviving spouse be "entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals," and that no person hold a power to appoint the property to anyone but the spouse. Both conditions are met here.

Paragraph 2 of the Amendment commands the trustee to "pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse ... all the net income from the trust estate" in "quarter annual or more frequent installments." That is precisely the mandatory, no-less-than-annual distribution scheme Congress required when it enacted the QTIP exception to the terminable interest rule. See I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(I). The reference to the trustee's "reasonable discretion" and to the "health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare" of the spouse "in accordance with the surviving spouse's accustomed manner of living" does not create a power to withhold income; it describes only the *manner* in which the mandatory distribution of income is to be applied for the spouse's benefit — whether paid directly to her or applied on her behalf. Nothing in the clause permits the trustee to accumulate income or divert it to any other person.

The Treasury regulations confirm that discretion over the mechanics of distribution does not defeat entitlement so long as the spouse ultimately receives all the income. Treas. Reg. § 20.2056(b)-7(d) instructs that the principles governing whether a spouse is "entitled to all the income" are the same as those applied under the pre-QTIP marital deduction regulations, which look to whether local law and the trust instrument, read as a whole, require the fiduciary to distribute all income currently. Where, as here, the operative verb is "shall pay," a subordinate clause identifying the purposes for which the income is to be applied does not convert a mandatory distribution into a discretionary one.

**II. Controlling and Persuasive Authority Confirms That a Support Standard Accompanying a Mandatory Income Distribution Does Not Defeat QTIP Qualification.**

This precise question — whether language tying income payments to the spouse's health, support, maintenance, and "accustomed manner of living" limits the spouse's entitlement to *all* the income — has been addressed and answered in the estate's favor. In Estate of Shelfer v. Commissioner, 103 T.C. 10 (1994), the Tax Court held that a trust directing the trustee to pay the spouse only so much income as necessary for her "support, maintenance and health ... in accordance with her accustomed manner of living" failed to qualify for QTIP treatment because the standard purportedly limited the spouse to less than all the income. The Eleventh Circuit reversed. Estate of Shelfer v. Commissioner, 86 F.3d 1045 (11th Cir. 1996). The court of appeals held that such support language, read against the governing trust instrument and state law obligating the trustee to distribute net income currently, did not operate as a restriction on the amount of income payable to the spouse; rather, it merely described the purposes for which the income — all of it — was to be used. The same reasoning controls here. The Amendment's support language does not cap or condition the *amount* of income Mrs. Davis receives; it simply directs how that income, in its entirety, is to be applied for her benefit.

This Court's own precedent likewise counsels that discretionary or administrative language governing the timing or method of distribution does not disqualify a trust from QTIP treatment where the spouse is ultimately assured of receiving the whole of the trust income. See Estate of Rapp v. Commissioner, 140 F.3d 1211 (9th Cir. 1998) (examining whether trust language nominally vesting the trustee with discretion nonetheless satisfied the requirement that the surviving spouse receive the income to which she was entitled). Read together, Shelfer and Rapp establish that a trustee's "reasonable discretion" to determine the mode of applying support-related income does not defeat the spouse's entitlement to "all the income" under section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(I).

**III. No Person Other Than the Surviving Spouse May Receive Trust Property During Her Life, Satisfying the Second Statutory Requirement.**

Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(II) requires only that "no person has a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse." Paragraph 5 of the Amendment permits invasion of principal solely "for the benefit of surviving spouse," and only after her own trust assets are exhausted. Paragraph 3 names Mrs. Davis as first successor trustee, with Pencke and Bennett serving only if she dies or becomes incapacitated — a standard administrative contingency, not a present power to divert principal or income to a third party during Mrs. Davis's lifetime. Because the remainder to the daughters vests, if at all, only upon Mrs. Davis's death, the interest she holds is a life interest with no competing current claim, precisely the arrangement section 2056(b)(7) was designed to accommodate.

**Conclusion**

Because Mrs. Davis is entitled under the Amendment to all the net income of the trust for life, payable at least quarterly, and because no other person may receive any part of the trust property during her lifetime, the trust satisfies both elements of section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). The Tax Court's decision should be reversed and the marital deduction restored.
