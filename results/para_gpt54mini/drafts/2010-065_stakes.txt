Respondent’s argument is that Bond may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to invalidate her conviction, because a private criminal defendant ordinarily lacks standing to assert structural federalism objections absent authorization from a State or a cognizable personal injury distinct from the claimed allocation of sovereign power. The Supreme Court’s decision in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), supplies the governing rule. There, the Court held that private utility companies “have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144. That holding is not dicta; it is the Court’s direct rejection of a private party’s federalism challenge. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101-02 (1998); Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005).

Bond’s claim fits squarely within Tennessee Electric. She is not a State, a state officer, or an authorized sovereign representative. Rather, she is a private defendant challenging Congress’s power to enact 18 U.S.C. § 229. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the Tenth Amendment protects the balance of federal and state authority, not a freestanding personal immunity from otherwise valid federal law. See New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144, 181-82 (1992); Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898, 918-22 (1997). Those cases permitted States or state officers to invoke federalism limits because the asserted injury was to state sovereignty. Bond alleges no comparable sovereign injury.

The courts of appeals have divided on whether private parties may ever raise Tenth Amendment objections, but the better-reasoned authorities follow Tennessee Electric and deny standing. In United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009), the Eighth Circuit held that private defendants lacked standing to mount a Tenth Amendment challenge to a federal criminal statute. The Ninth Circuit reached the same conclusion in Oregon v. Legal Services Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009). The Second Circuit likewise rejected private standing in Brooklyn Legal Services Corp. v. Legal Services Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006), and the First Circuit did so in Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 33-36. Those decisions correctly read Tennessee Electric as foreclosing a private litigant’s attempt to litigate structural federalism on behalf of the States. See also United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004).

Bond cannot evade Tennessee Electric by recasting her challenge as an attack on the scope of Congress’s enumerated powers. The Supreme Court has distinguished between statutes alleged to exceed federal authority and the threshold question whether this particular defendant may raise that objection. See Steel Co., 523 U.S. at 94-95. Standing is a jurisdictional prerequisite. Id. at 101-02. Because Bond asserts only that Congress invaded a domain reserved to the States, she seeks vindication of a structural allocation of authority, not relief from a personal legal injury distinct from her conviction. That is exactly the sort of generalized grievance Tennessee Electric rejected. 306 U.S. at 144.

Nor does the fact that § 229 implements a treaty alter the standing analysis. Whether Congress had power under the Necessary and Proper Clause and the Treaty Power to enact § 229 goes to the merits, not Bond’s entitlement to raise a Tenth Amendment objection in the first place. Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432-33 (1920), held that Congress may enact legislation necessary and proper to carry a valid treaty into effect; United States v. Lara, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004), reaffirmed the breadth of federal authority in this area. But those cases do not create private standing to challenge federal enactments on behalf of state sovereignty.

Bond’s reliance on federalism concerns therefore fails at the threshold. If a State believes a federal statute exceeds enumerated powers or unduly intrudes upon state criminal jurisdiction, the State may sue to vindicate its sovereignty. See New York, 505 U.S. at 181-82; Printz, 521 U.S. at 935. A private criminal defendant may assert defenses personal to herself, including vagueness, overbreadth, or lack of proof of the elements of the offense. See United States v. Powell, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975). But she may not transform her prosecution into a vehicle for litigating abstract federalism on behalf of the States. Tennessee Electric bars that course.

Because Bond lacks standing to present her Tenth Amendment challenge, the Court should affirm her conviction without reaching the constitutionality of § 229 under the federalism principles she invokes.
