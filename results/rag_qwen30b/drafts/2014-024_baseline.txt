**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—lies at the heart of the First Amendment’s protection against criminalizing speech. The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective standard for “true threats” violates the constitutional principle that speech may not be punished unless the speaker *intended* to threaten. This Court has long held that the First Amendment protects even offensive or disturbing speech, but permits the criminalization of speech that constitutes a “true threat.” However, the definition of a “true threat” is not coextensive with any statement that a reasonable person might interpret as threatening. Rather, it requires a showing that the speaker *subjectively intended* to threaten. The Court’s decision in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003), made clear that “a true threat is a knowing and willful expression of intent to harm,” and that “the speaker must have intended to threaten.” This subjective intent requirement is not a mere procedural formality—it is a constitutional safeguard against the criminalization of speech that is merely offensive, hyperbolic, or expressive of anger, even if it is understood by some as threatening.

The Third Circuit’s contrary holding—that a “true threat” may be established by an objective standard, under which a statement is deemed threatening if a reasonable person would foresee it as such—directly contradicts *Virginia v. Black*. There, the Court rejected the notion that a threat could be established solely by the perception of a reasonable observer. Instead, the Court emphasized that “the First Amendment protects the expression of political views, even when they are offensive or unpopular,” and that “the government may not punish speech simply because it is offensive.” Id. at 359. The Court further stated that “a true threat is a knowing and willful expression of intent to harm,” and that “the speaker must have intended to threaten.” Id. at 359. This language is not merely dicta—it is the core of the Court’s holding that the “true threat” exception to the First Amendment is not a license to punish speech based on its effect on others, but only speech that is *intended* to cause fear of violence.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), to justify its objective standard is misplaced. *Kosma* predated *Virginia v. Black* and did not address the constitutional requirement of subjective intent. Indeed, *Kosma* held that a statement is a true threat when “a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat.” 951 F.2d at 557. But *Virginia v. Black* made clear that such an objective standard, if applied without regard to the speaker’s intent, risks criminalizing speech that is constitutionally protected. As the Court explained, “the First Amendment protects the expression of political views, even when they are offensive or unpopular.” 538 U.S. at 359. To allow the government to punish speech based solely on how a reasonable person might interpret it—without regard to the speaker’s intent—is to invite the criminalization of hyperbolic, artistic, or satirical expression, even when the speaker never intended to threaten.

The facts of this case underscore the danger of the Third Circuit’s approach. Elonis’s Facebook posts were written in the form of rap lyrics, a genre known for its use of exaggeration, metaphor, and dramatic expression. His wife testified that she took the posts seriously, but she also acknowledged that she had never seen Elonis write rap lyrics before. Trial Tr. 97. The jury was instructed that a statement is a true threat if a reasonable person would foresee it as such—regardless of whether Elonis himself intended to threaten. This instruction allowed the jury to convict based on the *perception* of the audience, not the *intent* of the speaker. Such a standard is constitutionally impermissible. As this Court has held, “the First Amendment protects the expression of political views, even when they are offensive or unpopular.” *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. To punish speech based on its effect on others, rather than the speaker’s intent, is to allow the government to suppress speech simply because it causes fear—even if the speaker never intended to cause fear.

The government’s argument that an objective standard is necessary to protect individuals from the fear of violence is unavailing. The First Amendment does not require the government to protect individuals from all fear, only from threats that are *intended* to cause fear. The Court in *Virginia v. Black* recognized that “the government has a legitimate interest in protecting individuals from the fear of violence,” but that interest “must be balanced against the First Amendment’s protection of speech.” 538 U.S. at 359. The balance is struck by requiring proof of subjective intent. Without such a requirement, the government may criminalize speech that is merely offensive, even if it is not intended to threaten. This is precisely the danger the First Amendment guards against.

The judgment of the Third Circuit must be reversed. The Constitution requires that a conviction under § 875(c) be based on proof that the defendant *subjectively intended* to threaten. The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective standard for “true threats” violates *Virginia v. Black* and the First Amendment. The judgment below is therefore contrary to settled constitutional law and must be reversed.
