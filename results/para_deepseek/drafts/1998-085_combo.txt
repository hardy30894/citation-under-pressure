ARGUMENT

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 authorizes punitive damages under Title VII only where the plaintiff demonstrates that the employer engaged in a discriminatory practice “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That language is a term of art borrowed from the common law of exemplary damages. When Congress employs a statutory phrase with a settled common-law meaning, it is presumed to adopt that meaning. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952). The common law never permitted punitive damages for every intentional wrong; it reserved them for conduct so egregious or outrageous—so oppressive, wanton, or insulting—that the public interest demanded punishment and deterrence.

I. The Common Law Made the Egregious Character of the Defendant’s Conduct the Touchstone of Punitive Liability.

From this Court’s earliest punitive-damages decisions, the inquiry focused on the objective quality of the defendant’s conduct, not merely on his subjective state of mind. In The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. 546, 558 (1818), the Court explained that enhanced damages are appropriate where the wrongdoer has acted with “wanton and malicious oppression.” Likewise, Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1852), approved exemplary damages “where gross fraud, malice, or oppression appears.” The decisive words—“gross,” “wanton,” “oppression”—describe conduct of an aggravated and outrageous character, not simply an intent to harm.

This Court later synthesized the authorities in Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), holding that exemplary damages may be awarded only where the wrongful act is accompanied by malice, wantonness, oppression, or that “reckless indifference to the rights of others” which the law treats as equivalent to an intentional violation. That phrase describes a course of conduct evincing a depraved disregard of the victim’s rights, not bare knowledge that one’s act is unlawful. An ordinary intentional tort, however deliberate, remains outside the doctrine.

The Court’s other decisions apply the same objective measure. Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore Railroad Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202 (1858), sustained an exemplary award only because the defendant’s ejection of a passenger presented circumstances of insult and aggravation. Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), limited exemplary damages to acts done wantonly or oppressively, or with such gross negligence as to imply malice. Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489 (1875), likewise required wanton or reckless conduct of an aggravated kind. And Southern Express Co. v. Byers, 240 U.S. 612 (1916), refused to sustain a punitive award where the carrier’s conduct, though unwelcome, did not amount to an aggravated breach of duty.

Even in sustaining statutory multiple-damages provisions, this Court emphasized that punishment is predicated on aggravated misconduct. Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Humes, 115 U.S. 512 (1885), upheld a doubling statute on the ground that the defendant had acted with reckless disregard of obligations owed to the public. Minneapolis & St. Louis Railway Co. v. Beckwith, 129 U.S. 26 (1889), recognized the same principle, insisting on a quality of wrong beyond mere fault. In every instance, the inquiry ran to what the defendant did—whether the act itself was outrageous—and never stopped at what the defendant knew or intended.

II. Petitioner’s Reading Collapses the Punitive Standard Into the Liability Standard.

Petitioner would hold that any intentional discrimination—any employer who knowingly makes a decision because of sex—has acted with “malice” for purposes of § 1981a(b)(1). That reading empties the statutory standard of independent meaning. Title VII liability already requires proof of intentional discrimination. If the employer’s state of mind alone satisfied the punitive standard, then every intentional violator would face punitive damages as a matter of course, and the common-law requirement of egregious, outrageous, oppressive conduct would be silently discarded. Congress is presumed to have imported the established common-law meaning, and the interpretive memorandum of the statute’s sponsors confirms that punitive damages were contemplated for “extraordinarily egregious cases,” not for routine discrimination.

The record here illustrates the point. The ADA promoted a candidate it considered better qualified after a selection process—whatever its imperfections—in which positions were posted, applications were considered, and interviews were conducted. Even crediting the jury’s finding of intentional discrimination, and even crediting Petitioner’s account of offensive remarks by an employee who did not make the promotion decision, nothing in this record approaches the wanton and malicious oppression that the common law punished. A closer case may come; this is not it. The district court correctly denied punitive damages.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
