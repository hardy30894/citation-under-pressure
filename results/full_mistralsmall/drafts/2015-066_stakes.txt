**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Sixth Amendment’s Right to Counsel Applies to Tribal Court Convictions Used as Predicate Offenses in Federal Prosecutions Under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right ... to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right attaches at every “critical stage” of a criminal proceeding, *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218, 224 (1967), and applies to all “formal confrontations” where the “presence of counsel is necessary to preserve the defendant’s basic right to a fair trial,” *id.* at 227. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal or state courts but extend to any proceeding where the government seeks to impose criminal punishment. *See Argersinger v. Hamlin*, 407 U.S. 25, 31 (1972) (holding that the right to counsel applies to misdemeanor prosecutions where imprisonment is imposed); *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963) (extending the right to counsel to state felony prosecutions).

The government cannot circumvent these protections by routing prosecutions through tribal courts. The Sixth Amendment’s command is not limited by the jurisdictional source of a conviction. *See, e.g., Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967) (holding that a prior uncounseled conviction used to enhance punishment under a recidivist statute violated the Sixth Amendment, regardless of whether the conviction occurred in state or federal court). The Court has made clear that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are “inflexible” and apply to all criminal prosecutions, including those involving recidivist enhancements. *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 745 (1994) (citing *Baldasar v. Illinois*, 446 U.S. 222, 223 (1980) (per curiam)).

Here, the government seeks to use Bryant’s tribal court convictions as predicate offenses to elevate a misdemeanor assault to a felony under § 117(a). This is not a sentencing enhancement but an element of the offense itself. The government must prove these prior convictions beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel therefore applies with full force. *See, e.g., United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389, 1394 (9th Cir. 1989) (holding that uncounseled tribal court convictions could not be used as predicate offenses in a federal prosecution because the Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches to such proceedings).

II. **Tribal Courts Are Not Exempt from the Sixth Amendment’s Requirements When Their Convictions Are Used in Federal Prosecutions.**

The government argues that tribal courts are exempt from the Sixth Amendment because the Amendment does not expressly apply to tribal governments. *See* 25 U.S.C. § 1302 (Indian Civil Rights Act does not guarantee a right to appointed counsel in tribal courts). However, this argument conflates the source of the conviction with the use of that conviction in a federal prosecution. The Sixth Amendment does not depend on the sovereign that issued the conviction; it applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in federal court. *See Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 115.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that the government can evade constitutional protections by relying on convictions obtained in other forums. *See, e.g., Custis v. United States*, 511 U.S. 485, 490 (1994) (holding that a defendant may collaterally attack a prior conviction used as a predicate offense under the Armed Career Criminal Act if it violated the Sixth Amendment). The Court has emphasized that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are “a fundamental right” that cannot be circumvented by procedural maneuvers. *Id.* at 492.

The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized this principle in *Ant*, holding that tribal court convictions used as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions must comply with the Sixth Amendment. *Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1394. The court reasoned that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal or state courts but apply to any proceeding where the government seeks to impose criminal punishment. *Id.* This reasoning aligns with the Supreme Court’s decisions in *Burgett* and *Custis*, which refused to allow the government to use uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses regardless of their jurisdictional source.

III. **The Use of Uncounseled Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Violates the Sixth Amendment’s Reliability Concerns.**

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel serves a critical reliability function. *See Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 344 (noting that the right to counsel is essential to ensuring a fair trial). The Court has held that uncounseled convictions are inherently unreliable and cannot be used to enhance punishment in federal prosecutions. *See Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 115.

The government argues that *Nichols* permits the use of uncounseled convictions in sentencing because the Sentencing Guidelines allow defendants to challenge the reliability of such convictions. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747-48. However, *Nichols* involved a sentencing enhancement, not an element of the offense. The Court emphasized that sentencing courts can consider a broader range of evidence and apply a lower burden of proof. *Id.* at 748. Here, by contrast, the government must prove the predicate offenses beyond a reasonable doubt. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. The Sixth Amendment’s reliability concerns are therefore heightened, not diminished.

The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that *Nichols* does not sanction the use of uncounseled convictions as elements of an offense. *See Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1394. The court recognized that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are most critical at the guilt phase, where the government must prove each element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* The use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) therefore violates the Sixth Amendment.

**Conclusion**

The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in federal court where tribal court convictions are used as predicate offenses. The government cannot evade these protections by relying on tribal courts that do not provide the same constitutional guarantees. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the use of Bryant’s uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) violated his Sixth Amendment rights. This Court should affirm.
