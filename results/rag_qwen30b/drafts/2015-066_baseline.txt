**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an uncounseled tribal court conviction may be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), thereby violating the Sixth Amendment—requires this Court to resolve a critical tension between federal criminal law and the constitutional rights of defendants. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Bryant, Jr.*, 769 F.3d 671 (9th Cir. 2014), and its subsequent refusal to rehear the matter en banc, rest on a flawed interpretation of *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), and a fundamental misunderstanding of the Sixth Amendment’s role in the guilt phase of criminal proceedings. The Court should reverse, holding that the use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as an essential element of a federal felony offense violates the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of due process and reliability.

The core of the Ninth Circuit’s error lies in its attempt to apply *Nichols*—a sentencing enhancement case—to a guilt-phase determination. In *Nichols*, the Court upheld the use of an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction to increase a defendant’s criminal history score under the Sentencing Guidelines. 511 U.S. at 746–47. The Court emphasized that sentencing is not a guilt phase, and that the government need only prove sentencing enhancements by a preponderance of the evidence. Id. at 748. The Court further noted that the sentencing scheme allowed defendants to challenge the reliability of prior convictions, and that the lesser burden of proof at sentencing accommodated concerns about reliability. Id. at 747–48. Nothing in *Nichols* suggests that an uncounseled conviction may be used to establish an essential element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt.

This distinction is constitutionally vital. As the Court held in *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), “the prosecution must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.” The use of a prior conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) is not a mere sentencing factor—it is an essential element of the offense. The statute requires the government to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the defendant has two prior convictions for domestic violence offenses. 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). To permit the use of an uncounseled conviction in this context is to permit the government to rely on a judgment that may have been obtained in violation of the defendant’s constitutional rights—precisely the kind of unreliable evidence *Winship* was designed to exclude.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is misplaced. *Ant* held that tribal court misdemeanor convictions that do not provide an equivalent right to counsel may not be used to establish guilt in a subsequent federal prosecution. Id. at 1396. That decision was grounded in the principle that the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of reliability applies when the government seeks to prove an essential element of a crime. The Ninth Circuit’s concurrence in *Bryant* attempts to distinguish *Ant* by claiming that *Nichols* “leaves open the question” of whether an unreliable conviction passes muster at the guilt phase. But *Nichols* did not leave anything open—it was a sentencing case, and its holding was limited to that context. To extend *Nichols* to the guilt phase would contradict *Winship* and undermine the fundamental fairness of criminal trials.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that tribal court convictions are “constitutionally infirm” because they do not afford the Sixth Amendment right to counsel ignores the fact that tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323–24 (1978), affirmed that tribal courts exercise “the continued exercise of retained tribal sovereignty.” The Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. § 1302, provides tribal courts with procedural safeguards, including an equivalent right to counsel in certain cases, but it does not impose the full panoply of Sixth Amendment rights. To treat tribal court proceedings as constitutionally defective simply because they do not mirror federal or state procedures is to impose judicial paternalism and erode tribal sovereignty. *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 811 (9th Cir. 1997), recognized that “comity does not require that a tribe utilize judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts.” The federal courts must respect tribal jurisprudence, not impose federal constitutional standards where Congress has not mandated them.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision also creates a dangerous circuit split. The Eighth and Tenth Circuits have held that uncounseled tribal court convictions may be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a), so long as they are valid under tribal law. *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997–98 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011). The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the need for uniformity in the application of federal law” is a compelling reason for review. *United States v. Johnson*, 486 U.S. 578, 585 (1988). This case presents precisely such a need.

Finally, the Ninth Circuit’s decision undermines Congress’s intent in enacting § 117(a). The statute was designed to combat the epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country, where tribal courts often lack the resources to prosecute repeat offenders. The National Congress of American Indians has emphasized the urgency of this issue. To invalidate the use of tribal court convictions—valid under tribal law and often the only available mechanism for holding repeat offenders accountable—is to cripple the very tool Congress created to protect victims. The Court should not allow a technical reading of *Nichols* to undermine the effectiveness of a vital federal statute.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Ninth Circuit and hold that the use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of due process and reliability
