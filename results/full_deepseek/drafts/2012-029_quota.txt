**ARGUMENT**

The Texas Supreme Court erred in holding that exclusive federal jurisdiction strips state courts of authority over this legal malpractice action. The court misapplied the settled test for “arising under” jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). Under *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988), federal patent jurisdiction extends only to cases where “patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims.” But even when a federal issue is necessary, it must also be “substantial” and its resolution must not “disturb any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). The patent issue here fails the *Grable* test on multiple grounds.

**I. The Patent Issue Is Not Substantial**

The experimental-use exception is a narrow, fact-bound inquiry that does not implicate the core uniformity concerns of patent law. The Supreme Court has long cautioned that federal jurisdiction does not arise merely because a state claim “involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law.” *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Here, the only patent question is whether the TEXCEN lease was primarily experimental or commercial—a factual determination that turns on the parties’ intent, not on the construction of any patent statute. *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Such a routine factual dispute is not “substantial” in the sense required by *Grable*. *See Merrell Dow Pharm. Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 814 (1986) (no federal jurisdiction where federal issue is “not substantial” and state law provides the cause of action).

The Fifth Circuit, applying *Grable* to a similar malpractice claim involving trademark law, held that “the federal issue is not substantial” because it “does not require the resolution of a novel or important federal question.” *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 384, 390 (5th Cir. 2008). The same reasoning applies here. The experimental-use exception is well-settled; its application to Minton’s facts presents no novel question of patent law. *See also New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 270 Neb. 264, 702 N.W.2d 336, 345 (2005) (no federal jurisdiction over patent malpractice claim because “patent issues are merely implicated incidentally”).

**II. Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the State-Federal Balance**

Congress has not indicated that every state malpractice claim touching on patent law must be heard in federal court. To the contrary, the *Grable* test requires that federal jurisdiction not “displace state courts’ traditional authority over legal malpractice.” *Singh*, 538 F.3d at 392. Legal malpractice is a quintessential state-law tort. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). Allowing every such claim that requires a “case within a case” analysis of patent law to be removed to federal court would “snare” routine disputes and “disrupt the federal-state line drawn by Congress.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. Numerous courts have recognized this danger. *See E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 189 Cal. App. 4th 1140, 1152 (2010) (no federal jurisdiction because “the subject matter of patent law is relevant” but “does not present a question of patent law that is substantial”); *Roof Technical Servs., Inc. v. Hill*, 679 F. Supp. 2d 749, 753–54 (N.D. Tex. 2010) (adjudication of patent issues in malpractice case “will not create or destroy any patent rights”).

**III. The Federal Circuit’s Contrary Precedent Is Inconsistent with *Grable***

The Texas Supreme Court relied on *Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), but that decision failed to conduct the federalism analysis required by *Grable*. *See Davis v. Brouse McDowell, L.P.A.*, 596 F.3d 1355, 1362 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (applying *Air Measurement* without citing *Grable*). The Supreme Court has made clear that “the absence of a federalism analysis” is fatal. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. The Fifth Circuit itself initially rejected the Federal Circuit’s approach in *Singh*, 538 F.3d at 390, and even when it later adopted a similar result, it did so without conducting its own federalism analysis. *See USPPS, Ltd. v. Avery Dennison Corp.*, 647 F.3d 274, 278 n.1 (5th Cir. 2011) (merely quoting *Immunocept*). This Court should not follow precedent that ignores the Supreme Court’s directive.

**IV. The Patent Issue Is Not Necessarily Dispositive**

Even if the experimental-use exception were applied, Minton would still need to prove causation and damages—state-law elements that do not depend on patent law. *See Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936) (federal issue must be “real and substantial” and not “merely incidental”). The state court can resolve the malpractice claim without definitively adjudicating the validity of the ’643 patent. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 810 (federal jurisdiction requires that the patent issue be “necessary to the plaintiff’s right to relief”). Here, the patent issue is but one link in the causal chain; it is not the foundation of the claim.

**Conclusion**

Because the patent issue is not substantial, would disturb the state-federal balance, and is not necessarily dispositive, exclusive federal jurisdiction does not lie. The Texas Supreme Court’s judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for consideration on the merits.
