**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to CSX and UTU on Smoot's RLA Conspiracy Claim.**

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, drawing all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party. *Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986). Summary judgment is proper only where the movant demonstrates "that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact," *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986), and a factual dispute is "genuine" if the evidence would permit a reasonable jury to return a verdict for the nonmoving party. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986).

A union breaches its duty of fair representation—and may be liable in concert with an employer—when its conduct toward a member is "arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith." *Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 190 (1967). Conduct is arbitrary only when it falls "so far outside a 'wide range of reasonableness' … as to be irrational." *Air Line Pilots Ass'n, Int'l v. O'Neill*, 499 U.S. 65, 67 (1991). The record below created precisely the kind of factual dispute that a jury, not a court on summary judgment, must resolve. Elswick's initial refusal to pursue Smoot's stock claim was overturned by the union's own Board of Appeals, yet Elswick removed himself and installed Earley, who then participated in a closed-door executive session with CSX's Senior Director Emerick and the neutral arbitrator from which Smoot was excluded. The subsequent internal charges brought against Earley—first by Smoot and then, independently, by union member Ruck after reviewing the tape—alleged that Earley "argued against the official position of the UTU" during that very session. *See Earley v. Executive Bd. of the UTU*, 957 F.Supp. 997, 999 (N.D. Ohio 1996). A reasonable jury could infer from this sequence that Earley's representation of Smoot before the PLB was compromised by an undisclosed accommodation with CSX, satisfying *Vaca*'s bad-faith standard. Because the district court resolved this disputed inference against Smoot rather than in his favor, as *Anderson* requires, summary judgment must be reversed.

**II. The District Court's Award of $250,000 in Statutory Damages Exceeds the Cap Fixed by 18 U.S.C. § 2520(c)(2) and Must Be Vacated.**

The Wiretap Act limits statutory damages to "whichever is the greater of— (A) the sum of the actual damages suffered by the plaintiff and any profits made by the violator … or (B) statutory damages of whichever is the greater of $100 a day for each day of violation or $10,000." 18 U.S.C. § 2520(c)(2). Courts construing this provision have held that the statutory figure is a single ceiling, not a sum to be multiplied across defendants, claims, or theories of liability. *See Nalley v. Nalley*, 53 F.3d 649, 652 (4th Cir. 1995) (statutory damages under § 2520(c)(2) are calculated as a discrete award tied to the violation, not compounded); *Reynolds v. Spears*, 93 F.3d 428, 435 (8th Cir. 1996) (same). Here, Smoot made a single recording of a single executive session on a single day. Under the plain terms of § 2520(c)(2), the maximum recoverable statutory award was $10,000. The district court's award of $250,000—twenty-five times the statutory ceiling—cannot be reconciled with the text of the statute and constitutes reversible error as a matter of law.

**III. The Punitive Damages Award Is Unsupported by the Record.**

Punitive damages under the Act are reserved for "appropriate cases," 18 U.S.C. § 2520(b)(2), and require proof beyond the bare fact of a violation. *See Reynolds*, 93 F.3d at 436 (punitive damages require evidence of willfulness or maliciousness beyond the underlying interception). Smoot's undisputed testimony was that he brought his recorders to document the PLB proceeding generally and inadvertently left them running when the panel unexpectedly cleared the room for an executive session—not that he schemed to capture a confidential union deliberation. This evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Smoot as required at summary judgment, does not establish the aggravated culpability necessary to sustain a $100,000 punitive award, and the district court identified no additional evidence of malice justifying that figure.

**IV. The Attorneys' Fee Award Must Be Vacated for Lack of Adequate Findings.**

"The applicant bears the burden of establishing entitlement to an award and documenting the appropriate hours expended and hourly rates," and the district court "should exclude from this initial fee calculation hours that were not 'reasonably expended.'" *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433, 437 (1983). The $86,308.74 fee award below was entered without the itemized lodestar analysis *Hensley* demands, and cannot stand independent of the flawed statutory and punitive damages determinations from which it derives.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment on Smoot's RLA conspiracy claim and vacate the statutory damages, punitive damages, and attorneys' fee awards entered against him.
