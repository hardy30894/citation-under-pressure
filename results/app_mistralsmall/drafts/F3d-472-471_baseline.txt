**ARGUMENT**

**I. BURNETT PROVIDED SUFFICIENT NOTICE OF HIS NEED FOR LEAVE UNDER THE FMLA**

The FMLA requires an employee to provide notice of the need for leave "as soon as practicable." 29 U.S.C. § 2612(e)(1). Notice is "sufficient" if it "giv[es] the employer notice of the employee’s need for leave and the anticipated timing and duration of the leave." *Carr v. Gates, 195 F.3d 292, 296 (7th Cir. 1999)* (citing 29 C.F.R. § 825.302(c)). The notice need not invoke the FMLA by name; it is enough that the employer "knows of the employee’s serious health condition and the need for leave." *Id.* (quoting *Rankin v. Seagate US LLC, 246 F.3d 1145, 1149 (8th Cir. 2001)*).

Here, Burnett’s communications with Habitat—beginning in October 2003 and continuing through January 2004—clearly apprised his employer of his serious health condition and his need for leave. In October 2003, Burnett told Polo he was experiencing medical difficulties, specifically a "weak bladder," and that he was seeing a doctor. *See* 29 C.F.R. § 825.302(c) (notice may be oral). In December 2003, Burnett provided a doctor’s note justifying prior absences and informed Polo of his upcoming medical appointments, including a prostate biopsy. *SeeCarr*, 195 F.3d at 296 (notice need not be formal). Finally, on January 7, 2004, Burnett explicitly notified Habitat of his biopsy scheduled for January 27. *See id.* (anticipated timing of leave need not be exact).

The district court erred in holding that Burnett’s notice was insufficient. *SeeBrady v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 531 F.3d 1307, 1314 (11th Cir. 2008)* (notice is sufficient if employer "has enough information to know that the employee’s absences may be due to a serious health condition"). Habitat was on notice of Burnett’s serious health condition and his need for leave well before his termination. *SeeCarr*, 195 F.3d at 296.

**II. BURNETT’S COMMUNICATIONS WITH HABITAT SATISFIED THE ADA’S NOTICE REQUIREMENT**

The ADA requires an employee to provide notice of a disability and the need for accommodation. *See 42 U.S.C. § 12112(b)(5)(A)*; *EEOC v. Sears, Roebuck & Co., 417 F.3d 789, 797 (7th Cir. 2005)*. Notice may be informal, and the employee need not use "magic words" to trigger the employer’s obligations. *Id.* at 798 (citing *Bultemeyer v. Fort Wayne Cmty. Schs., 100 F.3d 1281, 1285 (7th Cir. 1996)*).

Burnett’s disclosures to Habitat—particularly his discussions of his prostate condition and upcoming medical procedures—were sufficient to put Habitat on notice of his disability and need for accommodation. *SeeSears*, 417 F.3d at 798 (notice is adequate if employer "knows or should know" of the disability). Habitat was aware of Burnett’s serious health condition and his need for leave, yet it failed to engage in the interactive process or provide reasonable accommodations. *See id.* at 797-98.

**III. HABITAT’S FAILURE TO ENGAGE IN THE INTERACTIVE PROCESS VIOLATED THE ADA**

The ADA imposes a duty on employers to engage in an interactive process to determine whether reasonable accommodations are available. *See 42 U.S.C. § 12111(9)*; *EEOC v. Sears, Roebuck & Co.*, 417 F.3d at 796. The employer must "communicate with the employee to determine whether an accommodation is needed and, if so, what it would entail." *Id.* at 797 (quoting *Bultemeyer*, 100 F.3d at 1285).

Here, Habitat never engaged in the interactive process. Despite Burnett’s repeated disclosures of his medical condition and need for leave, Habitat issued reprimands, suspended him, and ultimately terminated him. *See id.* at 798 (employer violates ADA by failing to engage in interactive process). The district court erred in granting summary judgment on this claim. *See id.* at 796-98.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Burnett’s notice under the FMLA and ADA was sufficient, and Habitat’s failure to engage in the interactive process violated the ADA. The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and this matter remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
