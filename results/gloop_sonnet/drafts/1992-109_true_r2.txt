ARGUMENT

I. THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A SEPARATE, HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD FOR A DEFENDANT WHO PLEADS GUILTY OR WAIVES COUNSEL.

A. This Court Has Never Recognized Two Tiers of Mental Competence.

The Ninth Circuit held that a defendant may be competent to stand trial yet incompetent to plead guilty or discharge counsel unless he also satisfies a "reasoned choice" standard. That bifurcated approach has no footing in this Court's due process jurisprudence. Where this Court has confronted the mental capacity of a defendant to make fundamental decisions about his own case, it has applied a single, unitary inquiry into sanity and understanding, not a hierarchy of competency standards keyed to which right is at stake.

In United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi, 344 U.S. 561 (1953), the state court's competency finding—found consistent with due process—asked simply whether there was "nothing in this record which convinces us that this relator was insane when he committed the murder charged or when he pleaded guilty or at the time he was sentenced to death." Id. at 561. That formulation draws no distinction between the mental capacity required to stand trial and the capacity required to plead guilty; sanity, not some elevated "reasoned choice" faculty, was the touchstone.

Carter v. Illinois, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), likewise treated waiver of counsel as governed by ordinary capacity to choose: "The right to be represented by counsel is one which the defendant may waive or claim, as he shall determine." Id. at 174. Nothing in Carter suggests that determination requires proof of a superior mental faculty beyond what is necessary to stand trial.

And in Palmer v. Ashe, 342 U.S. 134 (1951), this Court framed the due process inquiry into an uncounseled guilty plea around whether there were "special circumstances showing that without a lawyer a defendant could not have an adequate and a fair defense." Id. at 134. Again, the inquiry is one of fairness under the circumstances, not a freestanding, heightened competency test.

B. The Constitutional Benchmark Is a Knowing and Intelligent Waiver, Not "Reasoned Choice."

This Court's settled test for the validity of a waiver of trial rights is whether it reflects "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), quoted in Brookhart v. Janis, 384 U.S. 1, 4 (1966). Applying that standard in Brookhart, this Court asked only whether the petitioner "intelligently and knowingly waived his right to cross-examine the witnesses whose testimony was used to convict him." Brookhart, 384 U.S. at 4. The Court did not ask whether the petitioner possessed some enhanced decisional capacity beyond ordinary competence; it asked whether the waiver was knowing and intelligent.

McCarthy v. United States, 394 U.S. 459 (1969), confirms that the safeguards surrounding a guilty plea are satisfied when the trial court ascertains that the defendant understands what he is giving up. There, after the district judge asked petitioner "if he understood that such a plea waived his right to a jury trial and subjected him to imprisonment," petitioner "stated that he understood these consequences and wanted to plead guilty." Id. at 461. That is the constitutional inquiry—understanding and voluntariness—not a separate mental-capacity threshold layered on top of ordinary competence.

C. A Novel "Reasoned Choice" Standard Would Disserve the Values Underlying Habeas Review of State Convictions.

Federal courts sitting in habeas review of state criminal judgments have long been cautioned against layering new substantive standards onto proceedings that were otherwise regularly conducted. In Matter of Moran, 203 U.S. 96 (1906), this Court examined a habeas petition premised on claimed defects in the trial court's jurisdiction, explaining that the grounds asserted in the petition were "alleged to go to the jurisdiction of the trial court." Id. at 97. That decision reflects the principle that habeas review properly reaches fundamental defects in the proceeding, not the wholesale substitution of a new substantive rule for one this Court has already supplied.

Rees v. Peyton, 384 U.S. 312 (1966), is instructive by contrast. When doubt arose concerning a habeas petitioner's capacity to withdraw his own petition, this Court did not invent a special standard; it ordered further proceedings "because evidence cast doubt on Rees' mental competency." Id. at 313. This Court thus employed the ordinary competency framework—not a bespoke "reasoned choice" test—to resolve a question about a defendant's capacity to make a decision affecting his own case.

* * *

The competency standard this Court has articulated for standing trial, see Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), together with the knowing-and-intelligent waiver requirement of Johnson v. Zerbst and Brookhart v. Janis, fully protects a defendant's rights when he chooses to plead guilty or proceed without counsel. Respondent Moran was examined by two psychiatrists and found to understand the charges against him and to be capable of assisting in his defense—precisely the inquiry due process demands. No additional "reasoned choice" standard is required by the Constitution, and the Ninth Circuit erred in imposing one.
