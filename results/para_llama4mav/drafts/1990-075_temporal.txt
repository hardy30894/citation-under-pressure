The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The non-delegation doctrine, as established by the Supreme Court, permits Congress to delegate authority to another branch of government as long as it provides an "intelligible principle" to guide the delegatee's actions (J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). 

The CSA satisfies this requirement by specifying the factors the Attorney General must consider when scheduling substances, including their potential for abuse, scientific evidence of their pharmacological effect, and the risk they pose to public health (21 U.S.C. § 811(c)). This guidance is sufficient to ensure that the Attorney General's actions are not arbitrary or capricious.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has consistently upheld delegations of power to administrative agencies, even when the delegated authority involves significant discretion (Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245 (1947)). In Yakus, the Court upheld a delegation of power to the Price Administrator to establish maximum prices for commodities, despite the broad discretion granted to the Administrator (321 U.S. at 426-27). Similarly, in Fahey, the Court upheld a delegation of power to the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to regulate the activities of savings and loan associations (332 U.S. at 249-50).

The CSA's delegation of authority to the Attorney General is analogous to these cases. The Attorney General is tasked with scheduling substances based on specific criteria, and the statute provides a framework for the exercise of this authority. The fact that the Attorney General has delegated this authority to the DEA Administrator does not render the original delegation unconstitutional, as the Attorney General remains responsible for the actions of the DEA (United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516 (1911)).

In Grimaud, the Court held that the Secretary of Agriculture's delegation of authority to forest supervisors to make rules and regulations for the protection of national forests was a valid exercise of delegated power (220 U.S. at 516). Similarly, the Attorney General's delegation of authority to the DEA Administrator is a valid exercise of the authority delegated to him by Congress.

In conclusion, the CSA's delegation of authority to the Attorney General is constitutional, as it provides sufficient guidance and is consistent with the Supreme Court's precedents on the non-delegation doctrine. The delegation of authority to the DEA Administrator is also proper, as it is a valid exercise of the Attorney General's delegated power.
