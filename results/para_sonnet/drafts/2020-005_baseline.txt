# ARGUMENT

## I. Due Process Permits Jurisdiction Where a Defendant's In-State Conduct "Relates To" the Plaintiff's Claims, Even If That Conduct Did Not Directly Cause the Injury.

Since *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), this Court has recognized that a State may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant that maintains "certain minimum contacts" with the forum such that suit does not offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *Id.* at 316. The touchstone is not mechanical causation but the defendant's own conduct in "purposefully avail[ing] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws." *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472 (1985) (quoting *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958)).

Critically, the governing standard has always been phrased in the disjunctive: a claim must "arise out of *or* relate to" the defendant's forum contacts. *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984). Ford's proposed "causation" test collapses that disjunctive standard into a single, narrower "but-for" requirement found nowhere in this Court's precedent. Nothing in *International Shoe* or its progeny demands that a plaintiff prove the specific unit of product that injured her was itself sold, designed, or manufactured within the forum. Rather, the inquiry asks whether the defendant's contacts with the forum are such that it should reasonably anticipate being subject to suit there on claims of the type presented. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (jurisdiction is proper where a defendant "delivers its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State").

## II. Ford's Extensive, Purposeful Activities in Montana Relate Directly to the Claims at Issue.

Ford does not merely place vehicles into a national stream of commerce and hope they wash up somewhere. Ford advertises, sells, and services Explorers—the very model at issue—throughout Montana. It maintains dealerships that sell new and used Explorers, and it distributes replacement parts, including tires and components like those alleged to have failed here, to those same Montana dealers and service centers. This is precisely the kind of "continuous and systematic" solicitation of business within the forum, directed at the very product line giving rise to the suit, that satisfies purposeful availment. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 476 (jurisdiction proper where defendant's connection with forum "proximately result[s] from actions by the defendant himself that create a substantial connection with the forum State").

This Court has already recognized that a defendant who "purposefully avails [it]self of the benefits of an economic market in [a] state" may permissibly be subject to that state's jurisdiction, even where the market activity is not the precise transaction underlying the plaintiff's claim. *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 781 (1984). Ford's Montana sales of Explorers, and its marketing of the Explorer brand to Montana consumers, gave Ford every reason to anticipate that a Montana resident might be injured by an Explorer's alleged defect while driving on a Montana highway—exactly what happened to Ms. Gullett. Ford's contacts thus "relate to" the claims even though the particular vehicle first entered commerce out of state.

## III. Montana Has a Powerful Interest in Adjudicating Claims by Its Own Residents Injured Within Its Borders.

This Court has long recognized that the forum State's interest in providing redress to its own citizens is a central consideration in the specific-jurisdiction analysis. In *World-Wide Volkswagen*, the Court explained that a State "has a manifest interest in providing effective means of redress for its residents" who are injured by defective products. 444 U.S. at 297. Montana's interest here is undiminished by the vehicle's circuitous path to the state: Ms. Gullett was a Montana resident, was injured on a Montana road, and died as a result. Litigating her estate's claims in Montana—where the accident occurred, where evidence and witnesses are located, and where Ford itself conducts substantial business—offends no notion of fair play. *Asahi Metal Indus. Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987) (fairness factors include "the interstate judicial system's interest in obtaining the most efficient resolution of controversies" and "the forum State's interest in adjudicating the dispute").

## IV. Bristol-Myers Squibb Does Not Bar Jurisdiction Here.

Ford's reliance on *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 137 S. Ct. 1773 (2017), is misplaced. There, the Court held that California lacked jurisdiction over claims by *nonresident* plaintiffs who were neither injured in California nor treated there, emphasizing that "[w]hat is needed" is "a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue." *Id.* at 1781. That connection is present here in full: a Montana resident, injured in Montana, suing a defendant that markets and sells the very product line at issue throughout Montana. *Bristol-Myers* confirms, rather than forecloses, jurisdiction in this case.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
