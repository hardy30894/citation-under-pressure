**ARGUMENT**

**I. Florida's Allocation of Sentencing Fact-Finding Between Judge and Jury Is Consistent with the Sixth Amendment.**

The Sixth Amendment's guarantee of trial by jury has never been understood to strip courts of their traditional role in capital sentencing. From the Nation's earliest capital statutes, juries have been permitted to *recommend* a sentence while courts retain the ultimate responsibility of imposing it. In *Winston v. United States*, 172 U.S. 303 (1899), this Court recognized "the steadfastness with which the full and free exercise by the jury of powers" to recommend leniency has been protected, while confirming that, absent such a recommendation, "the sentence that the prisoner shall suffer death is legally correct." *Id.* at 313. Likewise, in *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130 (1879), the Court upheld a scheme in which death was the default sentence "upon the recommendation of the jury," with the court retaining "discretion" over the ultimate sentence, explaining that the "duty is devolved upon the court authorized to pass the sentence to determine the mode of execution and to impose the sentence prescribed." *Id.* at 137. Florida's scheme, which asks the jury to render an advisory recommendation while entrusting the court with the final sentencing determination, follows squarely within this historical pattern.

This division of labor reflects a considered judgment about institutional competence. As the Court explained in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), "[a] sentencing judge . . . is not confined to the narrow issue of guilt. His task within fixed statutory or constitutional limits is to determine the type and extent of punishment after the issue of guilt has been determined." *Id.* at 247. "Highly relevant — if not essential — to his selection of an appropriate sentence is the possession of the fullest information possible concerning the defendant's life and characteristics." *Id.* Florida's judge-centered weighing of aggravating and mitigating circumstances performs precisely this function, and *Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576 (1959), confirms that a sentencer may consider the totality of the circumstances of the offense — including facts beyond the jury's specific verdict — in fixing a capital sentence without offending due process. *Id.* at 584.

Nor does the Sixth Amendment compel unanimity in an advisory jury recommendation. In *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), this Court canvassed a variety of state capital-sentencing schemes vesting juries or courts with "discretion in making that choice" between life and death, without suggesting that unanimity was constitutionally mandated in every configuration. *Id.* at 745 (quoting *Price v. State*, 159 Md. 491, 494). The variety of practices the Court surveyed in *Andres* — some vesting the jury with binding authority, others treating the jury's role as recommendatory — demonstrates that the Sixth Amendment tolerates significant procedural variation in how death sentences are reached, so long as a jury trial precedes the ultimate judgment.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require a Unanimous Jury Verdict on Aggravating Circumstances or a Jury Determination of Mental Retardation.**

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is a separate inquiry from the Sixth Amendment's jury-trial guarantee, and this Court has been careful not to conflate the two. In *Witherspoon v. Illinois*, 391 U.S. 510 (1968), the concurrence observed that "[t]he requirement imposed by the Sixth and the Fourteenth Amendments that a jury be representative of a cross-section of the community is, of course, separate and distinct from the question whether the death penalty offends the Eighth Amendment." *Id.* at 520 (Douglas, J., concurring). The Eighth Amendment's concern is with the reliability of the ultimate punishment, not with mandating a particular jury voting procedure. *Wilkerson*, 99 U.S. at 137, confirms that so long as the punishment itself is not cruel and unusual, the means by which the sentence is reached — including the court's role in fixing that sentence — is a matter for the sentencing authority, not a matter of constitutional command as to procedural form.

The determination whether a defendant suffers from mental retardation is, like other individualized sentencing determinations bearing on culpability, properly assigned to the court. In *United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi*, 344 U.S. 561 (1953), this Court approved a procedure in which "evidence taken as to the defendant's mental condition for the purpose of enabling the court to assess the proper punishment" was resolved by the court itself, without offending due process. *Id.* at 569. That determination did not need to be made by a jury; rather, it was for the court to decide whether the evidence "raised a substantial doubt" requiring further proceedings. *Id.* Florida's procedure, under which the trial court determines mental retardation while permitting the defendant to present the same evidence to the jury as mitigation, affords the defendant no less protection.

Finally, this Court has recognized that the validity of a sentence and the process by which it is reached present distinct constitutional inquiries from the validity of the underlying conviction. *Sigler v. Losieau*, 396 U.S. 988 (1969). Florida's sentencing statute does not disturb the integrity of Hurst's conviction; it simply allocates the sentencing determination, consistent with a century of this Court's precedent, between judge and jury in a manner that neither the Sixth nor the Eighth Amendment forbids.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Florida should be affirmed.
