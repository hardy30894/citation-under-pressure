**ARGUMENT**

**I. STANDING TO ENFORCE THE ARBITRATION AWARD**

Bryant has standing to seek enforcement of the arbitration award under the Federal Arbitration Act ("FAA"), 9 U.S.C. § 1 et seq. The FAA "creates a body of federal substantive law establishing and regulating the duty to honor an agreement to arbitrate." *Moses H. Cone Mem'l Hosp. v. Mercury Constr. Corp.*, 460 U.S. 1, 24 (1983). The Supreme Court has held that the FAA "leaves no place for the exercise of discretion by a district court, but instead mandates that district courts shall direct the parties to proceed to arbitration in accordance with the terms of the agreement." *Dean Witter Reynolds Inc. v. Byrd*, 470 U.S. 213, 218 (1985). The arbitration award in this case was issued pursuant to a collective bargaining agreement ("CBA") between Bell Atlantic and the CWA, which incorporated the FAA. Thus, Bryant, as the prevailing party in arbitration, has standing to seek its enforcement in federal court. *See id.* at 221 ("By agreeing to arbitrate, a party 'trades the procedures and opportunity for review of the courtroom for the simplicity, informality, and expedition of arbitration.'") (quoting *Mitsubishi Motors Corp. v. Soler Chrysler-Plymouth, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 614, 628 (1985)).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO APPLY THE SUPREME COURT’S CLARIFICATION OF THE BURDEN-SHIFTING STANDARD IN TITLE VII AND SECTION 1981 CASES**

Bryant contends that the district court erred in failing to apply the Supreme Court’s clarification of the burden-shifting standard in *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133 (2000). In *Reeves*, the Court held that a plaintiff’s prima facie case, combined with sufficient evidence to reject the employer’s legitimate, nondiscriminatory explanation for its actions, may permit a rational factfinder to conclude that the employer intentionally discriminated. *Id.* at 148. The Court explained that "a plaintiff's prima facie case, combined with sufficient evidence to find that the employer's asserted justification is false, may permit the trier of fact to conclude that the employer unlawfully discriminated." *Id.* at 147-48.

Here, Bryant presented evidence that Bell Atlantic’s stated reason for his termination—repeated insubordination—was pretextual. Bryant’s evidence included: (1) Bell Atlantic’s refusal to accommodate his child care responsibilities despite the CWA’s request; (2) the inconsistent application of the Red Letter Day policy, as Bryant was disciplined for conduct that had previously gone unpunished; and (3) the temporal proximity between Bryant’s complaints about the policy and his termination. *See id.* at 147 ("[A] plaintiff's prima facie case, combined with sufficient evidence to find that the employer's asserted justification is false, may permit the trier of fact to conclude that the employer unlawfully discriminated."). The district court erred in failing to apply *Reeves* and in granting summary judgment on Bryant’s discrimination and retaliation claims.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT REMAIN IN DISPUTE**

Summary judgment is inappropriate where, as here, there are genuine issues of material fact. Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c); *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 247-48 (1986). The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Bryant’s discrimination and retaliation claims because there are genuine disputes of material fact regarding Bell Atlantic’s intent and the legitimacy of its proffered reasons for termination.

First, Bryant presented evidence that Bell Atlantic’s stated reason for his termination—insubordination—was pretextual. *See Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 147-48. Second, Bryant’s evidence of disparate treatment—his prior unpunished refusals to work overtime due to child care responsibilities—creates a genuine dispute of fact regarding whether Bell Atlantic’s actions were motivated by discriminatory or retaliatory animus. *See id.* at 148 ("[A] plaintiff's prima facie case, combined with sufficient evidence to find that the employer's asserted justification is false, may permit the trier of fact to conclude that the employer unlawfully discriminated."). Third, the temporal proximity between Bryant’s complaints about the Red Letter Day policy and his termination supports an inference of retaliation. *See Clark County Sch. Dist. v. Breeden*, 532 U.S. 268, 273 (2001) (per curiam) ("[P]roximity in time between an employer's knowledge of protected activity and an adverse employment action can be sufficient to establish causality.").

The district court’s grant of summary judgment was erroneous because these genuine disputes of material fact preclude summary judgment. *See Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 247-48.
