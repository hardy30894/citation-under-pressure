ARGUMENT

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of § 1255. That conferral constitutes an “admission” under § 1255(a). The Third Circuit’s contrary holding rests on an artificial distinction between “admission” and “status” that the statute does not support.

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). When Congress grants TPS, it necessarily authorizes the alien’s continued presence through inspection by the Secretary. See Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017). The grant therefore supplies the “lawful entry . . . after inspection and authorization” that § 1101(a)(13)(A) requires. Id.

Statutory context confirms the point. Section 1255(k)(1) permits adjustment when an alien is “present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission.” TPS satisfies that condition because § 1254a(f)(4) places the recipient in lawful nonimmigrant status. See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (“where Congress includes particular language in one section . . . but omits it in another . . . it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally”). Congress knew how to exempt classes from the admission requirement, as it did for special immigrants under § 1255(h) and (i); it chose not to do so for TPS recipients. That choice is dispositive. See Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004).

The Third Circuit’s reliance on Hanif v. Attorney General, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), and Taveras v. Attorney General, 731 F.3d 281 (3d Cir. 2013), is misplaced. Those decisions addressed adjustment of status itself, not the distinct question whether a subsequent statutory grant of nonimmigrant status can constitute admission. See Gomez v. Lynch, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016) (distinguishing the two concepts). Nothing in the text of § 1254a(f)(4) limits its effect to post-admission maintenance of status.

The Third Circuit also erred in treating De Leon-Ochoa v. Attorney General, 622 F.3d 341 (3d Cir. 2010), as controlling. That case held only that TPS is not itself a program of entry; it did not address whether the grant of TPS satisfies § 1255(a)’s inspection-and-admission predicate once the alien is already present. See Saliba v. Attorney General, 828 F.3d 182, 186 (3d Cir. 2016) (recognizing that lawfully admitted nonimmigrants may obtain TPS).

Finally, the agency’s 1991 opinion letter is not entitled to deference. See Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984). The statute’s plain text controls, and that text equates TPS with lawful nonimmigrant status usable for adjustment. The judgment should be reversed.
