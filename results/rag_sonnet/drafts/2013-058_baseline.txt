# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF §522(b)(3)(C) AND (d)(12) REQUIRES THAT EXEMPT FUNDS BE "RETIREMENT FUNDS," A STATUS THE ACCOUNT HERE LACKS

Sections 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code exempt "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under" specified sections of the Internal Revenue Code. This Court has recognized that exemption provisions must be construed according to their function—asking what the asset actually *is* and how it operates in the debtor's hands, not merely how it is labeled. *See Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320 (2005) (examining whether IRA distributions functioned as payments "on account of illness, disability, death, age, or length of service" within the meaning of §522(d)(10)(E)).

The inherited IRA at issue fails this functional test. As the Seventh Circuit correctly held, "by the time the Clarks filed for bankruptcy, the money in the inherited IRA did not represent anyone's retirement funds." *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 562 (7th Cir. 2013). The Internal Revenue Code strips inherited IRAs of every attribute that makes an account a retirement vehicle: no new contributions may be made, the account cannot be rolled over, and—critically—distribution must begin "within a year of the original owner's death," with payout often required within five years. *Id.* The account is, in the Seventh Circuit's words, "a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner's retirement." *Id.*

This Court's decision in *Barker v. Kansas*, 503 U.S. 594 (1992), illustrates the proper method of analysis: courts must look past labels to the functional character of the benefit—there, examining whether military retirement pay constituted "reduced pay for reduced current services" or "deferred compensation for past services." *Id.* at 594. The same functional inquiry compels the conclusion that an inherited IRA, structured by Congress to force rapid liquidation, is not "retirement funds" for the beneficiary who inherits it.

## II. THE STATUTORY STRUCTURE CONFIRMS THAT EXEMPTIONS ATTACH TO THE DEBTOR'S PRESENT USE OF PROPERTY, NOT ITS HISTORICAL PEDIGREE

Petitioners' position—that funds retain "retirement" status forever because they originated in a retirement account—cannot be reconciled with how parallel exemptions operate throughout §522. The homestead exemption under §522(b)(3)(A) protects a "domicile" only if the debtor lived there for the statutorily required period; a house inherited and used as a rental property does not qualify merely because it was the decedent's home. The Seventh Circuit's analogy is instructive: "Exemption would depend on how Heidi used the property, not how her mother used it. Just so with retirement funds." *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564.

This Court's admonition in *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522 (1987), applies directly: "no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs," and "it frustrates rather than effectuates legislative intent simplistically to assume that whatever furthers the statute's primary objective must be the law." *Id.* at 525–26. Petitioners' argument—that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage savings and larger bequests—proves too much. As the Seventh Circuit observed, that logic "would equally" justify exempting "any asset acquired from one's relatives by will, insurance, annuity, or survivorship designation." *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564. Congress plainly did not intend such boundless exemption; it enacted "a specific exemption for retirement funds," and inherited IRAs do not qualify because they are not "savings reserved for use after their owners stop working." *Id.*

## III. TREATING INHERITED IRAS AS EXEMPT WOULD CONVERT A NARROW TAX-DEFERRAL MECHANISM INTO AN UNLIMITED ASSET-PROTECTION DEVICE

The Internal Revenue Code's treatment of inherited IRAs serves a specific, limited purpose: allowing beneficiaries to defer immediate taxation while ensuring that deferral does not become permanent avoidance. As the Seventh Circuit explained, "If recipients of inherited IRAs could hold the wealth until their own retirement, tax deferral might become tax exemption, as capital held in IRAs could pass down through the generations without ever being subject to income tax." *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 561.

Bankruptcy exemptions exist to protect a fresh start for debtors with respect to their *own* retirement savings—not to shield inherited windfalls from legitimate creditor claims. This Court's decision in *Patterson v. Shumate*, 504 U.S. 753 (1992), addressed the treatment of retirement plan interests in bankruptcy by examining the actual restrictions Congress imposed on the funds at issue—there, ERISA's anti-alienation provision. No comparable restriction protects inherited IRAs; to the contrary, Congress mandated their rapid distribution, the opposite of retirement protection.

The hypothetical posed by the Seventh Circuit resolves any doubt: had Ruth Heffron withdrawn her IRA funds and gifted them to Heidi before death, no one would contend those funds remained "retirement funds" in Heidi's hands. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. The fortuity of the funds passing at death rather than shortly before cannot transform ordinary inherited wealth into an exempt retirement account.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
