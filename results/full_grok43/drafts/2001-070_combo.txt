ARGUMENT

The Eighth Amendment forbids only those punishments that were considered cruel and unusual at the adoption of the Bill of Rights or that involve unnecessary infliction of pain. Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1878). Capital punishment for murder was not so regarded. In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890). The Amendment therefore permits the death penalty when the crime is murder and the offender possesses sufficient understanding to be held responsible. O'Neil v. Vermont, 144 U.S. 323, 339-40 (1892).

Mental deficiency short of insanity has never been held to bar criminal liability or its prescribed punishment. See Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 535-36 (1968) (chronic alcoholism does not excuse public intoxication). The common-law test of responsibility turned on capacity to know right from wrong, not on measured intelligence. See Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895). Mild mental retardation does not meet that test. Consequently, execution of such an offender cannot be deemed a punishment unknown to the law when the Eighth Amendment was adopted.

The Amendment also prohibits punishments that are excessive in relation to the offense. Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910). Death remains proportionate to murder even when the offender's intelligence is below average. Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660, 667 (1962) (status alone may not be punished, but conduct may). Virginia's statute requires proof of intent and deliberation; those elements supply the necessary moral culpability.

Nor does the method of execution become cruel because of the offender's mental condition. Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947). The State employs the same procedure for all capital defendants. Any subjective apprehension arising from limited intelligence does not transform an otherwise lawful penalty into one that offends the Constitution. Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 99 (1958) (plurality opinion) (cruelty measured by objective standards).

Finally, the judgment of the Virginia legislature that mentally retarded offenders may be executed for capital murder is entitled to substantial deference. See Ex parte Wells, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 307, 311 (1855). Courts do not sit as super-legislatures to revise the range of punishments the people have authorized through their representatives. Pervear v. Massachusetts, 72 U.S. (5 Wall.) 475, 480 (1866). Because the sentence imposed on Atkins conforms to these settled principles, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
