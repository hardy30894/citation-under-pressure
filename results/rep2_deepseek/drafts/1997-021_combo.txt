**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE BOARD'S "GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT" STANDARD FOR EMPLOYER POLLING IS IRRATIONAL AND INCONSISTENT WITH THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.**

The National Labor Relations Act nowhere prohibits an employer from polling its employees. The Act's fundamental purpose is to promote industrial peace through an accurate determination of employee majority status. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 33-34 (1937). An employer's duty to bargain arises only with respect to a union that in fact represents a majority of employees. *Medo Photo Supply Corp. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 678, 686-87 (1944). Where legitimate uncertainty exists about that majority, the employer must possess a lawful, non-coercive mechanism to ascertain the truth. Polling, conducted under procedural safeguards that ensure secrecy and non-coercion, is precisely such a mechanism. *See NLRB v. Virginia Electric & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. 469, 477-78 (1941) (employer may ascertain union sentiment absent coercion).

The Board's standard—permitting a poll only when the employer already possesses sufficient objective evidence to withdraw recognition—renders polling wholly illusory. If the employer had such evidence, it could simply withdraw recognition; no poll would be needed. Conversely, an employer who lacks conclusive evidence but entertains a sincere "good faith doubt" is punished for attempting to test that doubt. This anomaly has been recognized by the Fifth and Ninth Circuits. *NLRB v. Tex-Tan, Inc.*, 318 F.2d 472, 480 (5th Cir. 1963); *NLRB v. Yutana Barge Lines, Inc.*, 315 F.2d 524, 529 (9th Cir. 1963). Those courts correctly held that an employer with a good-faith doubt, supported by substantial objective evidence, may poll employees to resolve it. *Tex-Tan*, 318 F.2d at 480; *Yutana*, 315 F.2d at 529.

This Court has instructed that the Board must balance the legitimate interests of employers and employees. *Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793, 797-98 (1945). The Board's rule tips that balance decisively against the employer. The Board's stated rationale—that a poll after a valid doubt provides "certainty"—is circular. An employer with a reasonable doubt cannot safely withdraw recognition without risking an unfair-labor-practice finding if its assessment is wrong. A poll is the only means of resolving that uncertainty without risking a violation of the Act. By requiring a threshold of evidence sufficient for withdrawal, the Board forbids the very poll that would supply the certainty it professes to value. The standard is therefore irrational and cannot be harmonized with the Act.

**II. EVEN UNDER THE BOARD'S OWN STANDARD, THE FACTUAL FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED A REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.**

This Court's review of Board factual findings is limited to whether they are supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). That standard obliges the Court to consider "whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." *Id.* Here, the Board disregarded overwhelming, unrefuted objective evidence of the union's loss of majority support.

The Board counted only seven employees who made statements repudiating the union. Yet it improperly discounted the statement of union steward Mohr, who reported that employees were dissatisfied and that the union would lose an election, dismissing it as an "off-the-cuff" remark. No witness contradicted Mohr. The Board likewise rejected Bloch's report that the entire night shift disfavored the union. Unrebutted, internally consistent testimony may not be cast aside without adequate explanation. *NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 350 (1938).

The Board also refused to count statements made by Ridgick and Marsh during job interviews, invoking a presumption that such statements are insincere because the employer had indicated it might be non-union. That presumption lacks support in the record. The employees had no reason to anticipate a poll or to curtsy to the employer; they simply expressed their genuine views about union representation. *See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 609 (1969) (employer may act on a "good faith doubt" as to the union's majority status based on objective considerations). The Board's decision rests on an improper application of its own precedent and an unsupported factual inference. The poll itself, supervised by a neutral priest with secret ballots, was procedurally flawless.

The Board's standard disserves the Act's cardinal goal of promoting accurate, peaceful resolution of representation questions. *Jones & Laughlin*, 301 U.S. at 42-43. This Court should reverse.
