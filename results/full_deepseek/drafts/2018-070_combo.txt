**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), adopts state law as surrogate federal law only when necessary to fill a gap in federal law. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent”—misreads the statute and conflicts with this Court’s precedent.

**I. The OCSLA Incorporates State Law Only to Fill Voids in Federal Law**

The OCSLA’s text and history confirm that state law supplies a “body of law” where federal law is deficient. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). Congress recognized that federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). Accordingly, the Act borrows state law only “[t]o the extent that” it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent that” is a limiting qualifier: state law fills the interstices of federal law, not every subject on which state law touches.

This Court in *Rodrigue* applied that principle. There, federal admiralty law did not cover deaths on fixed platforms, so state wrongful-death law filled the gap. 395 U.S. at 366. The Court did not ask whether state law was merely “not inconsistent” with the Death on the High Seas Act; it asked whether federal law provided a remedy. *Id.* at 359–60. Because it did not, state law stepped in. That is the classic gap-filling model.

The Fifth Circuit has long applied this test. *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969) (state law applies only when “necessary to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law). That decision, predating the 1975 amendment, remains authoritative. The Ninth Circuit erred in rejecting it.

**II. The FLSA Leaves No Gap for State Wage-and-Hour Law**

The Fair Labor Standards Act is a comprehensive statutory scheme regulating wages, hours, and overtime. It contains detailed provisions, administrative regulations, and an express savings clause preserving more protective state law *within the states*. 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). But that savings clause does not extend the FLSA’s reach to the OCS; it merely preserves state law where it otherwise applies. On the OCS, the FLSA itself supplies the governing federal law. There is no “void or gap” for state law to fill.

Federal enclave precedent confirms this. When the United States acquires exclusive jurisdiction over land, state law ceases to operate unless Congress expressly adopts it. *James v. Dravo Contracting Co.*, 302 U.S. 134, 142 (1937); *Collins v. Yosemite Park & Curry Co.*, 304 U.S. 518, 528 (1938). The OCSLA creates a similar federal enclave. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1) (treating OCS as “an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction”). State law does not apply of its own force; it applies only as adopted federal law. *Pacific Coast Dairy v. Department of Agriculture*, 318 U.S. 285, 294 (1943) (state milk regulations did not apply on federal enclave because federal law occupied the field). Here, the FLSA occupies the wage-and-hour field, leaving no room for California law.

**III. The “Not Inconsistent” Standard Does Not Authorize Concurrent State Law**

The Ninth Circuit read “not inconsistent” as a permissive standard, allowing state law to supplement federal law whenever it does not conflict. That reading conflates preemption analysis with the OCSLA’s choice-of-law rule. Under ordinary preemption principles, state law may supplement federal law if not inconsistent. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941); *Florida Lime & Avocado Growers, Inc. v. Paul*, 373 U.S. 132, 142 (1963). But the OCSLA is not a preemption statute; it is a delegation of legislative authority. *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 293 (1958) (upholding prospective adoption of state law under federal enclave statute). The delegation is limited: state law applies only “to the extent” federal law is deficient. The “not inconsistent” clause is a separate condition, not an independent grant of authority.

If the Ninth Circuit were correct, state law would apply on the OCS whenever it is not preempted—even if federal law already covers the subject. That would displace uniform federal regulation with a patchwork of state laws, contrary to Congress’s intent. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 365 (emphasizing need for “a body of law” that is “certain and predictable”).

**IV. The Savings Clause Does Not Alter the OCSLA Analysis**

The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), states that the Act does not “excuse” compliance with more protective state law. But that clause operates only where state law already applies—i.e., within the states. On the OCS, state law does not apply unless adopted under the OCSLA. The savings clause cannot override the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision. *See Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. at 294 (federal enclave statute controls over general savings clauses).

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA adopts state law only to fill gaps in federal law. The FLSA is comprehensive and leaves no gap for California wage-and-hour law. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
