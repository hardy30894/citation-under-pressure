**Argument**

The judgment below should be affirmed. Under settled First Amendment doctrine, Massachusetts’s buffer-zone law regulates speech in a quintessential public forum only as to time, place, and manner; it is justified without reference to the content of speech, serves significant governmental interests, and leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941); *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147 (1939); *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939). The Commonwealth’s interest in protecting access to reproductive-health clinics and ensuring public safety on narrow sidewalks and driveways is more than sufficient. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574; *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940).

I. **The First Circuit correctly upheld the statute as a valid time, place, and manner regulation.**

The Court has long recognized that the State may impose reasonable restrictions on expression in public places so long as it does not suppress ideas because of disagreement with the speaker’s viewpoint. *Hague*, 307 U.S. at 515–16; *Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 160. In *Cox*, the Court upheld licensing and parade regulations designed to maintain public order, emphasizing that the State may protect the use of streets and sidewalks by all citizens. 312 U.S. at 574–76. Likewise, in *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), the Court held that government may control access to its property and preserve the property for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated. Id. at 47–48. Massachusetts’s statute serves the same legitimate end: ensuring safe ingress and egress at clinic entrances, exits, and driveways while leaving speakers on the sidewalks and public ways outside the marked zones.

The petitioners remain free to counsel, pray, distribute literature, display signs, and converse with willing listeners outside the buffer zone. The First Amendment has never guaranteed the right to speak at the precise location, distance, or volume preferred by the speaker. *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395 (1953); *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558 (1948). Here, the Commonwealth has not silenced petitioners; it has regulated the physical manner in which speech may occur at a congested and sensitive site. That is constitutionally permissible. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 576; *Adderley*, 385 U.S. at 47–48.

Petitioners contend that the law burdens their counseling because close, face-to-face contact is more effective. But the Constitution does not require the State to adopt the least restrictive alternative whenever it regulates speech in a public forum. The Court has upheld reasonable restrictions that leave speakers “ample alternative channels” for communication. *Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 160; *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 575–76. Massachusetts has done so here.

II. **The law is content-neutral and does not discriminate based on viewpoint.**

A regulation is not rendered unconstitutional merely because it applies in a setting where particular speech is common. The relevant inquiry is whether the law targets speech because of disagreement with its message. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303–04; *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1 (1949). This statute is keyed to location, not message. It applies to all persons in the buffer zone regardless of whether they support or oppose abortion, and its exemptions are functional, not ideological. See *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574–76. The First Circuit correctly treated the law as content-neutral.

III. **Hill should not be overruled; to the extent the Court reaches it, the decision should be limited.**

If the Court concludes that *Hill v. Colorado* applies, respondent submits that it should be left intact or, at most, confined to its facts. But the Court need not revisit any broader question to affirm here. The traditional public-forum cases already sustain this statute. *Hague*, 307 U.S. 496; *Schneider*, 308 U.S. 147; *Cox*, 312 U.S. 569. Those authorities are enough.

In any event, stare decisis is at its strongest where a decision has long governed the conduct of legislatures and lower courts. *Burnet v. Coronado Oil & Gas Co.*, 285 U.S. 393 (1932). Overruling should be reserved for cases of clear error and unworkable administration. *Smith v. Allwright*, 321 U.S. 649 (1944). Nothing in this record warrants that extraordinary step. The First Circuit correctly applied established First Amendment principles to a narrow access-protection statute, and the judgment should be affirmed.
