**Argument**

The Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power. Nor did the Attorney General exceed his authority in assigning temporary scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator. The judgment should be affirmed.

I. **Congress supplied an intelligible principle for temporary scheduling.**

Article I permits Congress to obtain assistance from another branch so long as it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of delegated authority. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress identified the policy, the governing factors, and the objective to be achieved. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372-79 (1989); *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426-27 (1944); *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947).

Section 811(h) comfortably meets that standard. Congress authorized temporary scheduling only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” and it limited the Attorney General to consideration of enumerated statutory factors addressing abuse history, scope, duration, significance, and public-health risk. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3); see *Touby v. United States*, 500 U.S. 160, 165-67 (1991) (temporary scheduling upheld). Congress further imposed procedural safeguards: notice in the Federal Register and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). This is far more constrained than the delegations sustained in *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373-79, and *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 426-27.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases invalidated statutes that left the executive with effectively unbounded authority and no meaningful policy standard. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415; *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 541-42. Section 811(h) is the opposite: Congress specified both the trigger for action and the criteria for decision, and it confined temporary orders to a short duration. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2), (3).

The fact that temporary scheduling may support criminal enforcement does not alter the analysis. The Court has upheld delegations even when regulated conduct carried criminal consequences. *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 427-28; *Touby*, 500 U.S. at 165-66. Congress may define crimes through a scheme that includes executive implementation, so long as it has itself made the essential policy choice. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 371-79.

II. **Congress validly authorized subdelegation to the DEA Administrator.**

The Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA was authorized by statute. Congress provided that “[t]he Attorney General may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). That text plainly permits subdelegation to the DEA Administrator, an officer within the Department of Justice. *Touby*, 500 U.S. at 163-64, 169-70. Where Congress expressly authorizes delegation within an executive department, courts enforce that authorization according to its terms. *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 514-17 (1974) (looking to statutory text to determine whether subdelegation is authorized); *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 694-96 (1974).

Nor is there any constitutional obstacle to that internal allocation of responsibilities. The Court has long recognized that executive authority may be exercised through subordinate officers when Congress or the President has lawfully assigned it. *United States v. Eaton*, 169 U.S. 331, 343-44 (1898); *Morrison v. Olson*, 487 U.S. 654, 691-93 (1988). The DEA Administrator acted as the Attorney General’s delegate within the Department, not as an independent lawmaker.

III. **Judicial review and procedural safeguards further confirm constitutionality.**

The Court has often considered the availability of judicial review and other safeguards in upholding delegations. *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952); *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374-75. Here, Congress required notice, HHS consultation, and limited duration. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2), (4). Those protections reduce any risk of arbitrary action and confirm that Congress, not the executive, made the governing policy judgment. *Touby*, 500 U.S. at 167-68.

Petitioners’ broader separation-of-powers argument likewise fails. The nondelegation doctrine has rarely been used to invalidate statutes, and the Court has “upheld every congressional delegation” challenged since 1935 except the two extraordinary New Deal cases. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373-79. This statute falls well within the constitutional mainstream.

**Conclusion**

Because Congress gave the Attorney General an intelligible principle, because Congress expressly authorized internal delegation within the Department of Justice, and because the DEA acted pursuant to validly delegated authority, the Court should affirm the convictions.
