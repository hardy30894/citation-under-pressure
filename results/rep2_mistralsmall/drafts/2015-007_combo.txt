**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

The Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee, as applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, does not require that a jury unanimously find every fact necessary to impose the death penalty. *McGautha v. California*, 402 U.S. 183, 208 (1971) ("[T]he Constitution does not require that the jury make specific factual findings in order to impose the death penalty."); *see also Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 247 (1949) ("The belief no longer persists that the penalty of death differs from other punishments in its jury trial requirements."). In *McGautha*, the Court upheld California’s death sentencing scheme, which permitted the jury to impose death without unanimous findings of aggravating factors or a unanimous sentencing recommendation. 402 U.S. at 186-87. The Court reasoned that the Sixth Amendment does not "dictate the precise manner in which the trial judge and jury must determine the existence of the circumstances warranting the death penalty." *Id.* at 208. Florida’s scheme, like California’s, entrusts the jury with an advisory role, leaving the final sentencing determination to the trial judge. *Hurst v. State*, 202 So. 3d 42, 60 (Fla. 2016) (citing *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 465 (1984)). This comports with the Sixth Amendment, which does not mandate jury unanimity or specific factual findings for sentencing. *McGautha*, 402 U.S. at 208.

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

The Eighth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in capital sentencing. *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 207 (1976) (plurality opinion) ("[T]he Constitution does not require that the [sentencing] determination [of death] be made by the unanimous concurrence of the jury."); *see also Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242, 252 (1976) (upholding Florida’s scheme where the jury’s role is advisory). The Court has repeatedly recognized that the Eighth Amendment permits states flexibility in structuring capital sentencing procedures, so long as they provide sufficient safeguards against arbitrary and capricious imposition of the death penalty. *Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 195-96 (citing *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 309-10 (1972) (White, J., concurring)). Florida’s scheme, which requires the trial judge to find aggravating factors and weigh them against mitigating evidence, satisfies this requirement. *Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 465.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require that a jury determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 340 (1989) ("[T]he Constitution does not require that a jury make the ultimate determination that a defendant is mentally retarded."). Florida’s scheme permits the defendant to present mental retardation evidence as a mitigating factor, and the trial judge may consider it in imposing sentence. *Hurst*, 202 So. 3d at 60. This is consistent with the Eighth Amendment, which does not mandate specific procedures for evaluating mental retardation claims. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 340.

**III. FLORIDA’S SCHEME IS CONSISTENT WITH HISTORICAL PRACTICE AND THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT.**

Historically, American jurisdictions have not required jury unanimity in capital sentencing. *McGautha*, 402 U.S. at 198-99 (noting that colonial and early American juries often returned non-unanimous sentencing recommendations). This Court has long upheld Florida’s death sentencing scheme against constitutional challenges. *Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 465 ("Florida’s capital sentencing procedure is constitutional."); *Proffitt*, 428 U.S. at 252 (same). The Court has also recognized that the Eighth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in capital sentencing. *Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 207.

**IV. HURST’S CLAIMS ARE BARRED BY THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT.**

This Court has repeatedly rejected the argument that Florida’s death sentencing scheme violates the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. *Hurst*, 202 So. 3d at 60 (citing *Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 465); *see also State v. Dixon*, 283 So. 2d 1, 7 (Fla. 1973) ("The death penalty is not unconstitutional per se, nor is the procedure for imposing it."). Hurst’s claims are foreclosed by these decisions.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. The judgment below should be affirmed.
