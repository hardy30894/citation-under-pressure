**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court may certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis—requires this Court to reaffirm a foundational principle of class action practice: *Rule 23(b)(3) demands more than a mere possibility of common damages; it requires a showing, supported by admissible evidence, that damages can be proven through common evidence, not individualized inquiries.* The Third Circuit’s decision to affirm class certification in this case, based on speculative and legally insufficient evidence, violates this principle and undermines the Seventh Amendment right to a jury trial. The District Court’s recertification order, which rested on a damages methodology that cannot be applied uniformly across the class, must be reversed.

### I. The District Court’s Certification of the Class Was Based on a Flawed and Inadequate Damages Methodology

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to the class members predominate” over individual issues. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. 2541, 2551 (2011). This predominance requirement is not satisfied by a mere assertion that a common theory of harm exists. It demands a “common methodology” capable of measuring damages on a class-wide basis. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008). The District Court’s reliance on Dr. McClave’s model—based on a hypothetical “but-for” market in which Comcast had not engaged in clustering—fails this test.

Dr. McClave’s model assumes that, absent anticompetitive conduct, the Philadelphia DMA would have had a competitive market with multiple overbuilders. But the record contains no admissible evidence that such a market would have existed. The model rests on speculative assumptions about the behavior of overbuilders like RCN, including their willingness and ability to enter the market, their investment decisions, and their pricing strategies. These are not common questions; they are individualized inquiries into the conduct of third parties and the economic viability of hypothetical competitors. As this Court has held, “a class-wide damages model that depends on assumptions about the behavior of third parties or the existence of hypothetical markets is not a common methodology.” *Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. at 2552.

Moreover, the model cannot be applied uniformly. The damages to each class member depend on their individual subscription history, the specific programming packages they purchased, and the timing of their service. The District Court acknowledged this when it narrowed the class’s theory to “deterrence of overbuilder entry,” but even then, the damages calculation required individualized assessments of whether a particular subscriber would have benefited from lower prices had competition existed. This is precisely the kind of individualized inquiry that Rule 23(b)(3) prohibits. *Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. at 2552.

### II. The Third Circuit’s Affirmance Was Based on an Erroneous Legal Standard

The Third Circuit held that the District Court “did not exceed its permissible discretion” in certifying the class, relying on the “preponderance of evidence” standard. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 622 F.3d 215, 231 (3d Cir. 2010). But this standard is inapplicable to the predominance inquiry. *Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. at 2551. The predominance requirement is not a burden of proof that can be met by a preponderance of the evidence; it is a threshold requirement that must be satisfied *before* trial. The Court in *Dukes* made clear that “a class certification decision that rests on a merits inquiry into the existence of a common theory of harm is not a proper use of the Rule 23(b)(3) predominance requirement.” Id. at 2552.

The Third Circuit’s decision to treat the damages methodology as a “common evidence” issue, rather than a merits issue, is inconsistent with *Dukes*. The Court in *Dukes* rejected the idea that “a class-wide damages model that depends on assumptions about the behavior of third parties or the existence of hypothetical markets is not a common methodology.” Id. The Third Circuit’s conclusion that Dr. McClave’s model was “common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis” is legally erroneous. It conflates the *existence* of a damages theory with the *feasibility* of proving it through common evidence. That is not the law.

### III. The Certification Violates the Seventh Amendment

The Seventh Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial in civil cases where the amount in controversy exceeds $20. *Fernandez v. City of New York*, 557 U.S. 1001 (2009). When damages are not capable of class-wide measurement, the jury must assess damages on an individual basis. *Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. at 2552. To allow a class action where damages are not capable of common proof is to deprive class members of their right to a jury trial. The Third Circuit’s decision permits a class-wide trial on damages that cannot be proven through common evidence, thereby violating the Seventh Amendment.

### Conclusion

The District Court’s certification of the class was based on a damages methodology that cannot be applied on a class-wide basis. The Third Circuit’s affirmance was based on an erroneous legal standard and failed to recognize that the predominance requirement is not satisfied by speculative or individualized assumptions. The judgment must be reversed.
