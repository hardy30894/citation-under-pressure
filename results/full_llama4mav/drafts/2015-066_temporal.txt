The use of Michael Bryant, Jr.'s uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses for his conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment. Although the Sixth Amendment does not directly apply to tribal courts, its principles inform the analysis of whether an uncounseled conviction can be used to support a subsequent federal prosecution.

In Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), this Court held that a conviction obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment right to counsel cannot be used to trigger or enhance a sentence under a recidivist statute. The Court reasoned that to permit such use would "erode" the constitutional guarantee of the right to counsel. Id. at 115. Similarly, in this case, using Bryant's uncounseled tribal court convictions to satisfy the predicate offense requirement of § 117(a) undermines the Sixth Amendment's protection.

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this principle in United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir.1989), holding that prior tribal court misdemeanor convictions that do not provide an equivalent right to counsel cannot be used as evidence of guilt in a subsequent federal prosecution. This approach is consistent with the Sixth Amendment's core interest in reliability, as emphasized in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), which established that the right to counsel is essential to a fair trial.

Furthermore, the use of uncounseled convictions in this context is distinguishable from their use in sentencing enhancements, as was the case in Williams v. Oklahoma, 358 U.S. 576 (1959). In Williams, the Court allowed the consideration of prior convictions during sentencing, but the issue here involves the use of such convictions as an essential element of a federal offense.

Therefore, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed, as the use of Bryant's uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses for his § 117(a) conviction violates the Sixth Amendment.
