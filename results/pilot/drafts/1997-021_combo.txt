**ARGUMENT**

The National Labor Relations Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employer polling is irrational, inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act, and unsupported by substantial evidence. The Board’s rule—requiring an employer to possess “objective indications” sufficient to raise a “reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status before conducting a poll—creates a legal paradox: it permits an employer to conduct a poll only when it already has enough evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition, thereby rendering the poll superfluous. This standard is not only illogical but also contrary to settled precedent, which affirms that an employer may lawfully poll employees to test union support when it has “substantial, objective evidence” of a loss of that support, even if such evidence falls short of justifying immediate withdrawal. The Board’s contrary rule is arbitrary and capricious, and its factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The National Labor Relations Act does not mandate a rigid evidentiary threshold for polling. Rather, it entrusts the Board with the authority to “develop and apply national labor policy” in a manner consistent with the Act’s purposes. *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990) (citing *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 30 (1937)). The Board’s authority to interpret the Act includes the power to determine the conditions under which employer polling may be conducted. *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 131 (1944). In *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), the Board acknowledged that polling is a “potentially disruptive and unsettling” practice and thus must be carefully circumscribed. But the Board’s own reasoning in that case confirms that polling serves a legitimate purpose: to allow an employer to test the truth of a union’s claim of majority support when there is a genuine, if not yet conclusive, basis for doubt. *Id.* at 1063. The Board’s decision to restrict polling to cases where the employer already has “objective indications” raising a reasonable doubt is not only inconsistent with the purpose of polling but also undermines the very rationale for permitting it.

The Board’s standard is irrational because it renders polling functionally useless. If an employer already has sufficient objective evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition, then the poll adds no new information. The employer may simply cease recognition and avoid the risk of a Board-supervised election. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41 (1987). But if the employer lacks such evidence, the Board prohibits polling altogether. This creates a Catch-22: the employer cannot poll unless it already has enough evidence to justify withdrawal, but if it already has that evidence, polling is unnecessary. The Board’s rule thus eliminates the utility of polling as a tool for resolving uncertainty. This is not the kind of administrative construction the courts should defer to. *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. at 131.

The courts of appeals have long recognized that an employer may conduct a poll when it has “substantial, objective evidence” of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. In *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1139, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981), the court held that an employer may poll employees “if there is other substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support” and if the poll complies with procedural safeguards. *Id.* (quoting *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967)). The court emphasized that the purpose of polling is to “test the validity of the union’s claim of majority support,” and that the employer need not have “conclusive” evidence to justify the poll. *Id.* at 1145. The same principle was affirmed in *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984), where the court held that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for an employer in “sincere doubt” about the union’s majority status. *Id.* The Sixth Circuit reached the same conclusion in *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982), stating that an employer may poll “if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient in itself to justify withdrawal.” *Id.* These decisions reflect a consistent judicial understanding that polling is a permissible and rational tool for employers seeking to resolve uncertainty about union support.

The Board’s contrary rule is not only irrational but also unsupported by substantial evidence in the record. The ALJ and the Board found that only seven employees made statements repudiating the union, a number insufficient to raise a reasonable doubt. But this conclusion ignores the totality of the evidence.
