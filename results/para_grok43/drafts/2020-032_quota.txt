ARGUMENT

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that revision of the challenged policies mooted petitioners’ claims. A case becomes moot when intervening events deprive the parties of a live controversy. Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc., 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013); County of Los Angeles v. Davis, 440 U.S. 625, 631 (1979). Georgia Gwinnett College’s revised policies “unambiguously terminated” the prior restrictions and left “no reasonable basis to expect” their return. City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 n.10 (1982). Petitioners’ graduation and cessation of campus activity further eliminated any prospect of future injury. DeFunis v. Odegaard, 416 U.S. 312, 319-20 (1974); Lewis v. Continental Bank Corp., 494 U.S. 472, 477 (1990).

Nominal damages alone cannot preserve jurisdiction. Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs, 868 F.3d 1248, 1263-64 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc). Section 1983 plaintiffs must allege “actual injury” beyond the abstract value of a constitutional right. Memphis Community School Dist. v. Stachura, 477 U.S. 299, 308 (1986); Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247, 254 (1978). The complaint alleged only “irreparable harm” without identifying concrete loss, rendering the nominal-damages request insufficient to avoid mootness. Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 71 (1997). When a controversy ends, the proper disposition is vacatur of the lower-court judgment. United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950).

Petitioners’ belated request for compensatory damages was never pleaded and cannot retroactively create jurisdiction. The district court acted within its discretion in denying an eleventh-hour amendment sought only in opposition papers. Because no live controversy remains, the judgment of dismissal should be affirmed.
