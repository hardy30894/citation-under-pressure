**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Supplementing the Statutory Term "Shielding" With an Overbroad, Judge-Made Definition.**

Section 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) does not define "shielding from detection," and when Congress leaves a term of criminal art undefined, courts must resolve any resulting ambiguity in favor of the defendant, not the prosecution. As Chief Justice Marshall explained nearly two centuries ago, "[t]he rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself." *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820). That principle was reaffirmed in *Bell v. United States*, 349 U.S. 81 (1955), where the Court held that "when choice has to be made between two readings of what conduct Congress has made a crime, it is appropriate, before we choose the harsher alternative, to require that Congress should have spoken in language that is clear and definite." *Id.* at 83. The district court's supplemental instruction did the opposite: it broadened, rather than narrowed, the reach of an undefined felony term, over Ye's objection, at the precise moment the jury signaled confusion.

The danger of such judicial gap-filling is not merely academic. Due process forbids penal statutes so indefinite that citizens are left to guess at their commands. "No one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes. All are entitled to be informed as to what the State commands or forbids." *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939). The same fair-warning principle animates *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612 (1954), and *Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337 (1952), both of which recognize that a criminal statute must give "a person of ordinary intelligence" adequate notice of what conduct is forbidden. When Congress itself leaves a term undefined, a court's improvised gloss cannot supply more precision than Congress provided; if anything, courts risk manufacturing the very vagueness the Constitution forbids. The instruction here, by defining "shielding" without any textual mooring, effectively told the jury that ordinary acts of assistance to aliens—housing, transportation, employment—could satisfy the element, regardless of whether Ye specifically intended to prevent government detection.

That is precisely the danger the Supreme Court addressed in *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945). There, the Court sustained an otherwise indefinite criminal statute only because it required proof of a specific, willful intent to violate a defined right—a limitation that "narrows the scope" of an otherwise open-ended prohibition. *Id.* at 101–04. Likewise, in *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), the Court cautioned that criminal intent requirements are not mere surplusage; the "contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion." *Id.* at 250. A definition of "shielding" that omits or dilutes the specific-intent requirement—intent to prevent detection by the government—strips the statute of the very limitation that keeps it within constitutional bounds and consistent with the "willful" and purposeful character of the offense.

Finally, when a jury expresses confusion mid-deliberation, the trial court's duty is to answer with precision, not improvisation. "When a jury makes explicit its difficulties a trial judge should clear them away with concrete accuracy." *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 612–13 (1946). The court's response here did the opposite, supplying an expansive, unbounded definition that invited the jury to convict on conduct—ordinary employer assistance—that Congress never intended to criminalize as "shielding." That error was not harmless; it went to the heart of the only contested element of the felony count and requires reversal.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Ye Possessed the Specific Intent to Prevent the Government From Detecting His Illegal-Alien Employees.**

Even under the correct, narrower understanding of "shielding," the government's proof falls short. On appellate review, a "verdict must be sustained if there is substantial evidence, taking the view most favorable to the Government, to support it," *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942), but that standard does not permit a conviction where the evidence, even construed favorably to the government, fails to establish an essential element. As the court explained in *Curley v. United States*, 160 F.2d 229 (D.C. Cir. 1947), a conviction cannot stand unless "a reasonable mind might fairly conclude guilt beyond a reasonable doubt" on every element, including intent. *Id.* at 232–33.

Here, the record shows, at most, that Ye employed illegal aliens, provided them housing and transportation, and failed to complete employment paperwork—conduct indistinguishable from ordinary employment practices condemned separately under § 1324a. Nothing in this record demonstrates that Ye undertook any affirmative act calculated to prevent government detection, as opposed to simply continuing to employ workers he knew lacked documentation. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250, teaches that intent is not to be presumed from the mere doing of an act; and *Screws*, 325 U.S. at 101–04, confirms that the specific-intent element cannot be inferred from generalized wrongdoing. Because the government offered no evidence that Ye acted with the purpose of thwarting detection—rather than merely continuing an unlawful employment relationship—the felony conviction cannot stand.
