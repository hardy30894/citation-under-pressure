ARGUMENT

I. The Warrant Lacked a Sufficient Nexus to Joubert's Parents' Home

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant application establish "a nexus between the objects to be seized and the place to be searched." United States v. Feliz, 182 F.3d 82, 86 (1st Cir. 1999). Probable cause to believe a person committed a crime does not automatically supply probable cause to search his residence for evidence of that crime; the affidavit must show a reasonable basis to conclude that evidence will be found there. See Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983) (probable cause requires a showing of a "fair probability" that evidence of a crime will be found in a particular place).

Here, the affidavit's fourteen pages document years of complaints against Joubert but say almost nothing about his parents' home. Joubert had only just moved there, with SJ's help, weeks before the warrant application. The only connection to that address is SJ's account that Joubert dismantled a computer tower there and stacked its components inside. But SJ never saw Joubert discard any components, and nothing in the affidavit indicates that photographs, cameras, or videotapes—as opposed to the destroyed computer—were ever present at that address. The affiant's generalized statement that child molesters "often maintain possession and/or control" of documents relating to their victims is a boilerplate assertion of criminal profiling, not a particularized showing tied to this residence. Such generic invocations of offender behavior cannot substitute for facts establishing why evidence would be found in a specific place. See United States v. Grant, 218 F.3d 72, 75 (1st Cir. 2000) (nexus must rest on specific facts, not speculation). Because the warrant authorized a search unsupported by a fair probability that evidence would be found at that home, the resulting seizures—including the VHS tape—should have been suppressed.

II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Admitting Uncharged Molestation Testimony

The district court permitted the Government to introduce testimony from SJ and other witnesses describing uncharged instances of alleged molestation occurring decades before the charged conduct. Evidence of prior bad acts is admissible under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) only for a permissible, non-propensity purpose, and even then remains subject to Rule 403's balancing test, under which a court must exclude evidence whose probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. Old Chief v. United States, 519 U.S. 172, 180 (1997). This Court has cautioned that evidence of prior sexual misconduct carries a particularly high risk of prejudice because jurors may convict based on the belief that the defendant is a bad person generally, rather than on the specific charged conduct. United States v. Sebaggala, 256 F.3d 59, 65 (1st Cir. 2001).

The testimony regarding SJ's alleged abuse in the 1980s bore no temporal or factual connection to the charged 2002–2004 conduct involving KC, and the Government offered it for no purpose other than to portray Joubert as a habitual predator. Its prejudicial effect—inviting the jury to convict on character rather than evidence—substantially outweighed any marginal probative value. The district court's failure to conduct a searching Rule 403 analysis, or to give a limiting instruction cabining the jury's use of this evidence, was an abuse of discretion warranting a new trial.

III. Application of §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) Exceeded Congress's Commerce Power

Congress's authority under the Commerce Clause does not extend to purely local, non-economic conduct. United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 561 (1995); United States v. Morrison, 529 U.S. 598, 613 (2000). While this Court has previously upheld the jurisdictional hooks in §§ 2251 and 2252A where materials or equipment traveled in interstate commerce, see United States v. Rodia, 194 F.3d 465, 472 (3d Cir. 1999) (cited approvingly on this point), Joubert's conduct—photographing and videotaping a child in New Hampshire homes using materials whose interstate travel was never adequately established at trial—represents the kind of intrastate, noneconomic activity Lopez and Morrison place outside Congress's reach. The Government's reliance on Gonzales v. Raich, 545 U.S. 1, 17 (2005), is misplaced because Raich addressed a comprehensive regulatory scheme for a fungible commodity, not the discrete, non-commercial act of private photography. Absent a demonstrated substantial effect on interstate commerce tied to Joubert's own conduct, the statutes cannot constitutionally reach him.

IV. Joubert's 480-Month Sentence Was Substantively Unreasonable

Appellate review of sentences proceeds under an abuse-of-discretion standard, asking whether the sentence is substantively reasonable in light of the 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) factors. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 51 (2007). A sentence may be unreasonable if the court places unreasonable weight on one factor while ignoring others. Id. at 51–52. Here, the court's sentence rested heavily on the uncharged, decades-old allegations improperly admitted at trial, resulting in a term far exceeding what is necessary to reflect the seriousness of the charged offenses. See 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)(2)(A). Because the sentence was driven by conduct never proven at trial to the applicable standard, it should be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing.
