**Argument**

The court of appeals correctly held that Rule 23 does not permit certification on a damages theory untethered to the liability theory the class is certified to prove. Rule 23(b)(3) requires that common questions “predominate” and that the district court conduct a “rigorous analysis” before certifying a class. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 351-52 (2011); *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 33-34 (2013). That inquiry necessarily includes whether the proposed damages model measures only the harm attributable to the theory of antitrust impact the court has accepted for trial. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35. A district court abuses its discretion when it certifies a class on the basis of a model that cannot “measure damages on a classwide basis in a way that isolates the damages attributable to” the common theory of injury. *Id.* at 35.

Here, the Third Circuit faithfully applied that principle. It recognized that the district court had narrowed the case to a single liability theory: that Comcast’s clustering conduct deterred overbuilder competition in the Philadelphia DMA. See *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 655 F.3d 182, 188-90 (3d Cir. 2011), rev’d, 569 U.S. 27 (2013). But the damages model relied on below did not isolate damages caused by that theory alone. *Id.* at 202-05. Instead, the model bundled several theories of injury together, including theories the district court had rejected. *Id.* at 190-92. As the Supreme Court later confirmed, that is impermissible because a damages methodology must be tied to the theory of liability for which common proof is offered. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35-38.

That rule flows from longstanding class-certification precedent. Rule 23 is not satisfied by a complaint’s allegations; the district court must resolve disputes relevant to certification, even when they overlap with the merits. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177-78 (1974); *Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 316-20 (3d Cir. 2008). Predominance requires that the plaintiff’s proof be capable of establishing classwide injury and classwide damages through common evidence. *Newton v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc.*, 259 F.3d 154, 166-68 (3d Cir. 2001); *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 311-18. The district court therefore may not certify a class by accepting an expert model that “assumes” liability on theories not actually before the court. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 34-35. Where a model does not fit the theory of liability, it cannot establish predominance. *Id.* at 35-38.

The Court’s decision is also consistent with *General Telephone Co. of the Southwest v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982), which held that a “rigorous analysis” is required to ensure that Rule 23’s prerequisites are actually met. *Dukes* reiterated that requirement and rejected any approach that would relax certification simply because the merits are complex. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350-51. And in *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 623-24 (1997), the Court emphasized that predominance is a demanding standard, designed to test whether common issues truly dominate the litigation. Those decisions foreclose certification based on a damages presentation that masks individualized or theory-specific proof.

The Third Circuit’s approach also accords with *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d at 323-24, which held that the district court must determine by a preponderance of the evidence whether the Rule 23 requirements are met. A certification ruling cannot rest on speculation that plaintiffs might later present a proper damages methodology. *Id.* at 321-22. Nor may the court accept an expert’s say-so without asking whether the model measures the injury caused by the accepted theory of antitrust impact. *Id.* at 324-25. That is precisely what the Supreme Court later said in *Comcast*: “any model supporting a plaintiff’s damages case must be consistent with its liability case.” *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35.

Petitioners’ contrary position would gut Rule 23(b)(3). If a court could certify a damages class without admissible evidence that damages are measurable on a classwide basis for the theory actually being tried, then predominance would collapse into a pleading standard. That would conflict with *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 316-20, *Falcon*, 457 U.S. at 161, *Amchem*, 521 U.S. at 623-24, and *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 351-52. It would also ignore the central holding of *Comcast* itself, 569 U.S. at 35-38, that class certification fails when the damages model does not match the liability theory.

For those reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
