The federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over Vernon Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim because it necessarily involves a substantial question of federal patent law. The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal jurisdiction extends to cases where a well-pleaded complaint establishes that the plaintiff's right to relief depends on the resolution of a substantial federal question. As early as Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. 738 (1824), the Court held that federal jurisdiction is proper when a case involves a federal question, even if it is not the sole issue in the case. Id. at 822.

In this case, Minton's malpractice claim against his former attorneys turns on the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar, a critical component of federal patent law. The validity of Minton's patent, and thus his malpractice claim, depends on the resolution of this federal issue. The Court has consistently held that cases involving disputes over the validity, construction, or effect of federal law "arise under" that law. Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Minton's claim meets this standard, as the outcome depends on the resolution of the experimental use exception.

The Court has also emphasized the importance of uniformity in the application of federal patent law. In Wheaton v. Peters, 33 U.S. 591 (1834), the Court noted that the federal patent laws are intended to provide a uniform system of patent regulation. Id. at 658. Allowing state courts to adjudicate patent issues in the context of malpractice claims could undermine this uniformity, as different state courts may interpret federal patent law differently.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that federal jurisdiction is exclusive in cases involving patent law. In Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255 (1897), the Court held that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving the validity and infringement of patents. Id. at 259. This principle is consistent with the idea that federal law is supreme in matters involving patent law.

The Court has cautioned against defining "arising under" jurisdiction too broadly, instead adopting a "selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936). Applying this principle, it is clear that Minton's claim involves a substantial question of federal patent law that is necessary to the resolution of his state-based malpractice claim.

Other cases, such as Bloomer v. McQuewan, 55 U.S. 539 (1852), and Wilson v. Sandford, 51 U.S. 99 (1850), also support the proposition that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over patent law cases. These cases demonstrate that the federal courts have long been recognized as the proper forum for resolving disputes involving patent law.

In light of these precedents, it is clear that Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim arises under exclusive federal patent law jurisdiction. The experimental use exception is a critical component of federal patent law, and the resolution of this issue is necessary to determine Minton's malpractice claim. Therefore, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over this case.
