# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS OF CHAPTER 93-121 ARE UNCONSTITUTIONALLY OVERBROAD AND CHILL PROTECTED FIRST AMENDMENT ACTIVITY

Lobbying is core political speech. It is "beyond dispute that lobbying of legislators, if lawful, is protected by the First Amendment right to petition." *See generally Eastern R.R. Presidents Conference v. Noerr Motor Freight, Inc.*, 365 U.S. 127, 137-38, 81 S.Ct. 523, 529-30, 5 L.Ed.2d 464 (1961) (recognizing that "the right of petition is one of the freedoms protected by the Bill of Rights"). Because lobbying activity implicates the right to petition and associated speech rights, any statute regulating that activity must be judged against the exacting standards this Court applies to regulations touching core political expression.

Chapter 93-121's disclosure regime sweeps far beyond any legitimate state interest in transparency. The statute requires disclosure not merely of direct payments to lobbyists but of *all* lobbying expenditures, including expenditures made by the principal independent of the lobbyist, broken down into open-ended categories such as "entertainment," "research," and "communication." Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a). This granular, all-encompassing reporting requirement burdens protected associational and expressive activity far beyond what is necessary to inform the public or the legislature about who is attempting to influence government action.

The Supreme Court has recognized that compelled disclosure requirements, even outside the electoral context, may chill protected activity by exposing individuals to harassment or public opprobrium for engaging in lawful advocacy. *See NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 462, 78 S.Ct. 1163, 1172, 2 L.Ed.2d 1488 (1958) (holding that compelled disclosure of membership lists can constitute "an effective restraint on freedom of association" where it exposes members "to economic reprisal, loss of employment, threat of physical coercion, and other manifestations of public hostility"). The overbreadth doctrine exists precisely to guard against statutes whose legitimate sweep is dwarfed by their unconstitutional applications; such statutes may be challenged facially by parties whose own conduct might otherwise be regulable, because of the statute's *in terrorem* effect on those not before the court. *See Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 612, 93 S.Ct. 2908, 2916, 37 L.Ed.2d 830 (1973) (permitting facial overbreadth challenges because "persons whose expression is constitutionally protected may well refrain from exercising their rights for fear of criminal sanctions").

Here, the League's members face exactly that dilemma. Faced with a statute that requires disclosure of virtually every category of expenditure connected to their professional advocacy—without any showing that such minute disclosure serves an anti-corruption interest distinct from the interest already served by disclosure of direct lobbyist compensation—members of the League must either curtail their advocacy or risk civil sanction for incomplete or inaccurate reporting under a statute of uncertain scope. This is precisely the choice condemned in *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 152-53, 87 S.Ct. 1507, 1517-18, 18 L.Ed.2d 681 (1967), and it demonstrates that the statute's legitimate applications are vastly outnumbered by its unconstitutional chilling effect on protected petitioning activity.

## II. THE CONTINGENCY-FEE BAN VIOLATES THE FIRST AMENDMENT AND SHOULD NOT BE UPHELD ON THE STRENGTH OF SUPERSEDED PRECEDENT

The contingency-fee ban of Fla. Stat. § 11.047 prohibits lobbyists from contracting for compensation tied to legislative or executive success. This provision directly burdens the ability of citizens—particularly those without resources to pay flat fees—to secure representation before their government, and thereby burdens the right to petition itself.

This Court has suggested it feels bound by "old pronouncements" of the Supreme Court sustaining contingency-fee bans, even while acknowledging that "recent cases may have cast" doubt on their continuing vitality. That tension should be resolved in favor of the constitutional principle, not the antiquated precedent. The Supreme Court has since recognized that contingent-fee arrangements for advocacy before government bodies may themselves constitute protected speech and association. *See In re Primus*, 436 U.S. 412, 422, 98 S.Ct. 1893, 1899-1900, 56 L.Ed.2d 417 (1978) (holding that solicitation of clients for litigation "in vindication of civil liberties" is protected activity, and noting the state's interest in regulating the financial arrangements of such advocacy must be weighed against associational and expressive interests). While *Primus* arose in the litigation context, its logic—that advocacy on behalf of a cause, including the financial structuring of that advocacy, warrants constitutional solicitude—applies with equal or greater force to lobbying, which the Petition Clause protects independently of any judicial forum.

Moreover, this Court is not powerless to recognize that intervening Supreme Court decisions have undermined the reasoning of earlier cases even without an explicit overruling. Lower courts must apply the reasoning of the Supreme Court's most recent pronouncements where those pronouncements are irreconcilable with older precedent, rather than mechanically applying superseded doctrine. Because the contingency-fee ban directly restricts the financial terms on which citizens may obtain advocacy before their government—advocacy that lies at the heart of the Petition Clause—it cannot survive under the analytic framework the Supreme Court now applies to restrictions on expressive and associational conduct.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the League respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and hold Chapter 93-121 unconstitutional in the respects argued above.
