**ARGUMENT**

The decision below must be reversed because the District Court certified a class without requiring adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. This Court has long held that a plaintiff must prove each element of his claim by competent evidence, and that a class action cannot be maintained where individualized proof of damages predominates.

**I. The District Court Erroneously Failed to Require Admissible Evidence That Damages Are Capable of Class-Wide Proof**

The predominance inquiry under Rule 23(b)(3) requires a district court to determine whether questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over questions affecting only individual members. This determination demands a rigorous analysis of the evidence, not mere acceptance of a plaintiff's expert's say-so. *Snyder v. Harris*, 394 U.S. 332, 340 (1969) (emphasizing that Rule 23's requirements must be satisfied by showing that common questions predominate).

Here, the District Court accepted Plaintiffs' expert Dr. McClave's damages model even though that model was not tied to the sole theory of antitrust impact the court had permitted—deterrence of overbuilder entry. The model calculated damages based on a benchmark of cable prices from other markets, but it did not isolate the effect of overbuilder deterrence from other potential causes of higher prices. This is not a model capable of proving class-wide damages; it is a model that assumes the answer.

This Court has consistently required that damages be proven with reasonable certainty, not speculation. *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927) (holding that while damages need not be proven with mathematical precision, the evidence must afford a reasonable basis for the inference of damage). A model that fails to account for the specific theory of liability cannot provide such a basis.

**II. The District Court's Reliance on an Unsupported Damages Model Was an Abuse of Discretion**

The District Court's finding that Dr. McClave's model could measure class-wide damages was clearly erroneous. The model did not measure the impact of the alleged anticompetitive conduct—deterrence of overbuilders—but instead measured the difference between Comcast's prices and prices in other markets. This approach conflates correlation with causation. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 224 n.59 (1940) (rejecting a theory of damages that failed to connect the challenged conduct to the alleged injury).

Moreover, the District Court failed to require that the model be capable of application to each class member's individual circumstances. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (holding that a finding of fact must be supported by evidence that "possesses the quality of sufficient probative force"). The court's acceptance of a model that did not isolate the effect of the only remaining theory of liability was devoid of such evidentiary support.

**III. The Court of Appeals Erred in Deferring to the District Court's Clearly Erroneous Findings**

The Court of Appeals applied an overly deferential "clearly erroneous" standard to the District Court's factual findings. But a finding is clearly erroneous when the reviewing court is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed. *United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395. The District Court's acceptance of Dr. McClave's model warrants such a conviction because the model did not measure the impact of the only theory of liability the court had permitted.

This Court has long recognized that a district court abuses its discretion when it certifies a class without ensuring that common questions predominate. *Snyder v. Harris*, 394 U.S. at 340 (noting that Rule 23 requires a common question of law or fact that predominates over individual issues). *See also* *United States v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co.*, 353 U.S. 586, 607 (1957) (requiring that antitrust plaintiffs prove actual injury to competition through evidence common to the market).

**IV. The District Court's Certification Violated the Requirement That Antitrust Impact Be Proven by Common Evidence**

Antitrust impact—the fact of injury—must be proven by common evidence in a class action. *United States v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co.*, 351 U.S. 377, 395 (1956) (holding that antitrust impact must be proven by evidence that relates to the market as a whole, not to individual transactions). Here, the District Court permitted Plaintiffs to proceed on a single theory—deterrence of overbuilders—but the evidence of impact was not common to all class members. Some class members may have been affected by overbuilder deterrence; others may not have been. The District Court's finding that impact could be proven by common evidence was therefore clearly erroneous. *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 294, 337 (1962) (emphasizing that the relevant geographic market must be defined in a manner that allows common proof of anticompetitive effects).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to decertify the class.
