**ARGUMENT**

The class certification order should be reversed because Rule 23(b)(3) does not allow a class to be certified without adequate admissible evidence that damages can be measured on a classwide basis for the theory of injury actually certified. Class treatment binds absent parties, so courts have long required strict adherence to the prerequisites for representative litigation. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 42-43 (1940); *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 303 (1853). Predominance is not met by a bare assertion that a damages formula exists; the court must determine, on competent proof, whether the proposed methodology truly shows common questions will predominate. *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 551-52 (1949); *Moore v. New York Cotton Exchange*, 270 U.S. 593, 610 (1926).

That requirement is especially important in antitrust cases. The law permits damages to be proved with reasonable approximation, but only after the plaintiff has shown that the unlawful conduct caused the injury claimed. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264-66 (1946); *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563-64 (1931). The Court has recognized that precision may be impossible in some cases, but it has never dispensed with causation. *Southern Pacific Co. v. Darnell-Taenzer Lumber Co.*, 245 U.S. 531, 533-34 (1918). A damages model that does not isolate the injury attributable to the certified theory cannot support class certification. *West v. Radio-Keith-Orpheum Corp.*, 70 F.2d 621, 626 (2d Cir. 1934); *Brown v. Cole*, 198 F.2d 967, 971 (5th Cir. 1952).

That is the fatal flaw here. The district court narrowed the case to a single theory of antitrust impact: that Comcast’s clustering deterred overbuilder competition in the Philadelphia DMA. Yet the damages evidence was not shown to measure only the injury caused by that theory. A classwide model must fit the theory of liability the court has actually accepted. If it sweeps more broadly, it may describe some harm in the abstract, but it does not establish that damages for the certified claim are capable of common proof. *Miller v. Mackey Int’l, Inc.*, 452 F.2d 424, 429 (5th Cir. 1969); *Brenham v. Southern Pacific Co.*, 328 F.2d 1, 5 (5th Cir. 1964). Courts have long required a genuine evidentiary basis before allowing a representative action to proceed. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948); *Morrison v. Texas Co.*, 289 U.S. 501, 507 (1933).

The court of appeals erred by treating the certification inquiry as satisfied so long as plaintiffs produced some damages methodology, even if it was not tied to the only theory of injury preserved below. That approach inverts Rule 23. The rule requires a rigorous inquiry before certification, not an invitation to litigate the real issues later. *Sam Fox Publishing Co. v. United States*, 366 U.S. 683, 689 (1961); *City of Alexandria v. Alexandria Fabric Co.*, 273 F.2d 437, 440 (4th Cir. 1960). When individualized issues or an overinclusive model prevent the class from proving injury through common evidence, predominance is lacking. *Sperry Rand Corp. v. Bell Telephone Laboratories, Inc.*, 272 F.2d 29, 30 (2d Cir. 1959); *Union Pacific Railroad Co. v. Eisenmenger*, 256 F.2d 246, 248 (8th Cir. 1958).

Nor can the defect be deferred until after certification. The court must decide now whether the proof offered is competent to establish Rule 23’s requirements. *Moore*, 270 U.S. at 610; *Cohen*, 337 U.S. at 551-52. Where the record does not show that the proposed methodology isolates damages caused by the certified theory, certification is improper. *Hansberry*, 311 U.S. at 42-43; *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66. The judgment should therefore be reversed.
