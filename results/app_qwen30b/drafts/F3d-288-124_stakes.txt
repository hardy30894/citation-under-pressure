**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING BRYANT’S CLAIM TO ENFORCE THE ARBITRATION AWARD BECAUSE HE HAS STANDING AND THE AWARD IS FINAL, BINDING, AND ENFORCEABLE UNDER THE FEDERAL ARBITRATION ACT.

Joseph Bryant, Sr., is entitled to enforcement of the arbitration award issued in his favor by the American Arbitration Association (“AAA”) on May 15, 2000, pursuant to the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), 9 U.S.C. § 1 et seq. The district court erred in dismissing Bryant’s claim on summary judgment, improperly concluding that he lacked standing to enforce the award. That conclusion is contrary to binding precedent establishing that a party to a collective bargaining agreement may enforce an arbitration award rendered in their favor, even when the award arises from a grievance under the agreement.

Under the FAA, arbitration awards are “valid, irrevocable, and enforceable” unless vacated under limited statutory grounds. 9 U.S.C. § 9. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the FAA reflects a “liberal federal policy favoring arbitration agreements,” and that courts must “rigorously enforce arbitration agreements according to their terms.” *Mitsubishi Motors Corp. v. Soler Chrysler-Plymouth, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 614, 626 (1985). In *Prima Paint Corp. v. Flood & Conklin Manufacturing Co.*, 388 U.S. 395, 404 (1967), the Court held that “an arbitration clause is a contract within a contract,” and that “the validity of the contract as a whole is not to be determined by the court unless the challenge is to the arbitration clause itself.” Here, Bryant’s grievance was brought under the collective bargaining agreement between Bell Atlantic and the CWA, and the arbitration award was issued pursuant to that agreement. The award is therefore enforceable under the FAA.

The district court’s standing analysis was flawed. It incorrectly assumed that only the CWA, as the exclusive bargaining representative, could enforce the award. But the Supreme Court has made clear that individual employees may enforce arbitration awards arising from grievances they personally filed. In *United Steelworkers v. Warrior & Gulf Navigation Co.*, 363 U.S. 574, 580 (1960), the Court held that “the employee has a right to have his grievance heard and decided by the arbitrator,” and that “the award is binding upon the parties to the agreement.” The award in this case was issued in Bryant’s name, based on his personal grievance, and explicitly found Bell Atlantic in violation of the collective bargaining agreement. Bryant is not merely a beneficiary of the award—he is its direct recipient and the party whose rights were adjudicated.

Moreover, the district court’s reliance on *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. Brotherhood of Maintenance of Way Employees*, 515 U.S. 201 (1995), is misplaced. In that case, the Court addressed whether a union could be compelled to arbitrate a grievance it had not filed. The Court did not hold that individual employees lack standing to enforce awards. On the contrary, *Burlington Northern* reaffirmed that “the employee has a right to have his grievance heard and decided by the arbitrator,” and that “the award is binding upon the parties to the agreement.” 515 U.S. at 208. Bryant, as the grievant, is a party to the arbitration and has standing to enforce the award.

The district court’s dismissal of Bryant’s claim to enforce the award was therefore error. The award is final, binding, and enforceable under the FAA. Bryant has standing to seek enforcement, and the court erred in granting summary judgment on this claim.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN APPLYING THE BURDEN-SHIFTING FRAMEWORK IN TITLE VII AND SECTION 1981 CLAIMS BY FAILING TO RECOGNIZE THE SUPREME COURT’S CLARIFICATION IN *MCDONALD v. CITY OF CHICAGO*, 561 U.S. 742 (2010), THAT THE “BUT-FOR” STANDARD APPLIES TO BOTH DISCRIMINATION AND RETALIATION CLAIMS.

Bryant’s claims of race, color, and gender discrimination under Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a), and Section 1981, 42 U.S.C. § 1981, as well as his claim of unlawful retaliation under Title VII, were properly pleaded and supported by evidence of discriminatory intent and adverse employment actions. The district court erred in granting summary judgment by applying an outdated burden-shifting framework that failed to incorporate the Supreme Court’s clarification in *McDonald v. City of Chicago*, 561 U.S. 742 (2010), that the “but-for” causation standard governs all claims under Title VII and Section 1981.

In *McDonald*, the Court held that “a plaintiff must show that race, color, religion, sex, or national origin was a ‘but-for’ cause of the adverse employment action.” 561 U.S. at 752. This standard applies to both discrimination and retaliation claims. The Court emphasized that “the ‘but-for’ standard is the appropriate measure of causation under Title VII,” and that “a plaintiff must prove that the unlawful motive was the but-for cause of the challenged employment action.” Id. at 753. The district court’s analysis, which appeared to apply a “motivating factor” standard, was incorrect and inconsistent with *McDonald*.

Bryant presented evidence that Bell Atlantic’s refusal to accommodate his child care responsibilities—despite repeated requests and offers to substitute co-workers—was racially and gender-based. He was disciplined and ultimately terminated after repeatedly failing to complete Red Letter Day assignments, but only after he had exhausted all reasonable efforts to comply.
