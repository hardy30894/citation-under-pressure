**Argument**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that Temporary Protected Status does not satisfy § 1255’s threshold requirement that an applicant have been “inspected and admitted or paroled” into the United States. The proper reading of the INA is that when Congress directs that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4), it has conferred the functional equivalent of admission for purposes of adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The statute should be read as a coherent whole, not as isolated phrases. See FDA v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp., 529 U.S. 120, 133 (2000); Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004).

1. **Section 1254a(f)(4) supplies the operative status that § 1255 requires.**  
Section 1255 is a status-adjustment provision; it asks whether the alien may adjust “the status of an alien who was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). But TPS does more than create a temporary stay of removal. Congress expressly provided that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). That is the very kind of lawful immigration posture Congress identified as relevant to adjustment. Congress knew how to limit TPS benefits to mere protection from removal; instead, it chose language that affirmatively places the recipient in lawful nonimmigrant status. See Carachuri-Rosendo v. Holder, 560 U.S. 563, 576–77 (2010) (courts must apply the statute Congress enacted, not an atextual narrowing construction).

2. **The INA’s own structure shows that admission and status are distinct, but not mutually exclusive.**  
It is true that “admission” and “status” are not identical concepts. See, e.g., Hanif v. Attorney General, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012); Taveras v. Attorney General, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013). But the question is not whether the two terms are identical; it is whether Congress’s express command that a TPS recipient be treated as a lawful nonimmigrant suffices for § 1255 purposes. It does. The INA elsewhere uses “admitted” and “lawful” status together to describe eligibility, confirming that Congress understood lawful immigration status to carry operative legal consequences beyond the initial point of entry. See 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(1); 8 U.S.C. § 1258(a). And when Congress has wanted to exclude a class from adjustment unless it had been admitted in the ordinary way, it has said so expressly. See 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a), (c).

3. **The statute’s purpose supports allowing TPS recipients to adjust status.**  
TPS is a humanitarian protection for people from countries devastated by armed conflict, natural disaster, or other extraordinary conditions. See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Congress enacted TPS to permit eligible noncitizens to remain in the United States lawfully while conditions abroad are unsafe. Interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) to bar those very recipients from adjusting status—despite Congress’s command that they be treated as lawful nonimmigrants—would frustrate the statute’s humanitarian design. Statutes should be interpreted to further, not defeat, their evident purpose. See King v. Burwell, 576 U.S. 473, 492 (2015); Clark v. Martinez, 543 U.S. 371, 381–82 (2005).

4. **The Government’s contrary reading improperly makes TPS a dead letter for many long-term residents.**  
The Government’s reading would mean that a person who has lived, worked, and remained lawfully in the United States for years under TPS remains forever barred from adjusting status solely because the initial entry was without inspection. But Congress’s insertion of § 1254a(f)(4) would then do no meaningful work for § 1255 purposes, contrary to the settled rule that statutes should be construed to give effect to all their provisions. See Hibbs, 542 U.S. at 101. Congress did not enact an empty formality. It enacted a rule that TPS recipients are to be treated as lawful nonimmigrants, and that treatment must carry the ordinary legal consequences of lawful nonimmigrant status when they seek adjustment under § 1255.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
