**ARGUMENT**

**I. The IRS Cannot Collect a Partnership’s Tax Deficiency from Individual Partners Without First Making Individualized Assessments Against Those Partners.**

The Internal Revenue Code requires that an assessment be made against a specific “taxpayer” before the government may collect the tax. 26 U.S.C. § 6203. A “taxpayer” is defined as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Because an individual partner and a partnership are distinct “taxpayers” under the Code, an assessment against the partnership does not constitute an assessment against the individual partners. *United States v. Galletti*, 298 F.3d 1107, 1112 (9th Cir. 2002).

The statute of limitations for assessment is three years from the date the return is filed. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). If the IRS fails to assess a taxpayer within that period, it is barred from collecting the tax. *Id.* Here, the IRS assessed the partnership in 1994, 1995, and 1996, but it never assessed the individual partners. Because the three-year limitations period has long since expired, the IRS cannot now collect from the partners. *Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1112.

The IRS argues that its timely assessment against the partnership extends the collection period to ten years under 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). But that extension applies only to “the taxpayer” against whom the assessment was made. *Id.* The partnership is the only taxpayer assessed; the individual partners were not. As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, “the assessment against the Partnership extended the statute of limitations only with respect to the Partnership, but it left unaltered the limitations period applicable to Debtors.” *Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1113.

This Court’s decision in *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), supports this conclusion. In *Coson*, the IRS assessed unpaid taxes against a partnership and later claimed a lien against an alleged partner. *Id.* at 454. The district court held that “such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property … unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). This Court affirmed, rejecting the government’s attempt to collect from the partner without an individual assessment. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 462-63.

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the partner voluntarily paid the tax and then sought a refund. *Id.* at 910. The Court held only that a partner who pays a partnership tax cannot recover a refund merely because the assessment did not name him. *Id.* The Court did not hold that the IRS could *compel* payment from an unwilling partner without an individual assessment. As the Ninth Circuit explained, “the district court’s holding … was much narrower than the IRS acknowledges, namely, that ‘[a] person liable for taxes may not recover a refund of taxes he paid because of the fact the assessment did not name him.’” *Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1114 (quoting *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959)).

**II. State Partnership Law Does Not Permit the IRS to Collect from Individual Partners Without a Judgment.**

The IRS argues that California partnership law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, and that this state-law liability allows the IRS to collect directly from the partners. But California law requires more. Under California Corporations Code § 16307(c), “[a] judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” *Accord* 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001) (“[T]he partner must be individually named and served in the action or in a later suit, and judgment entered against that partner, in order to reach the partner’s personal assets.”).

The IRS has obtained no judgment against the individual partners. The time to do so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). As the Ninth Circuit held, “the assessment extended the statute of limitations only as to the Partnership.” *Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1116. Without a timely assessment or judgment, the IRS’s state-law claim is unenforceable. *Id.* at 1115-16.

The IRS’s reliance on *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is unavailing. In *Wright*, the IRS assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only question was whether the statute of limitations was tolled during the partnership’s bankruptcy. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562-63. The Seventh Circuit “had no opportunity to address the question before us.” *Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1109.

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS failed to assess the individual partners within the three-year statute of limitations. The assessment against the partnership does not extend the limitations period for the partners. State law does not permit collection without a judgment, and the time to obtain one has passed. The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
