**ARGUMENT**

**I. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is valid as applied to state-owned lands within the boundaries of the National Park System.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (ANILCA), 16 U.S.C. § 3193(c), does not withdraw National Park Service (NPS) authority over nonfederal lands within conservation system units (CSUs) in Alaska. The plain text of § 103(c) preserves NPS jurisdiction over “public lands” while merely clarifying that federal land management statutes do not apply to nonfederal lands. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3193(c) (“Only those lands within the boundaries of [CSUs] which are public lands (as such term is defined in this Act) shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit.”). This Court has repeatedly recognized that Congress may impose federal regulations on nonfederal lands when necessary to effectuate a broader statutory scheme. *See, e.g., South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 693 (1993) (“Congress has the power to abrogate state jurisdiction over federal lands within a State.”); *Organized Village of Kake v. Egan*, 369 U.S. 60, 72 (1962) (“The State’s jurisdiction over lands within its borders is not absolute.”).

The hovercraft ban at issue here is a valid exercise of NPS authority under 16 U.S.C. § 1 (the Organic Act), which grants the Secretary of the Interior broad regulatory power over the National Park System. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 1 (“The Secretary shall promulgate and enforce such rules and regulations as he may deem necessary or proper for the use and management of the parks.”). This authority extends to all lands and waters within park boundaries, regardless of ownership. *See Colorado v. Toll*, 268 U.S. 228, 230 (1925) (“The regulations forbid any person to reside permanently, engage in any business, or erect buildings in the park without permission in writing from the Director of the National Parks Service.”). The Ninth Circuit properly deferred to the NPS’s interpretation of its own regulations, as this Court has instructed. *See Nat’l Cable & Telecomms. Ass’n v. Brand X Internet Servs.*, 545 U.S. 967, 982 (2005) (“A court’s prior judicial construction of a statute trumps an agency construction otherwise entitled to Chevron deference only if the prior court decision holds that its construction follows from the unambiguous terms of the statute.”).

**II. Sturgeon lacks standing to challenge the NPS regulation on state-owned lands.**

Sturgeon’s claimed injury—his inability to use a hovercraft on the Nation River—is insufficient to establish Article III standing. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon failed to demonstrate a credible threat of prosecution, as he had not been charged with any violation and had not used his hovercraft since receiving a warning. *See Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus*, 134 S.Ct. 2334, 2342 (2014) (“A plaintiff satisfies the injury-in-fact requirement where he alleges ‘an intention to engage in a course of conduct arguably affected with a constitutional interest, but proscribed by a statute, and there exists a credible threat of prosecution thereunder.’”). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that past enforcement actions or warnings do not suffice to establish standing unless they are coupled with a genuine threat of future prosecution. *See, e.g., City of Los Angeles v. Cnty. of Kern*, 581 F.3d 841, 845 (9th Cir. 2009) (citing *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992)).

Moreover, Sturgeon’s reliance on the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment is misplaced. The Court has narrowly construed this clause, limiting it to rights “which owe their existence to the Federal Government, its National Character, its Constitution, or its laws.” *Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. 36, 79 (1872). The right to navigate navigable waters is not among those protected rights. *See Courtney v. Goltz*, 736 F.3d 1152, 1160 (9th Cir. 2013) (rejecting a similar claim).

**III. Alaska lacks standing to challenge the NPS regulation.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Alaska failed to establish standing. Alaska’s claimed injuries—such as increased staff time and expense in complying with NPS permit requirements—are not redressable by a favorable ruling, as the challenged conduct has already occurred and is not ongoing. *See Lujan*, 504 U.S. at 564 (“Past exposure to illegal conduct does not in itself show a present case or controversy regarding injunctive relief.”). Additionally, Alaska’s assertion of a sovereign right to regulate its lands and waters is insufficient to establish standing, as the Court has repeatedly held that a state may not sue to protect the interests of its citizens. *See Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971 (9th Cir. 2009) (quoting *Massachusetts v. EPA*, 549 U.S. 497, 520 n.17 (2007)).

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision should be affirmed. The NPS’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of federal authority under ANILCA and the Organic Act, and Sturgeon and Alaska lack standing to challenge it.
