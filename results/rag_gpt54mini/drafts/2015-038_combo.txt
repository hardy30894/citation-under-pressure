ANILCA § 103(c) does not strip the National Park Service of all regulatory authority over nonfederal lands and waters within Alaska park boundaries. The statute provides that “only those public lands within the boundaries of any conservation system unit … which are administered by the Secretary [of the Interior] … shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit,” while also stating that “waters subject to the jurisdiction of the State of Alaska … and lands underlying such waters are not included as part of such unit.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). But that text addresses what lands are counted as “part of” a conservation system unit; it does not nullify generally applicable federal regulations that operate within park boundaries and protect federal interests. The better reading is confirmed by this Court’s repeated recognition that federal land management authority can extend to private, state, and other nonfederal lands within park or reservation boundaries. See Colorado v. Toll, 268 U.S. 228 (1925); Curtin v. Benson, 222 U.S. 78 (1911); Collins v. Yosemite Park & Curry Co., 304 U.S. 518 (1938); Hynes, Regional Director, Fish & Wildlife Service v. Grimes Packing Co., 337 U.S. 86 (1949).

This Court has long rejected the premise that the presence of nonfederal ownership inside a federal reservation disables federal regulation. In Curtin v. Benson, 222 U.S. 78 (1911), the Court upheld park regulations governing the use of lands inside Yosemite National Park, explaining that the owner of patented lands was entitled to “full use and enjoyment,” but that use remained subject to park regulation. Id. at 78. Likewise, in Collins v. Yosemite Park & Curry Co., 304 U.S. 518 (1938), the Court recognized a congressional cession of “exclusive jurisdiction” over Yosemite, while preserving limited state powers over process, taxation, and voting. Id. at 518. The case demonstrates that jurisdictional boundaries inside national parks are allocation questions, not a blanket negation of all federal authority. Colorado v. Toll, 268 U.S. 228 (1925), is to the same effect: the Court described complaints that federal park regulations intruded on “the sovereign rights of the State” and affected private lands and highways within the park, yet the very framing of the dispute assumed federal regulatory power inside park boundaries. Id. at 228.

ANILCA’s structure also confirms that Congress did not intend to create an enclave of state immunity from federal regulation. In Organized Village of Kake v. Egan, 369 U.S. 60 (1962), the Court held that “absolute” federal jurisdiction is not invariably exclusive jurisdiction, and that a state’s disclaimer of title was a disclaimer of “proprietary rather than governmental interest.” Id. at 60. That distinction matters here. Section 103(c) ensures that state and Native lands and waters are not converted into federal “public lands” merely because they lie within a conservation system unit; it does not erase the federal government’s separate governmental authority to regulate conduct that affects park purposes. Metlakatla Indian Community, Annette Islands Reserve v. Egan, 369 U.S. 45 (1962), is especially instructive: Congress had set aside waters for the Community, yet the Court recognized that Alaska retained regulatory authority in the fisheries context only because Congress had withheld or accommodated it. Id. at 45. That history shows Congress knows how to displace federal regulation when it wishes; it did not do so here.

Petitioner’s contrary reading would also conflict with longstanding cases recognizing federal authority over navigation and other activities in territorial and federal enclaves. In United States v. Pacific & Arctic Railway & Navigation Co., 228 U.S. 87 (1913), the Court held that federal law could control transportation “in our territory,” even where routes crossed multiple jurisdictions. Id. at 87. In Interstate Commerce Commission v. United States Ex Rel. Humboldt Steamship Co., 224 U.S. 474 (1912), the Court accepted that federal jurisdiction could extend to carriers operating in Alaska. Id. at 474. And in Territory of Alaska v. Troy, 258 U.S. 101 (1922), the Court sustained federal regulation of commerce in and through Alaska, emphasizing Congress’s plenary power over the Territory. Id. at 101. These decisions reinforce that Congress does not lose regulatory power simply because the regulated conduct occurs on waters or lands with a state-law character.

Ward v. Race Horse, 163 U.S. 504 (1896), likewise confirms the background principle: “The power of a State to control and regulate” activities on lands within its borders exists unless federal law provides otherwise. Id. at 504. Here, Congress did provide otherwise by creating national park units in Alaska and authorizing federal administration of those units. The question is the scope of that administration. Nothing in § 103(c) suggests Congress meant to strip the National Park Service of regulations necessary to manage access, safety, and resource protection within park boundaries merely because the regulated riverbed or water column is state-owned.

Finally, Hynes, Regional Director, Fish & Wildlife Service v. Grimes Packing Co., 337 U.S. 86 (1949), shows that federal regulations may validly operate within a defined area and on the instrumentalities used there, even when they affect activities on waters subject to overlapping interests. Id. at 86. Petitioner seeks a rule far broader than anything recognized in the precedents: that state ownership of submerged lands within Alaska park boundaries nullifies all National Park Service regulation. No pre-1970 authority supports that sweeping proposition, and several reject it.

The judgment should be affirmed.
