**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state-based legal malpractice claims that involve patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue—requires this Court to determine whether a state-law claim for legal malpractice, which necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial federal patent issue, “arises under” federal law within the meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). The Supreme Court has made clear that such jurisdiction is not automatic. Rather, it is limited to cases where a federal issue is both *necessary* and *substantial*, and where federal adjudication does not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. See *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314, 125 S. Ct. 2363, 162 L. Ed. 2d 257 (2005). Applying this test, we conclude that federal courts do *not* possess exclusive jurisdiction in this case—and that the Texas Supreme Court correctly held that the state court retained subject-matter jurisdiction.

### I. Federal Jurisdiction Under § 1338(a) Requires More Than a "Substantial" Patent Issue

Petitioner’s malpractice claim arises under Texas law and seeks damages for negligence in the prosecution of a prior patent infringement suit. While the outcome of that prior litigation turned on the application of federal patent law, including the on-sale bar and the experimental use exception, the *existence* of the malpractice claim itself is rooted in state tort law. As the Supreme Court emphasized in *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09, 108 S. Ct. 2166, 100 L. Ed. 2d 811 (1988), federal jurisdiction under § 1338(a) exists only when “the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” That is, the federal issue must be *essential* to the cause of action—not merely relevant or incidental.

Here, the patent law issues are not essential to the malpractice claim; they are *evidentiary*. Petitioner must prove that his attorneys failed to raise a viable defense—the experimental use exception—that could have changed the outcome of the underlying patent case. But the *validity* of that defense, and its applicability to the facts of the lease, are questions of fact and law that may be resolved by a state court without creating any conflict with federal patent policy. Indeed, as the Fifth Circuit recognized in *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 384, 388 (5th Cir. 2008), “federal jurisdiction does not arise simply because a state-law claim involves a federal issue.” The mere presence of a federal question does not trigger exclusive federal jurisdiction.

### II. The Patent Issues Are Not Actually Disputed or Substantial in This Context

Even if the patent issues were substantial, they are not *actually disputed* in the context of the malpractice claim. In *Grable*, the Court stressed that federal jurisdiction requires that the federal issue be “actually disputed”—that is, genuinely contested by the parties. 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S. Ct. 2363. Here, the experimental use exception was never raised by petitioner’s original counsel, and no evidence suggests that it was ever a viable defense given the commercial nature of the TEXCEN lease. The lease explicitly permitted Stark to use TEXCEN for business purposes, and Minton himself acknowledged that he intended to commercialize the software. See *Minton v. Nat’l Ass’n of Sec. Dealers, Inc.*, 336 F.3d 1373, 1379 (Fed. Cir. 2003) (affirming summary judgment based on on-sale bar).

Moreover, the Federal Circuit has consistently held that the experimental use exception applies only when the sale is primarily for testing, not for profit. See *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005). The record here shows no such intent. Thus, the patent issue is not truly disputed—it is a legal conclusion that can be drawn from undisputed facts. As the Supreme Court warned in *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18, 57 S. Ct. 96, 81 L. Ed. 70 (1936), “a suit does not so arise [under federal law] unless it really and substantially involves a dispute respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law.” The absence of genuine dispute renders the federal issue insufficient to trigger exclusive jurisdiction.

### III. Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities

Finally, federal jurisdiction would disrupt the balance Congress intended between state and federal courts. In *Grable*, the Court emphasized that “federal jurisdiction will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S. Ct. 2363. This principle is especially important in malpractice cases, where state courts are well-equipped to assess attorney conduct under local standards of care. To hold otherwise would allow federal patent law to swallow state tort law, undermining the integrity of state courts.
