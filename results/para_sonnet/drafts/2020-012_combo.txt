**ARGUMENT**

The Railroad Retirement Act permits judicial review only of a "final decision of the Board" and, under this Court's construction of parallel statutory schemes, that review authority does not extend to an agency's discretionary refusal to reopen a claim it has already finally decided. The Fifth Circuit correctly so held, and this Court should affirm.

**I. The statute authorizes review only of a decision on the merits, not a discretionary refusal to reopen.**

Section 355(f) contemplates review of a decision reached through the Board's adjudicatory process on the merits of a benefits claim. Congress modeled that limitation on similar restrictions Congress placed on review of other benefit-granting agencies, including the scheme this Court construed in *Social Security Board v. Nierotko*, 327 U.S. 358 (1946), where the Court examined the scope of matters properly subject to review as final agency determinations under such statutes. A decision declining to reopen a matter already fully and finally adjudicated is not a new adjudication of the claim's merits; it is instead the agency's exercise of a separate, discretionary power to revisit its own completed work. Nothing in the text of § 355(f) suggests Congress intended that discretionary power to be folded into the review provision Congress enacted for merits determinations.

**II. This Court has long distinguished a reviewable final order from a subsequent discretionary refusal to reopen or rehear it.**

This distinction is not novel; it reflects longstanding administrative-law doctrine. In *United States v. Los Angeles & Salt Lake R.R. Co.*, 273 U.S. 299 (1927), the Court held that an order of the Interstate Commerce Commission denying a petition for rehearing does not itself present a new, independently reviewable order, because the decision whether to reopen a completed proceeding rests in the agency's own discretion. The Court reinforced that principle in *Utah Fuel Co. v. National Bituminous Coal Commission*, 306 U.S. 56 (1939), explaining that the denial of a petition for rehearing does not create a fresh basis for review separate from the underlying order it leaves undisturbed. And in *Federal Power Commission v. Metropolitan Edison Co.*, 304 U.S. 375 (1938), the Court likewise treated an agency's disposition of a rehearing request as a matter of the agency's own procedural discretion, not a merits determination subject to ordinary review.

This Court applied the identical principle to the very kind of agency action at issue here. In *Interstate Commerce Commission v. Jersey City*, 322 U.S. 503 (1944), the Court held that a Commission order denying rehearing of a completed proceeding does not reopen the underlying decision for review, and is not converted into a new, independently reviewable order merely because the agency chose to entertain the request. The Court recognized that treating such refusals as reviewable would invite endless rounds of relitigation through successive requests for reconsideration, defeating the repose that finality provisions are meant to secure. The same concern applies with equal force to the Railroad Retirement Act's reopening regulations, which exist to give claimants a limited, discretionary avenue for correcting error—not an alternative, unlimited route to merits review of stale claims.

The concept of a reviewable agency "order" must likewise be given a workable, bounded construction. In *Rochester Telephone Corp. v. United States*, 307 U.S. 125 (1939), the Court cautioned against a construction of "order" so broad that it would sweep in every subsidiary or incidental agency action arising after a proceeding has concluded. And in *Switchmen's Union v. National Mediation Board*, 320 U.S. 297 (1943), the Court declined to supply judicial review of an agency's discretionary determination where Congress had not provided a statutory avenue for reviewing that particular exercise of discretion, recognizing that the scope of reviewability is a legislative choice which courts should not enlarge by implication. The same restraint governs here: Congress provided for review of final merits decisions "after a hearing," 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), and said nothing about review of the Board's separate, discretionary reopening authority.

**III. Finality and exhaustion principles confirm that the 2007 denial, not the 2016 refusal to reopen, was the relevant final decision.**

This Court has also recognized that administrative finality attaches once a claimant fails to pursue the process available for challenging an agency determination within the time allowed. In *Estep v. United States*, 327 U.S. 114 (1946), the Court explained that finality is not undone simply because an agency retains discretionary authority to revisit its own determinations at a later time. Salinas's failure to pursue further administrative appeal from the Board's February 1976 denial rendered that decision final for reopening purposes. The Board's 2016 discretionary refusal to reopen that stale claim, nearly a decade later, did not restart the limited review Congress authorized for merits decisions; it merely left the original final decision undisturbed.

**Conclusion**

Permitting review of refusals to reopen as though they were merits decisions would eviscerate the finality that both Congress and this Court's precedents have long protected, inviting perpetual relitigation of claims through repeated requests for reconsideration. See *Jersey City*, 322 U.S. 503; *Los Angeles & Salt Lake R.R. Co.*, 273 U.S. 299. The Fifth Circuit correctly held that it lacked jurisdiction to review the Board's discretionary refusal to reopen Salinas's 2006 claim. The judgment below should be affirmed.
