The Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court's decision in Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), does not require a jury to determine whether a capital defendant is mentally retarded or to unanimously sentence a defendant to death.

First, regarding the determination of mental retardation, the Supreme Court in Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), left it to the states to determine the manner in which the constitutional restriction on execution of mentally retarded defendants would be enforced. Florida law sets forth a three-pronged test to determine mental retardation as a bar to the death penalty, which is to be determined by the trial court. See Nixon v. State, 2 So.3d 137, 142 (Fla. 2009); § 921.137(1), Fla. Stat. (2012). The trial court's determination of mental retardation is a factual finding that is not required to be made by a jury under the Sixth Amendment, as Ring only applies to the finding of aggravating factors that increase the maximum penalty. See Ring, 536 U.S. at 609.

Second, regarding the requirement of a unanimous jury verdict, Florida's capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment. While Ring held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find aggravating factors that increase the maximum penalty, it did not require a unanimous jury verdict. See Ring, 536 U.S. at 609. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury trial for factual findings that increase the maximum penalty, but it has not required unanimity in jury verdicts in capital sentencing cases. See, e.g., Harris v. Alabama, 513 U.S. 504 (1995). 

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury verdict in capital sentencing cases. The Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of guided procedures that promote a reasoned judgment by the trier of fact in capital cases, see Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), but it has not held that a unanimous jury verdict is required. Florida's capital sentencing scheme, which requires a judge to weigh aggravating and mitigating factors and make an independent determination of the sentence, provides sufficient guidance and reliability to satisfy the Eighth Amendment.

Finally, while Justice Pariente's dissent in the lower court opinion notes that Florida is an outlier among states that impose the death penalty in not requiring a unanimous jury finding of an aggravating circumstance, this fact alone does not render Florida's capital sentencing scheme unconstitutional. The Supreme Court has not held that a unanimous jury verdict is required in capital sentencing cases, and the fact that other states have different procedures does not necessarily mean that Florida's scheme is unconstitutional. See, e.g., Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447 (1984).

In conclusion, the Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The trial court's determination of mental retardation and the non-unanimous jury verdict in capital sentencing cases are both constitutional under the Supreme Court's precedents.
