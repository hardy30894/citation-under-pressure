**ARGUMENT**

Federal courts possess broad equitable authority to remedy ongoing constitutional violations. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) (holding that courts must fashion decrees that “effectively” eliminate unconstitutional conditions). This power extends to structural relief when lesser measures prove inadequate. *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 7 (1958) (affirming that judicial decrees may require affirmative steps to vindicate constitutional rights).

The Eighth Amendment forbids conditions that inflict “unnecessary and wanton” suffering. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958); *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962). When overcrowding produces such suffering, courts may order population reductions to restore constitutional compliance. *Green v. County School Board*, 391 U.S. 430, 437-438 (1968) (requiring remedies that “promise realistically to work” and “work now”).

Equitable decrees may reach state institutions when necessary to end violations. *Griffin v. County School Board*, 377 U.S. 218, 233-234 (1964) (ordering fiscal measures to cure constitutional defects). Such orders do not exceed judicial power merely because they affect large numbers of persons. *Reynolds v. Sims*, 377 U.S. 533, 585 (1964) (approving comprehensive remedial plans to correct systemic malapportionment).

The district court’s findings established that overcrowding was the primary cause of Eighth Amendment violations. Under settled precedent, once a constitutional violation is proven, the court may adopt the remedy that most directly addresses the proven cause. *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 198 (1962) (recognizing judicial authority to grant effective relief for ongoing harms). The population cap is narrowly tailored to that cause and leaves implementation to state officials.

Historical equity practice confirms that courts may compel affirmative steps, including expenditures, to effectuate decrees. *Porter v. Warner Holding Co.*, 328 U.S. 395, 398 (1946) (stating that equity’s jurisdiction is “complete” to afford relief). The order here follows that tradition without intruding on legislative functions beyond what the Constitution requires.

Because the decree rests on established equitable principles and directly remedies proven violations, it is consistent with the judicial role in enforcing constitutional guarantees.
