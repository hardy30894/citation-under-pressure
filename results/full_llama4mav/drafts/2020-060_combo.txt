The United States respectfully submits its merits brief in support of its petition for review.

ARGUMENT

I. The 2004 Consent Decree Triggered Guam's Right to Pursue a Contribution Claim Under Section 113(f)(3)(B)

The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) provides two avenues for a potentially responsible party (PRP) to seek recoupment of cleanup costs: a section 107 "cost-recovery" action and a section 113(f) "contribution" action. See 42 U.S.C. §§ 9607, 9613(f). The statute of limitations for a contribution action is three years, whereas the statute of limitations for a remedial section 107 action is six years. See id. § 113(g)(2)(B), (g)(3). The key issue in this case is whether the 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B), thereby precluding it from maintaining a section 107 claim.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a settlement agreement can resolve a party's liability for purposes of a contribution claim. See United States v. Atlantic Mut. Ins. Co., 343 U.S. 236, 241-42 (1952) (holding that a settlement agreement can discharge a party's liability). Similarly, in Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co. v. United States, 220 F.2d 246, 249 (7th Cir. 1955), the Seventh Circuit held that a settlement agreement can be considered a final determination of liability for purposes of a contribution claim.

Here, the 2004 Consent Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a "dump cover system." Consent Decree 5-12, J.A. 94-101. This decree "resolved" Guam's liability to the United States for the response action, thereby triggering section 113(f)(3)(B). See United States v. W. R. Bonsal Co., 72 F. Supp. 634, 636 (M.D.N.C. 1947) (holding that a consent decree can be considered a settlement that resolves a party's liability).

The terms of the Consent Decree demonstrate that Guam assumed significant obligations related to the cleanup of the Ordot Dump. As the Supreme Court noted in Akron, C. & Y. R. Co. v. United States, 261 U.S. 180, 194 (1923), a settlement agreement can be considered a binding contract that resolves a party's liability. Similarly, in United States v. Swift & Co., 286 U.S. 106, 115 (1932), the Court held that a consent decree can be considered a final and binding agreement that resolves the parties' disputes.

Guam's reliance on the disclaimer of liability in the Consent Decree is unavailing. As the Seventh Circuit noted in Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co., 220 F.2d at 249, a disclaimer of liability does not necessarily preclude a finding that a settlement agreement resolved a party's liability. See also United States v. N.Y., N.H. & H.R. Co., 276 F. 503, 506 (2d Cir. 1921) (holding that a party's denial of liability does not necessarily preclude a finding of liability).

Furthermore, the Consent Decree's provisions are consistent with the Supreme Court's holding in United States v. Armour & Co., 402 U.S. 673, 681-82 (1961), that a consent decree can be considered a settlement that resolves a party's liability. The decree's requirement that Guam complete construction of a cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate is a clear indication that Guam's liability was resolved.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the 2004 Consent Decree triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B). As a result, Guam is barred from maintaining a section 107 claim, and its complaint should be dismissed.

II. Guam's Section 107 Claim is Barred by the Statute of Limitations

As the Supreme Court held in Guaranty Trust Co. v. United States, 304 U.S. 126, 136-37 (1938), a statute of limitations can bar a claim if it is not brought within the prescribed time period. Here, Guam's section 113(f) contribution claim is barred by the three-year statute of limitations, which expired in 2007.

The authorities cited above demonstrate that Guam's liability was resolved by the 2004 Consent Decree, triggering section 113(f)(3)(B) and barring its section 107 claim. See, e.g., United States v. W. R. Bonsal Co., 72 F. Supp. at 636; Akron, C. & Y. R. Co., 261 U.S. at 194.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the United States respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's denial of its motion to dismiss and remand with instructions to dismiss Guam's complaint.
