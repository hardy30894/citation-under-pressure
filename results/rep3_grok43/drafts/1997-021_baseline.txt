ARGUMENT

I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employer polling is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.

An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of continuing majority support. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). An employer may overcome that presumption with “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status.” NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). The Board permits polling only when the employer already possesses evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-63 (1989). This equivalence renders polling “useless,” because an employer satisfying the standard may simply withdraw recognition without incurring the risks of an unsupervised poll. Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984).

The Board’s justification—that polling remains useful to confirm actual loss of majority support—fails. An employer that already possesses objective evidence sufficient to rebut the presumption may lawfully withdraw recognition; the additional step of polling adds no statutory protection and exposes the employer to unfair-labor-practice liability if the poll is later deemed insufficiently supported. The Board’s preference for Board-conducted elections over employer polls does not authorize imposing identical evidentiary thresholds on both. Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978) (Board must exercise its authority consistently with the Act). The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits therefore correctly held that an employer may poll upon “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence would not alone justify withdrawal. A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); Mingtree, 736 F.2d at 1299.

II. The Board’s finding that Allentown lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence.

The Board counted statements from only seven employees as repudiating the union. Allentown presented additional evidence: statements from employees Ridgick and Marsh during hiring interviews that they saw no need for a union and received no value from dues; Bloch’s report that the entire night shift opposed the union; and steward Mohr’s statement that the union would lose any vote. The Board discounted these statements on grounds that expressions during interviews are unreliable and that reports of others’ views lack probative value. Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc., 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978); Destileria Serralles, Inc., 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988). These categorical exclusions are arbitrary. The record contains no evidence that the statements were coerced or insincere, and the Board’s refusal to credit them leaves the factual finding without substantial support in the record as a whole. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951).
