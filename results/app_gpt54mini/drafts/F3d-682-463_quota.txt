## Argument

### I. The record permits a reasonable jury to find sex-based harassment under Title VII and the ELCRA.

Title VII forbids discrimination “because of . . . sex,” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), and a plaintiff may prove a hostile work environment by showing unwelcome harassment “based on sex” that is “sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim’s employment and create an abusive working environment.” *Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986); *Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993). The Sixth Circuit has long recognized that harassment need not arise from explicit sexual propositions to be actionable; the question is whether the conduct would not have occurred “but for” the plaintiff’s sex. *Williams v. Gen. Motors Corp.*, 187 F.3d 553, 565-66 (6th Cir. 1999). Michigan’s ELCRA is construed consistently with Title VII principles. *Radtke v. Everett*, 442 Mich. 368, 382, 501 N.W.2d 155 (1993); *Haynie v. State*, 468 Mich. 302, 309-10, 662 N.W.2d 74 (2003).

On this record, a reasonable jury could conclude that Wasek was harassed because he was a man. Ottobre repeatedly subjected Wasek to sexual touching, sexually explicit remarks, and homophobic taunting. Conduct of that nature is classic evidence of sex-based harassment. In *Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc.*, 523 U.S. 75, 80-81 (1998), the Court held that same-sex harassment is actionable where the harasser’s conduct is “because of sex,” including where the harasser uses sexually explicit conduct or sex-specific humiliation to target the victim. Here, Ottobre’s grabbing Wasek’s buttocks, poking him in the rear, and saying “you’ve got a pretty mouth” and “you know you like it sweetheart” plainly support an inference of sexualized hostility. Such conduct goes well beyond mere workplace incivility.

The district court erred by treating the dispute as ordinary bullying divorced from sex. That approach conflicts with *Oncale* and with the Sixth Circuit’s recognition that “[h]arassing conduct need not be motivated by sexual desire to support an inference of discrimination on the basis of sex.” *Williams*, 187 F.3d at 565. A jury could find that Ottobre’s conduct reflected a gender-based effort to feminize, humiliate, and dominate Wasek through sexualized acts and comments. See *Smith v. City of Salem*, 378 F.3d 566, 575-76 (6th Cir. 2004) (sex stereotyping is actionable sex discrimination); *Vickers v. Fairfield Med. Ctr.*, 453 F.3d 757, 764-65 (6th Cir. 2006) (same). The issue is not whether Ottobre was attracted to Wasek, but whether the harassment was sex-based. *Oncale*, 523 U.S. at 80-81.

The severity and pervasiveness requirement is also satisfied. Harassment is actionable when the environment is both objectively and subjectively hostile. *Harris*, 510 U.S. at 21-22. Relevant factors include frequency, severity, and whether the conduct is humiliating or physically threatening. *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775, 787-88 (1998). Ottobre’s conduct was persistent, physical, and degrading. It included repeated genital and buttocks touching, sexually explicit remarks, mockery, and interference with Wasek’s work. This Court has found sufficient evidence where conduct was less physically intrusive and less sustained. See *Jackson v. Quanex Corp.*, 191 F.3d 647, 661-62 (6th Cir. 1999); *Gallagher v. C.H. Robinson Worldwide, Inc.*, 567 F.3d 263, 272-75 (6th Cir. 2009) (en banc).

### II. Arrow Energy is liable because a supervisor and management failed to act after notice.

Employer liability turns on the harasser’s status and the employer’s response. Where a supervisor’s harassment culminates in tangible action, liability follows; where there is no tangible action, the employer may invoke the *Faragher/Ellerth* defense only by showing reasonable care to prevent and correct harassment and an unreasonable failure by the employee to use preventive measures. *Burlington Indus., Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742, 765 (1998); *Faragher*, 524 U.S. at 807-08. Even where co-worker harassment is at issue, the employer is liable if it knew or should have known of the harassment and failed to take prompt, appropriate corrective action. *McCoy v. Sw. Airlines Co.*, 246 F.3d 371, 379 (5th Cir. 2001); *Hafford v. Seidner*, 183 F.3d 506, 513-14 (6th Cir. 1999).

Here, Tripp observed some of the incidents and laughed rather than intervened. Wasek also complained directly to Tripp, who warned him not to “make waves” and threatened retaliation if Wasek reported higher up. That is evidence of notice and managerial indifference, not reasonable correction. When Wasek escalated the complaint to Smillie and Strong, they responded by telling him to “kick [Ottobre’s] ass” and “duke it out.” Such responses would permit a jury to find that Arrow failed to take prompt remedial action reasonably calculated to end the harassment. *Hafford*, 183 F.3d at 514; *Jackson*, 191 F.3d at 663-64.

### III. Wasek also presented a constructive discharge claim.

A constructive discharge occurs when working conditions become so intolerable that a reasonable person would feel compelled to resign. *Pennsylvania State Police v. Suders*, 542 U.S. 129, 141-42 (2004); *Bauer v. Curators of Univ. of Mo.*, 680 F.3d 1043, 1046-47 (8th Cir. 2012). Wasek repeatedly sought help, was mocked by supervisors, and was told to fight his harasser rather than receive protection. A jury could conclude that Arrow’s failure to act forced his departure. See *Kraus v. Sobel Corrugated Containers, Inc.*, 915 F.2d 227, 231-32 (6th Cir. 1990).

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
