The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention. Construction of the provision must avoid serious constitutional questions that would otherwise arise. Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886) (aliens within the United States are entitled to equal protection of the laws); Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896) (“all persons within the territory of the United States are entitled to the protection guaranteed by” the Fifth and Sixth Amendments).

Indefinite detention without criminal trial contravenes the historic limits on executive power. Ex parte Bollman, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 136 (1807) (habeas corpus protects against unlawful restraint); Ng Fung Ho v. White, 259 U.S. 276, 284-285 (1922) (deportation proceedings must afford due process). Prolonged confinement after the removal period therefore exceeds statutory authority. Bridges v. Wixon, 326 U.S. 135, 156 (1945) (detention must rest on a lawful basis and may not become punitive).

The statute’s text and purpose confirm that detention serves only the limited end of effecting removal. Ex parte Endo, 323 U.S. 283, 297 (1944) (detention authority “must be construed as a grant of power to detain only for the duration of the” authorized purpose). Once removal ceases to be reasonably foreseeable, continued custody lacks statutory warrant. Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 538 (1952) (detention must bear a reasonable relation to its statutory object).

Even where removal remains theoretically possible, the Constitution forbids detention that has become indefinite. Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 218-219 (1953) (distinguishing temporary from permanent restraint). The provision must therefore be read to impose an implicit time limitation measured by the period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal. United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194, 198 (1957) (statutes conferring broad supervisory power are construed to avoid “serious constitutional questions”).

These authorities establish that the Attorney General lacks power to detain removable aliens indefinitely beyond the removal period. The judgment below should be reversed.
