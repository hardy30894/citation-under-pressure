**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Courts Do Not Possess Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Malpractice Claims That Merely Implicate Patent Law.**

The question presented is whether a state-law legal malpractice claim that requires application of federal patent law necessarily arises under federal patent law for purposes of exclusive federal jurisdiction. It does not. This Court has long held that a case does not "arise under" federal law simply because a federal question is an ingredient of the claim. *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 112 (1936). Rather, the federal question must be the "real substance" of the controversy. *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912).

**A. The Federal Issue Must Be the Essence of the Claim, Not Merely Incidental.**

In *Gully*, this Court explained that "the federal nature of the right to be established is decisive—not the source of the authority to establish it." 299 U.S. at 112. A state malpractice claim, like Minton’s, is fundamentally a dispute about attorney negligence, not patent validity. The patent issue is merely a factual predicate to proving causation. As this Court stated in *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180, 199 (1921), federal jurisdiction exists only when the "right to relief depends upon the construction or application of the Constitution or laws of the United States." Here, Minton’s right to relief depends on whether his attorneys breached a duty of care—a state-law question. The patent issue is secondary.

**B. The Federal Issue Must Be Substantial, Not Collateral.**

In *Hurn v. Oursler*, 289 U.S. 238, 243 (1933), this Court held that federal jurisdiction does not attach when the federal question is "merely incidental" to the state claim. Minton’s malpractice claim requires proof of a "case within a case"—that but for his attorneys’ negligence, he would have prevailed in the patent litigation. But the patent issue is not the "real substance" of the claim; it is a collateral inquiry. *See Gully*, 299 U.S. at 118. The substantiality requirement is not satisfied by a federal issue that is "merely a step in the process of determining a state-law question." *Id.* at 117.

**C. The Federal Issue Must Be Disputed, Not Hypothetical.**

In *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346, 361 (1911), this Court emphasized that federal jurisdiction requires an "actual controversy" involving a "substantial" federal question. Here, the patent issue is not actually disputed in the malpractice suit; it is a hypothetical inquiry into what would have happened in the prior litigation. This Court has consistently rejected jurisdiction over claims that require "speculation" about federal law. *See Fairchild v. Hughes*, 258 U.S. 126, 129 (1922).

**D. Federal Jurisdiction Must Not Disrupt the Balance Between State and Federal Courts.**

In *Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938), this Court recognized the importance of preserving state court authority over state-law claims. Allowing federal jurisdiction over every malpractice case that touches on patent law would "swallow the rule" that state courts are the primary arbiters of state-law claims. *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117. This Court has repeatedly warned against "opening the door" to federal jurisdiction over state claims that merely "involve" federal law. *Id.* at 118.

**II. The Federal Circuit’s Approach Conflicts with This Court’s Precedent.**

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), which the Supreme Court of Texas relied upon, misapplies this Court’s "arising under" jurisprudence. That decision treats any patent issue as substantial, ignoring the requirement that the federal question be the "real substance" of the claim. *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569. This Court has never held that a state malpractice claim "arises under" federal law simply because it requires proof of a federal element. *See Gully*, 299 U.S. at 112.

**III. The Patent Issue Here Is Neither Necessary Nor Substantial.**

Minton’s malpractice claim could be resolved without deciding the patent issue. The trial court granted summary judgment because Minton failed to produce evidence that the TEXCEN lease was primarily experimental. That is a factual question, not a legal one. *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Even if the patent issue were necessary, it is not "substantial" because it does not affect the validity of the patent itself—only the outcome of a state malpractice claim. *See Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed, and the case remanded for consideration on the merits.
