**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency process creates a constitutionally protected liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects “life or liberty” interests that are not merely created by state law, but are “fundamental” in nature and “deeply rooted in our history and tradition.” *Moore v. City of East Cleveland*, 431 U.S. 494, 503 (1977). While the Supreme Court has long recognized that clemency is not a matter of right, *Conn. Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981), it has also held that when a state creates a procedure that significantly affects a prisoner’s liberty, due process protections attach. *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539, 557 (1974). Here, Ohio’s clemency process is not a mere formality; it is the final, constitutionally significant opportunity for a death row inmate to avoid execution. The state’s own procedures confirm this: under Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07, clemency applications must be submitted to the Ohio Adult Parole Authority (APA), and the APA is statutorily required to conduct a clemency review 45 days before any scheduled execution. This mandatory review, triggered by the state’s own timeline, transforms what might otherwise be a discretionary act into a structured, binding process. *See Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 484 (1995) (holding that a liberty interest arises when a state’s procedures “create a legitimate claim of entitlement”).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the “mere existence of a state-created procedure” may give rise to a protected liberty interest when that procedure “significantly affects the duration of a prisoner’s confinement.” *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Neb. Penal & Corr. Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 12 (1979). In *Wolff*, the Court held that even though parole was not a right, the procedures surrounding it created a liberty interest because they “affect the duration of confinement.” 418 U.S. at 557. Similarly, in *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976), the Court acknowledged that while the state may transfer inmates without a hearing, such transfers may implicate due process when they “significantly affect the conditions of confinement.” Here, the APA’s clemency review is not a mere formality—it is the last procedural step before execution. The state’s own regulation, the “Death Penalty Clemency Procedure,” mandates a review and provides for a personal interview. This structured, state-mandated process creates a legitimate expectation that the inmate’s fate will be determined through a formalized, non-arbitrary process. *See Cleveland v. United States*, 329 U.S. 14, 18 (1946) (a liberty interest arises when a state “creates a right to be free from arbitrary deprivation”).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized that the “finality” of a death sentence makes the clemency process “an integral part of the system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant.” *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985). While *Evitts* dealt with appellate counsel, the principle applies with equal force to clemency: when a state provides a mechanism to reconsider a death sentence, that mechanism becomes part of the adjudicative process. *See Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956) (due process applies to “all stages of the adjudicatory process”). The APA’s clemency review is not a mere administrative formality—it is the last chance for a prisoner to present mitigating evidence, to explain his conduct, or to seek mercy. To deny due process at this stage is to render the entire criminal justice system hollow. *See *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 370 (1970) (due process requires that “the State must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt” at every stage of adjudication).

II. The APA’s clemency process violates the Fifth Amendment by coercing a waiver of the right against self-incrimination.

The Fifth Amendment protects individuals from being compelled to be witnesses against themselves. *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436, 460 (1966). This right is not forfeited simply because a person is incarcerated. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 318 (1976). The APA’s clemency procedure, however, creates a “Hobson’s choice”: either participate in a voluntary interview and risk self-incrimination, or remain silent and forfeit any chance of clemency. This is not a mere theoretical concern. The APA’s regulation explicitly states that any statement made during the interview “shall be considered at the clemency hearing.” *See* Ohio Admin. Code § 5120-1-07(C)(2). Thus, the state effectively conditions the opportunity to seek mercy on the waiver of a constitutional right.

The Supreme Court has long held that a state may not condition the receipt of a benefit on the waiver of a constitutional right. *Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 446 (1985).
