# ARGUMENT

## THE THREE-YEAR LIMITATIONS PERIOD REQUIRES SUBJECTIVE, ACTUAL AWARENESS OF THE FACTS UNDERLYING A CLAIM—NOT MERE ACCESS TO DISCLOSED INFORMATION—BUT THAT AWARENESS MAY BE ESTABLISHED BY CIRCUMSTANTIAL EVIDENCE OF DISCLOSURE AND RECEIPT

### A. The Statutory Text Requires "Actual Knowledge," Which Congress Deliberately Distinguished From Constructive Knowledge

Section 1113(2) bars suit unless filed within "three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The word "actual" is not surplusage. When Congress first enacted ERISA, § 1113 contained both an actual-knowledge trigger and a separate constructive-knowledge trigger tied to when a plaintiff "could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge" from public filings. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330 (repealing constructive-knowledge provision). Congress's deliberate elimination of the constructive-knowledge alternative confirms that "actual knowledge" means genuine subjective awareness, not merely the opportunity to acquire knowledge that a reasonable plan participant would have exploited.

This Court has long recognized that ERISA's structure reflects considered congressional choices about the balance between fiduciary accountability and repose. *See Lockheed Corp. v. Spink*, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996) (describing ERISA's imposition of "a duty of care with respect to the management of existing trust funds, along with liability for breach of that duty, upon plan fiduciaries"). Just as this Court declined in *Mertens v. Hewitt Associates*, 508 U.S. 248 (1993), to expand ERISA's remedial scheme beyond what Congress specified, this Court should not stretch § 1113(2)'s actual-knowledge requirement to encompass constructive knowledge that Congress expressly removed from the statute.

### B. "Actual Knowledge" Requires That the Plaintiff In Fact Be Aware of the Underlying Facts, Not Merely That Those Facts Were Disclosed

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that a defendant's compliance with disclosure obligations does not, standing alone, establish that a plaintiff had "actual knowledge" under § 1113(2). Disclosure creates an opportunity for knowledge; it does not itself constitute knowledge. A plaintiff who receives a document but does not read it, or who reads it but does not retain or register its contents, has not thereby become subjectively aware of the facts disclosed.

This reading follows the ordinary meaning of "actual" as opposed to "constructive" or "presumed" knowledge—a distinction this Court applies routinely in construing similar limitations language. *Cf. Heimeshoff v. Hartford Life & Accident Ins. Co.*, 571 U.S. 99 (2013) (enforcing plan-specified limitations periods according to their terms while recognizing the general principle that limitations periods commence according to statutory or contractual triggers, not open-ended equitable inquiry). Requiring genuine subjective awareness also comports with this Court's general reluctance to read into ERISA's carefully calibrated remedial provisions requirements that Congress did not enact. *See Mertens*, 508 U.S. at 254 (declining to expand statutory remedies beyond text); *LaRue v. DeWolff, Boberg & Assocs., Inc.*, 552 U.S. 248 (2008) (construing ERISA's remedial sections according to their specific terms).

Petitioner respectfully submits that the courts below correctly rejected the notion that a fiduciary can trigger the shorter three-year bar simply by placing information in a participant's file or on a website, regardless of whether the participant ever actually processed that information. Such a rule would effectively resurrect the constructive-knowledge standard that Congress deliberately repealed.

### C. Actual Knowledge May Nonetheless Be Proved Through Circumstantial Evidence, Including the Nature and Timing of Disclosures

While actual knowledge cannot be presumed from disclosure alone, it may be established through circumstantial evidence—including the fact, content, and manner of disclosure—when that evidence permits no reasonable inference other than that the plaintiff was in fact aware of the relevant facts. This Court's fiduciary-duty jurisprudence under ERISA has consistently permitted fact-intensive inquiries into what a plan participant actually knew and did. *See Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch*, 489 U.S. 101 (1989) (analyzing participant and beneficiary status by reference to concrete factual circumstances rather than formalistic presumptions).

Where a plan participant signs acknowledgment forms confirming receipt and review of specific disclosures, opens password-protected account statements detailing specific investment allocations, or takes affirmative actions (such as selecting or declining investment options) that are logically explicable only if the participant possessed the relevant knowledge, a factfinder may conclude that the participant had actual knowledge notwithstanding a later claim of non-recollection. The statute does not require an admission of subjective awareness; it requires proof of such awareness, which—like state of mind in other contexts—may rest on inference from action and circumstance.

### D. This Framework Respects Congress's Balance Between Fiduciary Accountability and Repose

ERISA's dual limitations structure—six years running from the last act constituting the breach, and three years running from actual knowledge—reflects a considered legislative balance. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 1113. The six-year period protects fiduciaries against stale claims regardless of a plaintiff's subjective awareness; the three-year period shortens that window only when the plaintiff's own actual knowledge demonstrates that repose is warranted sooner. Collapsing "actual" into "constructive" knowledge would upset that balance by allowing fiduciaries to unilaterally shorten the limitations period through disclosure alone, without regard to whether disclosure achieved its purpose.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed insofar as it holds that actual knowledge requires genuine subjective awareness of the underlying facts, while permitting that awareness to be established through appropriate circumstantial proof on remand.
