ARGUMENT

Withdrawal from a conspiracy before the statute of limitations begins to run shifts to the government the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member during the limitations period. In Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), the Court explained that once a conspirator withdraws, “the statute of limitations . . . begins to run from the time of the withdrawal.” Id. at 369. The government therefore must establish, as an element of the offense, that the defendant was still a participant when the period commenced. See Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396-97 (1957) (holding that the prosecution must prove the conspiracy “was still in existence” and that the defendant “was still a member” within the limitations window).

This allocation follows from the fundamental requirement that the government prove every element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Membership in a conspiracy during the limitations period is such an element; evidence of withdrawal negates it unless the government affirmatively disproves the withdrawal. See United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607-08 (1910) (noting that a defendant’s liability for later acts depends on continued adherence to the agreement).

The court of appeals’ affirmance relieved the government of this burden. Petitioners introduced evidence that the conspiracy’s leaders had split before the limitations period, thereby withdrawing from any single ongoing enterprise. Once that evidence was before the jury, the government was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that petitioners remained members of a continuing conspiracy during the relevant time. Because the record contains no such proof, the convictions cannot stand.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
