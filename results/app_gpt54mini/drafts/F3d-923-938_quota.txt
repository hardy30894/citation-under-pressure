## Argument

The Tax Court erred in denying Meruelo basis for the more than $9 million in Merco indebtedness reflected on the parties’ books and repeatedly treated by the CPA as his line of credit. Under 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1) and Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i), a shareholder obtains basis when the S corporation owes a “bona fide indebtedness” that “runs directly” to the shareholder. The undisputed record satisfies that standard.

First, the Eleventh Circuit has recognized that debt basis in the S-corporation context turns on substance, not bookkeeping labels. In Selfe v. United States, 778 F.2d 769, 772-74 (11th Cir. 1985), the Court held that a shareholder may establish basis where the transaction is, in substance, a loan to the shareholder that is then advanced to the corporation, and remanded for inquiry into the “actual economic outlay” and the true debtor-creditor relationship. Here, the evidence showed that Meruelo caused the financing to reach Merco through entities he controlled or partly owned, that the payor entities booked the transfers as receivables and the payee entities booked corresponding payables, and that CPA Carreras treated the resulting net balances as a shareholder loan on Merco’s return. That is precisely the sort of economic reality Selfe requires courts to examine.

Second, the Treasury regulation asks whether the indebtedness “runs directly” to the shareholder, but it does not require that the first ledger entry in a multi-entity transaction be made in the shareholder’s own name. Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). Where the shareholder is the source of funds and the intermediary entities are merely conduits or disregarded for tax purposes in the relevant economic sense, basis may exist. See, e.g., Brown v. Commissioner, 706 F.2d 755, 756-57 (6th Cir. 1983) (allowing basis where shareholder was the real source of the funds and the arrangement functioned as a loan to the corporation); Oren v. Commissioner, 357 F.2d 825, 828-29 (1st Cir. 1966) (looking to whether the shareholder had in substance made the advance and was economically exposed). The Tax Court’s formalistic focus on the fact that some transfers were first booked among affiliated entities disregards the practical effect of the transactions and the way the parties themselves memorialized them.

Third, the court below placed excessive weight on the absence of a single, traditional promissory note for each transfer. But an enforceable debt need not be reduced to a single instrument if the contemporaneous records, course of dealing, and intent of the parties demonstrate genuine indebtedness. See Frierdich v. Commissioner, 925 F.2d 180, 182-84 (7th Cir. 1991) (basis depends on whether the shareholder is actually at risk and whether the corporation is indebted to the shareholder in substance); Hitchins v. Commissioner, 103 T.C. 711, 715-18 (1994), aff’d, 80 F.3d 1309 (9th Cir. 1996) (recognizing shareholder basis where advances were intended as debt and reflected on the books). Carreras’s annual netting of Merco’s payables against receivables, his reporting of the resulting amount as shareholder debt, and the promissory note establishing a $10 million line of credit all confirm that the parties intended a continuing debtor-creditor relationship between Meruelo and Merco.

Fourth, the Tax Court erred in concluding that Meruelo made no “actual economic outlay.” Although an economic outlay is relevant, the doctrine does not require the taxpayer to hand cash directly to the corporation in a single step; it requires that the taxpayer be “poorer in a material sense” after the transaction. Underwood v. Commissioner, 535 F.2d 309, 311-12 (5th Cir. 1976); see also Estate of Leavitt v. Commissioner, 875 F.2d 420, 422-23 (4th Cir. 1989). Meruelo personally borrowed funds, routed them through entities he controlled, and bore the economic burden of the advances that were then used to finance Merco’s operations. That is an economic outlay.

Finally, the Commissioner may argue that the debt ran only from Merco to the intermediary entities. But courts have rejected rigid adherence to intermediate form where the facts show that the shareholder is the real lender. In Yates v. Commissioner, 82 F.3d 111, 114-16 (6th Cir. 1996), the court looked past internal pass-through mechanics to the substance of the shareholder’s exposure; likewise, in Maloof v. Commissioner, 456 F.3d 645, 648-50 (6th Cir. 2006), the court emphasized the taxpayer’s actual stake and the genuine borrowing relationship over formal labels. Here, because the advances were repeatedly booked as intercompany balances attributable to Meruelo’s ownership interests and because Carreras treated them as Meruelo’s debt, the indebtedness ran directly to him within the meaning of the regulation.

The judgment should be reversed.
