## Argument

Florida’s death-sentencing scheme, as applied here, does not violate the Sixth Amendment or the Eighth Amendment. The Supreme Court’s Sixth Amendment cases require a jury, not a judge, to find the facts that increase a defendant’s authorized punishment. But Florida’s system satisfies that constitutional rule because the capital jury determines guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, and the facts supporting death are found in the course of the penalty phase pursuant to the jury’s recommendation and the trial court’s sentencing order. And nothing in the Eighth Amendment requires jury unanimity or a separate jury determination of mental retardation as a prerequisite to execution.

### I. Ring does not invalidate Florida’s capital sentencing scheme.

In *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), the Court held that any fact, other than a prior conviction, that increases the penalty beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), applied that principle in capital sentencing, holding that aggravating circumstances functioned as the “functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense” and therefore had to be found by a jury. But *Ring* did not announce a rule requiring jury unanimity in capital sentencing, nor did it hold that every aspect of a state’s penalty-phase procedure must be decided by the jury. See *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609; *Kansas v. Carr*, 577 U.S. 108, 119-20 (2016).

Florida’s system has always differed from Arizona’s in a constitutionally significant way. In *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638 (1989), the Court upheld Florida’s capital sentencing procedure, explaining that the jury’s role in recommending sentence, combined with the trial judge’s sentencing responsibility, did not offend the Constitution. Although *Hildwin* predated *Ring*, it remains relevant because *Ring* itself was limited to the specific Arizona scheme at issue. See *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 597. Florida’s procedure gives the defendant the protections that the Sixth Amendment requires: a jury decides the factual questions that make death legally available, and the judge imposes sentence only after those facts are established.

Florida’s Supreme Court has likewise recognized that *Ring* does not compel the additional requirements Hurst seeks. See *Bottoson v. Moore*, 833 So. 2d 693 (Fla. 2002); *Brice v. State*, 815 So. 2d 817 (Fla. 2002). And this Court has previously rejected the claim that *Ring* requires a unanimous jury recommendation of death. See *Coday v. State*, 946 So. 2d 988 (Fla. 2006). The Constitution requires jury factfinding of aggravation; it does not dictate the particular form of the jury’s advisory verdict.

### II. The Eighth Amendment does not require unanimity or a jury finding of mental retardation in Florida.

The Eighth Amendment forbids executing the mentally retarded. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002). But *Atkins* expressly left to the States “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” Id. at 317. Florida has done so through its statutory procedures for presenting and adjudicating intellectual-disability claims. See *Bottoson*, 833 So. 2d at 695; *State v. Wood*, 648 So. 2d 71 (Fla. 1994). Nothing in *Atkins* requires that the jury, rather than the court, decide that threshold issue.

Nor does the Eighth Amendment require a unanimous jury recommendation of death. The Court has repeatedly recognized that capital sentencing must be reliable, but it has not constitutionalized unanimity. See *Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242 (1976); *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153 (1976); *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447 (1984). In *Spaziano*, the Court upheld Florida’s judge-sentencing system and rejected the argument that the Constitution demands jury unanimity as a prerequisite to a death sentence. Id. at 464-65. Although *Ring* abrogated *Spaziano* to the extent it permitted a judge, rather than a jury, to find aggravating facts, *Spaziano* remains instructive on the absence of any constitutional unanimity requirement.

The Court’s Eighth Amendment cases also do not support Hurst’s claim. *Tuilaepa v. California*, 512 U.S. 967 (1994), explains that capital sentencing procedures are valid so long as they channel discretion and permit individualized consideration. *Zant v. Stephens*, 462 U.S. 862 (1983), likewise upheld a structured capital regime that allowed the sentencer to weigh aggravating and mitigating evidence. Florida’s procedure fits comfortably within that framework.

### III. Even if Florida’s procedure were imperfect, any error would not entitle Hurst to relief on the claims presented.

The sentencing record confirms that the jury heard extensive aggravation and mitigation evidence, and the trial court made the operative sentencing findings. The Constitution does not demand perfection; it demands the procedures specifically identified by controlling precedent. See *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1 (1999); *Washington v. Recuenco*, 548 U.S. 212 (2006). Hurst cannot transform his dissatisfaction with Florida’s allocation of responsibilities into a freestanding constitutional violation.

Because *Ring* does not require jury unanimity, because *Atkins* leaves implementation to the States, and because the Eighth Amendment has never been read to mandate the procedure Hurst demands, the judgment below should be affirmed.
