The revision of Georgia Gwinnett College’s policies during litigation ended any live controversy. The students no longer confront the prior restrictions on expressive activity, rendering their requests for declaratory and injunctive relief moot. See Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 310 (1952) (“the case is therefore unlike” one presenting ongoing interference); Board of Ed. of Central School Dist. No. 1 v. Allen, 392 U.S. 236, 241 (1968) (constitutional challenge fails once the operative statute is superseded); Monroe v. Board of Commissioners of Jackson, 391 U.S. 450, 459 (1968) (once discriminatory mechanism is removed, further equitable relief is unavailable).

A bare demand for nominal damages supplies no independent basis for adjudication. See New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 277 (1964) (“The constitutional guarantees require” an actual controversy, not an abstract claim); Murdock v. Pennsylvania, 319 U.S. 105, 109 (1943) (religious activity claims require concrete interference, not hypothetical injury); Jones v. Opelika, 316 U.S. 584, 593 (1942) (licensing dispute becomes moot once enforcement ceases). Courts do not “decide abstract, hypothetical or contingent questions.” Millsaps College v. City of Jackson, 275 U.S. 129, 132 (1927).

The same principle bars continuation of an as-applied challenge once the conduct at issue has been abandoned. See Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District, 393 U.S. 503, 514 (1969) (no ongoing interference once students “went about their ordained rounds”); Robert Zwicker v. James Boll, 391 U.S. 353, 354 (1968) (per curiam) (protest activity claims moot after arrests ended and statute no longer applied); Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts, 388 U.S. 130, 144 (1967) (constitutional issues require “a real dispute” persisting through final judgment).

Because the policies have been “unambiguously terminated” and no reasonable expectation of reenactment exists, the entire action is moot.
