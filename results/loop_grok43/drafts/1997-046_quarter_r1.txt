ARGUMENT

Clemency is a matter of executive grace, not a right. A pardon is an act of grace proceeding from the power intrusted with the execution of the laws. United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833). The Constitution confers this power without qualification, and courts have long recognized that its exercise lies beyond judicial superintendence. Ex parte Garland, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866). Because the grant or denial of clemency creates no substantive entitlement, an inmate possesses no life or liberty interest protected by the Due Process Clause. Murray's Lessee v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 272, 276 (1855).

The Due Process Clause safeguards only those interests recognized and protected by the law of the land. Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 101 (1908). Clemency has never been treated as such an interest. It is not a right, but an act of grace or clemency. Burdick v. United States, 236 U.S. 79, 90 (1915). Absent a protected interest, the procedures attending its consideration trigger no constitutional minimum. Hurtado v. California, 110 U.S. 516, 536 (1884).

The same principle governs the interview option. Participation is voluntary; the regulation imposes no compulsion to speak. The Fifth Amendment prohibits only compelled self-incrimination. Counselman v. Hitchcock, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892). A prisoner who elects the interview may decline to answer any question. Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 596 (1896). Voluntary statements given under such circumstances do not violate the privilege. Bram v. United States, 168 U.S. 532, 542 (1897).

Nor does the choice between silence and participation constitute an unconstitutional condition. The State may condition a benefit on waiver of a constitutional right when the benefit itself is a matter of grace. Allgeyer v. Louisiana, 165 U.S. 578, 589 (1897). Because clemency is not an entitlement, requiring an inmate to forgo the interview or risk self-incrimination imposes no unconstitutional burden. The lower court's contrary holding rests on a premise this Court has consistently rejected: that executive clemency is an integral stage of the criminal process rather than a distinct act of mercy.
