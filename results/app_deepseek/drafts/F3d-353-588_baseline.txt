**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine disputes of material fact exist as to whether Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled under the ADA and MHRA, and because sovereign immunity does not bar Epps’s state-law retaliation claim.

**I. The district court erred in holding that Epps failed to create a genuine issue regarding whether Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled.**

Under the ADA, a “regarded as” claim requires proof that the employer mistakenly believed the employee had a physical impairment that substantially limits a major life activity. *Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999). The employer’s perception, not the employee’s actual impairment, is dispositive. *Id.* at 490–91. A genuine issue exists when the record shows the employer believed the employee’s impairment prevented him from performing a broad class of jobs. *Webb v. Mercy Hosp.*, 102 F.3d 936, 940 (8th Cir. 1996).

Here, Chief Hardy’s March 2000 memorandum stated that Dr. Mirkin’s note—which diagnosed degenerative disk disease and opined that Epps would “very likely need to seek a different type of occupation”—meant Epps “will never be able to function as a regular officer.” Hardy also noted that bending, squatting, and running were required of all officers. Pine Lawn’s Board voted to terminate Epps based on that recommendation. This evidence shows that Pine Lawn believed Epps’s impairment substantially limited his ability to work in the entire class of law-enforcement positions, not merely his particular job. The district court erroneously focused on whether Epps was actually disabled, ignoring the employer’s mistaken perception. *See, e.g., Wooten v. Farmland Foods*, 58 F.3d 382, 386 (8th Cir. 1995) (employer’s belief that employee could not perform “heavy lifting” jobs sufficient to create jury question). Because the MHRA is analyzed under the same framework, *see Daugherty v. City of Maryland Heights*, 231 S.W.3d 814, 819 (Mo. banc 2007), the district court’s identical error on the state-law claim also requires reversal.

**II. The district court erred in holding that sovereign immunity barred Epps’s retaliation claim under Mo. Rev. Stat. § 287.780.**

Missouri’s workers’ compensation retaliation statute provides that “[n]o employer . . . shall discharge . . . any employee because of the employee’s exercise of his or her rights under this chapter.” Mo. Rev. Stat. § 287.780. The statute defines “employer” to include “political subdivisions of the state.” *Id.* § 287.030.1(1). By creating a private cause of action against “any employer” for retaliatory discharge, the Missouri legislature expressly waived sovereign immunity for such claims. *See State ex rel. City of St. Louis v. Ryan*, 676 S.W.2d 1, 3 (Mo. banc 1984) (holding that a statutory cause of action against a municipality necessarily abrogates sovereign immunity). The district court’s blanket application of sovereign immunity ignores this explicit statutory waiver. Moreover, Pine Lawn’s purchase of workers’ compensation insurance further waives immunity under Mo. Rev. Stat. § 537.610.1. *See, e.g., Richardson v. City of St. Louis*, 293 S.W.3d 133, 137 (Mo. App. E.D. 2009). Epps presented evidence that he filed workers’ compensation claims, that Chief Hardy cited those claims in his termination memoranda, and that the Board terminated him shortly after his surgery. This creates a genuine dispute whether the termination was retaliatory.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the district court’s summary judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
