ARGUMENT

Section 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication as a threat. The statute’s text—“any threat to injure the person of another”—imports the common-law understanding that a threat is a communication expressing the speaker’s intent to inflict harm. See United States v. Prochaska, 222 F.2d 1, 2 (7th Cir. 1955). An objective “reasonable speaker” test, United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), therefore misapplies the statute.

The First Amendment independently demands the same subjective standard. In Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), the Court defined true threats as “those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” Id. at 359. That formulation requires the speaker’s purpose, not merely foreseeability. The Court reiterated that the prohibition on true threats protects against “the fear of violence” and the “disruption that fear engenders,” id. at 360, interests that an objective test cannot reliably serve without sweeping in protected speech.

Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), confirms the point. There the Court reversed a conviction because the defendant’s statement was “political hyperbole” rather than a serious expression of intent. Id. at 708. The distinction turns on the speaker’s actual intent, not on how a reasonable listener might construe the words.

An objective standard chills core First Amendment speech. Speakers cannot reliably predict whether their words will be deemed threatening by some hypothetical reasonable person, especially in the context of rap lyrics, satire, or emotional venting on social media. See New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 279-80 (1964) (requiring actual malice to avoid self-censorship). The Third Circuit’s rule therefore “fails to provide the breathing space” the Constitution demands. NAACP v. Button, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963).

Because the district court instructed the jury solely under the objective standard, the convictions rest on an erroneous legal premise. The judgment should be reversed.
