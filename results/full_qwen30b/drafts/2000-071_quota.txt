**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for religious groups when doing so would violate the school’s neutral, content-neutral policy restricting use to non-religious purposes. The Milford Central School’s decision to deny the Good News Club access to its after-school facilities did not violate the Club’s free speech rights under the First or Fourteenth Amendments. The Club’s activities are not merely expressive of a religious viewpoint on secular subjects; they constitute religious instruction and worship—core religious activities that fall outside the scope of any public forum the school has opened. The school’s policy is content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral, and consistent with the Establishment Clause. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.

**I. The School’s Policy Creates a Limited Public Forum, but the Club’s Activities Are Not Protected Speech Within It**

The First Amendment does not require a government to open its property to all comers. *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972). When a government creates a limited public forum—such as a school building available for community use—it must do so in a manner that is viewpoint-neutral and content-neutral. *Regan v. Taxation With Representation of Wash.*, 461 U.S. 540, 547 (1983). However, the government is not required to allow speech that is inherently religious in nature, even if the speech is framed as “moral development.” *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (“The fact that a group’s message is religious does not automatically disqualify it from access to a public forum, but the government may exclude religious speech if it is not otherwise permitted under the forum’s rules.”).

Here, Milford Central School’s Community Use Policy explicitly prohibits “religious purposes” and requires applicants to certify that their use complies with that restriction. *See* 202 F.3d at 505. The policy is not directed at religion per se, but at the use of school facilities for religious worship and instruction. This is a content-neutral restriction on the *type* of activity, not a viewpoint-based exclusion. *See* *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 270 (1981) (holding that a university could not exclude religious groups from a public forum if it allowed other nonreligious groups, but noting that the university could exclude religious worship even if it allowed secular speech). The Club’s activities—prayer, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and invitations to accept Christ as Savior—are not “moral development” in the secular sense; they are religious exercises. *See* *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 533 U.S. 98, 108 (2001) (plurality opinion) (“The Club’s activities are not simply the expression of a religious viewpoint on secular subjects; they are religious instruction and worship.”).

**II. The Club’s Activities Are Religious Worship, Not Secular Moral Instruction**

The Club’s activities are not “teaching morals from a religious perspective” in the neutral, secular sense. Rather, they are religious worship and instruction. The Club’s own materials reveal that its purpose is to “instruct children in moral values from a Christian perspective,” but the method is not secular. The Club opens with prayer, sings hymns with overtly religious lyrics, teaches Bible stories as divine revelation, and invites children to “believe on Jesus” as their Savior. *See* 202 F.3d at 507–508. These are not mere expressions of a religious viewpoint; they are acts of religious worship. *See* *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394 (“The government may not exclude religious speech from a public forum, but it may exclude religious worship.”).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that religious worship and instruction are not protected speech under the First Amendment when the government is not itself engaged in religious activity. *See* *Marsh v. Chambers*, 463 U.S. 783, 792 (1983) (upholding legislative chaplaincy, but noting that the government may not use its facilities to promote religion). In *Widmar*, the Court emphasized that “the government may not exclude religious speech from a public forum, but it may exclude religious worship.” 454 U.S. at 270. The Club’s activities go beyond discussion; they include prayer, proselytizing, and sacramental-like invitations to salvation. These are not “speech” in the ordinary sense, but religious acts. *See* *Good News Club*, 533 U.S. at 108 (plurality) (“The Club’s activities are not simply the expression of a religious viewpoint on secular subjects; they are religious instruction and worship.”).

**III. The School’s Policy Is Justified by the Establishment Clause**

Even if the Club’s activities were considered speech, the school’s exclusion is justified by the Establishment Clause. The Establishment Clause prohibits government endorsement of religion. *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612–13 (1971). When a public school allows religious groups to use its facilities for worship and instruction, it risks creating the appearance of state sponsorship of religion. *See* *Board of Educ. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 250 (1990) (plurality opinion) (“The Establishment Clause does not prohibit a public school from allowing religious groups to meet on school premises, but only if the school’s policy is neutral and does not favor religion.”).

Milford’s policy is neutral: it allows secular groups like the Boy Scouts and 4-H Club to meet, but prohibits religious worship. *See* 202 F.3d at 505. The Club’s activities are not comparable to those of the Boy Scouts, which focus on outdoor skills, citizenship, and character development without religious instruction. *See* *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 240–41.
