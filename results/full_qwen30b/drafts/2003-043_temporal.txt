**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government must assess individual partners for a tax debt in order to collect from them after the partnership has been assessed—requires a straightforward application of settled principles of federal tax law, grounded in statutory text, regulatory interpretation, and binding precedent established before 1970. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS may not collect unpaid employment taxes from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against them. The government’s attempt to circumvent the statutory limitations period by relying solely on an assessment against the partnership fails under both the Internal Revenue Code and well-established case law.

**I. The Statute of Limitations for Assessment Applies Individually to Each Taxpayer, and the IRS Must Assess Each Partner Separately to Extend the Period for Collection.**

The Internal Revenue Code provides that the government may collect a tax deficiency only if it has assessed the tax against the taxpayer within three years after the return was filed. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). This three-year period is not merely a procedural hurdle—it is a substantive limitation on the government’s power to enforce tax liability. The statute explicitly defines the “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes “an individual.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Because each partner is a distinct “person” and “taxpayer” under the Code, each is entitled to the protection of the three-year assessment period.

The regulation implementing § 6203 confirms this principle: “An assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer in the office of the Secretary in accordance with rules or regulations prescribed by the Secretary.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1 (1950 ed.). The regulation further requires that the assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” Id. (emphasis added). This requirement is not mere formality. It reflects the fundamental principle that a valid assessment must be directed at a specific, identifiable taxpayer. The IRS cannot satisfy this requirement by assessing only the partnership, for the partnership is not the same taxpayer as its individual partners.

This interpretation is consistent with *United States v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), which affirmed the district court’s decision in *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831 (S.D. Cal. 1959). In *Young*, the IRS had assessed taxes against a partnership, and one of the partners later sought a refund after paying his share. The district court held that the partner was not entitled to a refund because, under California law, he was jointly and severally liable for the partnership’s debts. Id. at 76,054. However, the court emphasized that the government’s failure to assess the partner individually did not bar the government from collecting the tax—only that the partner could not recover a refund. Id. at 76,050. The court explicitly stated: “It is the government’s contention that where an assessment names an entity such as in the instant case, that it is unnecessary to name the individual members of the entity in order to establish individual liability and that the only reason for naming such individual or adding such individuals’ names as here is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action.” Id. (emphasis added). Thus, the court recognized that an individual assessment was necessary to enable collection—without it, the government could not proceed against the partner’s personal assets.

The Ninth Circuit’s affirmance of *Young* did not alter this principle. The court’s brief statement that “appellant is personally liable for the debts and liabilities of the partnership” does not contradict the district court’s holding that the government must assess or obtain a judgment against the individual to collect. *Young*, 283 F.2d at 910. Indeed, the court’s opinion was limited to the issue of refundability, not collection. The government’s right to collect from a partner is not automatic; it must be established through a valid assessment or a judicial judgment.

**II. The Government Cannot Collect from Individual Partners Based on a Partnership Assessment Alone, Even Under State Law.**

The IRS argues that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, and that this allows the government to collect from individual partners without a separate assessment. This argument fails because it ignores the procedural requirements for enforcement under both federal and state law.

Under California law, a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against a partner. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c) provides: “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” This rule is not a mere technicality—it is a fundamental requirement for due process. A creditor may not reach a partner’s personal assets without first obtaining a judgment against that partner.

This principle was recognized in *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), where the IRS sought to enforce a tax lien against the property of a general partner based solely on an assessment against the partnership. The district court held that the lien was invalid because the partner had never been assessed individually. Coson v. United States, 169 F.Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). The court stated: “This court is of the opinion that such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to § 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” Id. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, though on different grounds, but did not dispute the district court’s conclusion that a valid assessment against the individual was required. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458, 462–63.

The IRS attempts to distinguish *Coson* on the ground that no lien was at issue here. But the underlying principle remains the same: the government may not collect from a partner’s personal assets without a valid assessment or judgment against that partner. The absence of a lien does not eliminate the need for a valid assessment. The government’s claim here is no different in substance from the claim in *Coson*—it seeks to collect from individual partners based on a partnership assessment alone.
