**ARGUMENT**

**I. OSHA’s Statutory Authority Over Workplaces, Including Vessels, Is Not Preempted by the Coast Guard’s Regulatory Role.**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) applies to “employment performed in a workplace.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The Act defines “workplace” broadly to include “any establishment, job site, or premises where work is performed by an employee.” *Id.* § 652(10). The Fifth Circuit has recognized that this definition encompasses vessels engaged in industrial activity, such as drilling barges. *See Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 2001 WL 1245600, at *3 (E.D. La. Oct. 19, 2001) (citing *Martin v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n*, 499 U.S. 144, 152 (1991)).

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act excludes from OSHA’s jurisdiction only those workplaces “with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard’s authority under 14 U.S.C. § 2 and 46 U.S.C. § 6301 does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction over occupational safety on vessels because the Coast Guard’s regulatory role is limited to maritime safety, not occupational safety. *See Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825, 827 (5th Cir. 1983) (holding that OSHA’s exclusion applies only where another agency’s authority “affects occupational safety or health”).

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), does not compel a contrary result. *Clary* involved a seaman’s personal injury claim, not an OSHA enforcement action, and the court’s observation that OSHA does not apply to “working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation” was dicta. *Id.* at 1122. Moreover, *Clary* did not address whether the Coast Guard’s authority preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction over industrial vessels like drilling barges. *See Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826 (distinguishing *Clary* on its facts).

**II. The Coast Guard’s Limited Regulation of Uninspected Vessels Does Not Preempt OSHA’s Jurisdiction.**

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is confined to discrete safety matters, such as lifesaving equipment and fire suppression. 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. These regulations do not address occupational hazards unique to industrial operations, such as drilling activities. *See Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 828 (noting that the Coast Guard’s regulations are “geared to the safety of the vessel and its crew, not to the safety of the workplace environment”).

The Fifth Circuit has repeatedly rejected the argument that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction over vessels preempts OSHA’s authority. *See Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826–27; *Martin*, 499 U.S. at 152. The Supreme Court has emphasized that OSHA’s jurisdiction is not displaced unless another federal agency’s authority “affects occupational safety or health.” *Martin*, 499 U.S. at 152. The Coast Guard’s regulations do not meet this standard.

**III. The OSH Act’s Coverage of Vessels Is Consistent with Congressional Intent.**

Congress enacted the OSH Act to ensure “every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions.” 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). The Act’s coverage extends to all workplaces, including those on navigable waters. *See id.* § 653(a). The Fifth Circuit has recognized that drilling barges are workplaces under the Act. *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 2001 WL 1245600, at *3.

The Coast Guard’s limited role in regulating uninspected vessels does not negate OSHA’s jurisdiction. *See Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 829 (rejecting the argument that OSHA’s jurisdiction fluctuates based on a vessel’s inspection status). The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Mallard Bay* conflicts with this well-established precedent and should be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should hold that OSHA has jurisdiction over occupational safety on uninspected vessels like Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52. The judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed.
