**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Vests Title to Riverbeds in the State Only Where the River Was Navigable at the Time of Statehood.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State acquired title to the riverbeds underlying PPL’s dams under the equal footing doctrine only because the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time Montana achieved statehood in 1889. *See United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (“The title thus vested in the State is subject to the paramount power of Congress to control the navigation of the waters within the State.”); *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) (explaining that the equal footing doctrine grants states title to lands beneath navigable waters). This Court has long recognized that the navigability inquiry for title purposes is fixed at statehood. *See, e.g., Packard v. Baird*, 84 U.S. 168, 177 (1872) (“The question of navigability is to be determined as of the time the State was admitted into the Union.”); *Bonelli Cattle Co. v. Arizona*, 414 U.S. 313, 329 (1973) (holding that the test for navigability for title purposes is whether the river was navigable at statehood).

PPL’s contention that the Court should consider later periods of commercial use is foreclosed by this Court’s precedents. *See Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9, 10 (1971) (“The test of navigability is the same as that applied in ascertaining the scope of the federal navigational servitude.”); *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14 (navigability for title purposes is determined by conditions at statehood). The Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to relitigate navigability based on post-statehood events. *See, e.g., Packard*, 84 U.S. at 177; *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 79 (1931) (holding that navigability is determined as of the time of statehood, not by later commercial developments).

**II. The Montana Supreme Court Properly Applied the Federal Test for Navigability at Statehood.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the federal test for navigability at statehood, which examines whether the river was capable of supporting interstate or foreign commerce at that time. *See The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870) (“Those rivers must be regarded as public navigable rivers in law which are navigable in fact. And they are navigable in fact when they are used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water.”); *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 407-08 (1940) (same). The Court has consistently applied this test to determine whether a river was navigable for title purposes. *See, e.g., Bonelli Cattle*, 414 U.S. at 329; *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14.

PPL’s reliance on post-statehood evidence to challenge the navigability determination is misplaced. The Court has made clear that the navigability inquiry is fixed at statehood and does not consider later commercial developments. *See Packard*, 84 U.S. at 177; *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 79. The Montana Supreme Court properly relied on historical evidence of navigability at statehood, which is admissible under the federal rules and this Court’s precedents. *See Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(e)* (allowing affidavits based on admissible evidence); *Smith v. First Nat’l Bank*, 2005 MT 219, ¶ 39 (Mont. 2005) (affidavits must be based on personal knowledge or admissible hearsay).

**III. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt the State’s Claims for Compensation.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the Federal Power Act (FPA) does not preempt the State’s claims for compensation under the Hydroelectric Resources Act (HRA). The FPA establishes a “dual system” of federal and state regulation, preserving state authority over proprietary rights. *See First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 167-68 (1946) (“The Federal Power Act established a dual system of control over the development of water power, with the Federal Government exercising its authority primarily through the licensing provisions of the Act, and the States exercising their traditional authority over the appropriation and use of water.”); *Public Utility Dist. No. 1 of Pend Oreille Cty. v. City of Seattle*, 382 F.2d 666, 671-72 (9th Cir. 1967) (holding that the FPA does not preempt state law claims for compensation).

The Court has repeatedly recognized that the FPA does not preempt state property rights or claims for compensation. *See, e.g., Niagara Mohawk Power Corp. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 347 U.S. 239, 249-51 (1954) (holding that the FPA does not preempt state law claims for compensation); *Nez Perce Tribe v. Idaho Power Co.*, 847 F. Supp. 791, 812 (D. Idaho 1993) (holding that the FPA does not preempt state common law claims against FERC licensees). The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that the State’s claims for compensation under the HRA are not preempted by the FPA.

**IV. The Federal Navigational Servitude Does Not Bar the State’s Claims.**

The federal navigational servitude does not bar the State’s claims for compensation. The servitude grants the federal government the power to regulate navigation but does not divest the State of its title to riverbeds or its authority to seek compensation for their use. *See United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596-97 (1941) (explaining the scope of the federal navigational servitude); *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. at 551 (holding that the servitude does not extinguish state title to riverbeds).

The Court has consistently held that the navigational servitude does not preempt state law claims for compensation. *See, e.g., Niagara Mohawk*, 347 U.S. at 249-51; *Central Stockholders’ Corp. of Vallejo v. United States*, 52 F.2d 322, 331-32 (9th Cir. 1931) (holding that the servitude does not preempt state law claims for compensation). The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that the federal navigational servitude does not bar the State’s claims.

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the federal test for navigability at statehood, held that the FPA does not preempt the State’s claims for compensation, and determined that the federal navigational servitude does not bar the State’s claims. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
