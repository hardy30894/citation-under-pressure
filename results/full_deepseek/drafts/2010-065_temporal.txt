**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO INVOKE THE TENTH AMENDMENT.**

The Tenth Amendment is a structural protection for the States, not a personal right of individuals. This Court has long held that private parties may not challenge federal action on the ground that it exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers and invades state sovereignty. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939). In *Tennessee Electric*, private utility companies argued that the sale of electricity by the federally chartered TVA violated the Tenth Amendment. This Court rejected the claim, holding that “absent the states or their officers, [the private parties] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Id.*

That holding is binding here. Bond is a private individual, not a State or state officer. She seeks to challenge a federal statute on the ground that it intrudes upon powers reserved to the States. But the Tenth Amendment “is not a source of individual rights”; it “limits the power of the Federal Government by reserving to the States certain powers not delegated to the United States.” *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). Because the Amendment protects only state sovereignty, only a State—or a state officer acting on behalf of the State—may invoke it. *See Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 488 (1923) (private citizen lacks standing to challenge federal statute as infringing state powers because the “appellee is not a State” and “the State alone may vindicate its own authority”).

Bond’s reliance on *Stromberg v. California*, 283 U.S. 359 (1931), is misplaced. There, this Court permitted a private party to challenge a state statute as violating the First Amendment—a provision that secures individual liberties. The Tenth Amendment, by contrast, “does not confer any right upon an individual.” *Darby*, 312 U.S. at 124. Bond’s claim is not that her own rights have been violated, but that the federal government has encroached upon the domain of the States. That is a grievance that belongs to the States alone.

**II. THE STATUTE IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S POWER TO IMPLEMENT A TREATY.**

Even if Bond had standing, her Tenth Amendment challenge would fail because Congress had authority to enact 18 U.S.C. § 229 under the Necessary and Proper Clause in conjunction with the Treaty Power. The Constitution provides that the President, with the advice and consent of the Senate, “shall have Power, by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate, to make Treaties.” U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2. And Congress may “make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution” the powers vested in the federal government. U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18.

In *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), this Court upheld a federal statute implementing a treaty protecting migratory birds, even though the statute regulated activity that would otherwise be within state control. The Court reasoned that “if the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute” enacted to carry it out. *Id.* at 432. The Court further observed that “it is obvious that there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could.” *Id.* at 433.

The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty. It obligates the United States to “prohibit natural and legal persons anywhere on its territory . . . from undertaking any activity prohibited . . . under this Convention, including enacting penal legislation with respect to such activity.” 32 I.L.M. 800, 810 (1993). Section 229 implements that obligation by criminalizing the possession and use of chemical weapons. Because the treaty is valid, the implementing statute is likewise valid under the Necessary and Proper Clause. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432.

Bond argues that the statute lacks a “federal interest” element, such as an effect on interstate commerce. But the Treaty Power is not so limited. In *Holland*, the Court upheld a statute that regulated the killing of migratory birds—a matter of local concern—because the treaty authorized it. *Id.* at 435. The Court explained that the treaty power “is not limited by the same restrictions as those which limit the powers of Congress in dealing with matters of internal concern.” *Id.* at 433. Thus, even if § 229 would be invalid under the Commerce Clause, it is valid as a necessary and proper means of implementing a treaty.

**III. THE STATUTE IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE.**

Bond’s vagueness challenge also fails. A statute is void for vagueness only if it “fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that his contemplated conduct is forbidden.” *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954). Section 229 plainly prohibits the “use” of a “chemical weapon,” defined as a “toxic chemical” that causes “death, temporary incapacitation or permanent harm.” 18 U.S.C. §§ 229(a)(1), 229F(8)(A). Bond repeatedly spread toxic chemicals on her victim’s doorknobs, car handles, and mailbox. Her conduct falls squarely within the statute’s core prohibition. *See Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 102 (1945) (statute is not vague as applied to conduct that clearly violates it).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
