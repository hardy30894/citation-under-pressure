# ARGUMENT

## I. The Equal Protection Clause Does Not Convert Every Individualized Municipal Decision Into a Constitutional Claim.

Grace Olech's complaint alleges no more than an ordinary, individualized dispute over the terms on which the Village would extend a municipal water connection to her lot. She does not allege that the Village singled her out because she belongs to any class defined by race, religion, or any other disfavored characteristic. Instead, she asks this Court to treat any difference in the treatment of one landowner, standing entirely alone, as a violation of the Equal Protection Clause. That theory cannot be squared with the structure of municipal utility regulation that this Court has long recognized.

Municipalities routinely impose individualized conditions—easements, connection fees, service charges—when extending utility service to particular parcels, because the physical and economic circumstances of each connection differ. In *Consumers' Co. v. Hatch*, 224 U.S. 148 (1912), the dispute arose because the water company "declined to make the desired connection because of the refusal of Hatch to pay, as required by the regulations of the company, $8.50, the cost of making the connection." Individualized charges of that kind are the ordinary business of water-service administration, not evidence of an equal protection violation. Similarly, in *Boise Artesian Hot & Cold Water Co. v. Boise City*, 230 U.S. 84 (1913), this Court recognized that water companies were "required . . . to furnish water at 'reasonable rates' to be determined by commissioners," confirming that rate- and condition-setting for water service is committed to administrative and legislative discretion, subject to review for reasonableness—not to constitutional attack whenever one customer's terms differ from another's.

## II. Municipal Decisions Regarding the Terms of Utility Extensions Are Entitled to Deference and Are Not Subject to Judicial Second-Guessing Absent Class-Based Discrimination.

This Court has repeatedly declined to treat municipal utility and infrastructure decisions as constitutionally suspect merely because they burden a particular property owner differently from others. In *Norwood v. Baker*, 172 U.S. 269 (1898), this Court quoted with approval the principle that a municipal body's power "to determine the question of the propriety and necessity of laying water mains and water pipes, and of erecting fire plugs and hydrants," is committed to municipal discretion, and that "their bona fide exercise of such power cannot be reviewed by the courts." That principle governs here. The Village's determination of the easement necessary to accommodate its water infrastructure at Olech's particular parcel is precisely the sort of engineering and administrative judgment that courts have long left to local officials, absent proof of an invidious, class-based motive.

Nor is deference to municipal utility decisions eroded merely because a plaintiff can point to other property owners who received more favorable terms. In *City of Joplin v. Southwest Missouri Light Co.*, 191 U.S. 150 (1903), this Court observed that "[t]here are presumptions . . . against . . . limitations upon the powers of government," and cautioned against construing municipal arrangements in ways that would hamstring a city's control over its own utility infrastructure. That presumption of regularity forecloses the inference—drawn by the Seventh Circuit below—that a demand for a larger easement from one landowner, without more, betrays an unconstitutional motive. Likewise, in *Skaneateles Water Works Co. v. Skaneateles*, 184 U.S. 354 (1902), this Court held that a municipality's decision to build and operate its own water works, even though it diminished the value of a competitor's property, worked no constitutional violation, because "[t]he property of the plaintiff remains wholly untouched." So too here: the Village's insistence on a 33-foot easement, whatever its wisdom, deprived Olech of nothing to which she was entitled, and differential treatment of that kind—absent proof of animus toward a protected class—is not a constitutional injury.

## III. Recognizing a "Class of One" Theory Untethered from Class-Based Discrimination Would Federalize Ordinary Zoning and Utility Disputes.

The decisions of this Court addressing municipal utility relationships—easements, connection charges, franchise terms—make clear that these are matters of local administration, reviewable, if at all, through state-law mechanisms and traditional arbitrary-and-capricious review, not through the Equal Protection Clause. In *Ohio Public Service Co. v. Ohio ex rel. Fritz*, 274 U.S. 12 (1927), and *Rogers Park Water Co. v. Fergus*, 180 U.S. 624 (1901), this Court treated disputes over the precise terms of municipal franchise and utility arrangements as questions of contract and statutory construction to be resolved according to "strict construction," not as occasions for constitutional override. If every landowner who receives terms less favorable than a neighbor's may sue under the Equal Protection Clause by alleging "spite," then every zoning variance, every road-easement negotiation, and every fee schedule becomes a potential federal case. That result cannot be reconciled with the deference this Court's precedents accord to municipal administration of water systems and other infrastructure.

## CONCLUSION

Because Olech alleged no class-based discrimination, and because the terms the Village required for connecting her property to municipal water fall squarely within the discretion municipalities have long exercised over utility extensions, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
