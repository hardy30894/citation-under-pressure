**Argument**

The district court erred in treating the severance agreements as a defense to Plaintiffs’ WARN claims. The WARN Act creates a statutory entitlement to advance notice of covered plant closings and mass layoffs, and that entitlement is not lightly relinquished. *Workers rarely waive statutory rights absent a clear and knowing relinquishment*, and the burden rests on the employer to show a valid waiver. See *Brooklyn Sav. Bank v. O’Neil*, 324 U.S. 697, 704–07 (1945); *Aetna Ins. Co. v. Kennedy*, 301 U.S. 389, 393–94 (1937).

I. The releases are unenforceable to the extent they purport to waive WARN rights.

Congress enacted WARN to protect workers and communities from sudden employment loss by requiring advance notice before a covered shutdown or layoff. *See* *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 115–17 (1941) (explaining Congress’s broad power to regulate employment conditions affecting interstate commerce). A private agreement cannot nullify the statutory policy unless Congress has clearly permitted waiver. *Brooklyn Sav. Bank*, 324 U.S. at 706–07. Here, the releases were presented only after DHL had already announced the end of domestic shipping and after the shutdown process was underway. They were not a negotiated exchange for surrender of a known, mature claim in the ordinary sense; they were part of a mass severance process designed to manage the consequences of a corporate decision already made.

The language of the release cannot do the work DHL asks of it. Although it mentions the WARN Act by name, boilerplate recitals are not enough where the surrounding circumstances show no “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). The employees were given short windows to decide, some while still working under urgent rebid deadlines, and the releases were embedded in severance paperwork. Such circumstances do not establish the “clear, decisive, and unequivocal” waiver required before a statutory protection is surrendered. *Aetna Ins. Co.*, 301 U.S. at 393–94; *Johnson*, 304 U.S. at 464.

II. The releases are invalid because they were induced by severance benefits to which DHL had already become obligated by the closure.

A release is supported by consideration only if the employee receives something new in exchange for relinquishing the claim. *See* *Robinson v. Jewett*, 116 N.Y. 40, 44–45 (1889) (release requires consideration). But benefits promised as part of a company-wide response to a shutdown cannot automatically serve as consideration for giving up statutory rights where the employer was already under a legal duty not to impose the injury complained of without notice. The WARN Act’s notice requirement is not a gratuity; it is the substantive protection Congress chose to require. *See* *Fleming v. A. H. Belo Corp.*, 121 F.2d 423, 425–26 (5th Cir. 1941), aff’d, 316 U.S. 624 (1942) (recognizing courts must respect the statutory purpose behind wage protections). DHL’s severance payments were plainly intended to cushion the very harm WARN addresses. Allowing those payments to purchase a waiver of the Act would undermine the statute’s enforcement scheme.

III. The class members’ acceptance of severance does not bar their claims.

DHL argues that by signing the release and resigning, the employees extinguished all claims. But surrender of a claim is effective only if the claimant fully understands the rights being surrendered and the release is not contrary to public policy. *Brooklyn Sav. Bank*, 324 U.S. at 704–07. The district court’s approach would permit an employer to announce a shutdown, offer time-pressured severance packages, and then buy immunity from WARN by inserting the statute’s name into a form release. That is not a valid waiver; it is an end run around federal law. Courts do not enforce private arrangements that defeat the operation of a statute enacted for worker protection. *Aetna Ins. Co.*, 301 U.S. at 393–94; *Johnson*, 304 U.S. at 464.

Moreover, the facts show individualized pressure and asymmetric information. The drivers had to decide their severance election at the same time they were rebidding shifts, some workers had only days to act, and the record shows that the company’s shutdown announcement had already forced the bargaining units into crisis bargaining. Under those conditions, the putative waiver was not the product of free and deliberate choice. *See* *Johnson*, 304 U.S. at 464. The fact that 506 workers signed does not transform an invalid waiver into a valid one; mass execution of a defective release remains defective.

IV. At minimum, any waiver must be construed narrowly and cannot cover statutory damages arising from DHL’s WARN violation.

Even if the Court were to accept that some employees could settle individual claims, the releases should be read narrowly. A general release is construed against the drafter and does not lightly surrender important statutory protections. *See* *United States v. Richards*, 281 U.S. 513, 520–21 (1930) (release terms construed in light of context). Here, the operative language refers to severance “beyond anything” already owed, yet the question in this suit is whether DHL’s failure to give WARN notice made the workers entitled to statutory damages. A release executed in the shadow of that violation cannot be read to erase the statute itself.

Because the WARN Act protects against exactly the sort of forced, last-minute employment losses DHL imposed, and because the record does not establish a knowing, voluntary, and supported waiver of those rights, the judgment should be reversed.
