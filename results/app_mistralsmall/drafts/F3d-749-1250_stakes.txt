**ARGUMENT**

**I. The district court plainly erred by admitting Agent Jones’s unreliable expert testimony on credibility.**

Agent Jones’s testimony that Stanley’s statements were “not worthy of credence” and that invoking God to support his truthfulness was a “deceptive statement” impermissibly invaded the jury’s province to assess credibility. *United States v. Azure*, 845 F.3d 1171, 1180 (10th Cir. 2017) (“Expert testimony on credibility is per se inadmissible under Rule 702.”). The Supreme Court has long cautioned that jurors do not need expert assistance to determine whether a witness is telling the truth. *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 313 (1998) (“The jury is the lie detector.”). Agent Jones’s reliance on supposed “evasive behaviors” and “deceptive” invocations of faith rested on nothing more than subjective speculation, not scientific or specialized knowledge. *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 592 (1993) (expert testimony must be “ground[ed] in the methods and procedures of science”).

The error was plain because the testimony directly contradicted settled law, and it affected Stanley’s substantial rights. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993). Agent Jones’s assertion that “never in [his] career” had he seen an innocent person make a particular statement is the epitome of unreliable expert opinion. *United States v. Roach*, 951 F.3d 1177, 1185 (10th Cir. 2020) (excluding expert testimony that improperly bolstered a witness’s credibility). The government’s attempt to shoehorn this testimony in under Rule 702 must fail. *Azure*, 845 F.3d at 1181 (“Rule 702 does not permit experts to opine on the credibility of witnesses.”).

**II. The district court erred by denying Stanley’s motion for a judgment of acquittal because the evidence was insufficient to prove he was a participant in the robbery.**

Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government, *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979), no rational jury could have found Stanley guilty beyond a reasonable doubt. The GPS tracking device led officers to the East Pine residence, where Stanley was found *two hours* after the robbery. The government presented no evidence that Stanley was involved in planning, executing, or benefiting from the robbery. His mere presence at the residence hours later, without more, is insufficient to establish participation. *United States v. Long*, 706 F.3d 1225, 1233 (10th Cir. 2013) (“Mere presence at the scene of a crime and association with the wrongdoers are insufficient to support a conviction.”).

The government’s theory shifted dramatically between trials—first alleging Stanley was a robber, then conceding he was only a getaway driver, and finally abandoning that theory altogether. This inconsistency underscores the lack of evidence. *United States v. Hanzlicek*, 187 F.3d 1228, 1233 (10th Cir. 1999) (“A conviction cannot stand on mere suspicion or conjecture.”). The evidence showed only that Stanley was at the residence where the stolen money was found, but proximity alone does not prove guilt. *United States v. McKissick*, 204 F.3d 1282, 1290 (10th Cir. 2000) (“The government must prove more than mere association with criminals or knowledge of criminal activity.”).

**III. Stanley’s conviction for being a felon in possession of a firearm must be reversed because the firearm was not shown to be in his actual or constructive possession.**

The government failed to prove Stanley constructively possessed the firearm. Constructive possession requires “ownership, dominion, or control over the [firearm] and knowledge of its presence.” *United States v. Jones*, 44 F.3d 860, 865 (10th Cir. 1995). The evidence showed only that a 9mm firearm was used in the robbery and that Stanley was at the residence where the tracking device stopped. There was no evidence linking the firearm to Stanley personally. *United States v. Rios*, 611 F.3d 1179, 1185 (10th Cir. 2010) (“Mere presence in a residence where contraband is found is insufficient to establish constructive possession.”).

The government’s case rested on speculation that Stanley must have possessed the firearm because he was at the residence. This is precisely the type of conjecture that the law forbids. *United States v. Valadez-Gallegos*, 167 F.3d 1262, 1265 (10th Cir. 1999) (“The government cannot convict a defendant based on mere speculation or inference.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Stanley’s conviction should be reversed. The admission of Agent Jones’s unreliable expert testimony on credibility was plain error. The evidence was insufficient to prove Stanley’s participation in the robbery, and the government failed to prove his constructive possession of the firearm. The judgment of the district court should be vacated, and Stanley should be discharged.
