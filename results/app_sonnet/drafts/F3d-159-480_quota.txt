ARGUMENT

I. Section 1951 Exceeds Congress's Commerce Clause Authority

Congress may reach only conduct that "substantially affects interstate commerce." United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 559 (1995). The Hobbs Act's jurisdictional element cannot be satisfied by speculative or de minimis effects; the government must prove an actual, not merely potential, connection between the charged robbery and interstate commerce. Stirone v. United States, 361 U.S. 212, 215 (1960) (Hobbs Act requires proof that the offense "in some way or degree obstructs, delays, or affects commerce"). Here, the government's only proof was that Mr. Goodcents purchases some goods from out-of-state suppliers and that business fell off after the robbery. That showing establishes, at most, an attenuated and speculative link—precisely the kind of reasoning Lopez rejected when it refused to permit Congress to "pile inference upon inference" to reach purely local criminal conduct. 514 U.S. at 567. Because the robbery of a single sandwich shop is not the kind of economic activity that, in the aggregate, substantially affects interstate commerce, § 1951 cannot constitutionally reach Mr. Pearson's conduct, and his conviction must be vacated.

II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Excluding the Alibi Evidence

The exclusion of alibi testimony as a discovery sanction is permissible only where the district court weighs the defendant's constitutional right to present a defense against the government's interest in avoiding surprise. Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400, 414-15 (1988) (recognizing that preclusion sanctions implicate the defendant's right "to present his own witnesses to establish a defense," while permitting exclusion for willful and blatant discovery violations). Notice-of-alibi rules exist to prevent unfair surprise, not to punish defendants for technical noncompliance where no prejudice results. Michigan v. Lucas, 500 U.S. 145, 152-53 (1991) (preclusion is an appropriate sanction only after the trial court considers "whether alternative sanctions are available"). The district court here undertook no such balancing. It refused categorically to permit Mr. Pearson to present testimony and documentary evidence—the watch-store receipt and pager records—corroborating that he was elsewhere at the time of the robbery, without considering lesser sanctions such as a continuance or an adverse instruction. Because the exclusion was neither willful nor prejudicial to the government, and because it deprived Mr. Pearson of his sole corroborated defense, the court abused its discretion under Taylor and Lucas.

III. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Support the Convictions

A conviction must be reversed unless, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, "any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). The government's case rested almost entirely on the testimony of two accomplices who had struck plea deals and on an eyewitness identification that was itself the product of suggestive procedures—Ms. Starks failed to identify Mr. Pearson from a photo array shortly after the crime, yet identified him in court only after seeing his photograph broadcast on television and after he was the only young black male present in the courtroom. This is not the kind of solid, corroborated proof that satisfies Jackson's rational-trier standard; rather, it reflects the very unreliability that undermines confidence in a verdict. No rational jury could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt on this record.

IV. The Felony-Murder Instruction Misstated the Required Mental State

Jury instructions are reviewed to determine whether, taken as a whole, they correctly state the governing law. United States v. Sasser, 971 F.2d 470, 473 (10th Cir. 1992). Although felony murder does not require premeditation, the underlying mens rea for the predicate felony must still be established, and courts have cautioned against instructions that permit liability for a killing wholly disconnected from any culpable intent regarding the risk of death. Tison v. Arizona, 481 U.S. 137, 157-58 (1987) (liability may attach where a defendant's participation is "major" and he exhibits "reckless indifference to human life," but not otherwise). Here, the instruction permitted the jury to convict Mr. Pearson of felony murder without any finding that he participated in, or was even aware of, the introduction of a firearm into the robbery. Because the instruction eliminated the mental-state inquiry that Tison and Sasser require, the conviction cannot stand.

V. The District Court Erred in Refusing the Requested Lesser-Included-Offense Instructions

A defendant is entitled to a lesser-included-offense instruction where the lesser offense requires proof of the same or fewer elements and the evidence would permit a rational jury to find the defendant guilty of the lesser but not the greater offense. Sansone v. United States, 380 U.S. 343, 349-50 (1965). This entitlement applies with particular force where, as here, the evidence of the defendant's participation in the more serious offense is contested. Keeble v. United States, 412 U.S. 205, 208 (1973) (a defendant is entitled to an instruction on a lesser offense if "the evidence would permit a jury rationally to find him guilty of the lesser offense and acquit him of the greater"). Mr. Pearson's own testimony, if credited, supported at most a finding of mere presence or minimal participation, not the armed robbery resulting in death charged by the government. The district court's refusal to instruct on the lesser offenses deprived the jury of the opportunity Keeble and Sansone guarantee, and requires reversal.
