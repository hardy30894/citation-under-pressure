**ARGUMENT**

**I. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) does not exempt state-owned lands and waters from NPS jurisdiction.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon’s interpretation of ANILCA § 103(c) is foreclosed by the statute’s plain text. *See* 9th Cir. Op. at 12. Section 103(c) provides that, within conservation system units (CSUs), “the Secretary may only acquire land and interests in land by donation, relinquishment, or exchange.” 16 U.S.C. § 3192(c) (emphasis added). The statute does not purport to limit the Secretary’s regulatory authority over nonfederal lands. *See id.* This Court has repeatedly emphasized that “[w]here the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, the judicial inquiry is complete.” *United States v. Oregon*, 366 U.S. 643, 648 (1961) (quoting *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917)). The plain text of § 103(c) does not exempt state-owned lands or waters from NPS jurisdiction.

**II. The “navigational servitude” doctrine confirms NPS’s authority over navigable waters within CSUs.**

The federal navigational servitude, a longstanding doctrine of constitutional law, vests the United States with plenary authority over navigable waters, regardless of underlying ownership. *See United States v. Commodore Park, Inc.*, 324 F.2d 129, 132 (9th Cir. 1963) (citing *United States v. Chicago*, 310 U.S. 119, 125 (1940)). The Supreme Court has held that “the regulation of navigable waters is a federal responsibility,” *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 424 (1940), and that this authority extends to “the waters themselves and the lands beneath them,” *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 79 (1931). The Nation River, where Sturgeon seeks to operate his hovercraft, is a tributary of the Yukon River, a navigable water of the United States. *See* 9th Cir. Op. at 10. Thus, even if the riverbed is state-owned, the navigational servitude empowers the NPS to regulate activities on those waters. *See Commodore Park*, 324 F.2d at 132.

**III. ANILCA § 103(c) does not repeal or modify the federal navigational servitude.**

Sturgeon’s argument that § 103(c) implicitly repeals the navigational servitude is foreclosed by settled principles of statutory construction. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3207 (providing that ANILCA does not “supersede[] ... existing laws” unless “specifically set forth” in the Act). This Court has held that “a statute will not be construed as repealing the common law or a prior statute by implication unless the later statute is so repugnant that the two cannot stand together.” *United States v. California*, 297 U.S. 175, 186 (1936). ANILCA § 103(c) contains no such repugnancy. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3192(c). Moreover, this Court has cautioned that “[a] statute ought not to be construed as interfering with the exercise of a constitutional power, unless the intent to do so is clearly expressed.” *United States v. California*, 297 U.S. at 186. ANILCA § 103(c) does not clearly express such an intent.

**IV. The NPS hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of the Secretary’s authority under the Property Clause.**

The Property Clause of the Constitution empowers Congress to “dispose of and make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States.” U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2. The Supreme Court has held that this clause “extends to all ‘needful’ regulations respecting the property of the United States.” *United States v. Gratiot*, 39 U.S. 526, 537 (1840). The NPS hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of this authority, as it regulates conduct on federal property (the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve) to protect park resources and the public interest. *See* 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e). This Court has upheld similar regulations under the Property Clause. *See, e.g., United States v. Alford*, 274 F.2d 910, 913 (9th Cir. 1960) (upholding federal regulation of hunting on federal lands).

**V. Sturgeon’s reliance on the “equal footing” doctrine is misplaced.**

Sturgeon invokes the “equal footing” doctrine, which holds that states admitted to the Union are entitled to sovereignty over navigable waters within their borders. *See* 9th Cir. Op. at 14 (citing *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212 (1845)). However, the Supreme Court has recognized that the federal navigational servitude is an exception to this doctrine. *See* *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (“The power of the United States over navigable waters is paramount and extends to all matters affecting navigation.”). Thus, even if Alaska holds title to the riverbed, the federal navigational servitude permits the NPS to regulate activities on the waters themselves. *See Commodore Park*, 324 F.2d at 132.

**VI. The NPS hovercraft ban is a reasonable exercise of the Secretary’s discretion.**

The Supreme Court has held that federal agencies are entitled to deference in interpreting ambiguous statutes under *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984). Even under the more deferential *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134 (1944), standard, the NPS hovercraft ban is a reasonable exercise of the Secretary’s discretion. The ban protects park resources, ensures visitor safety, and prevents environmental harm. *See* 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e). This Court has upheld similar regulations as reasonable exercises of agency authority. *See, e.g., United States v. 1,584.66 Acres of Land*, 248 F.2d 490, 494 (9th Cir. 1957) (upholding federal land-use regulations as reasonable).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) does not exempt state-owned lands or waters from NPS jurisdiction. The federal navigational servitude empowers the NPS to regulate activities on navigable waters within CSUs, regardless of underlying ownership. The NPS hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of the Secretary’s authority under the Property Clause and is a reasonable regulation of conduct on federal property. Sturgeon’s arguments to the contrary are foreclosed by settled principles of constitutional and statutory construction.
