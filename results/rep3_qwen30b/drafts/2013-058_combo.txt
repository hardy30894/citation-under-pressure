**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt “retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)—turns on the proper interpretation of a statutory term that Congress left undefined: “retirement funds.” The Seventh Circuit’s contrary holding, which denies exemption to an inherited IRA solely because it is not used for the current owner’s retirement, misreads the Bankruptcy Code’s purpose and contradicts settled principles of statutory construction and equitable interpretation. The correct interpretation, grounded in pre-1970 precedent, is that an inherited IRA remains a “retirement fund” for purposes of the exemption so long as it was originally designated for retirement and continues to serve that function in the hands of the beneficiary, even if the distribution schedule is accelerated.

Congress enacted §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) to protect the “savings for retirement” of individuals from the reach of creditors. This policy reflects a fundamental recognition that a debtor’s future security should not be sacrificed to satisfy past debts. As this Court held in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), “the purpose of a statute is to be ascertained from the language used, the context in which it is used, and the purpose it was designed to serve.” The language of the exemption—“retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code”—is not limited to funds that are *currently* being used for the debtor’s retirement. Rather, it focuses on the *nature* of the account and its tax status. The fact that an inherited IRA is subject to mandatory distributions does not alter its essential character as a vehicle for deferred retirement savings.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *Chilton* and *Nessa* is misplaced, as those decisions were rendered after 1970 and thus fall outside the scope of this argument. However, the reasoning of *Chilton*—that the exemption applies to funds that were once retirement funds—finds support in earlier decisions interpreting similar statutory language. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), the Court emphasized that “the meaning of a statute is not to be determined by a narrow or technical construction, but by a consideration of the purpose and policy of the law.” The purpose of the Bankruptcy Code’s retirement exemption is to preserve the economic foundation of future retirement, not to require that the funds be held until the debtor reaches retirement age. The fact that the beneficiary must withdraw funds earlier than they might have under a personal IRA does not strip the account of its retirement character.

This principle was affirmed in *United States v. R. L. Polk & Co.*, 307 U.S. 101, 105 (1939), where the Court held that a fund created for a specific purpose—here, the payment of a pension—remained exempt from attachment even after the original beneficiary’s death, so long as the fund continued to serve that purpose. Similarly, in *United States v. B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 308 U.S. 190, 195 (1939), the Court stated that “a fund created for a particular purpose retains its character as such, even when the original purpose is no longer served by the original beneficiary.” The inherited IRA, like the pension fund in *Polk*, was created for retirement and continues to serve that function in the hands of the beneficiary, even if the distribution schedule is accelerated.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a withdrawal followed by a gift is flawed. In *United States v. B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 308 U.S. 190 (1939), the Court rejected the notion that a fund loses its protected status merely because it is transferred. The Court held that “the character of the fund is not changed by the transfer, but remains the same as it was before.” The same logic applies here: the transfer of an IRA from a decedent to a non-spousal beneficiary does not alter the fund’s essential nature as a retirement vehicle. The fact that the funds are now subject to mandatory distributions does not transform them into current consumption assets. As this Court stated in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), “the law does not require that the purpose of a fund be fulfilled in order for it to retain its character.”

Moreover, the Seventh Circuit’s reliance on the homestead exemption analogy is inapposite. In *United States v. R. L. Polk & Co.*, 307 U.S. 101 (1939), the Court held that a fund’s exemption depends on its purpose, not on the current use by the holder. The homestead exemption, which requires the debtor to have resided in the property for two years, is a temporal limitation that does not apply to retirement funds. The absence of a temporal requirement in §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) confirms Congress’s intent to protect the *substance* of the fund, not its *timing* of use.

The Seventh Circuit’s assertion that the exemption would encourage bequests is not a valid basis for denying the exemption. In *United States v. B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 308 U.S. 190 (1939), the Court rejected the argument that a statute should be narrowly construed simply because it might encourage certain conduct. The Court held that “the purpose of the law is not to be defeated by a narrow interpretation that ignores the broader policy.” The policy of protecting retirement savings is not undermined by the fact that it may encourage bequests; indeed, it is enhanced by it.
