The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States by the Fourteenth Amendment, protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal. Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969); North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That protection extends to any proceeding in which the State seeks to establish facts that increase punishment, once it has elected to try those facts in a formal hearing. Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967).

In this case the State chose to try the prior-conviction allegation in a separate, bifurcated proceeding governed by Penal Code section 1025. The trial court received documentary evidence, heard argument, and rendered a formal finding that the allegation was not proved. That finding operated as an acquittal on the enhancement. Once rendered, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the same allegation, for the Clause forbids any second jeopardy “for the same offence.” U.S. Const. amend. V.

The historical distinction between trial and sentencing does not remove the bar. Although a sentencing judge traditionally may consider a wide range of information without the procedural safeguards of a trial, Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 245-46 (1949), that latitude exists only where the legislature has not required a formal adjudication of specific facts. When the State instead provides a discrete hearing at which the prosecution must prove a prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt, the proceeding assumes the character of a trial on a distinct charge. Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15, 17-18 (1919). Retrial after an adverse finding would subject the defendant to successive prosecutions for the same offense, contrary to the Clause’s command.

The Court of Appeal correctly held that the evidence was insufficient and that the Double Jeopardy Clause therefore precludes further proceedings on the allegation. The judgment of the Supreme Court of California should be reversed.
