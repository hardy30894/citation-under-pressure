**ARGUMENT**

**The Ninth Circuit's "predominantly internal" gloss on Exemption 2 cannot be reconciled with FOIA's text, and the ESQD arc maps do not qualify for withholding under any faithful reading of that text.**

FOIA exempts from disclosure only matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Ninth Circuit did not apply that text. It applied a judicially invented substitute—"predominantly internal"—borrowed from the D.C. Circuit, and then layered atop it a second judicial invention, a "risk of circumvention" inquiry that appears nowhere in the statute. Neither gloss can survive elementary principles of statutory construction.

*First*, "the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Congress chose the word "solely," not "predominantly," "primarily," or "chiefly." Those words are not interchangeable. "Solely" means exclusively; "predominantly" means merely more likely than not. By substituting the latter for the former, the Ninth Circuit did not construe the statute—it rewrote it. This Court has long insisted that "there is . . . no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress's chosen word, "solely," is the beginning and the end of the inquiry; it forecloses an exemption for documents that serve any substantial purpose beyond internal personnel administration, regardless of whether internal use predominates.

*Second*, this Court's decision in United States v. Alger, 152 U.S. 384 (1894), confirms that an agency's own practice cannot override statutory text that is otherwise clear. There the Court explained that although "the practical construction by the [Navy] Department would be entitled to great weight" if the statute's meaning were doubtful, "as the meaning of the statute . . . appears to this court to be perfectly clear, no practice inconsistent with that meaning can have any effect." Id. at 390. The Navy's practice of treating the ESQD arc maps as internal cannot expand Exemption 2 beyond documents "solely" related to personnel rules, no matter how convenient the Navy finds that practice.

*Third*, exceptions to remedial legislation—and FOIA's disclosure mandate is precisely that—must be narrowly construed to preserve the statute's purpose. This Court held that "[a]ny exemption from such . . . remedial legislation must therefore be narrowly construed, giving due regard to the plain meaning of statutory language and the intent of Congress." A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945); accord Mabee v. White Plains Publishing Co., 327 U.S. 178, 181 (1946) (exemptions to remedial statutes are to be given a narrow, not expansive, reading). The Ninth Circuit's approach does the opposite: it enlarges a narrow textual exemption—"solely" internal personnel matters—into a broad category covering any document an agency finds administratively convenient to keep secret, so long as disclosure might theoretically let a member of the public anticipate or avoid agency action.

*Fourth*, the "risk of circumvention" test is an atextual addition with no mooring in § 552(b)(2). Where Congress wished to protect information whose disclosure could compromise security or enforcement, it said so expressly, in Exemptions 1 and 7. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1), (b)(7). This Court has cautioned against reading into a statute a standard Congress did not enact, particularly where, as here, the interpretation collapses distinctions between separately enumerated exemptions. Cf. Cole v. Young, 351 U.S. 536, 546–47 (1956) (narrowly construing a security-related exception to a protective personnel statute, and confining it to the class of positions Congress actually described, rather than permitting the exception to swallow the statute's general rule). If Exemption 2's "internal personnel rules" language may be stretched by a judge-made "risk of circumvention" test to cover safety-engineering data having nothing to do with personnel administration, then Exemptions 1 and 7—each carefully calibrated with express standards for national-security and law-enforcement material—become superfluous. This Court does not construe statutes to render other provisions redundant. See Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp., 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (specific statutory provisions control over resort to more general ones, and a court should not adopt a construction that renders the specific provision meaningless).

*Fifth*, the historical antecedent to Exemption 2 confirms its narrow scope. Before FOIA, the "housekeeping statute," R.S. § 161, gave agency heads broad discretion to control the release of "official files, documents, records and information," a discretion this Court sustained in United States ex rel. Touhy v. Ragen, 340 U.S. 462, 468 (1951). FOIA was enacted precisely to cut back that unbounded discretion and substitute a regime of specific, enumerated exemptions. See Helvering v. Hallock, 309 U.S. 106, 119 (1940) (courts must read a later statute against the background principle it was designed to displace). Reviving Touhy-style discretion under the label "predominantly internal" defeats the very reform Congress intended.

The ESQD arc maps—engineering calculations used to protect surrounding civilian communities from explosive risk—are not, under any faithful reading of "solely," documents "related . . . to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." This Court should reverse.
