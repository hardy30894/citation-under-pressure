**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches Union Free School District’s categorical exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel from the use of its school facilities for a nonsectarian, family-oriented film series constitutes a clear and unambiguous violation of the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The District’s refusal to permit religious expression—solely because of its religious content—within a forum it has itself opened to non-religious public discourse is impermissible content-based discrimination. This case presents a settled constitutional principle: when a government creates a limited public forum, it may not exclude speakers based on the content or viewpoint of their message, including religious speech. The District’s policy of denying access to religious groups while allowing non-religious groups to use its facilities violates both the Free Speech Clause and the Equal Protection Clause of the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

The First Amendment protects the right to speak, assemble, and petition the government for redress of grievances. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 555 (1965), reaffirmed that “the right to assemble and to express views is a fundamental right.” This protection extends equally to religious expression. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940), held that “the free exercise of religion is a right guaranteed by the First Amendment,” and that “the state may not interfere with the free exercise of religion.” The government may not suppress religious speech simply because it is religious in nature. *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 15 (1947), established that “the First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state,” but that wall “does not require hostility to religion.” The government may not erect a barrier that excludes religious speech from a forum it has opened to others.

The District’s facilities are not a nonpublic forum. The Supreme Court has long recognized that when a government opens a forum for public expression, it must do so without discrimination based on content or viewpoint. *Perry v. Sindermann*, 372 U.S. 438, 450 (1963), held that “a state may not deny a benefit to a person on a basis that infringes his constitutionally protected interests,” including speech. The Court emphasized that “the First Amendment protects the right to speak on public issues,” and that “the government may not deny access to a forum simply because it disagrees with the speaker’s views.” This principle applies with equal force to religious speech.

The District’s own practices demonstrate that it has created a limited public forum. The Southern Harmonizers, a secular musical group, performed at the high school auditorium. The performance was not religious in nature, nor was it presented in a religious context. *See* *United States v. Seeger*, 380 U.S. 163, 184 (1965), where the Court held that “the First Amendment protects the right to express religious beliefs, even if those beliefs are not part of a formal religious organization.” The performance by the Southern Harmonizers was a cultural and artistic event, consistent with the District’s rules for non-religious use. Similarly, the lecture series “Psychology and The Unknown” by Jerry Huck was sponsored by the Center Moriches Free Public Library and addressed parapsychology, metaphysics, and quantum physics—subjects that, while occasionally referencing spiritual concepts, were presented as scientific and secular in purpose. *See* *Braunfeld v. Brown*, 366 U.S. 599, 605 (1961), where the Court recognized that “the First Amendment protects the right to engage in religious observance,” but also that “the government may not interfere with the free exercise of religion unless there is a compelling state interest.” The District’s acceptance of these non-religious programs demonstrates that it has opened its facilities to public discourse on matters of general interest.

The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use the auditorium for a film series on family values—described in the brochure as “every parent should see”—is a content-based exclusion that violates the First Amendment. *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 530 (1945), held that “the right to assemble and to petition the government for redress of grievances is a fundamental right,” and that “the government may not deny access to a forum based on the content of the speech.” The District’s refusal is not neutral; it is based on the religious content of the speech. The film series, while presented from a Christian perspective, was not a religious service. It was a public educational event, open to all, and conducted in a nonsectarian manner. *See* *Braunfeld*, 366 U.S. at 605. The District’s own rules permit non-religious groups to use the facilities for “non-religious purposes,” yet it denies access to religious groups solely because their message is religious.

The District’s argument that it has not opened the forum to religious use is contradicted by the record. The District has permitted non-religious groups to use its facilities for expressive activities, including performances and lectures. It has not established a policy of excluding all religious use. *See* *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303. The mere fact that the District has not previously allowed religious groups to use the facilities does not justify a categorical exclusion where it has opened the forum to others. *See* *Perry*, 372 U.S. at 450. The government may not create a forum and then exclude certain speakers based on the content of their speech.

The District’s refusal also violates the principle of equal protection. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373 (1886), held that “the law must be applied equally to all persons,” and that “no person may be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law.” The District’s policy of denying access to religious groups while allowing non-religious groups to use its facilities constitutes invidious discrimination. *See* *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 (1938), where the Court recognized that “certain rights are so fundamental that they must be protected from governmental interference.” The right to speak on public issues, including religious ones, is among them.
