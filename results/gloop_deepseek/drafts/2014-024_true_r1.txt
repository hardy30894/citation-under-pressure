**ARGUMENT**

**I. The First Amendment and 18 U.S.C. §875(c) Require Proof of Subjective Intent to Threaten.**

The court of appeals erred by affirming a conviction under 18 U.S.C. §875(c) based solely on an objective standard—whether a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement as threatening. This Court has long held that the “true threat” exception to First Amendment protection requires proof that the speaker subjectively intended his words to be understood as a threat. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003) (“‘True threats’ encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.”). The Third Circuit’s contrary rule criminalizes speech without regard to the speaker’s mental state, chilling protected expression and violating bedrock principles of criminal law.

**A. The Objective Standard Impermissibly Dilutes the Mens Rea Required for Criminal Threats.**

The government must prove that a defendant acted with a culpable state of mind. *Waley v. Johnston*, 316 U.S. 101, 102 (1942) (recognizing that coerced confessions—obtained through threats—are involuntary and violate due process). The objective standard applied below dispenses with this requirement, punishing a speaker for what a reasonable person would foresee, even if the speaker himself had no intent to threaten. This Court has consistently rejected such strict liability in the context of protected speech. *Lee v. Mississippi*, 332 U.S. 742, 745 (1948) (holding that a confession obtained through “duress, threats and violence” is inadmissible, emphasizing the subjective effect on the defendant). The same principle applies here: a speaker’s subjective intent is the touchstone of whether his words constitute a “true threat” unprotected by the First Amendment.

**B. The Third Circuit’s Reliance on *United States v. Kosma* Is Inconsistent with This Court’s Precedent.**

The court below relied on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), which held that a statement is a true threat when a reasonable speaker would foresee it as threatening. But this Court’s decision in *Virginia v. Black* supersedes that standard. *Black* held that a true threat requires that “the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” 538 U.S. at 359. The Third Circuit’s refusal to apply *Black*’s holding undermines the First Amendment’s protection of speech that is merely crude, offensive, or hyperbolic. *Cole v. Arkansas*, 333 U.S. 196, 201 (1948) (holding that a defendant cannot be convicted on a theory not presented to the jury). Here, the jury was instructed only on an objective standard, never asked to find that Elonis subjectively intended his posts as threats. That was constitutional error.

**C. The Objective Standard Fails to Distinguish True Threats from Protected Speech.**

The First Amendment protects a wide range of expression, including angry, profane, or even disturbing speech. *Dombrowski v. Pfister*, 380 U.S. 479, 486 (1965) (recognizing that “threat of prosecution” can chill protected speech). An objective standard sweeps too broadly, punishing speech that a reasonable person might misinterpret as threatening, even when the speaker had no such intent. This Court has long required that criminal statutes be construed to avoid chilling protected expression. *Hygrade Provision Co. v. Sherman*, 266 U.S. 497, 501 (1925) (holding that equity will intervene to prevent prosecution under an unconstitutional statute that threatens property rights). The same logic applies here: the objective standard criminalizes speech that the speaker never intended to be threatening, violating the First Amendment.

**D. The Subjective Intent Standard Is Consistent with This Court’s Treatment of Other Speech Crimes.**

This Court has consistently required proof of subjective intent for other speech-based offenses. *Rogers v. Richmond*, 365 U.S. 534, 540-41 (1961) (holding that a confession is involuntary if it is the product of “fear” or “pressure,” focusing on the defendant’s subjective state of mind). Similarly, in *Brown v. Pierce*, 74 U.S. 205, 208 (1869), this Court recognized that duress *per minas*—threats that induce fear—requires proof that the threat actually overcame the victim’s will. The same principle governs here: a statement is a “true threat” only if the speaker subjectively intended to communicate a threat, and the recipient actually feared harm. The objective standard ignores this subjective component, rendering the statute overbroad.

**E. The Third Circuit’s Policy Concerns Do Not Justify Abandoning Subjective Intent.**

The court below argued that a subjective intent standard would fail to protect individuals from the fear of violence. But this Court has never permitted the government to criminalize speech based solely on the reaction of a reasonable listener. *Sexton v. California*, 189 U.S. 319, 323 (1903) (holding that a threat to accuse another of a crime must be evaluated in light of the specific statutory context). The proper balance between public safety and free expression is achieved by requiring proof that the speaker intended his words as a threat. *Ex Parte Century Indemnity Co.*, 305 U.S. 354, 357 (1938) (recognizing that procedural safeguards are essential to protect substantive rights). The objective standard impermissibly shifts the focus from the speaker’s intent to the listener’s perception, chilling speech that is protected under the First Amendment.

**F. The Jury Instruction Was Plain Error.**

The district court instructed the jury that a “true threat” exists when a defendant “intentionally makes a statement in a context or under such circumstances that a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat.” This instruction omitted any requirement that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten. *Iron Mountain & Helena R.R. v. Johnson*, 119 U.S. 608, 611 (1887) (holding that forcible entry statutes require proof of “force, violence, or terror,” not merely objective reasonableness). Because the jury was never asked to find that Elonis intended his posts as threats, his conviction must be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury be required to find that the defendant subjectively intended his statements as threats.
