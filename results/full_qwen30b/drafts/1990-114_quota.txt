**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL” UNDER 29 U.S.C. § 630(f)

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) does not extend to appointed state court judges because they are “appointees on the policymaking level” within the meaning of § 630(f). This statutory exception is not limited to executive or legislative branch officials; rather, it applies to any individual whose duties involve the exercise of significant discretion and judgment in shaping the law and administration of government. The role of a judge—particularly in a state like Missouri, where the judiciary is constitutionally empowered to interpret law, set procedural rules, and supervise the entire court system—necessarily involves policymaking at the highest level.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that judicial decisionmaking is not merely mechanical application of preexisting rules but an inherently policymaking function. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954), the Court emphasized that “the Constitution is not a static document,” and that “the interpretation of constitutional provisions is a dynamic process.” This interpretive authority is not confined to constitutional law; it extends to statutory and common law as well. In *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 703 (1974), the Court held that “the judiciary is the final arbiter of the meaning of the Constitution,” underscoring the policymaking nature of judicial power. Similarly, in *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803), the Court declared that “it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is,” a function that inherently involves the creation and shaping of legal policy.

The Eighth Circuit correctly concluded that Missouri’s appointed judges are “appointees on the policymaking level” because they exercise substantial discretion in interpreting law, setting precedent, and supervising the administration of justice. The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, has the authority to adopt rules governing the conduct of the bar and the operation of courts, and to resolve internal disputes within the judiciary—powers that are not merely administrative but fundamentally policymaking. See *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457, 460 (Mo.1985) (en banc); *In re Rules of Circuit Court of Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 757 S.W.2d 590, 592 (Mo.1988) (en banc). These responsibilities are not unlike those of a cabinet officer or agency head, who also exercise broad discretion in implementing and interpreting laws.

Appellants’ argument that judges are not policymakers because they “decide cases between litigants” ignores the reality that judicial decisions often resolve legal ambiguities and create new standards. As the First Circuit observed in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), “policymaking” is “indisputably a part of the function of judging” when it involves “lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority.” This principle is consistent with *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984), which recognized that agencies (and by analogy, courts) must interpret ambiguous statutes, a process that is inherently policymaking. The same logic applies to judges, whose interpretations of statutes and constitutions shape the legal landscape for decades.

Moreover, the legislative history of § 630(f) confirms that Congress intended the “policymaking level” exception to apply broadly. The Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers noted that the exemption was meant to cover “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments.” 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180. The judiciary is a core agency of state government, and its appointed members are no less policymaking than those in the executive or legislative branches. To hold otherwise would render the exception meaningless in the context of state courts, a result Congress could not have intended.

Finally, the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutory exemptions must be interpreted in light of their context and purpose. In *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971), the Court held that “in traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved.” Here, Congress did not clearly intend to override state constitutional provisions governing judicial tenure. The ADEA’s silence on the judiciary, coupled with its explicit exclusion of “elected” officials and “appointees on the policymaking level,” reflects a deliberate choice to preserve state autonomy in judicial selection and tenure.

II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SURVIVES EQUAL PROTECTION REVIEW UNDER RATIONAL BASIS

The Missouri Constitution’s mandatory retirement provision for judges at age 70 is rationally related to legitimate state interests and thus satisfies the rational basis test under the Fourteenth Amendment. The Supreme Court has consistently upheld age-based classifications when they are reasonably related to the government’s interest in efficient administration and public safety. In *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976), the Court held that “a classification based on age is not inherently suspect,” and that “the State has a legitimate interest in ensuring that public employees are physically and mentally capable of performing their duties.” The Court reaffirmed this principle in *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 446 (1985), stating that “the rational basis test is satisfied if the classification is rationally related to a legitimate government interest.”
