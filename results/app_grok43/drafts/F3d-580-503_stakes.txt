**ARGUMENT**

The district court abused its discretion by denying Winbush’s motion for a fingerprint expert and the accompanying motion to continue. A criminal defendant must be afforded “a fair opportunity to defend against the accusations.” Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 294 (1973). When the government intends to present expert testimony on a contested issue, fundamental fairness requires that the defendant have a meaningful chance to meet that proof. See Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68, 77 (1985).

Here the government noticed two fingerprint experts five weeks before trial. One would testify that no latent prints of value were recovered; the other would explain the “difficulties in obtaining latent fingerprints from different materials and surfaces.” Winbush responded six days after the final pretrial conference—still before trial—by requesting a defense expert who could address the significance of that absence. The magistrate judge rejected the request solely because it came “on the Friday before trial in a case that’s been pending for over two years.”

That timing-based denial ignored the substance of the government’s disclosures and left Winbush without any means to rebut the inference that the lack of prints was inconsequential. The same ruling simultaneously denied the motion to continue, preventing any opportunity to secure the needed assistance. A “myopic insistence upon expeditiousness in the face of a justifiable request for delay” can render the right to defend an empty formality. Ungar v. Sarafite, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964).

Because the government’s case rested in part on physical evidence from which no identifiable prints were recovered, the inability to present expert testimony explaining that fact deprived Winbush of a fair opportunity to contest an essential element of the charged offenses. The convictions and sentence should therefore be vacated.
