# ARGUMENT

## I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and drawing all reasonable inferences in that party's favor. *Torgerson v. City of Rochester*, 643 F.3d 1031, 1042 (8th Cir. 2011) (en banc). Summary judgment is proper only where "there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact." *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). Because credibility determinations and the weighing of evidence are jury functions, courts may not resolve genuine factual disputes at summary judgment. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). Nor may the district court grant summary judgment simply because the moving party's version of events is more plausible; if the record, taken as a whole, "could lead a rational trier of fact to find for the nonmoving party," judgment must be denied. *Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986). The district court failed to apply these settled principles here.

## II. The FRSA's Contributing-Factor Framework, Not McDonnell Douglas, Governs This Case

Retaliation claims under the FRSA are analyzed under the burden-shifting framework of the Wendell H. Ford Aviation Investment and Reform Act, 49 U.S.C. § 42121(b)(2)(B), not the McDonnell Douglas standard applicable to Title VII claims. *Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co.*, 768 F.3d 786, 789 (8th Cir. 2014). Under this framework, an employee need only show that protected activity was "a contributing factor" in the adverse action—defined as "any factor which, alone or in connection with other factors, tends to affect in any way the outcome of the decision." *Id.* at 791 (quoting *Araujo v. N.J. Transit Rail Operations, Inc.*, 708 F.3d 152, 158 (3d Cir. 2013)). This is a "broad and forgiving" standard, deliberately less demanding than the "motivating factor" test used elsewhere. *Araujo*, 708 F.3d at 157. Only after the employee makes this modest showing must the employer demonstrate, by clear and convincing evidence, that it would have taken the same action absent the protected conduct. 49 U.S.C. § 42121(b)(2)(B)(iv); *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 791. The district court's analysis conflated this framework with a pretext inquiry more familiar from Title VII litigation, improperly requiring Appellants to disprove BNSF's stated rationale rather than merely to show that their protected reports contributed to the discipline.

## III. Appellants Engaged in Protected Activity by Reporting a Hazardous Safety Condition

Section 20109(a)(1)(C) protects an employee who "notif[ies], or attempt[s] to notify, the railroad carrier ... of a work-related personal injury or work-related illness of an employee." Appellants' written statements to Trainmaster Dixon on April 2, and their subsequent recorded statements to Claims Representative Murphy, expressly identified the absence of side rails and lighting on the bridge—the very condition that caused Moore's fall. Foster likewise informed Murphy that BNSF had previously been told the crew-change location was dangerous. These reports are precisely the sort of safety notifications the statute was designed to protect, and BNSF does not seriously dispute that they occurred.

## IV. Genuine Fact Issues Preclude Summary Judgment on Contributing-Factor Causation

Temporal proximity between protected activity and an adverse action is itself sufficient evidence from which a reasonable jury could infer contributing-factor causation. *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 791; *Van Asdale v. Int'l Game Tech.*, 577 F.3d 989, 1003 (9th Cir. 2009) (proximity in time between protected activity and adverse action supports an inference of retaliation). Indeed, the Federal Circuit has held that "close temporal proximity" is enough, standing alone, to raise a genuine factual dispute about contributing-factor causation under the identical statutory scheme borrowed by the FRSA. *Marano v. Dep't of Justice*, 2 F.3d 1137, 1141 (Fed. Cir. 1993). Here, BNSF opened its investigation into the crew members the day after Foster's recorded statement, and Kline and Snyder repeated their safety complaints in their April 12–13 statements even as the disciplinary hearing loomed. A jury could reasonably infer that Appellants' repeated identification of the unlit, railless bridge—reports that implicated BNSF's own decision to direct crew changes at that location—contributed to management's decision to pursue discipline rather than accept the crew's account that they were told to stop west of the bridge. The district court's contrary conclusion improperly resolved this inference against Appellants at the summary judgment stage, in violation of *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255.

## V. BNSF Has Not Established by Clear and Convincing Evidence That It Would Have Disciplined Appellants Regardless of Their Reports

Even accepting that BNSF identified rule infractions on the video, the disputed factual question—whether crew changes were, in practice, orally authorized west of the bridge—goes to the heart of whether BNSF's stated rationale is genuine or pretextual. Appellants and their fellow crew members uniformly testified that supervisors instructed them to cross the bridge; Knutstrom and Dixon disputed this. Such a direct factual conflict cannot be resolved on summary judgment, particularly where the employer bears the heightened clear-and-convincing burden. *Araujo*, 708 F.3d at 158–59; *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 791.

## VI. The Increased Testing and Resulting Loss of Preferred Assignments Constitute Actionable Retaliation

Retaliatory conduct need not be formal discipline; it need only be conduct that "well might have dissuaded a reasonable worker" from engaging in protected activity. *Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). The heightened, covert operations testing directed at Kline and Snyder, and their consequent avoidance of less experienced coworkers and forfeiture of more lucrative assignments, satisfies this standard and independently supports reversal.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
