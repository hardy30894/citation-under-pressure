**ARGUMENT**

**I.  The Text of the Immigration and Nationality Act Unambiguously Treats a Grant of TPS as an “Admission” for Purposes of Adjustment of Status.**

The Third Circuit erred in reading a distinction between “lawful status” and “admission” where the statute provides none. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a) requires an alien to be “inspected and admitted or paroled” to adjust status. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that these statutory provisions are inextricably linked. In *Ramirez v. Brown*, the Ninth Circuit concluded that the statutory text of § 1254a(f)(4) “equates ‘being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant’ with the ‘lawful admission’ required by § 1255(a).” 852 F.3d at 961. The court reasoned that “lawful status” is a term of art that encompasses the “lawful admission” prerequisite for adjustment of status. Id. at 960–61.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.* to distinguish between the “date of gaining a new status” and the “date of the physical event of entering the country” is misplaced. *Hanif* distinguishes between the *initial* entry and a subsequent change in status, but it does not address the situation where the grant of status *itself* constitutes the admission for the purpose of a subsequent statutory benefit. Here, the grant of TPS is the legal event that confers the status necessary to satisfy § 1255(a). As the Ninth Circuit noted, “the statutory text of § 1254a(f)(4) equates ‘being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant’ with the ‘lawful admission’ required by § 1255(a).” 852 F.3d at 961. To read § 1254a(f)(4) as creating a status that is *not* an admission is to render the statute’s reference to “lawful status” meaningless in the context of § 1255.

**II.  The Purpose of the TPS Statute Supports the Interpretation That TPS Constitutes an Admission.**

The purpose of the TPS program is to provide humanitarian relief to aliens in the United States facing dangerous conditions in their home countries, while also allowing them to remain in the United States and contribute to the economy. The Third Circuit’s interpretation creates a “dead end” for TPS beneficiaries who entered the country without inspection, leaving them with temporary status but no path to permanent residence. This result is contrary to the purpose of the statute.

In *Flores v. Lynch*, the Ninth Circuit emphasized that “Congress intended to provide a mechanism for TPS beneficiaries to remain in the United States and eventually adjust their status.” 828 F.3d at 403. The court noted that the TPS statute was designed to allow beneficiaries to “make use” of the adjustment of status process. Id. at 401. The Third Circuit’s rigid distinction between admission and status ignores the reality that the TPS program is a form of temporary relief that, by its very nature, facilitates the long-term presence of aliens in the United States. To deny that this presence constitutes an admission for the purpose of adjustment of status is to frustrate the clear intent of Congress to allow TPS beneficiaries to eventually become permanent residents.

**III.  The Third Circuit’s Interpretation Creates an Absurd Result.**

The Third Circuit’s interpretation creates a paradox where an alien can be considered “in lawful status” for years, yet be statutorily ineligible to adjust that status because they were never “admitted.” This interpretation also creates an anomalous result where TPS beneficiaries who entered the country illegally are treated differently from those who entered legally, even though both are now long-term residents with families in the United States.

The Board of Immigration Appeals has recognized that “an adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission.” 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). This principle supports the view that the legal act of conferring status—whether through TPS or another mechanism—constitutes the admission required by § 1255. The Third Circuit’s refusal to apply this principle to TPS beneficiaries is inconsistent with the broader framework of the Immigration and Nationality Act. By holding that TPS is not an admission, the Third Circuit effectively requires TPS beneficiaries to leave the country and re-enter legally to adjust their status, a requirement that is impossible for those who entered without inspection. This outcome is contrary to the purpose of the TPS statute and the text of the INA.
