ARGUMENT

The text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) extends Longshore Act benefits only when “disability or death of an employee result[s] from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The phrase “injury occurring” is most naturally read to require that the injury itself take place on the Shelf. See Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 361 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc) (“The plain meaning of the statute supports a situs requirement.”).

This reading is confirmed by Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986). There the Court observed that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage … would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Id. at 219. In the accompanying footnote the Court stated that “§ 1333(b) makes compensation for the death or injury of an ‘employee’ resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act,” and that this provision “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement.” Id. at 219 n.2. Although the footnote addressed a different provision, its description of § 1333(b) as subject to the “otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement” is consistent with the statutory text and with the uniform geographic limitation that governs every other subsection of § 1333.

The Fifth Circuit has therefore held that an employee must suffer injury “on an OCS platform or the waters above the OCS” in addition to satisfying a but-for causal link. Mills, 877 F.2d at 362; see also Becker v. Tidewater, Inc., 586 F.3d 358, 366-67 (5th Cir. 2009). The contrary view adopted by the Third Circuit in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), and by the Ninth Circuit below, dispenses with any geographic limitation on the injury and thereby converts the statute into a status-based workers’ compensation scheme untethered to the Shelf itself. That result cannot be reconciled with Tallentire’s characterization of OCSLA coverage as “principally” situs-driven.

The legislative history reinforces the textual and doctrinal requirement. Congress enacted § 1333(b) to fill a narrow gap created by the geographic limit of the Longshore Act, not to create a free-floating federal compensation regime for any worker whose duties bear some relation to Shelf operations. See Mills, 877 F.2d at 361-62. Because Juan Valladolid’s fatal injury occurred at an onshore facility in California, it fell outside the coverage of § 1333(b). The judgment of the Benefits Review Board should be affirmed.
