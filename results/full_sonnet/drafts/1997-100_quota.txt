# ARGUMENT

## THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE BARS RETRIAL OF A PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION WHERE THE FIRST PROCEEDING BORE THE HALLMARKS OF A TRIAL AND ENDED IN A FINDING OF EVIDENTIARY INSUFFICIENCY

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), protects a defendant "against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That protection does not evaporate merely because the "offense" at issue is a sentencing enhancement rather than the underlying substantive crime. This Court has already held that when a sentencing proceeding possesses the "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence," the Double Jeopardy Clause applies with full force. *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981). The proceeding below possessed precisely those hallmarks, and the Court of Appeal's reversal for insufficient evidence was the functional equivalent of an acquittal that the Constitution forbids the State from relitigating.

### A. *Bullington*'s Trial-Like-Proceeding Analysis Is Not Confined to Capital Cases

In *Bullington*, this Court held that double jeopardy principles barred Missouri from seeking the death penalty on retrial after a jury at the first trial had already declined to impose it. 451 U.S. at 445-446. The Court reasoned that Missouri's capital sentencing procedure resembled a trial because it required the prosecution to prove statutorily defined aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, imposed formal evidentiary rules, and provided the defendant with rights akin to those enjoyed in a trial on guilt. *Id.* at 438. Three years later, in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), the Court reaffirmed this approach, asking whether the sentencing proceeding possessed "characteristics" that "make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause." *Id.* at 209-210.

Nothing in *Bullington* or *Rumsey* confines this analysis to capital cases. The touchstone is not the severity of the punishment but the *procedural character* of the proceeding used to determine it. As this Court recognized in *United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117 (1980), double jeopardy protections historically attach where a proceeding functions as an adjudication of a discrete factual issue rather than an exercise of sentencing discretion. *Id.* at 132-134. California's prior-conviction proceeding is just such an adjudication.

### B. California's Prior Conviction Proceeding Bears the Hallmarks of a Trial

California requires the prosecution to prove a prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt, *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993), applies the ordinary rules of evidence, *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 224 (1996), and grants the defendant rights of confrontation and cross-examination, *id.* at 228 n.6 (quoting *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967)). It further affords a statutory right to a jury determination. Cal. Penal Code § 1025. These are not the informal, discretionary procedures this Court sanctioned in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), where the sentencing judge was free to consult probation reports and hearsay without any burden of proof at all. *Id.* at 245-246. Rather, California has constructed an adversarial, evidence-bound proceeding functionally indistinguishable from the "trial" contemplated by *Bullington*. Where a State elects to erect such trial-like machinery around a sentencing determination, it must accept the double jeopardy consequences that attach to trials generally. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438.

### C. A Reversal for Insufficiency of the Evidence Is an Acquittal Barring Retrial

Once a proceeding is recognized as trial-like, ordinary double jeopardy principles govern its outcome. This Court held in *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1 (1978), that a reversal for insufficiency of the evidence is equivalent to an acquittal and that the Double Jeopardy Clause absolutely forecloses a second trial to cure the government's failure of proof. *Id.* at 18. The Court explained that "the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes a second trial once the reviewing court has found the evidence legally insufficient" because such a reversal means "the government's case was so lacking that it should not have even been submitted." *Id.* This rule rests on the principle, stated in *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184 (1957), that the State is entitled to "one fair opportunity to offer whatever proof it could assemble," *id.* at 187, and no more.

Here, the Court of Appeal found the evidence legally insufficient to prove that Monge personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury, as required by California's serious-felony definition. That determination is materially indistinguishable from the insufficiency finding in *Burks*. Just as the government cannot use a second trial to patch the holes in a failed prosecution of guilt, the State cannot use a retrial to patch the holes in a failed prosecution of a sentencing enhancement that was tried with all the trappings of a criminal trial.

### D. *Monge v. California* Does Not Turn on the Capital/Noncapital Distinction

Respondent will rely on *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), and *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28 (1985) (per curiam), for the proposition that *Bullington* is confined to capital sentencing. But *Caspari* addressed only whether extension of *Bullington* to noncapital cases was a "new rule" for habeas purposes, expressly declining to resolve the underlying constitutional question. 510 U.S. at 392-393. Similarly, *Goldhammer* did not purport to hold that double jeopardy protections categorically vanish outside the capital context. Neither case forecloses the functional inquiry *Bullington* and *Rumsey* prescribe.

Because California voluntarily adopted trial-like procedures for adjudicating prior conviction allegations, and because the Court of Appeal's ruling was a determination of evidentiary insufficiency equivalent to an acquittal, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial. This Court should reverse the judgment of the California Supreme Court.
