**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the equal footing doctrine, grounded in the Constitution, mandates that a state acquires title to the beds of rivers navigable *at the time of statehood*. This principle is not only well-established in federal jurisprudence but is also consistent with the foundational structure of the federal land system and the original understanding of state sovereignty upon admission to the Union.

The Constitution provides that new states shall be admitted “on an equal footing with the original States in all respects whatsoever.” U.S. Const. Art. IV, § 3, cl. 1. This guarantee of equal footing is not merely a political aspiration; it is a constitutional mandate that ensures that states entering the Union receive the same property rights as the original states. *See* *Illinois v. Wisconsin*, 307 U.S. 390, 398 (1939) (“The equal footing doctrine is a constitutional principle that requires new states to be admitted to the Union with the same rights and privileges as the original States.”). One of the most significant of these rights is title to the beds of navigable rivers within their borders. *See* *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915) (“The title to the beds of navigable rivers, as incident to the sovereignty of the state, passes to the state upon its admission into the Union.”).

The Supreme Court has long held that the test for navigability for title purposes is *historical*—that is, whether the river was navigable *at the time of statehood*. In *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 54 (1926), the Court stated: “The question of navigability is one of fact, to be determined by the condition of the river at the time of statehood.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Waddell & Clark*, 94 U.S. 76 (1876), where the Court held that “the title to the bed of a river, which is navigable at the time of the admission of a state into the Union, passes to the state.” The Court emphasized that the determination must be made “according to the condition of the river at the time of statehood,” not according to its present condition or future potential. *Id.* at 82.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled constitutional principle. It found, based on substantial historical evidence—including reports from early territorial surveys, accounts of river travel by fur traders and explorers, and records of commercial use—that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time Montana became a state in 1889. The trial court’s findings were supported by the record and were not clearly erroneous. The State’s evidence, though largely hearsay, was properly admitted under Montana’s rules of evidence as self-authenticating documents under M. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), and (6), and as public records under M. R. Evid. 803(8). The trial court did not err in relying on such evidence, which is routinely accepted in navigability determinations where direct testimony is unavailable. *See* *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 438 F.2d 1055, 1058 (9th Cir. 1971) (upholding use of historical maps and reports to establish navigability at time of statehood).

PPL’s argument that the test should be based on the river’s condition *today* or on its potential for future navigation is contrary to precedent and undermines the equal footing doctrine. The doctrine is not a flexible standard to be adjusted based on modern conditions or economic utility. *See* *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. at 470 (“The title to the bed of a navigable river, as incident to the sovereignty of the state, passes to the state upon its admission into the Union.”). If the test were not historical, states could be deprived of title to riverbeds that were navigable at statehood but are now obstructed or altered—precisely the kind of inequity the equal footing doctrine was designed to prevent.

Moreover, PPL’s claim that the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempts the State’s right to compensation is without merit. The FPA does not displace state property rights; rather, it operates *in tandem* with them. *See* *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Commn.*, 328 U.S. 152, 175–76 (1946) (“The Federal Power Act does not displace state law with respect to proprietary rights.”). The FPA requires licensees to comply with state law regarding the use of riverbeds and water rights, and it explicitly contemplates compensation for property interests. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 802(b), § 797(b). The FPA does not “occupy the field” of property rights, nor does it conflict with state efforts to enforce title to riverbeds acquired at statehood. *See* *Vitullo v. Int’l Bhd. of Elec. Workers*, 2003 MT 219, ¶ 14, 317 Mont. 142, 75 P.3d 1250 (defining the three forms of preemption). There is no express preemption, no field preemption, and no conflict between the FPA and Montana’s Hydroelectric Resources Act.

In sum, the Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes. The State acquired title to the riverbeds of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers at the time of statehood because those rivers were navigable at that time. The trial court’s findings were supported by the record, and the use of historical evidence was proper under Montana’s rules of evidence. The judgment of the District Court is affirmed.
