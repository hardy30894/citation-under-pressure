**ARGUMENT**

**Section 875(c) requires proof that the defendant intended his communication as a threat, or at least knew it would be understood that way.** Congress did not create a strict-liability offense for speech. Where a statute is silent on mens rea, this Court presumes Congress “intended to require a defendant to possess a guilty mind.” *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250-52 (1952). That presumption is especially strong when the statute reaches speech, because criminal punishment without culpable intent risks chilling protected expression. *Winters v. New York*, 333 U.S. 507, 509-10 (1948); *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 150-54 (1959).

Section 875(c) contains no express mental-state requirement. But this Court has long rejected the notion that silence authorizes punishment without fault. In *Carter v. United States*, 530 U.S. 255, 269-70 (2000), the Court explained that when a statute omits mens rea, courts ordinarily read in the “relevant” mental state to separate wrongful from innocent conduct. That principle tracks earlier cases recognizing that “wrongdoing must be conscious to be criminal.” *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 252. A person who posts crude, hyperbolic, or artistic language does not become a felon unless he subjectively means to threaten, or at minimum is aware that his words will be taken as a threat.

The Constitution points the same way. A “true threat” is not protected speech, but the exception exists to punish intentional intimidation, not careless language. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707-08 (1969) (per curiam), recognized that context matters and that political exaggeration is not a threat. Earlier First Amendment cases likewise warn against standards that permit punishment of speech based on its likely effect alone. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 310-11 (1940); *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949). If liability turns only on how a reasonable listener reacts, speakers cannot know in advance when vehement expression crosses the line. That is especially true on social media, where rhetoric, irony, and persona are common modes of communication.

This Court’s pre-1970 cases involving threats also point to a culpable-mental-state requirement. In *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), the Court upheld conviction only where the words were “used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger.” In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (per curiam), the Court held that advocacy may be punished only when directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it. Although this case concerns threats rather than incitement, the principle is the same: speech may be criminalized only when the speaker’s own culpable purpose is shown.

The Government and court below relied on an objective “reasonable person” test. That test is inadequate because it detaches criminal guilt from personal blame. In criminal law, the “guilty intent” of the defendant is the core of liability. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 251-52; *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605-06 (1994). The common law likewise required intent for crimes involving intimidation and breach of the peace. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453-58 (1939), rejected vague criminal prohibitions that failed to give fair warning. An objective-only standard here is equally problematic because a speaker cannot reliably predict what an unfamiliar audience, or a jury, will deem threatening.

The jury instruction below permitted conviction even if Elonis meant only to rap, vent, or compose dark and provocative commentary. That is constitutional error. The First Amendment does not allow the State to punish speech unless the defendant himself crossed the line into threat-making. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 707-08; *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 310-11; *Terminiello*, 337 U.S. at 4-5. And criminal law does not allow a felony conviction absent proof of a culpable mind. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250-52; *Smith*, 361 U.S. at 150-54.

Because the Government was required to prove that Elonis subjectively intended to threaten, or at least knew his statements would be understood as threats, his conviction under §875(c) cannot stand.
