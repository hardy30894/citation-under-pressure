The Third Circuit erred in affirming Anthony Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. §875(c) without requiring the government to prove that Elonis subjectively intended his Facebook posts to be understood as threats. The "true threats" exception to First Amendment protection, which §875(c) codifies, requires a subjective intent to threaten. As the Supreme Court held in Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003), "‘True threats’ encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intention to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." The Court's emphasis on the speaker's intent is clear, and it necessitates a subjective standard.

The Third Circuit's reliance on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), which established an objective foreseeability standard for true threats, is misplaced in light of Black. The Black decision effectively overruled Kosma by introducing a subjective element into the true threats analysis. The Third Circuit's contrary conclusion ignores the Supreme Court's clear guidance on the importance of the speaker's intent.

Furthermore, an objective standard for true threats is inconsistent with the requirement that the government prove the defendant's criminal intent beyond a reasonable doubt. As the Court has long recognized, "the standard for judging the intent of a person making a statement" is a subjective one (see, e.g., Hess v. Indiana, 414 U.S. 105, 108-09 (1973) (per curiam)). Requiring subjective intent to threaten ensures that individuals are not punished for speech that they did not intend to be threatening.

The government's argument that a subjective intent standard would fail to protect individuals from the fear of violence is unpersuasive. The fear of violence is mitigated by other laws that prohibit harassment and stalking, and by the fact that a speaker's subjective intent is not the only factor that determines whether a statement is perceived as threatening. The context and content of the speech also play a crucial role.

In conclusion, Elonis' conviction under §875(c) should be reversed because the jury was not required to find that he subjectively intended his Facebook posts to be understood as threats. The Third Circuit's decision to the contrary should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with a subjective intent standard.
