**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—must be answered in the negative. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander Consumer USA, Inc., as a purchaser of defaulted consumer debt who collected those debts on its own behalf, was not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA. The statutory text, legislative history, and binding precedent compel this conclusion.

The FDCPA’s core purpose is to regulate *debt collectors*, not creditors. As the Supreme Court has made clear, the Act “was designed to protect consumers from abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors, not by creditors collecting their own debts.” *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995). The Act defines “debt collector” in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) with a two-part structure: first, it identifies who *is* a debt collector; second, it specifies who *is not*. The threshold inquiry is whether the entity satisfies one of the definitions in the first part of the subsection. Only if it does, and does not fall within an exclusion, does the entity qualify as a debt collector.

The second definition of “debt collector” is dispositive here: “any person who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). This definition hinges on *who the debt is owed to at the time of collection*, not when it was originally incurred or when it was acquired. The phrase “debts owed or due another” refers to the status of the debt *at the time of collection*, not at the time of origination. As this Court has held, “our inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether Capital One regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to Capital One; our inquiry is whether Capital One regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection.” *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1318 (11th Cir. 2015). The same principle applies here.

Santander did not collect debts owed to another. It purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto on December 1, 2011, and thereafter collected the debts *for its own account*. The complaint itself acknowledges that Santander “acquired defaulted consumer debt ... for a few cents on the dollar” and that it “began communicating with [the plaintiffs] ... in an attempt to collect on the alleged debts.” This is the conduct of a creditor, not a debt collector. The fact that the debt was in default at the time of purchase is irrelevant to the statutory definition. Congress did not intend the FDCPA to regulate every entity that buys defaulted debt and then collects it—such a rule would sweep in virtually every financial institution that engages in loan portfolios, including banks, credit unions, and investment firms.

The plaintiffs’ argument—that a purchaser of defaulted debt is automatically a debt collector—rests on a flawed syllogism. They claim that because § 1692a(4) excludes from the definition of “creditor” any person who receives a debt in default “solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another,” such a person must be a debt collector. But this logic misapplies the statute. The exclusion in § 1692a(4) applies only to those who *receive* defaulted debt *for another*—not to those who *purchase* it and then collect it for themselves. The exclusion does not create a presumption that a purchaser of defaulted debt is a debt collector; it merely clarifies that a servicer collecting for a third party on a defaulted loan is not a creditor. *See* *S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698* (“The committee does not intend the definition [of debt collector] to cover the activities of ... mortgage service companies and others who service outstanding debts for others, so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing”). That exclusion is inapplicable here because Santander did not service the debt for CitiFinancial Auto—it *owned* it.

Moreover, the plaintiffs’ interpretation would render the FDCPA’s exclusions meaningless. Section 1692a(6)(F)(iii) explicitly excludes from the definition of “debt collector” any person collecting a debt that was *not in default at the time it was obtained*. If the plaintiffs were correct, then a person who buys a debt *after* default would always be a debt collector, regardless of whether it collects for itself or another. But that would make the exclusion in (F)(iii) superfluous—Congress could not have intended to exclude only those who collect nondefaulted debts if all purchasers of defaulted debts were automatically debt collectors. *See* *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538 (1955) (“Where two construction are possible, one which would make some language surplusage and one which would give effect to all the language, the latter is to be preferred”).

The Fourth Circuit correctly applied this framework. It held that Santander’s status as a creditor collecting its own debts—purchased in a commercial transaction—placed it outside the FDCPA’s reach. The complaint failed to allege that Santander collected on behalf of another, or that it regularly collected debts owed to others. It alleged only that Santander purchased the debts and then collected them for itself. That is not the conduct of a debt collector under the FDCPA.

Finally, the plaintiffs’ claim that Santander’s prior role as a servicer makes it a debt collector *after* purchase is legally untenable. A company’s past conduct does not determine its status in a new transaction. Once Santander purchased the debt, it became the creditor.
