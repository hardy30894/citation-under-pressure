**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Navy’s Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—technical safety data used to design and operate the Naval Magazine Indian Island (NMII) ammunition storage facilities—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA Exemption 2 as “predominantly internal” personnel rules and practices. The court’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard, without proper statutory or constitutional grounding, expands Exemption 2 beyond its narrow statutory limits and contravenes the Supreme Court’s directive that FOIA exemptions “must be narrowly construed” and are “explicitly exclusive.” *United States Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Adm’r FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)). The ESQD maps are not “internal personnel rules and practices” within the meaning of § 552(b)(2), nor do they present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation. Their disclosure would not endanger national security, nor would it enable adversaries to exploit vulnerabilities in the Navy’s safety infrastructure. The Navy’s refusal to classify these documents—despite asserting they could be reverse-engineered to reveal sensitive weapon systems—undermines its claim of national security risk and reveals a political, not operational, motive for nondisclosure.

**I. The ESQD Arc Maps Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices” Under Exemption 2**

Exemption 2 applies only to information “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has made clear that this exemption is not a catch-all for any information that might be useful to an agency’s internal operations. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. Rather, it is limited to “matters that are of a purely internal nature, such as rules governing employee conduct, leave policies, or office procedures.” *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 655 (9th Cir. 1980). The D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard, adopted by the Ninth Circuit in this case, is inconsistent with this narrow construction. It allows agencies to withhold any information that is “predominantly” used internally—even if it is technical, scientific, or operational in nature—so long as it is not “solely” for public use. But the statute does not permit such a broad reading. The phrase “related solely” demands a more precise fit than the majority’s test allows.

The ESQD arc maps are not internal personnel rules. They are technical safety calculations used to determine safe distances between explosive storage facilities and to prevent sympathetic detonations. As Commander Whitbred acknowledged, the Navy uses these maps “to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” This is not a personnel practice; it is a matter of engineering and operational safety. The maps are not rules governing employee behavior, nor are they internal procedures for managing staff. They are technical data used to comply with the Navy’s own safety manual, the OP-5, which governs the handling and storage of explosives. *See* *Tallman*, 618 F.3d at 1084. The fact that the Navy uses the maps internally does not make them “internal” for purposes of Exemption 2. *See* *Bibles v. Oregon Natural Desert Ass’n*, 519 U.S. 355, 361 (1997) (per curiam) (“The only relevant public interest in the FOIA balancing analysis is the extent to which disclosure of the information sought would ‘she[d] light on an agency’s performance of its statutory duties.’”).

**II. The Navy Has Failed to Demonstrate a Risk of Circumvention**

Even if the maps were internal, Exemption 2 requires a showing that disclosure would “present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. The Navy has not met this burden. Commander Whitbred’s affidavit claims that a person could “reverse engineer” the ESQD maps to discover “particular ammunition, explosive[s and] weapons systems.” But this is speculative. The maps are not classified, nor are they marked as sensitive. If they posed a genuine risk to national security, the Navy would have classified them under Exemption 1. *Weinberger v. Catholic Action of Hawaii/Peace Education Project*, 454 U.S. 139, 148 (1981) (“classify information if its release would pose a threat to national security”). The fact that the Navy has not classified the ESQD maps for NMII—while voluntarily releasing identical maps for the Bangor base—strongly suggests that the risk is not real, but political. The Navy’s selective disclosure undermines its claim of national security risk and reveals a pattern of avoiding public scrutiny where political sensitivity is high.

Moreover, the maps are not “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” which would bring them under Exemption 7(F). *John Doe Agency*, 488 U.S. 1306, 1307 (1989) (per curiam). The ESQD data is not used to investigate or enforce laws; it is used to design safe storage facilities. The Navy’s own statements confirm this: the maps are “designed to be a long term planning tool for the Navy.” *Tallman*, 618 F.3d at 1084. The Supreme Court has held that “the mere fact that information may be useful to a criminal or civil defendant does not make it exempt under Exemption 7.” *Abramson*, 456 U.S. 615, 625 (1982). The Navy has not shown that disclosure would “reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(F). The public has a right to know the risks posed by the Navy’s operations, especially when those operations are located just two miles from populated communities.
