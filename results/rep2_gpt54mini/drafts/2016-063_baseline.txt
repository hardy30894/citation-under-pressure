**Argument**

The proper forum for review of the Merit Systems Protection Board’s jurisdictional dismissal of Perry’s mixed case is the district court. The Civil Service Reform Act provides that “cases of discrimination” are reviewed in district court, while ordinary Board decisions go to the Federal Circuit. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2); 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A). The statute defines covered mixed cases as those in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [he] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board” and alleges discrimination. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1); *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 56-57, 133 S. Ct. 596, 607-08 (2012).

That rule turns on the employee’s claim and the action challenged, not on the label the Board later places on its disposition. In *Kloeckner*, the Supreme Court held that a mixed case dismissed by the Board on a nonmerits procedural ground belongs in district court. 568 U.S. at 56, 133 S. Ct. at 607. The Court explained that Congress routed mixed cases to district court whether the Board reaches the merits or disposes of the case otherwise, because “the MSPB’s dismissal of a mixed case for procedural reasons does not take the case outside of § 7703(b)(2).” *Id.* at 55-56, 133 S. Ct. at 607.

This case falls within *Kloeckner*’s rule. Perry alleged that the removal proceedings and resulting retirement were tainted by discrimination and retaliation. The Board did not reach the merits of those claims. It dismissed for lack of jurisdiction after concluding that the settlement and retirement were voluntary. A jurisdictional dismissal, no less than a procedural one, leaves the Board without a merits ruling on the discrimination claims. Under the statute, that means judicial review lies in district court.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with *Kloeckner*. Before *Kloeckner*, some courts—including the Federal Circuit—drew a distinction between procedural dismissals and jurisdictional dismissals. See *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597, 599-600 (D.C. Cir. 1998); *Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board*, 713 F.3d 1111, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013). But the Supreme Court rejected the premise that forum depends on whether the Board’s disposition was merits-based or nonmerits-based. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 54-56, 133 S. Ct. at 606-07. The Court’s reasoning was categorical: “mixed cases” belong in district court. *Id.* at 56, 133 S. Ct. at 607.

The government’s attempt to preserve a jurisdictional/procedural distinction also conflicts with the text of § 7702(a)(1). That provision asks whether the employee “has been affected by an action which [he] may appeal to” the Board and alleges discrimination. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). Perry satisfies that standard because he challenged an adverse action that, if involuntary, is appealable to the Board, and he alleged discrimination as the basis for that action. The Board’s later conclusion that the settlement was voluntary does not retroactively erase the mixed-case character of the appeal. As the Supreme Court observed in *Kloeckner*, the statute speaks in terms of the action affecting the employee, not the Board’s eventual jurisdictional ruling. 568 U.S. at 54-55, 133 S. Ct. at 606-07.

Nor does a jurisdictional dismissal become “ordinary” simply because the Board labels it jurisdictional. The question under § 7703 is where Congress sent review of the employee’s case. Congress sent “cases of discrimination” to district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). That includes a case like this one, where discrimination is alleged and the Board disposes of the matter without resolving the discrimination claims on the merits. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 56-58, 133 S. Ct. at 607-08.

Finally, review in district court best accords with the structure of judicial review Congress created for mixed cases. In *Kloeckner*, the Court rejected the argument that review should depend on whether the Board’s dismissal was on the merits, procedural, or jurisdictional, because “the statute does not ask us to sort cases into two categories.” *Id.* at 54, 133 S. Ct. at 606. The same is true here. Once Perry alleged discrimination in an appealable adverse-action case, his path to judicial review ran to district court. *Id.* at 56-58, 133 S. Ct. at 607-08.

For those reasons, the decision below should be reversed, and the case should be held reviewable in district court.
