**ARGUMENT**

The Tenth Amendment reserves powers to the States, not to individuals. A private party therefore lacks standing to invoke that Amendment as a shield against federal prosecution. Because Carol Anne Bond seeks to assert an interest that belongs exclusively to the States, her Tenth Amendment challenge must be dismissed for want of jurisdiction.

**I. The Tenth Amendment Protects State Sovereignty, Not Individual Rights**

The Tenth Amendment provides that “powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.” This language is a structural limitation on federal authority, not a source of personal liberty. *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941) (the Amendment “states but a truism” that “has been limited to the powers of the States”). Accordingly, only a State—or an officer acting on its behalf—may complain that a federal statute invades the State’s reserved domain. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. TVA*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939) (private utility companies “absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment”).

**II. Bond Cannot Assert a Claim That Belongs to a Third Party**

It is a settled principle that a litigant “must assert his own legal rights and interests, and cannot rest his claim to relief on the legal rights or interests of third parties.” *Alabama Power Co. v. Ickes*, 302 U.S. 464, 479 (1938). Bond’s federalism argument is precisely such a third-party claim: she contends that § 229 usurps the States’ traditional police power. But the States themselves have not objected to the statute, and Bond may not step into their shoes. *Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 488 (1923) (a private party “has no such interest in the alleged invasion of the reserved powers of the State as will enable him to challenge the constitutionality of the act”). The same reasoning applies here.

**III. Bond’s Status as a Criminal Defendant Does Not Confer Standing**

Standing requires a “personal stake” in the outcome—a “direct and substantial” injury that is “distinct from the general public.” *Ex parte Levitt*, 302 U.S. 633, 634 (1937). Bond’s conviction is a consequence of her own voluntary conduct, not an invasion of state sovereignty. She has suffered no injury that is traceable to the alleged federalism violation. *Ashwander v. TVA*, 297 U.S. 288, 324 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring) (a court will not “pass upon the constitutionality of a statute at the instance of one who has not shown that he is injured by its operation”). The Tenth Amendment claim is a generalized grievance about the allocation of power between the federal and state governments, and such grievances are not justiciable when raised by private individuals. *Fairchild v. Hughes*, 258 U.S. 126, 129-30 (1922) (a private citizen lacks standing to challenge the constitutionality of a statute “unless he is directly and adversely affected”).

**IV. Only States May Vindicate Their Own Sovereign Interests**

The proper party to assert a Tenth Amendment claim is the State whose authority is allegedly infringed. *Florida v. Mellon*, 273 U.S. 12, 17 (1927) (a State may sue to protect its “quasi-sovereign rights”); *Stearns v. Minnesota*, 179 U.S. 223, 230 (1900) (a State must assert its own rights, not those of its citizens). Here, no State has challenged § 229. Bond cannot substitute for a sovereign that has chosen not to object. *Tyler v. Judges of Court of Registration*, 179 U.S. 405, 406 (1900) (a party “must have a direct personal interest in the determination of the question” to invoke federal jurisdiction).

**V. The Standing Requirement Is Jurisdictional and Must Be Satisfied Before Reaching the Merits**

Because standing implicates the court’s subject-matter jurisdiction, it must be resolved before any merits inquiry. *Ashwander*, 297 U.S. at 324. Bond’s failure to demonstrate a personal injury from the alleged Tenth Amendment violation compels dismissal of that claim. *Tennessee Electric*, 306 U.S. at 144. The court of appeals correctly held that Bond lacked standing, and its judgment should be affirmed.

**CONCLUSION**

The Tenth Amendment is a structural safeguard for the States, not a sword for private litigants. Bond has no standing to wield it. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
