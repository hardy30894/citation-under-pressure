**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS OF CHAPTER 93-121 ARE FACIALLY INVALID UNDER THE FIRST AMENDMENT BECAUSE THEY ARE NOT NARROWLY TAILORED TO SERVE A COMPELLING STATE INTEREST.**

The Florida disclosure regime sweeps far beyond any legitimate government interest in transparency, chilling core First Amendment activity. As this Court has recognized, "[t]he First Amendment protects the right to petition the Government." *Eastern Railroad Presidents Conference v. Noerr Motor Freight, Inc.*, 365 U.S. 127, 138 (1961). The compelled disclosure of expenditures—including categories as vague as "research" and "communication"—implicates associational and expressive rights by forcing lobbyists to reveal strategic and financial details that may discourage participation in the legislative process. *See Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, 64-66 (1976) (holding that disclosure requirements must be closely tied to preventing corruption or the appearance thereof).

This Court has repeatedly struck down disclosure regimes that are not narrowly tailored. In *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449 (1958), the Supreme Court invalidated a compelled disclosure requirement because it "may constitute as effective a restraint on freedom of association as the imposition of a fine or other penalty upon the association." *Id.* at 463. Similarly, in *Sweezy v. New Hampshire*, 354 U.S. 234 (1957), the Court recognized that "[t]he essentiality of freedom in the community of American universities is almost self-evident." *Id.* at 250. The Florida statute’s broad reach—requiring disclosure of expenditures by category without any showing of corruption or quid pro quo—fails exacting scrutiny. *See Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310, 366-67 (2010) (holding that disclosure requirements must be "justified by a compelling interest" and "narrowly tailored").

The statute’s lack of a materiality threshold further underscores its overbreadth. Unlike the federal lobbying disclosure regime, which requires reporting only of expenditures "in connection with" lobbying contacts, *see* 2 U.S.C. § 1603(a)(3), Florida’s statute demands disclosure of all expenditures "whether made by the lobbyist or by the principal." Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a). This indiscriminate sweep risks exposing innocent associational activity to public scrutiny, thereby deterring participation in the legislative process. *See Buckley*, 424 U.S. at 64-66 (requiring narrow tailoring to avoid chilling protected speech).

**II. THE CONTINGENCY-FEE BAN IS UNCONSTITUTIONAL UNDER THE FIRST AMENDMENT BECAUSE IT RESTRICTS LAWFUL COMMERCIAL SPEECH.**

The Florida statute’s prohibition on contingency-fee lobbying directly burdens lawful commercial speech. The Supreme Court has held that "[c]ommercial speech is constitutionally protected if it concerns lawful activity and is not misleading." *Edenfield v. Fane*, 507 U.S. 761, 767 (1993). Lobbying is a lawful profession, and contingency-fee arrangements are a common and legitimate means of compensating lobbyists for their services. *See Ohralik v. Ohio State Bar Ass’n*, 436 U.S. 447, 456 (1978) (recognizing that commercial speech enjoys First Amendment protection).

This Court has repeatedly struck down restrictions on commercial speech that are not narrowly tailored. In *Central Hudson Gas & Elec. Corp. v. Public Serv. Comm’n of N.Y.*, 447 U.S. 557 (1980), the Supreme Court articulated a four-part test for commercial speech restrictions: (1) the speech must concern lawful activity and not be misleading; (2) the asserted government interest must be substantial; (3) the regulation must directly advance the asserted interest; and (4) the regulation must be narrowly tailored. *Id.* at 566. The Florida statute fails this test.

First, contingency-fee lobbying is lawful activity, and the statute does not allege that such arrangements are misleading. Second, while the state has a substantial interest in preventing corruption, the ban on contingency fees is not narrowly tailored to that end. *See McConnell v. FEC*, 540 U.S. 93, 136-37 (2003) (upholding restrictions on soft money contributions but emphasizing the need for narrow tailoring). The statute’s broad prohibition sweeps in lawful commercial activity without regard to whether the arrangement creates an actual or apparent quid pro quo. *See Citizens United*, 558 U.S. at 366-67 (requiring narrow tailoring to avoid chilling protected speech).

Moreover, this Court is bound by the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612 (1954), which upheld a federal lobbying disclosure regime but did not address contingency-fee arrangements. The Florida statute’s ban on contingency fees goes beyond the Harriss framework and directly burdens lawful commercial speech. *See Edenfield*, 507 U.S. at 767 (holding that commercial speech enjoys First Amendment protection).

**III. THE STATUTE IS FACIALLY INVALID BECAUSE IT LACKS A SAVINGS CLAUSE OR ADMINISTRATIVE PROCEDURE TO CURE ITS OVERBREADTH.**

The Florida statute’s facial invalidity is compounded by the absence of a savings clause or administrative procedure to limit its overbroad reach. In *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601 (1973), the Supreme Court held that facial challenges are disfavored but may proceed where the statute’s overbreadth is "real and substantial." *Id.* at 615. The Florida statute’s lack of a materiality threshold or administrative guidance exacerbates its overbreadth, leaving lobbyists with no clear path to compliance. *See Abbott Lab. v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 152-53 (1967) (holding that facial challenges are appropriate where compliance risks serious penalties).

The statute’s vague categories—such as "research" and "communication"—invite arbitrary enforcement and chill protected speech. *See Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104, 108-09 (1972) (holding that vague laws violate due process by failing to provide fair notice). Without a savings clause or administrative procedure to cabin its reach, the statute is facially invalid. *See Salerno*, 481 U.S. at 745 (holding that facial challenges are the most difficult to mount but may proceed where the statute’s overbreadth is real and substantial).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the disclosure provisions and contingency-fee ban of Chapter 93-121 are facially invalid under the First Amendment. The judgment of the district court should be reversed.
