**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s post-trial instruction striking the rebuttal testimony concerning Morbark’s design change was error. Under Rule 407, evidence of subsequent remedial measures is excluded only when offered to prove negligence or culpable conduct; it is not excluded when offered for another proper purpose. The Supreme Court has long recognized the related principle that otherwise inadmissible evidence may come in to answer a misleading impression created by the opposing party. See *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Ward*, 140 U.S. 76, 88-89 (1891); *Hertz v. Woodman*, 218 U.S. 205, 213-14 (1910). That is exactly what occurred here.

Morbark first obtained a pretrial exclusion of evidence that the infeed chute was lengthened after the accident. But at trial Morbark then repeatedly suggested to the jury that the city continued to use the same machine and, by implication, that no material design change had ever been made. In opening statement, Morbark told the jury that “the Army Corps of Engineers ordered thirty machines just like the one that is involved in this case.” Once Morbark created the impression that the machine remained unchanged, Wood was entitled to correct that misleading inference. The testimony that the chute had been lengthened was therefore not offered to prove defect in the first instance; it was offered to rebut the false suggestion that the machine was still the same in all relevant respects. The law has always permitted a party to answer an adversary’s misleading use of the evidence. See *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Ward*, 140 U.S. at 88-89; *Hertz v. Woodman*, 218 U.S. at 213-14.

The common-law rule is that a party may not “take advantage of his own wrong.” *Sampson v. Murray*, 13 Wall. 61, 72 (1871). Morbark invited the very response it later sought to suppress. The district court itself recognized the fairness problem, telling counsel that if he suggested the city was still using the chipper, “it’s certainly fair for the other side to point out that there have been modifications to that chipper.” That ruling was correct at trial. The later admonition to the jury to disregard the rebuttal testimony nullified it and allowed Morbark to retain the benefit of its misleading presentation. A trial court abuses its discretion when it allows one side to create a false impression and then prevents the other from dispelling it. See *Vicksburg & Meridian R.R. Co. v. Putnam*, 118 U.S. 545, 553-54 (1886); *Hertz v. Woodman*, 218 U.S. at 213-14.

This Court should also reject any suggestion that the rebuttal went “beyond the scope” of the direct examination. Morbark’s questioning sought to establish that the city still had “that Morbark chipper machine” and that it was “the same machine.” Once those questions were asked, Wood was entitled to show that the machine was not “the same” in the relevant sense because the chute had been extended. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that when one party opens a subject, the opponent may explore it to avoid misleading the trier of fact. See *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Ward*, 140 U.S. at 88-89; *Hertz v. Woodman*, 218 U.S. at 213-14.

Nor can the error be dismissed as harmless. This was a strict products-liability case turning on whether the chute was unreasonably short and dangerously designed. Morbark’s central theme was that the machine remained in use unchanged and therefore was safe. The excluded rebuttal testimony directly undercut that theme. Where evidence bears directly on the contested issue and on the credibility of a party’s central factual claim, its improper exclusion cannot be treated as immaterial. See *Vicksburg & Meridian R.R. Co. v. Putnam*, 118 U.S. at 553-54. Because the jury was instructed to disregard evidence it had properly heard for impeachment and rebuttal, Wood was denied a fair opportunity to answer Morbark’s misleading argument.

The proper course is reversal and a new trial. The common law has never required a party to sit silent while an adversary creates a false impression from a prior ruling. It demands the opposite: that the jury hear the whole truth. See *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Ward*, 140 U.S. 76 (1891); *Hertz v. Woodman*, 218 U.S. 205 (1910).
