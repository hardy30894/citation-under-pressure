## ARGUMENT

### I. The district court erred in holding that Epps failed to raise a triable issue under the ADA and MHRA.

Summary judgment is reviewed de novo, with all reasonable inferences drawn in Epps’s favor. *Meyer v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 144 F.3d 564, 567 (8th Cir. 1998). Under the ADA, an employer unlawfully discriminates if it takes adverse action against a qualified individual because of an actual or perceived disability. 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a); *Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999). A plaintiff is “regarded as” disabled when the employer mistakenly believes the employee has a substantially limiting impairment or mistakenly believes an actual impairment substantially limits a major life activity. *Id.* at 489; *Sullivan v. River Valley Sch. Dist.*, 197 F.3d 804, 810 (8th Cir. 1999).

Epps produced ample evidence that Pine Lawn regarded him as unable to perform the broad class of police officer duties. Chief Hardy reviewed Epps’s file, noted his many workers’ compensation claims and extensive missed work, and relied on Dr. Mirkin’s statement that Epps would likely need a different occupation. Hardy’s first memorandum recommended termination because Epps was unable to perform his duties; his second memorandum repeated that there were no light-duty jobs and that officers had to bend, squat, and run. On this record, a factfinder could conclude that Pine Lawn viewed Epps not as temporarily injured, but as permanently unable to function as a regular police officer.

That is enough to create a fact issue under Eighth Circuit precedent. In *Webb v. Mercy Hosp.*, 102 F.3d 958, 960-61 (8th Cir. 1996), the court held that an employer may regard an employee as disabled where it believes the employee is substantially limited in the ability to work. Likewise, in *Breiland v. Advance Circuits, Inc.*, 976 F.2d 1033, 1034-35 (8th Cir. 1992), the court explained that the question is the employer’s perception, not the employee’s actual condition. Pine Lawn’s own memoranda show that it treated Epps’s condition as disqualifying for police work. At minimum, a jury could find that the City regarded him as substantially limited in the major life activity of working.

The district court relied too heavily on the fact that Pine Lawn initially refused to terminate Epps. But an employer’s delay in acting does not negate evidence of discriminatory perception when the ultimate decision rests on the same disability-based assumptions. See *Snow v. Ridgeview Med. Ctr.*, 128 F.3d 1201, 1207 (8th Cir. 1997). The relevant question is whether the adverse action was motivated by a perceived disability, and the memoranda here supply that inference.

Epps’s MHRA claim rises or falls with the ADA analysis. Missouri courts interpret the MHRA in harmony with federal disability-discrimination law. *Kobusch v. Handicapped, Inc.*, 116 S.W.3d 27, 33 (Mo. Ct. App. 2003). Because the record would allow a finding that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled, summary judgment on the ADA and MHRA claims must be reversed.

### II. Sovereign immunity does not bar Epps’s retaliatory-discharge claim under § 287.780.

The district court also erred in holding that Missouri’s sovereign immunity statute bars Epps’s claim under Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780. Section 287.780 creates a statutory cause of action for retaliation when an employee is discharged for filing or pursuing a workers’ compensation claim. Missouri courts have recognized that retaliation for seeking workers’ compensation benefits is contrary to the public policy embodied in the Workers’ Compensation Law. *Davis v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co. Consol.*, 516 S.W.2d 856, 862 (Mo. Ct. App. 1974); *Jenkins v. McGraw-Hill Cos.*, 130 S.W.3d 472, 482 (Mo. Ct. App. 2003).

A political subdivision is not automatically immune from every statutory claim. Missouri’s sovereign immunity statute preserves immunity for tort liability, but the legislature may waive that immunity by statute, and courts must give effect to express statutory remedies. *Richardson v. State Highway & Transp. Comm’n*, 863 S.W.2d 876, 879-80 (Mo. banc 1993). Section 287.780 is such a specific remedial statute. It directly regulates employer conduct and authorizes damages for a retaliatory discharge. The district court’s application of general immunity to defeat that specific legislative remedy conflicts with Missouri’s rule that a specific statute controls over a general one. *State ex rel. Sikeston R-6 Sch. Dist. v. Ashcroft*, 828 S.W.2d 372, 376 (Mo. banc 1992).

Moreover, the City acted in its capacity as Epps’s employer, not as a sovereign exercising uniquely governmental functions. Missouri courts have permitted retaliation claims to proceed against public employers when the statutory prerequisites are met. See *Twain v. Western Mo. Med. Ctr.*, 95 S.W.3d 470, 477-78 (Mo. Ct. App. 2002). Epps alleged that Pine Lawn terminated him because he filed workers’ compensation claims, and the City’s memoranda expressly referenced those claims as part of the termination rationale. That evidence is sufficient to allow the retaliation claim to proceed.

### III. The record does not support summary judgment on the intentional-infliction claim.

Finally, Epps’s emotional-distress claim should not have been dismissed on immunity grounds. Missouri recognizes intentional infliction of emotional distress where conduct is extreme and outrageous. *Pretsky v. Southwestern Bell Tel. Co.*, 896 S.W.2d 353, 355-56 (Mo. Ct. App. 1995). A municipality is not immune from liability for intentional torts committed outside the scope of any protected governmental immunity where a statute permits suit or where immunity has been waived. *Southers v. City of Farmington*, 263 S.W.3d 603, 611-12 (Mo. banc 2008).

Here, Epps presented evidence that the City repeatedly relied on his work injuries and compensation claims to justify termination. Combined with the claimed termination after years of service and medical leave, a jury could find severe and outrageous conduct if the City acted with the purpose of punishing him for asserting statutory rights. At minimum, the district court should not have resolved that claim as a matter of law.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial on all claims.
