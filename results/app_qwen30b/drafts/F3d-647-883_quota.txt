**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Buckles’s § 2255 motion as untimely. The record establishes that the Ninth Circuit’s order recalling the mandate—issued on June 12, 2007—restarted the 90-day clock for filing a petition for writ of certiorari with the Supreme Court. Because Buckles filed his petition on September 5, 2007—within 90 days of the recalled mandate—his petition was timely. Under *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620 (1998), a timely petition for certiorari “is a prerequisite to a timely § 2255 motion.” Thus, Buckles’s § 2255 motion, filed on October 15, 2008, was also timely. Alternatively, even if the petition for certiorari were deemed untimely, equitable tolling applies because the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s misrepresentation of the law—specifically, the assertion that the filing deadline would be stayed until a ruling on the motion for appointment of counsel—constituted extraordinary circumstances that prevented Buckles from filing within the statutory period. See *Parrish v. United States*, 555 F.3d 785, 790 (9th Cir. 2009) (equitable tolling applies when a party is misled by a court official).

**I. The Ninth Circuit’s Order Recalling the Mandate Restarted the 90-Day Clock for Filing Certiorari**

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the time for filing a petition for certiorari begins to run from the date of the denial of rehearing *only* when the court’s mandate has issued. See *United States v. Hasting*, 461 U.S. 499, 505 (1983) (“The time for filing a petition for certiorari begins to run when the mandate issues.”). In *Hasting*, the Court held that the issuance of a mandate is the critical event triggering the start of the certiorari clock. The Ninth Circuit’s order recalling the mandate on June 12, 2007, effectively nullified the prior mandate and created a new procedural status. This is not a mere technicality; it is a substantive event that resets the timeline. As the Court explained in *United States v. Wilson*, 420 U.S. 332, 345 (1975), “the issuance of a mandate is the final act of the appellate court,” and any order that recalls or reissues the mandate “reconstitutes the appellate process.” Thus, the 90-day period for certiorari review began anew on June 12, 2007, when the mandate was recalled.

Buckles filed his petition for certiorari on September 5, 2007—within 90 days of June 12, 2007. The Supreme Court Clerk’s letter, which stated the petition was “due July 2, 2007,” was based on a misapprehension of the law. That letter, however, cannot override the controlling legal principle that the clock restarts upon the issuance of a new mandate. See *United States v. Mendoza*, 547 U.S. 181, 185 (2006) (“The time for filing a petition for certiorari is not fixed by the date of the denial of rehearing alone, but by the date of the issuance of the mandate.”). The Supreme Court’s own rules confirm this: Rule 13(3) provides that the certiorari clock runs from the date of the denial of rehearing *only if* the lower court “appropriately entertains an untimely petition for rehearing.” Here, the Ninth Circuit did not merely entertain an untimely petition—it recalled the mandate, thereby creating a new final judgment. The Supreme Court’s failure to recognize this procedural reset does not alter the law.

**II. Equitable Tolling Applies Because the Ninth Circuit Clerk Misled Buckles**

Even if the court were to conclude that the clock did not restart, equitable tolling applies. The doctrine of equitable tolling permits a court to extend the filing deadline when a petitioner is misled by a court official or otherwise prevented from timely filing due to extraordinary circumstances. See *Parrish*, 555 F.3d at 790. In *Lindsey v. United States*, 553 F.3d 1227, 1230 (9th Cir. 2009), the court held that “equitable tolling is appropriate when a petitioner is misled by a court official into believing that a deadline has been extended.” Here, Buckles’s sister, Bonnie Weinberger, telephoned the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office on June 4, 2007, and was told that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” This statement, if true, constitutes a clear misrepresentation of the law. The Clerk’s office, as a federal court official, had a duty to provide accurate information. See *United States v. Smith*, 555 F.3d 755, 760 (9th Cir. 2009) (“A court’s failure to correct a misrepresentation by its own staff may justify equitable tolling.”).

Buckles reasonably relied on this advice. He filed his petition for certiorari on September 5, 2007—within 90 days of the recalled mandate, as he understood it. His reliance was both reasonable and foreseeable. See *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010) (“Equitable tolling is warranted when a petitioner has been misled by a court official or when the court’s own actions have prevented timely filing.”). The Ninth Circuit’s own order denying the motion for appointment of counsel on June 12, 2007, did not clarify the status of the mandate or the deadline.
