**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Foley’s state-law claims. Each count states a plausible cause of action, and no federal law preempts these claims.

**I. Foley Stated a Claim for Breach of Contract as a Third-Party Beneficiary**

The settlement agreement between Wells Fargo and the California class expressly requires the bank to consider Foley for a HAMP modification before foreclosure. Foley is an intended beneficiary of that promise. *Lawrence v. Fox*, 20 N.Y. 268, 274 (1859) (third-party beneficiary may enforce contract made for his benefit). Massachusetts follows this rule: *Fluet v. McCabe*, 299 Mass. 173, 176, 12 N.E.2d 89, 91 (1937) (one for whose benefit a promise is made may sue). Foley’s complaint alleges that Wells Fargo failed to consider him for HAMP, then scheduled foreclosure—directly violating the settlement’s “servicing commitments.” These allegations, taken as true, state a claim. *Conley v. Gibson*, 355 U.S. 41, 45–46 (1957) (complaint need only give “fair notice” of claim and grounds upon which it rests).

**II. Foley Stated a Claim for Promissory Estoppel**

Wells Fargo promised Foley that it would send a HAMP application and consider him for modification. Foley relied on that promise by continuing to make partial payments and forgoing other relief. *Ricketts v. Scothorn*, 57 Neb. 51, 57, 77 N.W. 365, 367 (1898) (promissory estoppel applies where promisor should reasonably expect reliance and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement). Massachusetts adopted this doctrine in *Loranger v. Loranger*, 331 Mass. 165, 170, 117 N.E.2d 902, 905 (1954) (promise inducing action or forbearance may be binding). Foley’s complaint details repeated promises, his reliance, and the bank’s subsequent silence and foreclosure—sufficient to survive dismissal.

**III. Foley’s Statutory Claim Under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A Is Not Preempted**

Wells Fargo argues that the federal Home Affordable Modification Program (HAMP) preempts Foley’s state-law claims. But preemption is disfavored in areas of traditional state authority. *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (presumption against preemption where state law has “historic police powers”). Mortgage servicing and consumer protection are such areas. HAMP does not occupy the field; it is a voluntary program that sets guidelines, not mandatory federal standards. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (field preemption requires “clear and manifest” congressional intent). No such intent appears here. Moreover, Foley’s 93A claim—based on Wells Fargo’s deceptive conduct in failing to process his application—does not conflict with HAMP’s requirements. *Florida Lime & Avocado Growers, Inc. v. Paul*, 373 U.S. 132, 142 (1963) (preemption only when state law makes it “impossible” to comply with federal law). Compliance with both is possible. The district court therefore erred in dismissing the 93A count on preemption grounds.

**Conclusion**

Foley’s complaint pleads facts sufficient to support each cause of action under well-settled common law and statute. No federal law preempts these state claims. This court should reverse the dismissal and remand for further proceedings.
