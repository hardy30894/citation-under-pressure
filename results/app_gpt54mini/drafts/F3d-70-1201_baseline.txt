**Argument**

The district court erred in reversing its own evidentiary ruling and admonishing the jury to disregard testimony that Morbark had “opened the door” to admission of. The testimony concerning the post-accident extension of the infeed chute was admissible to rebut Morbark’s repeated insinuations that the machine remained unchanged and therefore must have been safe. Excluding that rebuttal evidence deprived Wood of a fair opportunity to answer a misleading inference created by Morbark’s opening statement and cross-examination.

Federal Rule of Evidence 407 excludes subsequent remedial measures when offered to prove negligence or culpable conduct, but it does not bar such evidence when offered for another permissible purpose, including impeachment. Fed. R. Evid. 407; see **Josephs v. Harris Corp., 677 F.2d 985, 990–91 (3d Cir. 1982)** (Rule 407 permits use of subsequent remedial measures for impeachment where defendant creates misleading impression); **Kelley v. Crown Equipment Co., 970 F.2d 1273, 1277–78 (3d Cir. 1992)** (evidence admissible where necessary to rebut misleading testimony regarding safety); **Muzyka v. Remington Arms Co., 774 F.2d 1309, 1313–14 (5th Cir. 1985)** (Rule 407 does not permit a party to use the rule as both “sword and shield”). The district court’s pretrial ruling properly recognized that distinction. Once Morbark suggested to the jury that the same chipper was still in daily use and thus could not have been defective, Wood was entitled to show the machine had in fact been modified after the accident.

This Court has not previously decided whether Rule 407 applies in strict products liability cases, but that question need not be resolved to decide this appeal. Even courts applying Rule 407 in product cases recognize that the rule does not bar impeachment when the opposing party misleads the jury. See **Werner v. Upjohn Co., 628 F.2d 848, 855–56 (4th Cir. 1980)**; **Kenny v. Seaboard Coast Line R.R. Co., 716 F.2d 231, 240–41 (3d Cir. 1983)**. The rationale is straightforward: when a party suggests the product remained unchanged and thereby implies safety, fairness permits the adversary to correct the false impression. See **Petree v. Victor Fluid Power, Inc., 887 F.2d 34, 38–39 (3d Cir. 1989)**.

That is exactly what happened here. Morbark’s counsel told the jury that after Hurricane Andrew the Army Corps of Engineers ordered “thirty machines just like the one that is involved in this case,” implying the same model and design remained in circulation. R4-141-22. Counsel then reinforced the point through cross-examination of Infinger, eliciting that the city still had “that Morbark chipper machine,” and “it’s the same machine.” Id. at 44. Only after Morbark invited the inference that no material change had been made did Wood ask whether the chute had been altered. Infinger answered that “another chute has been, as seen on that, there has been welded another, made it that much longer.” Id. at 51. That testimony was classic impeachment, not forbidden proof of liability. See **United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45, 56 (1984)** (impeachment includes evidence that contradicts a witness’s testimony); **United States v. Havens, 446 U.S. 620, 627–28 (1980)** (evidence otherwise inadmissible may be used to impeach testimony elicited on cross-examination).

The district court’s later instruction to the jury that it should disregard the testimony because Wood had “opened the door” was legal error. A party does not “open the door” to exclusion by insisting on a false or misleading impression and then invoking Rule 407 against the corrective evidence. See **Josephs, 677 F.2d at 991**; **Petree, 887 F.2d at 39**. The “fair rebuttal” referenced by the court was precisely what the rules permit. The court’s admonition effectively told the jury that Morbark’s insinuation about unchanged design was beyond challenge, which skewed the central liability issue: whether the short infeed chute made the chipper unreasonably dangerous. See **Hull v. Chevron U.S.A., Inc., 812 F.2d 584, 586–87 (10th Cir. 1987)** (rebuttal of misleading impression is proper use of subsequent remedial measure evidence).

The error was not harmless. Morbark’s defense depended on persuading the jury that the machine’s original design was safe enough to remain in use, and the post-accident extension of the chute directly undercut that claim. Where excluded evidence bears on a central disputed issue and responds to a misleading assertion by the opposing party, a new trial is required. See **Kelley, 970 F.2d at 1278**; **Muzyka, 774 F.2d at 1314**.

The judgment denying a new trial should therefore be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial free from the erroneous curative instruction.
