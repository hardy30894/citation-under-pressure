I. Standing to Enforce the Arbitration Award

Bryant possesses standing to enforce the arbitration award because the collective bargaining agreement confers individual rights enforceable by the employee. Vaca v. Sipes, 386 U.S. 171, 183 (1967). An individual employee may sue to enforce an award when the union has breached its duty of fair representation or the employer has repudiated the agreement. Id. at 185-86. The district court therefore erred in denying enforcement on standing grounds. See Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Co., 415 U.S. 36, 49 (1974) (recognizing that Title VII rights are independent of contractual remedies).

II. Clarification of the Burden-Shifting Framework

The district court misapplied the burden-shifting standard clarified in Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133 (2000). Under McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973), a plaintiff establishes a prima facie case of discrimination by showing membership in a protected class, qualification for the position, adverse action, and circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination. Texas Dep't of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981). Once the employer articulates a legitimate reason, the plaintiff may prevail by showing that reason is pretextual. St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993). Reeves confirms that a prima facie case plus evidence of pretext permits the trier of fact to infer discrimination without additional proof. 530 U.S. at 148. The district court improperly required Bryant to produce evidence beyond pretext.

III. Genuine Issues of Material Fact

Summary judgment is improper when the record reveals genuine issues of material fact. Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986); Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). Bryant's evidence that similarly situated white employees received more favorable treatment on overtime assignments, that the Red Letter Day policy was applied inconsistently after his complaints, and that his termination followed protected activity creates triable issues on both the discrimination and retaliation claims under Title VII and 42 U.S.C. § 1981. The district court's resolution of these factual disputes on summary judgment was error.
