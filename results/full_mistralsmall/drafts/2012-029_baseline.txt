**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Circuit’s Decision in *Air Measurement* Correctly Recognizes That Federal Courts Have Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Legal Malpractice Claims That Require Resolution of Substantial and Necessary Patent Law Issues.**

The Supreme Court has long held that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. *See Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988) (holding that § 1338(a) grants federal courts jurisdiction over any case in which a plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law). This principle applies with equal force to state legal malpractice claims that require resolution of patent law issues. *See Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1271-73 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (finding exclusive federal jurisdiction over a state legal malpractice claim where the plaintiff’s right to relief depended on the validity of a patent).

In *Air Measurement*, the Federal Circuit applied the Supreme Court’s framework from *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308 (2005), to determine whether the state legal malpractice claim arose under federal patent law. *Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1271. The court held that federal jurisdiction was proper because (1) the plaintiff’s right to relief depended on the validity of a patent, (2) the patent issue was disputed and substantial, and (3) federal resolution of the patent issue would not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Id.* at 1272-73. This Court should adopt the same reasoning.

**II. The Patent Issue in This Case Is Necessary, Disputed, and Substantial, and Federal Resolution Will Not Disturb the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities.**

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Grable* provides the controlling framework for determining whether a state legal malpractice claim arises under federal patent law. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. Under *Grable*, federal jurisdiction exists where (1) resolving a federal issue is necessary to resolution of the state-law claim; (2) the federal issue is actually disputed; (3) the federal issue is substantial; and (4) federal jurisdiction will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Id.* All four factors are satisfied here.

First, the patent issue is *necessary* to Minton’s malpractice claim. To prevail, Minton must prove that Gunn’s negligence proximately caused his injury—that is, that he would have prevailed in the underlying patent infringement suit but for Gunn’s failure to raise the experimental use exception. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). This requires a determination of whether the experimental use exception applied to Minton’s patent, a question of federal patent law. *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (explaining the experimental use exception). Without resolving this federal patent issue, Minton cannot establish causation, and his malpractice claim must fail.

Second, the patent issue is *disputed*. Gunn does not dispute that the experimental use exception is a valid defense to the on-sale bar under § 102(b) of the Patent Act. *See* 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). Instead, Gunn argues that the exception was not factually viable because the primary purpose of the TEXCEN lease was commercial, not experimental. This dispute over the application of federal patent law is squarely presented in Minton’s malpractice claim.

Third, the patent issue is *substantial*. The experimental use exception is a well-established doctrine in patent law, and its application often turns on fact-intensive inquiries. *See, e.g., id.* (discussing the experimental use exception). The Federal Circuit has recognized that legal malpractice claims involving the experimental use exception implicate substantial federal patent law issues. *See Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1272-73. This Court should defer to the Federal Circuit’s expertise in patent law and recognize the substantiality of the patent issue here.

Finally, federal resolution of the patent issue will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. The patent issue is embedded in Minton’s malpractice claim, and federal courts are better equipped to resolve disputes involving federal patent law. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 818-19 (recognizing the federal interest in uniform patent law). Moreover, federal resolution of the patent issue will not open the floodgates to state courts adjudicating patent disputes, as the malpractice claim itself arises under state law.

**III. The Supreme Court of Texas Erred in Rejecting Federal Jurisdiction Over Minton’s Malpractice Claim.**

The Supreme Court of Texas held that it had jurisdiction over Minton’s malpractice claim because the patent issue could be resolved without creating a jurisdictional imbalance. *See* slip op. at 12. This reasoning misapplies the *Grable* framework. The Supreme Court has made clear that federal jurisdiction exists where a state-law claim necessarily raises a disputed and substantial federal issue, regardless of whether the federal issue can be resolved without disturbing the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *See Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314-15. The Supreme Court of Texas’ decision conflicts with *Grable* and the Federal Circuit’s decision in *Air Measurement*, and this Court should reverse.
