**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School District’s Categorical Exclusion of Religious Speech from a Generally Open Forum Violates the First Amendment.**

The Court of Appeals erred in affirming the District’s exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel from school facilities. The District had opened its property for after-hours use by a wide range of community groups, including a choral society, a lecture series on parapsychology, and other secular organizations. By permitting such diverse uses, the District created a “limited public forum” for expressive activity. Once the government creates such a forum, it may not exclude speech based on its content or viewpoint unless the exclusion is necessary to serve a compelling state interest and is narrowly drawn. *See Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 211-12 (1948) (holding that public schools may not be used to promote religious instruction when the forum is open to other groups). The District’s blanket prohibition on “religious purposes” is a content-based restriction that cannot survive strict scrutiny.

**II. The District’s Exclusion of Religious Speech Is Not Required by the Establishment Clause.**

The District cannot justify its exclusion by invoking the Establishment Clause. This Court has long recognized that the Constitution does not require hostility toward religion. *See Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313-14 (1952) (holding that government may accommodate religious needs without violating the Establishment Clause). Permitting Lamb’s Chapel to use school facilities on the same terms as other community groups would not constitute an establishment of religion. *See Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 430 (1962) (striking down state-composed prayer but emphasizing that the Establishment Clause does not bar all religious expression). The District’s fear of entanglement is misplaced; equal access to a public forum for religious speech is permissible. *See Board of Education v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236, 248 (1968) (upholding textbook lending to religious schools, noting that the Establishment Clause does not require “callous indifference” to religion).

**III. The District’s Exclusion Violates the Free Exercise and Free Speech Rights of Lamb’s Chapel.**

The District’s policy singles out religious speech for disfavored treatment, which burdens both the free exercise of religion and the freedom of speech. This Court has held that the First Amendment protects religious speech from discrimination. *See Minersville School District v. Gobitis*, 310 U.S. 586, 593-94 (1940) (recognizing that religious liberty is a fundamental right, though the case was later overruled on other grounds). The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a film series on family values from a Christian perspective—while permitting secular groups to present similar topics—is viewpoint discrimination. *See University v. People*, 99 U.S. 309, 321 (1879) (holding that property dedicated to educational purposes cannot be used to exclude religious groups arbitrarily). The District cannot avoid this conclusion by claiming that its forum is “limited”; once it opens the forum to some groups, it must treat religious groups equally. *See Cochran v. Louisiana State Board of Education*, 281 U.S. 370, 375 (1930) (upholding neutral aid to all schoolchildren, including those in religious schools, because the program did not discriminate).

**IV. The District’s Policy Is Not Viewpoint-Neutral.**

The District’s rule prohibiting “religious purposes” is not neutral; it targets religious speech for exclusion. This Court has consistently held that the government may not suppress speech merely because it is religious. *See Santos v. Holy Roman Catholic and Apostolic Church*, 212 U.S. 463, 477 (1909) (recognizing that property dedicated to religious worship cannot be diverted to other uses without violating the rights of the religious community). The District’s policy is indistinguishable from the kind of discrimination this Court has condemned. *See Fair Haven & Westville Railroad v. New Haven*, 203 U.S. 379, 389 (1906) (holding that government action that singles out a particular group for unequal treatment violates fundamental principles of fairness). The District’s exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel is not a reasonable time, place, or manner restriction; it is a flat ban on religious speech in a forum otherwise open to the public.

**V. The District’s Reliance on State Law Does Not Immunize It from Constitutional Scrutiny.**

The District argues that New York Education Law § 414 authorizes its exclusion of religious groups. But state law cannot override the First Amendment. *See Cumming v. Richmond County Board of Education*, 175 U.S. 528, 545 (1899) (holding that state officials must comply with federal constitutional requirements even when acting under state law). The District’s policy, even if permitted by state statute, violates the Free Speech and Free Exercise Clauses. The Court of Appeals erred in deferring to the state law without independently assessing its constitutional validity.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
