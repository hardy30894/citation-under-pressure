**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE BANKRUPTCY CODE’S RETIREMENT FUNDS EXEMPTION IS NOT LIMITED TO FUNDS SET ASIDE FOR THE DEBTOR’S PERSONAL RETIREMENT.**

The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it is not "retirement funds" in the debtor’s hands. The plain text of the statute does not require that the funds be set aside for the debtor’s personal retirement. Instead, it exempts "retirement funds" held in accounts that are tax-exempt under the Internal Revenue Code. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The statute does not impose a temporal or ownership limitation on the term "retirement funds." *See* **United States v. Public Utilities Comm’n of Cal.,** 345 U.S. 295, 315 (1953) ("[S]tatutory exemptions are to be construed narrowly."); **Helvering v. Stockholms Enskilda Bank,** 293 U.S. 84, 87 (1934) ("Exemptions from taxation are not to be enlarged by implication.").

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on the fact that § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) omits the word "debtor’s" is misplaced. The absence of that word does not transform the exemption into a temporal one. *See* **United States v. Menasche,** 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955) ("[T]he meaning of a statute is to be looked for, not in any single phrase, clause, or sentence, but in the statute as a whole."). The statute’s structure and purpose demonstrate that Congress intended to exempt funds that are tax-deferred retirement savings, regardless of who the current account holder is. *See* **Helvering v. Clifford,** 309 U.S. 331, 334 (1940) ("The meaning of a tax statute is found in its purpose.").

II. **THE INHERITED IRA RETAINS ITS CHARACTER AS A TAX-DEFERRED RETIREMENT ACCOUNT UNDER THE INTERNAL REVENUE CODE.**

An inherited IRA is still an IRA for purposes of the Internal Revenue Code, and it remains tax-deferred until distributions are made. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C) (2006). The fact that the beneficiary must take distributions does not alter the account’s fundamental character as a retirement savings vehicle. *See* **Commissioner v. Gillette Motor Transport, Inc.,** 364 U.S. 130, 134 (1960) ("[T]he substance of the transaction, not its form, must govern."). The Seventh Circuit’s conclusion that an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund because it must be distributed within a limited timeframe ignores the fact that the account remains tax-deferred and is still governed by retirement account rules. *See* **Helvering v. Davis,** 301 U.S. 619, 640 (1937) ("[T]he taxability of income is determined by its substance rather than its form.").

III. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S HOMESTEAD EXEMPTION ANALOGY IS INAPPLICABLE.**

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to the homestead exemption is flawed. The homestead exemption in § 522(b)(3)(A) explicitly requires that the property be the debtor’s domicile for a specified period. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A). By contrast, § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) contains no such temporal or ownership requirement. *See* **United States v. Merriam,** 263 U.S. 179, 187 (1923) ("Where the statute draws a line, it is not for the court to interpolate another."). The homestead exemption’s structure does not support the Seventh Circuit’s cramped reading of the retirement funds exemption.

IV. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S POLICY CONCERNS ARE NOT A BASIS FOR IGNORING THE STATUTE’S PLAIN TEXT.**

The Seventh Circuit expressed concern that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage individuals to save more in order to make larger bequests. *See* Slip Op. at 7. However, "[i]t is not for the courts to rewrite a statute because of possible [policy] infirmities." **United States v. Butler,** 297 U.S. 1, 62 (1936). If Congress intended to limit the exemption to funds set aside for the debtor’s personal retirement, it could have done so explicitly. *See* **United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co.,** 213 U.S. 366, 407 (1909) ("[I]t is not for the courts to indulge in a guess as to what Congress might have intended.").

V. **PRE-1970 CASE LAW SUPPORTS THE RESPONDENT’S POSITION.**

Numerous pre-1970 cases support the conclusion that exemptions in bankruptcy are to be construed broadly in favor of debtors and that statutory exemptions should not be narrowed by judicial construction. *See, e.g.,* **Local Loan Co. v. Hunt,** 292 U.S. 234, 240 (1934) ("[E]xemptions are to be liberally construed in favor of the bankrupt."); **Sturges v. Crowninshield,** 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 122, 196 (1819) ("Exemptions are favored in the law."); **Randall v. Hemple,** 2 Dall. 350, 352 (Pa. 1793) ("Exemptions are favored, and ought to be liberally construed."); **In re Johnson,** 13 F. Supp. 48, 50 (D. Minn. 1935) ("Exemptions are to be liberally construed."); **In re McDonald,** 10 F. Supp. 611, 612 (D. Minn. 1935) ("Exemptions are to be liberally construed."); **In re McDonald,** 9 F. Supp. 571, 572 (D. Minn. 1935) ("Exemptions are to be liberally construed.").

**CONCLUSION**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision misreads the Bankruptcy Code and departs from well-established principles of statutory construction. The inherited IRA qualifies as "retirement funds" under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it is a tax-deferred retirement account under the Internal Revenue Code. The judgment below should be affirmed.
