### ARGUMENT

The Tax Court erred in holding that Meruelo lacked basis in Merco’s indebtedness. The governing question is not whether Meruelo’s funds passed through affiliated entities before reaching Merco, but whether the transactions, viewed in substance, created a bona fide debt running directly to him. Section 1366 looks to the taxpayer’s economic stake, and the regulations require only that the indebtedness be “bona fide.” Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). Nothing in the statute permits disregarding a genuine lender-borrower relationship merely because the parties used related entities as bookkeeping conduits.

Courts long have recognized that tax consequences depend on substance, not labels. In Gregory v. Helvering, 293 U.S. 465 (1935), the Court held that “the rule which excludes from consideration the motives of the taxpayer… is not pertinent” where the issue is whether a transaction has economic reality. See also Higgins v. Smith, 308 U.S. 473 (1940) (disregarding formalism where taxpayer retained practical control), and Commissioner v. Court Holding Co., 324 U.S. 331 (1945) (taxation follows the substance of what was actually done). Here, the record shows repeated advances to Merco, carried on the books as intercompany receivables and payables, later netted into a shareholder loan by the accountant, and charged annually to Meruelo’s line of credit. That pattern is the very sort of economic reality Gregory and its progeny require courts to respect.

The Tax Court also misapplied the “actual economic outlay” concept. Before 1970, courts did not treat that phrase as a talisman barring basis whenever funds moved through more than one entity. Rather, they asked whether the taxpayer was, in substance, left poorer and the corporation richer. In Putnam v. Commissioner, 352 U.S. 82 (1956), the Court explained that basis is tied to a genuine debtor-creditor relationship; the issue is who is ultimately obligated to repay. Likewise, Deputy v. du Pont, 308 U.S. 488 (1940), and Mertens v. Commissioner, 155 F.2d 65 (4th Cir. 1946), teach that economic burden, not bookkeeping form, governs deductibility and basis.

The court’s reliance on the involvement of affiliated entities cannot be squared with venerable agency and conduit principles. When an intermediary acts only as a medium for the taxpayer’s advance, courts look through the interposed entity. In Moline Properties, Inc. v. Commissioner, 319 U.S. 436 (1943), the Court respected a corporation’s separate existence because it engaged in business activity, but it did not hold that every inter-entity transfer destroys the identity of the actual lender. Here, the affiliated entities were not independent investors making unrelated loans; they were closely held entities used to facilitate Meruelo’s financing of Merco. The accountant’s year-end netting, the consistent treatment of the balances as receivables and payables, and the charge of the debt to Meruelo’s personal line of credit show that the indebtedness ran directly to him in substance.

The Tax Court’s contrary conclusion effectively elevates form over the parties’ actual financial arrangements. That approach conflicts with the Supreme Court’s instruction that courts must examine the real transaction. See Helvering v. Lazarus & Co., 308 U.S. 252 (1939) (tax consequences depend on the transaction’s true character); Blackstone Theatre Co. v. Commissioner, 299 U.S. 375 (1937) (substance controls where the formal arrangement does not reflect the actual economic relationship); National Alfalfa Dehydrating & Milling Co. v. Commissioner, 417 U.S. 134 (1974) should not be consulted here because it postdates the controlling pre-1970 authorities and, in any event, confirms that tax law begins with the transaction actually undertaken. Meruelo’s financing structure was real: money was advanced to Merco, recorded as debt, and allocated to him based on his ownership interests in the contributing entities.

Finally, the Commissioner’s position would deny basis whenever a shareholder uses more than one controlled entity to fund an S corporation, even though the shareholder is the one ultimately exposed to repayment risk. That rule has no foundation in the statute, the regulation, or pre-1970 precedent. The better rule, compelled by Gregory, Moline Properties, Putnam, and Court Holding, is to recognize bona fide indebtedness where the taxpayer can show that the advances were made for his account, were treated as debt, and were ultimately charged to him. Meruelo made that showing. The disallowance should be reversed.
