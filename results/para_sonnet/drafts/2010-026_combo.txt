# ARGUMENT

## I. Exemption 2 Shields Only Matters "Related Solely to the Internal Personnel Rules and Practices of an Agency," and the Ninth Circuit's "Predominantly Internal" Standard Cannot Be Reconciled With That Text.

Congress wrote Exemption 2 to cover records "related **solely** to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2) (emphasis added). The Ninth Circuit did not apply that word. It applied a different one—"predominantly"—borrowed from the D.C. Circuit's decision in *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*. But a court may not rewrite a statute's chosen modifier to reach a preferred result. As this Court explained in *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), "[t]here is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes. Often these words are sufficient in and of themselves to determine the purpose of the legislation." Congress's words here are sufficient: "solely" means exclusively, not "for the most part" or "chiefly." Substituting "predominantly" for "solely" is not interpretation; it is amendment by judicial fiat.

That substitution is especially indefensible because FOIA exemptions are not ordinary statutory text to be construed for administrative convenience—they are exceptions to a broad remedial disclosure scheme, and this Court has long held that exceptions to remedial legislation must be read narrowly, not expansively. In *A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling*, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945), the Court held that "[a]ny exemption from such humanitarian and remedial legislation must therefore be narrowly construed, giving due regard to the plain meaning of statutory language and the intent of Congress." The same term was applied in *Addison v. Holly Hill Fruit Products, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 607, 617 (1944), where the Court refused to extend a statutory exemption "to other than those plainly and unmistakably within its terms and spirit," lest the courts "abuse the interpretative process and . . . frustrate the announced will of the people." FOIA embodies precisely that kind of remedial policy: disclosure is the rule, and exemptions from disclosure are the narrow exception. The Navy's arc maps and safety data are not "plainly and unmistakably within" a category limited to "internal personnel rules and practices." They are engineering and safety information whose consequences run directly to the residents of Port Hadlock and Port Townsend—communities located, respectively, a little more than one and two miles from Indian Island. That is the opposite of a matter "solely" internal to agency personnel management.

## II. An Agency's Policy Preference for Secrecy Cannot Justify Departing From the Statute's Plain Terms.

The Navy's argument, adopted below, is functionally a policy argument: broader withholding authority is useful because disclosure "may risk circumvention of agency regulation." But an agency's asserted operational need for a broader exemption does not license courts to stretch statutory language beyond its ordinary meaning. In *Federal Trade Commission v. Bunte Brothers, Inc.*, 312 U.S. 349, 351–52 (1941), this Court rejected the FTC's request to construe the jurisdictional term "commerce" broadly enough to satisfy the agency's regulatory objectives, holding that the statute's text, not the agency's functional preference, controls the scope of its own authority. The same principle applies with even greater force here, where the term at issue defines an *exception* to disclosure rather than a grant of power. Likewise, in *Federal Communications Commission v. American Broadcasting Co.*, 347 U.S. 284, 296 (1954), the Court declined to stretch the term "lottery" to cover give-away broadcast programs merely because the Commission believed such programs implicated the same policy concerns Congress had addressed—insisting instead on fidelity to the statute's actual terms. And in *Federal Trade Commission v. Sun Oil Co.*, 371 U.S. 505, 515–16 (1963), the Court refused to read a statutory qualifier out of the Robinson-Patman Act simply because doing so would make enforcement easier for the agency. In each case, this Court declined to let an agency's regulatory convenience substitute for Congress's chosen words. The Ninth Circuit should have done the same with Exemption 2's word "solely."

## III. Courts May Not Add Words to a Statute to Achieve a Perceived Balance of Policy Interests.

Finally, this Court has repeatedly cautioned against judicial insertion of qualifying language that Congress did not enact. In *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222, 227 (1957), the Court reaffirmed a settled textual construction of a federal statute, declining to depart from the statute's own terms absent the most compelling justification. And in *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 61 (1949), the Court construed two statutes to give operative effect to each provision's actual language rather than displacing one with judge-made modifications. The "predominantly internal" test does precisely what these decisions forbid: it displaces the word "solely," actually enacted by Congress, with an invented standard designed to accommodate the government's litigating position. That is not statutory construction; it is statutory revision, and it should be reversed.
