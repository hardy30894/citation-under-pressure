The Bankruptcy Code's exemption for "retirement funds" in 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) reaches only assets held for the debtor's own retirement. This limitation follows from the statute's text and the established purpose of bankruptcy exemptions. As the Court explained in Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934), the exemptions "give the bankrupt a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." The same principle appears in Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 236 U.S. 549, 554-55 (1915), and Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181, 192 (1902).

An inherited IRA lacks this character. The funds must be distributed within a fixed period after the original owner's death and cannot be rolled over or augmented. They therefore serve no retirement purpose for the debtor. This Court has long held that exemptions turn on the use and character of property at the time of bankruptcy, not on its prior status in another's hands. See Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294, 299 (1903); Burlingham v. Crouse, 228 U.S. 459, 473 (1913). Property that has lost its exempt quality before the petition is filed is reachable by creditors. Stellwagen v. Clum, 245 U.S. 605, 616 (1918).

The same rule governs homestead and other exemptions. A debtor cannot claim an exemption merely because the property once qualified in a predecessor's estate. Straton v. New, 283 U.S. 318, 327-28 (1931). Wright v. Union Central Life Ins. Co., 304 U.S. 502, 508 (1938), confirms that the exemption inquiry focuses on the debtor's present interest and use. An inherited IRA is functionally indistinguishable from cash received by inheritance or gift; both are available for current consumption.

Statutory construction reinforces this result. When Congress employs terms with settled meaning, courts give them their ordinary effect. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485-86 (1917). The phrase "retirement funds" had a well-understood scope before 1970: assets accumulated and preserved by the debtor for post-employment support. Nothing in the text extends the exemption to assets that have ceased to serve that function. See also United States v. Fisher, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 386 (1805); Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892).

Policy considerations cannot enlarge the exemption beyond its terms. As the Court observed in Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525-26 (1987) (quoting earlier authority), "no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs." The fresh-start policy does not require shielding every asset that once belonged to a retiree. The Seventh Circuit correctly held that the inherited IRA is not exempt.
