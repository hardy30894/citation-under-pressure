**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act**

The National Labor Relations Act does not explicitly address employer polling of union support. The Board’s current standard—permitting a poll only when an employer already possesses sufficient objective evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition—renders polling a hollow exercise. *See Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), *remanded as modified*, 923 F.2d 398 (5th Cir. 1991). If an employer already has enough evidence to withdraw recognition, a poll serves no legitimate purpose; the employer may simply cease bargaining. Conversely, if an employer lacks that evidence, the Board forbids the poll altogether. This circular logic eliminates the very utility of polling as a tool to avoid the risk of bargaining with a union that may have lost majority support—a risk the Act itself forbids. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 158(a)(2) (prohibiting employer assistance to a union that lacks majority support).

The Board’s position is also internally inconsistent. The Board has stated that Board-supervised RM elections are the “preferred method” for testing union support. *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061. Yet the Board applies the identical evidentiary standard to polls and to RM elections, while applying the same standard to unilateral withdrawal of recognition—a step that lacks any procedural safeguards. As the D.C. Circuit dissent correctly observed, the Board’s analysis “should have led it to impose a more stringent evidentiary standard on employers who withdraw recognition without having the results of a poll or an RM election.” *Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB*, 1997 WL 637640, at *6 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (Sentelle, J., dissenting). The Board’s failure to distinguish among these three options is arbitrary and unexplained.

The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have all rejected the Board’s standard, holding that an employer may poll employees if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient by itself to justify withdrawal of recognition.” *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *accord Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981). Those courts recognized that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for employers with “sincere doubts” about a union’s support. *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1297. The Board’s contrary rule is not a permissible construction of the Act. It is not “rational and consistent with the Act,” as required by *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 42 (1987). Rather, it is a policy choice that defeats the Act’s objective of ensuring that employees’ true wishes are honored.

**II. Even Under the Board’s Standard, the Record Contains Substantial Evidence of Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt**

The Board’s factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. The record shows that at least seven employees expressed clear opposition to the union. Additionally, the union steward, Ron Mohr, told management that “if a vote was taken the union would lose” and that employees did not want the union. *Allentown Mack*, 1997 WL 637640, at *7. The Board improperly discounted Mohr’s statement as an “off-the-cuff remark,” despite Mohr’s unique position as a union steward with direct knowledge of employee sentiment. Similarly, the Board rejected Kermit Bloch’s report that the entire night shift (five or six employees) opposed the union, solely because Bloch did not testify. But the employer is entitled to rely on such reports as objective indications of employee sentiment. *See Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 867-68 (reversing Board’s rejection of similar evidence).

The Board also erred in discounting statements made during job interviews by employees Dennis Marsh and Mike Ridgick. Marsh stated he was “paying $35 a month in union dues and not getting his money’s worth,” and Ridgick said “as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union.” The Board dismissed these as “equivocal” or “insincere,” but the employer had no reason to doubt their sincerity. The Board’s reliance on *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888 (1978), is misplaced; that case involved an employer who actively created a coercive atmosphere, unlike Allentown Mack, which simply informed applicants of its non-union status.

In sum, the evidence—including multiple employee statements, the union steward’s prediction, and the poll’s 19-13 result—overwhelmingly demonstrates that Allentown Mack had a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status. The Board’s contrary conclusion is arbitrary and capricious. This Court should reverse.
