**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code exempts "retirement funds" from a debtor's estate, but an inherited IRA does not constitute "retirement funds" in the hands of the debtor. *Clark v. Rameker*, 573 U.S. 207, 215 (2014). This Court should affirm the Seventh Circuit's judgment.

**I. An Inherited IRA Is Not "Retirement Funds" Under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) exempts "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under" specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C). The critical statutory phrase is "retirement funds." This Court held in *Clark v. Rameker* that the term "retirement funds" is ambiguous, but the "best interpretation" is that funds qualify only if they are "set aside for the day an individual stops working." *Clark*, 573 U.S. at 215.

An inherited IRA fails this test for three independent reasons. *First*, the holder of an inherited IRA may never contribute additional money to the account. *See* 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C). *Second*, the holder is required to withdraw funds from the account regardless of whether she has reached retirement age. *See* 26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(B). *Third*, the holder may withdraw the entire balance at any time without penalty. *See* 26 U.S.C. §72(t). As this Court explained, these features "reveal[] that inherited IRAs are not funds objectively set aside for retirement." *Clark*, 573 U.S. at 217.

Because the debtor here could withdraw the entire $300,000 inherited IRA immediately for any purpose—and indeed was *required* to begin withdrawals soon after her mother's death—the account does not represent "retirement funds" under §522. *Id.* at 218.

**II. The Exemption's Text and Structure Confirm That Inherited IRAs Are Not Exempt**

Section 522(d)(12) exempts "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under" the same Internal Revenue Code provisions. 11 U.S.C. §522(d)(12). This language is identical to §522(b)(3)(C). Both provisions focus on the *current* status of the funds, not on their historical origin.

The Bankruptcy Code's structure reinforces this conclusion. Other exemptions in §522 expressly refer to "the debtor's" interest. *See, e.g.,* 11 U.S.C. §522(d)(1) (exempting "the debtor's aggregate interest" in real property); §522(d)(5) (exempting "the debtor's interest" in personal property). By contrast, §522(d)(12) does not include the phrase "the debtor's." But that omission does not mean that *any* funds once designated as "retirement funds" remain exempt forever. Rather, it simply reflects Congress's intent to exempt funds held in tax-qualified accounts regardless of whether the debtor is the original accountholder or has succeeded to the account through a rollover or other permissible transfer. *Clark*, 573 U.S. at 219-20.

An inherited IRA is not a permissible successor account. It is a "time-limited tax-deferral vehicle," not a vehicle for holding wealth until the debtor's retirement. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013), aff'd sub nom. *Clark v. Rameker*, 573 U.S. 207 (2014). Treating it as exempt would "shelter from creditors a pot of money that can be freely used for current consumption." *Id.* at 562.

**III. The Policy of the Bankruptcy Code Supports This Result**

The Bankruptcy Code's purpose is to provide debtors a "fresh start" while ensuring equitable treatment of creditors. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). Exemptions are meant to protect necessary assets—not to allow debtors to shield inherited wealth that can be used for current consumption. *Clark*, 573 U.S. at 220.

Allowing inherited IRAs to be exempt would facilitate a "significant loophole" in the bankruptcy system. *Id.* Wealthy individuals could leave retirement accounts to their children, knowing that those accounts would be immune from creditors in bankruptcy. This would undermine the fresh start policy and unfairly disadvantage creditors. *Id.* at 220-21.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
