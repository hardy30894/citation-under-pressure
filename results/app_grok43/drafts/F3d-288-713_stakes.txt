The district court erred in denying habeas relief. A prosecutor who testifies as a witness in a case he is prosecuting creates an impermissible dual role that undermines the fundamental fairness of the trial. See Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (prosecutor “is the representative not of an ordinary party to a controversy, but of a sovereignty”). The Fifth Circuit has recognized that such conduct risks injecting the prestige of the prosecutor’s office into the fact-finding process and may require disqualification or mistrial. See United States v. Crockett, 514 F.2d 64, 76 (5th Cir. 1975).

Here, District Attorney Hill testified to rebut Riddle’s account of threats made at arraignment. After doing so, Hill remained lead prosecutor. The assistant prosecutor then urged the jury to disbelieve Riddle because Hill had “shown, through his testimony, that Riddle was a liar.” This sequence placed the state’s chief law-enforcement officer in the position of both witness and advocate, allowing the jury to equate Hill’s official status with credibility. The resulting prejudice is not cured by the assistant’s delivery of the argument; the jury heard the rebuttal from the elected prosecutor himself.

Because the error infected the capital trial with an appearance of partiality, it violated the Due Process Clause. See Tumey v. Ohio, 273 U.S. 510, 523 (1927) (due process requires “a fair trial in a fair tribunal”). The state courts’ rejection of the claim was contrary to clearly established federal law. 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)(1). The district court’s grant of summary judgment should therefore be reversed.
