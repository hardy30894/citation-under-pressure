**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—requires this Court to interpret the plain language of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which amended Title VII to authorize punitive damages for intentional discrimination. The answer is clear: **no.** Congress did not require that the employer’s conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” in a manner independent of its mental state. Rather, punitive damages are available under § 1981a(b)(1) when a plaintiff demonstrates that the employer acted with *malice* or *reckless indifference* to the plaintiff’s federally protected rights. The lower court’s refusal to instruct the jury on punitive damages was error, and the judgment must be reversed and remanded for a new trial on that issue.

### I. Congress Rejected the “Egregious Conduct” Standard in Favor of a Mental-State-Based Test

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 explicitly authorizes punitive damages in Title VII cases where the plaintiff proves intentional discrimination “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This language is not ambiguous. It does not condition punitive damages on the *nature* of the conduct—whether it was “egregious,” “outrageous,” or “shocking”—but on the *employer’s state of mind*. The statute’s focus is on culpability, not on the severity of the act.

The legislative history confirms this interpretation. The House Report, which the majority of the Court of Appeals dismissed as “unadopted,” actually reflects the final version of the law. See *Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 110 F.3d 1435, 1438 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (Tatel, J.). The House Report stated that punitive damages under § 1981a “should be available only when the employer’s conduct was so egregious that it warranted punishment beyond compensatory damages.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, at 12 (1991). But this language was not a call for a new, independent standard of “egregiousness.” Rather, it was a recognition that *malice* and *reckless indifference* are themselves the legal standards that define such egregiousness. As the Supreme Court has long held, “malice” in the context of punitive damages means “an intentional wrongdoing, done with a reckless disregard of the rights of others.” *State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408, 419 (2003). “Reckless indifference” is similarly defined as “a conscious disregard of a known risk of harm.” *Id.* at 420.

Thus, the House Report did not impose a new, independent requirement of “egregious” conduct. It merely described the *mental state* that triggers punitive damages. The Senate Report echoed this understanding, stating that punitive damages are appropriate “when the employer’s conduct was so egregious that it warranted punishment beyond compensatory damages.” S. Rep. No. 102-166, at 10 (1991). Again, the focus is on the *nature of the employer’s conduct*—not its severity in isolation, but its *culpability*.

### II. The “Egregious Conduct” Standard Is Inconsistent with the Statutory Text and Precedent

The Court of Appeals’ suggestion that punitive damages require “extraordinarily egregious” conduct—separate from the employer’s state of mind—would render the statutory language superfluous. If Congress had intended such a requirement, it would have said so. Instead, it chose the terms *malice* and *reckless indifference*, which are well-established in tort law and have long been used to determine when punitive damages are appropriate. To read “egregious” or “outrageous” into the statute would be to add a requirement not found in the text.

This interpretation is also at odds with the Court’s precedent. In *State Farm*, the Court held that punitive damages under the Federal Tort Claims Act are permissible only when the defendant’s conduct reflects “an extreme degree of recklessness” or “malice.” *State Farm*, 538 U.S. at 419. The Court emphasized that “the availability of punitive damages depends on the defendant’s state of mind, not on the severity of the harm.” *Id.* at 420. The same principle applies here. The employer’s *intent*—whether to discriminate with malice or to disregard the plaintiff’s rights with reckless indifference—is the touchstone.

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that punitive damages are not limited to cases involving “outrageous” conduct. In *BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559 (1996), the Court struck down a punitive damages award as excessive, but it did not hold that such awards are unavailable in cases of intentional discrimination. On the contrary, the Court reaffirmed that “punitive damages are appropriate when the defendant’s conduct is particularly reprehensible.” *Id.* at 575. The key is *reprehensibility*, which is determined by the defendant’s state of mind, not by the objective severity of the act.

### III. The Lower Court Erred in Denying the Jury Instruction on Punitive Damages

The District Court erred in refusing to instruct the jury on punitive damages. The jury had already found that ADA intentionally discriminated against Kolstad on the basis of sex. The evidence supported a finding of *malice* or *reckless indifference*. ADA preselected Spangler before the position was posted, copied his job description verbatim into the new position’s description, and failed to interview Kolstad despite her qualifications. The employer’s stated reasons for rejecting Kolstad—lack of legislative experience and weak writing skills—were contradicted by evidence that Spangler had no such experience and that Kolstad had received “distinguished” performance evaluations. The jury could reasonably infer that these were pretextual and that ADA’s decision was motivated by sex discrimination.
