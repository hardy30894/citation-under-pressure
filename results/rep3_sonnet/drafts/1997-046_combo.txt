# ARGUMENT

## I. Woodard Has No Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Protected by the Due Process Clause

Executive clemency has never been treated as an entitlement subject to judicial oversight; it is, and has always been, an act of grace lying outside the adjudicatory process that due process protects. This Court explained in Ex parte Garland, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866), that "[a] pardon is an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws, which exempts the individual on whom it is bestowed from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed." That characterization did not depend on the details of any particular clemency procedure; it flowed from the very nature of the power itself. Chief Justice Marshall likewise described the pardon as "a private, though official act of the executive magistrate, delivered to the individual for whose benefit it is intended, and not communicated officially to the court." United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 161 (1833). The pardon, in other words, sits outside the judicial machinery that produced the conviction and sentence; it is a discretionary act layered atop, not integrated into, the adjudicative process.

Nor does the discretionary, non-adjudicative character of clemency change simply because a State chooses to structure the manner in which applications are considered. In Biddle v. Perovich, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927), this Court described a commutation as reflecting nothing more than "the determination of the ultimate authority that the public welfare will be better served by inflicting less than what the judgment fixed." That determination is a political judgment committed to executive discretion, not a legal entitlement susceptible to the procedural protections this Court has required for judicial and quasi-judicial proceedings.

Due process protections attach only where the "precise nature of the interest" affected is one recognized by law, not one dependent entirely on grace. See Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm. v. McGrath, 341 U.S. 123 (1951). The touchstone, as this Court has repeatedly recognized, is that "the very nature of due process negates any concept of inflexible procedures universally applicable to every imaginable situation." Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961). Clemency review is the paradigmatic situation in which no such inflexible procedure is required, because the inmate has no underlying entitlement to commutation or pardon at all—only a hope that the executive will exercise a grace that no court, and no statute, can compel.

The Sixth Circuit's reliance on Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), to bootstrap due process protections into the clemency stage was misplaced. Griffin addressed appellate review that a State had made "an integral part of the ... system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant." Id. at 18. Clemency, by contrast, occurs only after guilt and sentence have been finally adjudicated through the ordinary judicial process, including direct appeal and collateral review. It is not a stage of adjudication at all, but a separate exercise of executive mercy layered on top of a judgment already final. Extending Griffin's logic to clemency proceedings would collapse the distinction this Court has long maintained between the judicial process, to which due process protections attach, and the executive's freestanding power of grace, to which they do not.

## II. The Voluntary Interview Option Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment

The privilege against self-incrimination protects only against compelled testimony; it has no application to a voluntary option that carries no penalty for declining it. This Court has made clear that the privilege guards "against real dangers, not remote and speculative possibilities." Hoffman v. United States, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951). Ohio's clemency regulation imposes no compulsion whatsoever: an inmate "may" request an interview, and nothing in the regulation obligates him to answer any question he does not wish to answer. A prisoner who declines the interview loses nothing that the law, as opposed to grace, entitles him to keep.

This Court has consistently distinguished between voluntary disclosures and compelled ones for Fifth Amendment purposes. See United States v. Monia, 317 U.S. 424 (1943); Ullmann v. United States, 350 U.S. 422 (1956). The privilege is not self-executing against every choice that might, in some contingent future proceeding, prove disadvantageous; it applies where the State exacts a price for silence. In Garrity v. New Jersey, 385 U.S. 493 (1967), compulsion existed because refusal to answer triggered automatic forfeiture of employment. No comparable sanction attaches here: Woodard's execution date, sentence, and legal status are unaffected by his choice whether to sit for an interview. Where, as here, "voluntariness" rather than compulsion governs the inquiry, this Court's traditional voluntariness framework controls. Rogers v. Richmond, 365 U.S. 534 (1961); Malloy v. Hogan, 378 U.S. 1 (1964). Because Ohio's procedure compels nothing, it violates nothing.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
