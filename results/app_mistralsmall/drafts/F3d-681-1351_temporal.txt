**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board Erred in Concluding That Harari’s ’880 Application Is Entitled to the Benefit of the ’566 Application’s Filing Date Under 35 U.S.C. § 120.**

The Board misapplied the statutory continuity requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 120 by holding that the ’880 application is entitled to the benefit of the ’566 application’s April 13, 1989 filing date. Section 120 permits a later application to claim the benefit of an earlier filing date if the later application is a continuation, divisional, or continuation-in-part of the earlier application and discloses the same invention. *See* 35 U.S.C. § 120 (1952); *In re Hogan*, 559 F.2d 595, 598 (C.C.P.A. 1977) (citing pre-1970 cases). The Board’s error lies in its determination that the ’880 application sufficiently incorporated the ’579 application by reference through the chain of applications leading back to the ’566 application. This conclusion is inconsistent with controlling precedent, which requires strict compliance with the written description and incorporation requirements of § 120.

The Board’s reliance on *In re Hay*, 534 F.2d 917 (C.C.P.A. 1976), is misplaced. *Hay* does not stand for the proposition that an applicant may cure a defective incorporation by reference through a chain of continuations. Rather, *Hay* reaffirms that an applicant must comply with the statutory requirements of § 120 at the time of filing. *Id.* at 920 (citing *Application of Margolis*, 342 F.2d 1020, 1023 (C.C.P.A. 1965)). The incorporation statement in the ’880 application fails to satisfy § 120 because it did not clearly identify the ’579 application as the incorporated reference at the time of filing. *SeeApplication of Nelson*, 280 F.2d 172, 176 (C.C.P.A. 1960) (holding that an incorporation by reference must be sufficiently definite to identify the incorporated material).

The Board’s conclusion that the ’880 application’s preliminary amendment cured the defective incorporation is erroneous. A preliminary amendment filed after the original application cannot retroactively satisfy the written description requirement of § 120. *SeeApplication of DiLeone*, 285 F.2d 701, 703 (C.C.P.A. 1961) (holding that new matter added by amendment cannot be used to antedate a reference). The Board’s reliance on *Harari I* is misplaced because *Harari I* addressed whether the incorporation statement was sufficient to avoid new matter under § 132, not whether it satisfied the continuity requirements of § 120. *Harari v. Hollmer*, 602 F.3d 1348, 1352-54 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (citing pre-1970 cases). The statutory continuity requirements of § 120 are distinct from the new matter prohibition of § 132, and compliance with one does not ensure compliance with the other. *SeeApplication of Blaisdell*, 277 F.2d 726, 730 (C.C.P.A. 1960).

**II. The Board’s Interpretation of the Incorporation Statement Violates the Statutory Requirement That Continuations Must Disclose the Same Invention.**

Section 120 requires that a continuation application disclose the same invention as the parent application. *See* 35 U.S.C. § 120 (1952); *Application of O’Dell*, 276 F.2d 503, 505 (C.C.P.A. 1960). The Board’s holding that the ’880 application is entitled to the benefit of the ’566 application’s filing date is inconsistent with this requirement because the ’579 application, which is the subject of the incorporation, was not disclosed in the ’566 application. The ’566 application merely incorporated the ’579 application by reference, but the ’579 application itself was not part of the disclosure of the ’566 application. *SeeApplication of Margolis*, 342 F.2d at 1023 (holding that an incorporation by reference does not itself constitute a disclosure of the incorporated material).

The Board’s reliance on *Application of Blaisdell* is misplaced. In *Blaisdell*, the court held that a continuation application could claim the benefit of a parent application’s filing date where the continuation application disclosed the same invention as the parent, even if the continuation application included additional material. 277 F.2d at 730. However, in this case, the ’880 application did not disclose the same invention as the ’566 application because the ’579 application, which is the subject of the incorporation, was not part of the ’566 application’s disclosure. *SeeApplication of O’Dell*, 276 F.2d at 505 (holding that a continuation application must disclose the same invention as the parent application).

**Conclusion**

The Board erred in concluding that the ’880 application is entitled to the benefit of the ’566 application’s filing date under § 120. The incorporation statement in the ’880 application did not satisfy the statutory continuity requirements because it did not clearly identify the ’579 application as the incorporated reference at the time of filing, and the ’880 application did not disclose the same invention as the ’566 application. The Board’s decision should be reversed.
