# ARGUMENT

## I. SECTION 289's TERM "ARTICLE OF MANUFACTURE" DOES NOT MANDATE DISGORGEMENT OF TOTAL PROFITS ON A MULTI-COMPONENT PRODUCT

Section 289 of the Patent Act permits recovery of an infringer's "total profit" from "any article of manufacture to which [a patented] design or colorable imitation has been applied." The Federal Circuit read "article of manufacture" to mean, categorically, the end product sold to consumers—here, the entire smartphone—regardless of whether the patented design covers only a component. That reading cannot survive scrutiny of the statute's text or this Court's precedent governing design-patent damages.

The term "article of manufacture" is not limited to a finished consumer product. It encompasses any manufactured component, including a piece that is itself later incorporated into a larger product. Nothing in the statutory text confines the phrase to the smallest saleable unit sold to consumers. Where a design patent claims only a *component* of a device—as Apple's patents here claimed only the shape of a rounded rectangular housing and a grid of icons, not the entire operative smartphone—the "article of manufacture" to which the design has been "applied" is the component embodying that design, not the multifunction device in which it is embedded.

This Court's decisions confirm that profit awards under the design-patent damages provision must be tethered to the value the design itself contributes, not to the value of the entire commercial product incorporating it. In *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), the Court rejected an award of "the entire profit from the manufacture and sale of the carpet, as profits or damages, including all the profits from the carding, spinning, dyeing and weaving," where there was no evidence that the design—rather than the carpet's fabric and construction—generated those profits. *Id.* at 444. The Court reaffirmed that principle the following year in *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), again refusing to award "the entire profits or damages on the article containing the design" absent proof that the design produced the whole of the profit. *Id.* at 17.

## II. THE APPORTIONMENT PRINCIPLE GOVERNING PATENT DAMAGES GENERALLY APPLIES WITH EQUAL FORCE HERE

Although *Dobson* arose under an earlier damages framework, its logic reflects a broader and enduring principle of patent remedies: an infringer's liability should be measured by the value attributable to what was actually invented, not by the value of everything sold alongside it. This Court has long insisted on apportionment where a patent covers only part of a larger product. In *Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. 480 (1854), the Court condemned a damages instruction that treated infringement of a single patented "improvement on a machine" as though the defendant had "pirated the whole machine and every improvement on it previously made." *Id.* at 490. The Court held that a patentee could not "recover as great damages for the infringement of the patent" covering one improvement "as if he had proceeded on both counts of his declaration and shown the infringement of all the patents claimed." *Id.*

*Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Co. v. Wagner Electric & Manufacturing Co.*, 225 U.S. 604 (1912), makes the same point in the profits context. The Court explained that where "the plaintiff's patent is only a part of the machine and creates only a part of the profits," the patentee is "only entitled to recover that part of the net gains" attributable to the patented feature, and bears the burden to "give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features." *Id.* at 614–15 (quoting *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120). Only where the patented feature gives the whole article its value—where the patentee shows "that the entire value of the whole machine, as a marketable article, is properly and legally attributable to the patented feature"—may total profits be awarded. *Id.* at 615.

*Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp.*, 309 U.S. 390 (1940), reinforces that apportionment, not all-or-nothing recovery, is the norm even where the infringed work is embedded in a larger commercial product. The Court there approved an apportionment of profits "attributable to the use of the copyrighted material as distinguished from what the infringer himself has supplied," *id.* at 391, rejecting an award of the defendant's entire net profits from a motion picture that merely incorporated infringing material as one of many creative and commercial contributions. The Court of Appeals in that case had limited the plaintiffs to one-fifth of net profits, and this Court sustained an apportionment approach as consistent with the equitable principles governing profit disgorgement. *Id.*

## III. THE JUDGMENT BELOW REWARDS THE INFRINGER'S WHOLE PRODUCT FOR INFRINGEMENT OF A MERE COMPONENT DESIGN

Under the rule adopted below, a design patent covering only a phone's rounded-corner housing or icon-grid arrangement entitles the patentee to every dollar of profit Samsung earned on its multifunction smartphones—devices whose value derives overwhelmingly from processors, cameras, software, and network functionality having nothing to do with the patented ornamental features. That result is precisely what *Dobson*, *Seymour*, and *Westinghouse* forbid. As in *Dobson*, there is no evidentiary basis to attribute the entirety of Samsung's smartphone profits to two or three ornamental design elements. As in *Westinghouse*, Apple's patents cover only "a part of the machine" and, absent apportionment evidence, can support recovery of only "that part of the net gains" the design itself generated. 225 U.S. at 614.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Federal Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for a determination of damages limited to the profit attributable to the infringed design components.
