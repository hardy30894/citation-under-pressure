**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE DOES NOT PRECLUDE RETRIAL OF PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATIONS IN NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDINGS.**

The Double Jeopardy Clause provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. By its plain text, the Clause applies to trials for criminal offenses, not to sentencing determinations. This Court has never held that the Double Jeopardy Clause extends generally to noncapital sentencing proceedings, and sound reasons of history, precedent, and policy confirm that it does not.

**A. Sentencing Proceedings Are Not “Trials” for Purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.**

The Double Jeopardy Clause “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). It has no application to the routine sentencing determinations that follow a valid conviction. This Court has long recognized that sentencing is a fundamentally different proceeding from the guilt phase of a criminal trial. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court held that the Due Process Clause does not require a judge to conduct a formal hearing or to apply trial-type procedural protections when determining the appropriate sentence. *Id.* at 251. The Court explained that “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” *Id.*

Because a State need not provide a trial of sentencing allegations at all, a State that elects to provide such a trial may lawfully circumscribe its procedural boundaries. *See U.S. v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 156 (1997) (reaffirming *Williams*). As this Court has recognized, sentencing judges may consider “information obtained outside the courtroom from persons whom a defendant has not been permitted to confront or cross-examine.” *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 245. The Double Jeopardy Clause, which by its terms governs trials for “offense[s],” does not automatically attach to every proceeding that bears some resemblance to a trial.

**B. This Court’s Capital Sentencing Precedents Do Not Control Noncapital Cases.**

Respondent acknowledges that this Court has applied the Double Jeopardy Clause to capital sentencing proceedings that “have the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981); *see also Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 209-10 (1984). In *Bullington*, the Court emphasized that the capital sentencing scheme at issue was “like a trial on the issue of guilt” because it required the jury to determine specific factual issues under a reasonable doubt standard. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438. That analysis, however, was “based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding.” *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994). This Court has never extended *Bullington* to the noncapital context, and it has expressly reserved the question whether “the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings.” *Id.* at 390; *see also Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28, 30 (1985) (per curiam).

The unique features that justified application of the Double Jeopardy Clause in capital cases—including the finality of a death sentence and the heightened procedural protections mandated by the Eighth Amendment—are absent here. California’s three-strikes law, which doubles the prison term for a recidivist offender, does not implicate the same “life or limb” concerns that drove the Court’s analysis in *Bullington*. Indeed, as the California Supreme Court correctly observed, “the degree of mental anguish faced by a criminal defendant subject to multiple prosecutions of enhancement provisions is insufficient to warrant double jeopardy protection.” *Monge v. California*, 61 Cal. App. 4th 156, 171 (1998) (internal quotation marks omitted).

**C. California’s Provision of Statutory Procedural Protections Does Not Trigger Constitutional Double Jeopardy Protections.**

Petitioner argues that because California has granted defendants a statutory right to a jury trial on prior conviction allegations, and has required proof beyond a reasonable doubt, the State has created a proceeding that “resembles a trial” and thus triggers double jeopardy protections. But a State’s decision to provide procedural protections beyond those required by the Constitution does not constitutionalize those protections. As this Court has repeatedly held, “the state may elect to provide a trial of sentencing allegations without also providing all the procedural guaranties that characterize a trial on guilt or innocence.” *Monge* (citing *People v. Vera*, 934 P.2d 1279, 1282 (Cal. 1997)). The Double Jeopardy Clause does not impose a “one-size-fits-all” requirement; rather, it applies only to proceedings that historically and functionally constitute trials for criminal offenses.

Retrial of a prior conviction allegation does not subject the defendant to double jeopardy because it is not a second prosecution for the same offense. The prior conviction has already been adjudicated, and the only question on retrial is whether that conviction qualifies for sentence enhancement under state law. This is a routine sentencing determination, not a second trial for a criminal offense.

**D. Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be affirmed. The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding.
