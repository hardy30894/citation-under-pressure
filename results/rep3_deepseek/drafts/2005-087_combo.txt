**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require Arizona to admit expert evidence of mental disease to negate the mens rea element of a crime. The State may constitutionally confine such evidence to the insanity defense, where the defendant bears the burden of proof. This Court has long recognized that “due process” is a flexible concept, and that states have broad latitude to define crimes, defenses, and evidentiary rules. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 110–11 (1908); *McFarland v. American Sugar Refining Co.*, 241 U.S. 79, 86 (1916).

**I. The State May Define the Elements of Crimes and the Relevance of Mental State Evidence**

A state may decide that evidence of mental disease is relevant only to the issue of legal insanity, not to the existence of the mental state required for the offense. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798–99 (1952), this Court upheld Oregon’s requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting the argument that due process required the state to allow such evidence to negate intent. The Court explained that “the state is not bound to adopt a particular form of procedure” and may allocate burdens as it sees fit. *Id.* at 799.

Similarly, in *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946), the Court held that a defendant has no constitutional right to have the jury consider evidence of diminished capacity to negate specific intent. The Court stated that “the state may, if it chooses, treat the capacity to form a criminal intent as a matter of defense.” *Id.* at 475. Arizona has made that choice, and the Due Process Clause does not forbid it.

**II. The State’s Rule Is a Permissible Evidentiary Restriction**

The Due Process Clause does not require states to admit all relevant evidence. *Stein v. New York*, 346 U.S. 156, 196 (1953) (no constitutional right to introduce evidence that is “irrelevant or of too slight a probative value”). Arizona’s rule that mental-disease evidence is admissible only on the insanity defense is a reasonable limitation. The state may “exclude evidence that is not sufficiently probative of a material fact.” *West v. Louisiana*, 194 U.S. 258, 262 (1904). Here, the state has determined that such evidence is probative of legal insanity, not of the absence of mens rea. That determination is not arbitrary.

In *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 535–36 (1968), this Court upheld a conviction for public drunkenness despite evidence that the defendant’s chronic alcoholism rendered his behavior involuntary. The Court rejected the argument that due process required the state to consider such evidence to negate the voluntariness element. *Id.* at 536. Similarly, Arizona may require that evidence of mental disease be channeled into the insanity defense rather than used to attack the mens rea element.

**III. The Burden of Proof on Insanity Does Not Violate Due Process**

Arizona places the burden of proving insanity on the defendant. This Court has repeatedly upheld such allocations. In *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 486–87 (1895), the Court held that while the federal government must prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt once the defendant introduces some evidence, the states are not so constrained. *Leland* confirmed that a state may require the defendant to prove insanity by a preponderance of the evidence or even beyond a reasonable doubt. 343 U.S. at 799.

The defendant’s argument conflates the state’s burden to prove mens rea with the state’s power to define what evidence is relevant to that element. The state may define the mental state element without reference to mental disease. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 523 (1958) (due process requires the state to prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, but the state defines the elements). Arizona has defined the mens rea element as the “intent to kill” without regard to the defendant’s mental illness. That definition is constitutional.

**IV. The Defendant’s Right to Present a Defense Is Not Absolute**

While a defendant has a constitutional right to present a defense, that right is subject to reasonable restrictions. *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884) (states may regulate the admission of evidence). Arizona’s rule does not bar all evidence of mental state; it merely channels it to the insanity defense. The defendant was free to present expert testimony to prove insanity, but he failed to meet his burden. The state is not required to allow that same evidence to be used for a different purpose.

In *McFarland*, this Court emphasized that due process does not require “any particular form of procedure” and that states may experiment with different rules. 241 U.S. at 86. Arizona’s rule is a legitimate experiment in allocating the complex issue of mental responsibility.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed. The Due Process Clause does not require states to admit expert evidence of mental disease to negate criminal intent when the state has chosen to confine such evidence to the insanity defense. This Court’s precedents from *Leland* to *Powell* confirm that states have broad discretion in this area.
