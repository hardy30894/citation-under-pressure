**ARGUMENT**

The District Court did not abuse its discretion in certifying the class under Rule 23(b)(3), and its finding that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis through common evidence was both legally sound and supported by the record. The Third Circuit correctly affirmed, and this Court should uphold the certification order because the district court conducted the “rigorous analysis” required by *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. 2541, 2551 (2011), and properly applied the *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), framework. The central question—whether a class may be certified without adequate admissible evidence of a common methodology for measuring damages—is answered definitively by precedent: **a district court may certify a class under Rule 23(b)(3) if the plaintiff demonstrates, by a preponderance of the evidence, that common issues predominate and that damages can be calculated on a class-wide basis using a common methodology.**

### I. The District Court Properly Applied the Predominance Standard Under Rule 23(b)(3)

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This is not a mere pleading standard; it demands a “rigorous analysis” of the evidence, including an inquiry into the merits where necessary to determine whether common issues predominate. *Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. at 2551. The Supreme Court has made clear that “frequently [the Rule 23] 'rigorous analysis' will entail some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff's underlying claim,” but cautioned that such an inquiry must not become a “mini-trial” on the merits. Id. at 2552 n.6.

Here, the District Court did not conduct a mini-trial. Instead, it held a four-day evidentiary hearing, reviewed 32 expert reports, and considered deposition testimony—all in service of determining whether common issues predominated. The court’s conclusion that the class could prove antitrust impact through a single, common theory—*that Comcast’s clustering conduct deterred overbuilder competition*—was not only plausible but supported by substantial evidence. The court did not assume liability; it assessed whether the *same evidence* could be used to prove liability and damages for all class members. That is precisely the inquiry mandated by *Dukes* and *Hydrogen Peroxide*.

Comcast’s argument that the court erred by accepting Plaintiffs’ damages methodology misapprehends the standard. The court did not accept the methodology as proven; it found that Dr. McClave’s model—based on a comparison of actual prices in the Philadelphia DMA to a hypothetical competitive market—was *capable of producing class-wide damages* through common evidence. The court’s conclusion was not that the model was correct, but that it was *available* to measure damages on a class-wide basis. That is all Rule 23(b)(3) requires. As this Court held in *Wal-Mart*, “the predominance inquiry is not whether the plaintiff will ultimately prevail, but whether the common issues are sufficiently central to the case to justify class treatment.” 131 S. Ct. at 2552.

### II. The District Court’s Finding of a Common Methodology for Damages Was Not Clearly Erroneous

Comcast contends that the court erred in certifying the class because there was no “adequate admissible evidence” that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. But this argument conflates the *burden of proof* at trial with the *burden of proof* at certification. At certification, the plaintiff need not prove damages by a preponderance of the evidence; rather, the plaintiff must show that damages *can be measured* on a class-wide basis using a common methodology. *Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. at 2552.

The District Court found that Dr. McClave’s model—using a “but-for” competitive market analysis—was a common methodology capable of producing class-wide damages. The court did not accept the model as conclusive, but found it *sufficiently reliable and applicable* to all class members. The court’s conclusion was based on expert testimony, peer-reviewed methodology, and a detailed analysis of market data. The Third Circuit correctly held that this finding was not clearly erroneous. *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985) (a finding is not clearly erroneous if “the district court’s account of the evidence is plausible in light of the record viewed in its entirety”).

Comcast’s own expert, Dr. David S. Evans, acknowledged that a “but-for” competitive market analysis is a standard tool in antitrust damages. The dispute was not over the methodology’s validity, but over its application. That is a dispute for trial, not certification. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, “the class certification decision is not a determination of liability or damages, but a determination of whether the case is suitable for class treatment.” *Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. at 2552.

### III. The Third Circuit Correctly Applied the Abuse of Discretion Standard

The Third Circuit reviewed the district court’s certification decision for abuse of discretion, applying the correct standard: a finding of fact is clearly erroneous only if the appellate court is “left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” *U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395. The court found that the district court’s factual findings were supported by the record and that its legal conclusions were sound. The court did not err in concluding that the class could prove antitrust impact through common evidence or that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis.

Comcast’s argument that the “relevant geographic market” must be defined at the individual household level is legally untenable. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the relevant market may be a metropolitan area, as in *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 337, 337 (1962), and the Third Circuit correctly rejected Comcast’s attempt to fragment the market into millions of individual households.
