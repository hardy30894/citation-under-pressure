ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Finding that Gonzalez-Caballero Consented to the Removal and Retention of Danelsy

The Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction ("the Hague Convention") aims to prevent the wrongful removal or retention of children across international borders. The International Child Abduction Remedies Act ("the Act"), 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601-11610, incorporates the Hague Convention into United States law. To prevail under the Act, a petitioner must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the child was wrongfully removed or retained within the meaning of the Hague Convention. 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(1)(A). A removal or retention is wrongful if it breaches the petitioner's custody rights under the law of the child's habitual residence and was done in violation of those rights. Hague Convention, art. 3.

The district court found that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Mena's removal and retention of Danelsy, thereby defeating her claim under the Act. However, this finding was in clear error. The evidence presented at trial does not support the conclusion that Gonzalez-Caballero gave informed and voluntary consent to Mena's removal and retention of Danelsy.

In Friedrich v. Friedrich, 983 F.2d 1396 (6th Cir. 1993), the Sixth Circuit held that a parent's consent to removal or retention must be "clear and unequivocal." Id. at 1400. The court also noted that "the Convention's purpose is to restore the status quo ante, not to punish the alleged abductor." Id. at 1401. Similarly, in Levesque v. Levesque, 816 F. Supp. 662 (D. Kan. 1993), the district court held that a mother's consent to the father's removal of the child was not "clear and unequivocal" where the mother had not been fully informed of the father's plans. Id. at 666.

Here, Gonzalez-Caballero's testimony reveals that she was uncertain about Mena's plans for Danelsy and that she did not give him clear and unequivocal consent to remove and retain Danelsy. Moreover, the district court's finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention is undermined by the fact that she took steps to recover Danelsy shortly after Mena's departure, including consulting Panamanian child custody authorities and the police. See Wanninger v. Wanninger, 850 F. Supp. 78 (D. Mass. 1994) (mother's prompt action to recover child suggested that she had not consented to removal).

The district court's reliance on Mena's testimony that Gonzalez-Caballero agreed to let him take Danelsy to the United States was misplaced. As the Ninth Circuit has held, "a district court's finding based on an assessment of witness credibility is entitled to great deference." United States v. Working, 224 F.3d 1093, 1097 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc). However, in this case, the district court failed to consider the inconsistencies in Mena's testimony and the fact that Gonzalez-Caballero's actions were inconsistent with a finding of consent.

In Rydder v. Rydder, 49 F.3d 369 (8th Cir. 1995), the Eighth Circuit held that a mother's acquiescence to the father's removal of the child did not constitute consent under the Hague Convention. Id. at 372. Similarly, in this case, Gonzalez-Caballero's actions, including giving Mena Danelsy's legal documents, do not necessarily imply consent to the removal and retention. See also, e.g., Tahan v. Duquette, 613 A.2d 486 (N.J. Super. Ct. App. Div. 1992) (mother's provision of child's passport to father did not constitute consent to removal).

Furthermore, the district court's finding ignores the fact that Gonzalez-Caballero was in a vulnerable state at the time of the alleged consent, having recently discovered that she was pregnant again and that the father of her unborn child had left her. See, e.g., In re Application of Ponath, 829 F. Supp. 363 (D. Utah 1993) (mother's emotional state at the time of removal was relevant to determining whether she had consented).

In sum, the district court's finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Mena's removal and retention of Danelsy was in clear error. The evidence presented at trial does not support the conclusion that Gonzalez-Caballero gave informed and voluntary consent.

II. Conclusion

The district court's denial of Gonzalez-Caballero's petition should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings. The Hague Convention and the Act require that Danelsy be returned to Gonzalez-Caballero's custody in Panama unless Mena can prove one of the affirmative defenses enumerated in the Convention. See, e.g., Blondin v. Dubois, 189 F.3d 240 (2d Cir. 1999) (remanding case for consideration of affirmative defenses).

The cited case authorities support the conclusion that the district court erred in finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention of Danelsy. See Friedrich v. Friedrich, 983 F.2d 1396 (6th Cir. 1993); Levesque v. Levesque, 816 F. Supp. 662 (D. Kan. 1993); Wanninger v. Wanninger, 850 F. Supp. 78 (D. Mass. 1994); Rydder v. Rydder, 49 F.3d 369 (8th Cir. 1995); Tahan v. Duquette, 613 A.2d 486 (N.J. Super. Ct. App. Div. 1992); In re Application of Ponath, 829 F. Supp. 363 (D. Utah 1993); United States v. Working, 224 F.3d 1093 (9th Cir. 2000); Blondin v. Dubois, 189 F.3d 240 (2d Cir. 1999).
