**Argument**

**I. Neither the Constitution nor this Court’s precedent recognizes a protected liberty or life interest in clemency proceedings.**

The Due Process Clause protects only interests that the Constitution itself or state law has made sufficiently concrete. But executive clemency has long stood apart from judicially enforceable entitlements. In **Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981)**, the Court held that a prisoner had no liberty interest in commutation where the Connecticut Board of Pardons retained unfettered discretion. The Court emphasized that “**pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.**” *Id.* at 464. Because the Board’s power was discretionary, the inmate could claim no entitlement to clemency and thus no protected liberty interest. *Id.* at 464-67.

That rule controls here. Ohio’s clemency authority is likewise discretionary. As in **Dumschat**, there is no mandatory substantive standard constraining the sovereign’s clemency decision, only a procedure for submitting applications and receiving consideration. A procedure alone does not create a substantive liberty interest. See **Olim v. Wakinekona, 461 U.S. 238, 250-51 (1983)**; **Kentucky Department of Corrections v. Thompson, 490 U.S. 454, 460-63 (1989)**.

Nor does **Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex, 442 U.S. 1 (1979)** help petitioner. Greenholtz recognized that a state parole statute may create a liberty interest only when it uses mandatory language and substantive predicates that limit official discretion. *Id.* at 11-12. Ohio’s clemency system does the opposite. It leaves the ultimate decision to executive grace, not entitlement. See **Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 464-67**.

Petitioner’s attempt to recast clemency as an “integral” part of the criminal adjudication process also fails. The Court has extended due process protections to appellate procedures only where the State has created a first appeal as of right and made it part of the adjudicatory process. **Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956)**; **Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387, 393-400 (1985)**. But the Court has never treated executive clemency as comparable to appeal. To the contrary, **Dumschat** squarely rejected the proposition that clemency is a judicially enforceable stage of criminal adjudication. 452 U.S. at 464-67.

This Court’s more recent due process decisions confirm that courts should be wary of turning discretionary prison or postconviction procedures into constitutional entitlements. See **Sandin v. Conner, 515 U.S. 472, 483-84 (1995)**; **Meachum v. Fano, 427 U.S. 215, 225-27 (1976)**. Ohio’s clemency process, unlike a first appeal as of right, remains a matter of grace. Petitioner therefore has no protected life or liberty interest in the proceeding itself.

**II. Allowing an inmate to choose whether to participate in a clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment prohibits compelled testimonial self-incrimination. **Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308, 316-20 (1976)**. But the Constitution is not violated merely because a person must make a difficult choice between remaining silent and pursuing a discretionary benefit. In **Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420, 427-34 (1984)**, the Court explained that the privilege is implicated by compulsion, not by every circumstance in which silence may carry practical consequences. Likewise, in **Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard, 523 U.S. 272 (1998)**, the Court held that Ohio’s voluntary clemency interview process did not violate the Fifth Amendment because the inmate “**faces no compulsion to speak in the clemency interview.**” *Id.* at 286 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment).

That is the controlling principle here. Ohio does not require an inmate to request an interview, to answer any question, or to waive the privilege as a condition of clemency consideration. The option is voluntary. A voluntary decision to speak to a clemency board is not compelled self-incrimination. See **Murphy, 465 U.S. at 427-34**; **Baxter, 425 U.S. at 316-20**.

Petitioner’s “Hobson’s choice” theory is also foreclosed by **McKune v. Lile, 536 U.S. 24, 35-48 (2002)**, which reiterated that not every pressure to participate in a state program amounts to unconstitutional compulsion. If the State may offer a benefit and attach consequences to refusal without violating the Fifth Amendment in the prison context, it certainly may offer a voluntary interview as part of a discretionary clemency review. See also **Chavez v. Martinez, 538 U.S. 760, 767-73 (2003)**.

Finally, petitioner’s challenge is especially weak because the asserted injury is speculative. Courts do not adjudicate hypothetical self-incrimination claims based on imagined future use of statements in some later proceeding. See **Texas v. Cobb, 532 U.S. 162, 172-73 (2001)**; **Murphy, 465 U.S. at 436**. A voluntary interview in a clemency proceeding does not itself compel testimony, and absent compulsion there is no Fifth Amendment violation.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
