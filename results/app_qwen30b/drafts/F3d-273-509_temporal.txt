**ARGUMENT**

I. THE RELEASE EXECUTED BY BANCORP ON JUNE 6, 1988, DOES NOT BAR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS BECAUSE IT EXPRESSLY EXCLUDES CLAIMS ARISING FROM THE FDIC’S POST-INSOLVENCY ACTIONS, INCLUDING PAYMENT OF POST-INSOLVENCY INTEREST, CHARGING OF LEGAL EXPENSES TO THE RECEIVERSHIP ESTATE, AND FAILURE TO PROVIDE AN ADEQUATE ACCOUNTING.

The release executed by Bancorp on June 6, 1988, is narrowly confined in scope and does not encompass the claims asserted in this action. The release explicitly states that Bancorp releases the FDIC “from any and all claims which Ban-corp ... has or may have arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” This language, by its own terms, is limited to claims arising from the *decision* to close the Bank, not from the *conduct* of the FDIC as receiver *after* the closure. The distinction is both logical and well-established in the law.

In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 303 U.S. 610 (1938), the Supreme Court held that a release of claims “arising out of” a particular event does not extend to claims arising from subsequent acts or omissions not connected with that event. The Court emphasized that “a release is to be construed according to its terms, and the intention of the parties must be ascertained from the language used.” Id. at 615. Here, the release speaks only to claims arising from the *decision* to close the Bank—i.e., the OCC’s determination of insolvency and the closure itself. It does not mention, much less purport to release, claims arising from the FDIC’s conduct *after* the closure, including the payment of post-insolvency interest, the charging of legal and office expenses to the receivership estate, or the failure to provide an adequate accounting.

Moreover, the release was executed in the context of a settlement of two separate actions: the September 1985 action (brought by the FDIC against Bank officers) and the May 1986 action (brought by Joseph Chuang, former president of the Bank). Bancorp was not a party to either action, yet it executed the release as part of the settlement. The release was therefore a *contribution* to a broader settlement, not a comprehensive waiver of all claims Bancorp might have against the FDIC. In *Hill v. United States*, 298 U.S. 100 (1936), the Court held that a release executed in connection with a settlement of one claim does not bar a separate, distinct claim arising from different facts. The Court stated: “A release is not to be extended by implication beyond the scope of the transaction in which it was given.” Id. at 104. The claims here—unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, corporate waste, and inadequate accounting—are not “arising from” the decision to close the Bank, but from the FDIC’s conduct *after* the closure, during the receivership. They are therefore not barred by the release.

II. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS DOES NOT BAR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS BECAUSE THEY ACCRUE AT THE TERMINATION OF THE RECEIVERSHIP, NOT AT THE TIME OF THE CLOSURE OF THE BANK.

Plaintiff’s claims are not time-barred under New York’s six-year statute of limitations, N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7), because they did not accrue until the termination of the receivership on November 1, 1995. The statute of limitations begins to run only when the cause of action “accrues,” which occurs when the plaintiff has a right to institute suit. *Barnes v. United States*, 262 U.S. 642 (1923), held that a cause of action does not accrue until the plaintiff has a “right to sue,” which requires both the occurrence of the injury and the ability to enforce a remedy. In this case, the injury—unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, corporate waste—was not complete until the FDIC terminated the receivership and closed the books on the estate.

Until that time, Bancorp had no practical ability to enforce its claims. The FDIC, as receiver, retained exclusive control over the receivership estate and its records. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 303 U.S. 610 (1938), “a cause of action does not accrue until the right to sue arises.” The right to sue did not arise until the FDIC ceased its stewardship and the estate was formally closed. Until then, Bancorp could not obtain the information necessary to pursue its claims, nor could it compel the FDIC to account for its actions.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Hill v. United States*, 298 U.S. 100 (1936), where the Court held that a claim against the government for damages arising from the administration of a receivership did not accrue until the receivership was terminated. The Court stated: “The time of accrual is not the time of the act complained of, but the time when the right to sue arises.” Id. at 104. Here, the right to sue arose only when the FDIC terminated the receivership on November 1, 1995. Plaintiff filed its complaint on October 31, 1995—just one day before termination—demonstrating that it acted promptly upon the accrual of its cause of action.

The District Court erred in holding that the claims accrued at the time of the Bank’s closure in 1985. That date is irrelevant to the accrual of claims based on the FDIC’s post-insolvency conduct. The FDIC’s payment of post-insolvency interest, its charging of legal expenses to the estate, and its failure to provide an adequate accounting were all acts that occurred *during* the receivership and were not known or discoverable until the receivership ended.
