**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 1407(a) Unambiguously Requires Remand After Pretrial Proceedings, Precluding a Transferee Court’s Self-Assignment Under Section 1404(a)**

The statutory text governing multidistrict litigation is unequivocal. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a), transferred actions are to be “remanded by the panel” to the transferor district “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded” “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” This mandatory language admits of no exception and leaves no room for a transferee court to retain a case for trial by invoking § 1404(a).

The Ninth Circuit majority conceded that “despite what appears to be a clear statutory mandate,” it could not find the district court’s order clearly erroneous because of contrary case law and Rule 14(b) of the Rules of Procedure for the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation. But a statute cannot be overcome by judicial policy preferences or a procedural rule. As Judge Kozinski observed below, “the federal courts have simply disregarded the statutory mandate,” beginning with *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971). The time has come to correct this error.

**II. The Text and Structure of Sections 1404 and 1407 Compel Reversal**

Section 1404(a) provides that “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice, a district court may transfer any civil action to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” As the Court has recognized, § 1404(a) is a codification of the common-law doctrine of forum non conveniens. *Norwood v. Kirkpatrick*, 349 U.S. 29, 32 (1955). But § 1407(a) is the more specific statute governing multidistrict litigation. Where a specific statute and a general one conflict, the specific governs. *HCSC-Laundry v. United States*, 450 U.S. 1, 6 (1981) (per curiam).

The conflict here is irreconcilable. Section 1407(a) limits the Panel’s transfer authority to “pretrial proceedings,” and mandates remand before trial. By authorizing a transferee court to use § 1404(a) to keep a case for trial, the Ninth Circuit permits precisely what § 1407(a) forbids. The statutes cannot be read harmoniously; one must yield. Because § 1407(a) is the specific mechanism Congress designed for coordinating related cases, its mandatory remand provision controls.

**III. The Transferee Court Lacks Authority to Retain Jurisdiction Under Section 1404(a) Because the Action Could Not “Have Been Brought” in That District in Any Event**

A § 1404(a) transfer is valid only to a district where the action “might have been brought.” The phrase refers to the plaintiff’s right to sue originally in the transferee district. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343-44 (1960). Here, Lexecon’s action was not initially filed in Arizona, nor could it have been brought there absent the Panel’s § 1407 transfer. The transferee court’s self-designation therefore rests on an impermissible bootstrapping: it relied on the § 1407 transfer—which was limited to pretrial proceedings—to manufacture venue power it never possessed.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on cases such as *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), does not withstand scrutiny. Those decisions justify self-assignment on grounds of convenience and judicial economy. But as this Court has repeatedly held, venue rules are “not merely a matter of convenience” but “a matter of substantive right.” *Brunette Mach. Works, Ltd. v. Kockum Indus., Inc.*, 406 U.S. 706, 714 n.7 (1972). Congress’s choice in § 1407(a) to return cases to the original district for trial reflects a deliberate balance; courts may not rebalance that calculus on utilitarian grounds.

**IV. The Panel’s Rule 14(b) Cannot Override an Act of Congress**

The Ninth Circuit cited J.P.M.L.R.P. 14(b) as authorizing a transferee court to transfer a case to itself for trial. But agency rules—and judicial rules—cannot supersede a statute. The Panel’s rule purports to bless a practice Congress did not authorize. A rule “interpreted in a manner that would conflict with the statute” is invalid. *United States v. W.R. Grace & Co.*, 429 U.S. 1022, 1026 n.2 (1976) (Burger, C.J., concurring). No procedural rule may nullify the mandatory remand obligation embodied in § 1407(a).

**V. The Practical Consequences of Affirmance Undermine the Statute’s Policy**

Affirmance would encourage transferee judges to retain cases for trial, thereby depriving plaintiffs of their chosen forum and undermining § 1407’s carefully calibrated structure. This Court has long protected a plaintiff’s forum choice as a “substantial right.” *See, e.g., Schnell v. Peter Eckrich & Sons, Inc.*, 365 U.S. 260, 262 (1961). Section 1407’s remand requirement is designed to preserve that right after coordinated pretrial proceedings conclude. The Ninth Circuit’s approach converts a temporary pretrial transfer into a permanent venue change—precisely what Congress sought to avoid.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to remand to the Northern District of Illinois.
