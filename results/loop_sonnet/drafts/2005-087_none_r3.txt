**ARGUMENT**

Arizona's rule confining Clark's psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry offends no principle of due process. Two independent, well-established lines of this Court's precedent so demonstrate: first, the States' broad substantive authority to define criminal responsibility and structure the defenses available to a criminal defendant; and second, the deference owed to state evidentiary and procedural rules that do not render a trial fundamentally unfair. Under either line of authority, Arizona's rule is constitutional.

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available against them, and the manner in which evidence of mental condition enters a criminal trial. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, explaining that "the process of formulating a rule of criminal responsibility... is one in which the legislature of each State has wide latitude," and that due process does not compel adoption of any particular test of criminal responsibility. *Id.* at 800–01. If a State may constitutionally require a defendant to carry the entire burden of proving insanity, it follows that a State may likewise determine the procedural channel through which mental-disease evidence is presented.

*Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), reinforces this deference. The plurality there declined to constitutionalize any single doctrine of criminal responsibility, reasoning that "traditional common-law concepts of personal accountability" remain matters of state law and that the Court should be "reluctant to take this step in the absence of some indication... that a change in that direction is required." *Id.* at 536. Arizona's rule is exactly the sort of line-drawing that *Leland* and *Powell* leave to the States rather than federalize under the Fourteenth Amendment.

This channeling rule also reflects deep historical practice. The insanity defense, as classically understood since *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treats mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant—not as a tool for piecemeal negation of the prosecution's mens rea proof. Arizona's rule preserves that traditional separation; it does not eliminate consideration of mental illness but assigns it to the doctrinal category in which Anglo-American law has historically placed it.

This Court has already sustained the exclusion of closely analogous psychiatric evidence offered to negate intent. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the Court upheld a refusal to instruct that evidence of mental deficiency could reduce murder to manslaughter by negating premeditation, holding that a jurisdiction may resist "ingrafting the doctrine of partial responsibility onto our jurisprudence" without violating due process. *Id.* at 475–76. If wholesale exclusion of such evidence to negate premeditation was permissible in *Fisher*, Arizona's far more modest rule—which still allows the same evidence full consideration on insanity—is a fortiori constitutional.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Leland* and *Powell*, Arizona's rule survives as a permissible procedural regulation. Due process does not constitutionalize state evidentiary rules wholesale; it requires only that such rules not offend "some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). Nor does the Fourteenth Amendment "assure uniformity of judicial procedure"; States remain free "to abolish old and to create new remedies... in the exercise of a wide discretion." *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 110–11 (1908).

This Court applied these principles directly to a state rule limiting the use of certain evidence in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), upholding Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence over the objection that it improperly influenced the jury's assessment of guilt. The Court explained that "the process of adjudication... normally requires the drawing of lines," and that state evidentiary rules merit deference absent a showing that they render the trial fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 562–64. Arizona's rule is, if anything, less intrusive than the one sustained in *Spencer*: it excludes nothing outright but channels Clark's evidence to the vehicle—the insanity defense—through which such evidence has traditionally been evaluated.

The touchstone remains whether "the absence of that fair play" so infects the proceeding as to violate due process. *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941). Here, the trial court admitted the full range of Clark's expert testimony and permitted the factfinder to weigh it on his insanity claim—the very evidence he now insists must also be available, unconstrained by that defense's burdens, to negate intent. Nothing in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), concerning the burden of proof on insanity under federal law, suggests that the Constitution requires evidence relevant to one defense to be automatically admissible for an entirely separate purpose.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's psychiatric evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, freed from the burdens attending that defense, to defeat the mens rea element the State must independently prove beyond a reasonable doubt. That is precisely the channeling rule sanctioned by *Leland*, *Powell*, *Fisher*, *Spencer*, and *Twining*. Arizona's rule offends no principle "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people" as to be fundamental, *Snyder*, 291 U.S. at 105, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
