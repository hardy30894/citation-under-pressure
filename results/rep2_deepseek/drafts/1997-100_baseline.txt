**ARGUMENT**

**THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE BARS RETRIAL OF A PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION IN A NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDING WHEN THE STATE HAS CREATED A TRIAL-LIKE PROCEEDING THAT RESEMBLES A GUILT PHASE.**

The Fifth Amendment guarantees that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” This protection applies to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). The Clause bars retrial after an acquittal, *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969), and this principle extends to sentencing proceedings that “have the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981).

In *Bullington*, the Court held that double jeopardy barred retrial of the death penalty after a sentencing jury had imposed life imprisonment. *Id.* at 446. The Court emphasized that Missouri’s capital sentencing proceeding “was itself a trial on the issue of punishment” where the State bore the burden of proving aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, and the defendant enjoyed the right to present evidence and cross-examine witnesses. *Id.* at 438-439. The Court later applied the same reasoning in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 209-210 (1984), holding that double jeopardy barred retrial of a death sentence after a finding of no aggravating circumstances, because the proceeding was “like a trial” and the judge’s determination was “the functional equivalent of an acquittal.”

The California Supreme Court erred in holding that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* are limited to capital cases. Nothing in those decisions restricts their reasoning to capital sentencing. The Court in *Bullington* focused on the *character* of the proceeding, not the *severity* of the sentence. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439. To be sure, the Court in *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994), observed that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* “were capital cases, and our reasoning in those cases was based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding.” But *Caspari* did not *hold* that *Bullington* is inapplicable to noncapital proceedings. *Id.* at 393. The Court merely concluded that such application was not “dictated by our precedents” for purposes of *Teague* retroactivity analysis. *Id.* The question remains open, and this Court should answer it in favor of the petitioner.

California’s three-strikes sentencing proceeding for prior conviction allegations is a trial-like proceeding that warrants double jeopardy protection. The State must prove the prior conviction allegation “beyond a reasonable doubt.” *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993). The rules of evidence apply. *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 224 (1996). The defendant has the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses. *Id.* at 228 n.6. These features are precisely the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence” that triggered double jeopardy protection in *Bullington* and *Rumsey*. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439.

The State’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. *Williams* held that due process does not require a formal trial at sentencing, *id.* at 251, but it did not address whether double jeopardy applies *when the State chooses to provide such a trial*. Here, California voluntarily created a trial-like proceeding for prior conviction allegations. Having done so, the State must live with the consequences of that choice, including the double jeopardy bar. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439-440 (applying double jeopardy where Missouri “chose to have a separate sentencing hearing before the jury” and “gave the defendant the right to present evidence and cross-examine witnesses”).

The lower court’s distinction between statutory and constitutional protections is unpersuasive. *Bullington* itself involved statutory procedural protections that were not constitutionally required. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438. The Court did not require that the protections be of constitutional origin; it required only that the proceeding “resemble a trial” for double jeopardy purposes. *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209-210.

Here, the California Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient to support the prior conviction allegation. Under *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 18 (1978), a finding of insufficient evidence at trial bars retrial under the Double Jeopardy Clause. Because the sentencing proceeding for prior conviction allegations is the functional equivalent of a trial, the same rule applies. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 443.

The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed, and the case remanded for resentencing without the prior conviction enhancement.
