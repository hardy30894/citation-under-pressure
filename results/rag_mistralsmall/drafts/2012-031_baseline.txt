**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Certifying a Class Without Adequate Evidence That Damages Could Be Measured on a Class-Wide Basis.**

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). To satisfy this standard, a plaintiff must show that damages can be proven on a class-wide basis using common evidence. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011) (“[A] class cannot be certified on the premise that [the defendant] will not be entitled to litigate its statutory defenses to individual claims.”). The Third Circuit’s decision in *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), confirms that district courts must conduct a “rigorous analysis” of whether such evidence exists before certifying a class. *Id.* at 318.

Here, the District Court erred by certifying a class despite the absence of admissible evidence demonstrating a feasible, class-wide damages methodology. The plaintiffs’ damages expert, Dr. James McClave, proposed four theories of damages, but the District Court expressly limited the class to a single theory—antitrust impact from Comcast’s alleged deterrence of overbuilder competition. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). However, even under this narrowed theory, Dr. McClave’s methodology failed to establish class-wide damages. His report conceded that damages would vary by household based on individual viewing habits, subscription tiers, and market conditions. *Id.* at 160-61. The expert’s own analysis thus demonstrated that damages could not be proven uniformly across the class.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that individualized damages inquiries preclude class certification. In *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27 (2013), the Court reversed a class certification where the plaintiffs’ damages model failed to tie alleged antitrust violations to the specific overcharge theory advanced at trial. The Court held that “Rule 23 does not set forth a mere pleading standard” and that a “model purporting to serve as evidence of damages in this case” must “measure only those damages attributable to the theory of liability.” *Id.* at 33-34. Similarly, in *Dukes*, the Court rejected a class where plaintiffs sought “millions of employment decisions at once” without a common method to calculate backpay. 564 U.S. at 350.

The Third Circuit’s decision in *Hydrogen Peroxide* reinforces this principle. There, the court held that a damages model must “provide a method to measure and allocate damages on a class-wide basis” and that “[i]f the model fails to do so, class certification is improper.” 552 F.3d at 320. The plaintiffs here failed to meet this standard. Their expert’s methodology did not isolate damages attributable to the single theory of liability adopted by the District Court, rendering class-wide damages unquantifiable. The District Court’s certification order was therefore an abuse of discretion.

**II. The District Court Improperly Relied on Plaintiffs’ Expert Testimony Without Conducting a Sufficient Daubert Analysis.**

Federal Rule of Evidence 702 and *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), require district courts to assess the reliability of expert testimony before admitting it. This gatekeeping function applies with equal force at the class certification stage. *In re Blood Litig.*, 769 F.3d 238, 250 (3d Cir. 2014) (“[A] district court must conduct a *Daubert* analysis when deciding whether to certify a class.”).

Here, the District Court failed to rigorously evaluate Dr. McClave’s methodology. The court heard four days of expert testimony but did not conduct an independent assessment of whether his damages model was scientifically valid or capable of class-wide application. Instead, the court deferred to the plaintiffs’ experts, accepting their conclusions without scrutinizing the underlying assumptions or data. This was error. As the Supreme Court explained in *Comcast*, “[t]he District Court’s analysis would be incomplete without considering whether the methodology [the expert] used to calculate damages was consistent with his liability theory.” 569 U.S. at 34.

The Third Circuit has similarly cautioned that courts must “probe[] the merits of the case only to the extent necessary to determine whether the requirements of Rule 23 are satisfied.” *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 317. By failing to conduct a *Daubert* analysis, the District Court abdicated its gatekeeping role and certified a class based on unreliable expert testimony. This constituted an abuse of discretion.

**Conclusion**

The District Court’s certification order must be reversed because it certified a class without admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis and without conducting a proper *Daubert* analysis. The Third Circuit should remand with instructions to decertify the class.
