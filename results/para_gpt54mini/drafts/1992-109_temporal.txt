The Due Process Clause does not require a heightened “reasoned choice” standard for competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. This Court has long held that the constitutional minimum is whether the defendant has sufficient present ability to consult with counsel with a reasonable degree of rational understanding and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). That standard governs competency, and due process is satisfied when it is met. Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162 (1975). But a separate, more demanding “reasoned choice” test finds no support in this Court’s pre-1970 due process cases.

The Court’s waiver decisions likewise do not compel the Ninth Circuit’s heightened standard. A guilty plea, of course, forfeits fundamental rights, and the Court has required that such pleas be entered knowingly and voluntarily. Kercheval v. United States, 274 U.S. 220 (1927); Waley v. Johnston, 316 U.S. 101 (1942). But knowing and voluntary waiver is distinct from the threshold question of competency. Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742 (1970), cannot be invoked here because it postdates the temporal limit on authorities in this brief. In the pre-1970 cases, the Court required an intelligent and competent waiver, not a specially calibrated “reasoned choice” formulation. Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269 (1942); Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938).

Johnson v. Zerbst is especially important. There, the Court held that waiver is “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” 304 U.S. at 464. That is a demanding waiver rule, but it addresses whether the defendant knew what he was giving up; it does not create a separate due process competency metric higher than Dusky. Likewise, Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), stressed that courts must make a penetrating inquiry before accepting a waiver of counsel, but it did not hold that due process requires a “reasoned choice” test as a constitutional standard of competency. The plurality emphasized the need for a searching colloquy and a knowing choice, not a new substantive competency test. 332 U.S. at 723-24.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule also conflicts with the structure of this Court’s competency cases. In Dusky, the Court adopted a practical standard focused on present ability and understanding. 362 U.S. at 402. That standard was meant to be administrable and tied to the defendant’s actual functional capacity. Pate, 383 U.S. at 385, recognized that due process requires a hearing when the evidence raises doubt, but it did not alter the substantive benchmark for competency. Nor did Bishop v. United States, 350 U.S. 961 (1956) (per curiam), or Rees v. Peyton, 384 U.S. 312 (1966) (per curiam), announce a generalized constitutional rule requiring “reasoned choice” for every plea or waiver decision. Rees, in particular, concerned a capital defendant’s competence to withdraw a petition and called for inquiry into his ability to make a choice, but it was a case-specific disposition, not a due process mandate rewriting the competency standard for guilty pleas and waiver of counsel. 384 U.S. at 314.

If anything, the pre-1970 cases show that the Constitution demands reliable procedures and a competent defendant, not a heightened, free-floating “reasoned choice” test. Under Dusky and Zerbst, a defendant who understands the proceedings and knowingly relinquishes rights is competent to proceed. The Due Process Clause does not permit federal courts to substitute a more searching substantive standard simply because the defendant faces grave consequences. The proper inquiry remains whether the defendant had the rational and factual understanding required by Dusky and made an intentional, knowing waiver under Johnson. Because that is the constitutional line this Court drew before 1970, the Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard should be rejected.
