The respondent respectfully submits that the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity plea.

The Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the Court held that the insanity defense is a separate and distinct issue from the question of whether the defendant formed the requisite criminal intent. The Court noted that "if the defense relied upon was insanity at the time of the commission of the alleged offense, it was necessary that the jury should be instructed as to the law applicable to that defense." Id. at 493. This distinction between the insanity defense and the issue of criminal intent supports Arizona's rule limiting the use of expert evidence to the insanity claim.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure that govern their criminal trials. In Lisenba v. California, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), the Court held that "the due process clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not... require the state to adopt a particular rule of evidence." Id. at 228. Similarly, in Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the Court noted that "the Fourteenth Amendment does not... forbid a state to adopt and enforce a rule of evidence that is rational and not arbitrary." Id. at 105.

Arizona's rule is rational and not arbitrary, as it is designed to prevent the confusion of the jury and to ensure that the prosecution is not unfairly prejudiced by the introduction of potentially unreliable expert evidence on the issue of criminal intent. See Shepard v. United States, 290 U.S. 96 (1933) (noting the potential for expert evidence to be "of slight probative value" and "highly conjectural").

The respondent's position is also supported by the principle that the prosecution bears the burden of proving the elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, while the defendant bears the burden of proving his insanity defense. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant. The Court noted that "the burden of proof of insanity is traditionally placed on the defendant." Id. at 799. Similarly, in Morrison v. California, 291 U.S. 82 (1934), the Court held that "the state is free to regulate the distribution of the burden of proof between the prosecution and the defendant." Id. at 88-89.

In addition, the Court has recognized that the admissibility of expert evidence is a matter committed to the discretion of the trial judge. In Commonwealth v. Rogers, 48 Mass. 500 (1844), the court held that the trial judge has discretion to determine the admissibility of expert evidence. See also People v. McCann, 16 N.Y. 58 (1857) (noting that the trial judge has discretion to determine the qualifications of expert witnesses).

The respondent acknowledges that the Court has also recognized the importance of allowing defendants to present evidence in their defense. In Chambers v. Mississippi is not available, we can look to older cases like  United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi, 344 U.S. 561 (1953) (Douglas, J., dissenting) (citing, inter alia,  Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934)) and  Holt v. United States, 218 U.S. 245 (1910). However, these cases do not establish a due process right to present expert evidence on the issue of criminal intent, separate from an insanity plea.

In conclusion, the Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent and does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause. The respondent respectfully submits that the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
