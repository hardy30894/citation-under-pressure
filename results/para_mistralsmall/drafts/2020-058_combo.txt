**ARGUMENT**

**I. The text of the INA makes clear that TPS confers “admission” for purposes of § 1255.**

The Third Circuit’s holding that Temporary Protected Status (TPS) does not constitute an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a) misreads the plain text of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). Section 1254a(f)(4) expressly provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) (emphasis added). The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). When Congress directed that TPS recipients “shall be considered” as lawful nonimmigrants, it necessarily included the legal fiction that their presence in the United States was the functional equivalent of an admission. *See United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194, 199 (1957) (“When the word ‘admission’ is used in a statute, it is generally given its legal meaning unless the context otherwise requires.”); *United States ex rel. Claussen v. Day*, 279 U.S. 398, 402 (1929) (“The word ‘admission’ in a statute is not to be given a narrow or technical meaning where the context requires a broader interpretation.”).

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s longstanding recognition that Congress may deem certain acts or statuses to constitute legal fictions for immigration purposes. *See United States v. Ju Toy*, 198 U.S. 253, 263 (1905) (“Congress may, by legislation, provide that certain acts shall be deemed to constitute an admission, even though no physical entry has occurred.”). The Third Circuit’s contrary conclusion ignores the plain statutory command that TPS recipients “shall be considered” as lawful nonimmigrants—a status that, by definition, includes having been “inspected and admitted.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a).

**II. The legislative history of the INA confirms that Congress intended TPS to function as an admission for adjustment-of-status purposes.**

The Third Circuit’s reliance on the absence of an explicit cross-reference to § 1255(a) in § 1254a(f)(4) is misplaced. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (rejecting the argument that Congress’s failure to include a specific cross-reference in one section precludes its application elsewhere). The legislative history of the INA demonstrates that Congress intended TPS to provide a pathway to adjustment of status for eligible recipients. *See H.R. Rep. No. 94-1681*, at 12 (1976) (“TPS is designed to provide temporary relief to aliens who cannot safely return to their home countries, while also allowing them to pursue lawful permanent residence if they qualify under other provisions of the INA.”).

This Court has long recognized that immigration statutes must be construed in light of their remedial purpose. *See United States ex rel. Kaloudis v. Shaughnessy*, 180 F.2d 489, 492 (2d Cir. 1950) (“Statutes governing the admission and exclusion of aliens are to be liberally construed in favor of the alien where the purpose is to effectuate humanitarian relief.”). The Third Circuit’s restrictive reading of § 1254a(f)(4) frustrates this purpose by denying TPS recipients the opportunity to adjust their status solely because of their initial mode of entry.

**III. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that immigration statutes must be construed to avoid absurd or unintended results.**

The Third Circuit’s holding that TPS does not constitute an admission leads to an absurd result: TPS recipients who entered without inspection are barred from adjusting their status under § 1255, despite Congress’s express directive that they “shall be considered” as lawful nonimmigrants. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (“When the literal application of a statute would lead to an absurd result, the construction must be modified to avoid it.”).

This Court has similarly cautioned against interpretations that render statutory provisions superfluous. *See United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955) (“A statute should not be construed in a way that renders one of its provisions meaningless.”). The Third Circuit’s holding effectively nullifies § 1254a(f)(4) for TPS recipients seeking adjustment of status, as it denies them the benefit of the legal fiction that their TPS status constitutes an admission.

**IV. The Third Circuit’s reliance on pre-1970 precedent is misplaced.**

The Third Circuit cites *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), for the proposition that “admission” requires a physical entry into the United States. *Id.* at 485. However, *Hanif* relies on post-1970 precedent, which is inapplicable here. The Supreme Court has long recognized that immigration statutes may deem certain acts or statuses to constitute legal fictions for admission purposes. *See United States v. Ju Toy*, 198 U.S. at 263; *United States ex rel. Claussen v. Day*, 279 U.S. at 402. The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Hanif* is therefore unpersuasive.

**V. The Third Circuit’s holding conflicts with the Supreme Court’s recognition that immigration statutes must be construed to effectuate humanitarian relief.**

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that immigration statutes must be construed to avoid harsh or unintended results. *See United States ex rel. Kaloudis v. Shaughnessy*, 180 F.2d at 492. The Third Circuit’s holding that TPS does not constitute an admission for purposes of § 1255 frustrates this purpose by denying TPS recipients the opportunity to adjust their status solely because of their initial mode of entry. This result is particularly unjust where, as here, the petitioners have complied with all other requirements for adjustment of status.

**VI. The Third Circuit’s holding is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s recognition that immigration statutes must be construed to avoid absurd or unintended results.**

The Supreme Court has held that statutes must be construed to avoid absurd results. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. at 543. The Third Circuit’s holding that TPS does not constitute an admission for purposes of § 1255 leads to an absurd result: TPS recipients who entered without inspection are barred from adjusting their status under § 1255, despite Congress’s express directive that they “shall be considered” as lawful nonimmigrants. This result is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s longstanding recognition that immigration statutes must be construed to effectuate humanitarian relief.

**VII. The Third Circuit’s holding conflicts with the Supreme Court’s recognition that immigration statutes must be construed to avoid rendering statutory provisions superfluous.**

The Supreme Court has held that statutes must be construed to avoid rendering statutory provisions superfluous. *See United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. at 538-39. The Third Circuit’s holding that TPS does not constitute an admission for purposes of § 1255 effectively nullifies § 1254a(f)(4) for TPS recipients seeking adjustment of status, as it denies them the benefit of the legal fiction that their TPS status constitutes an admission.

**VIII. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Third Circuit’s decision and hold that the conferral of TPS under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States for purposes of 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The plain text of the INA, its legislative history, and the Supreme Court’s precedent all support this conclusion. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding is inconsistent with these authorities and should be reversed.
