**ARGUMENT**

The Texas Supreme Court erred in holding that 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) confers exclusive federal jurisdiction over this state-law legal malpractice action. Congress has not extended the “arising under” grant to every case that merely touches patent law. *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). A state claim triggers § 1338(a) only when “a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” *Id.* at 809.

Minton’s malpractice claim is created by Texas common law. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). It therefore falls within federal jurisdiction, if at all, only under the second *Christianson* prong. The Supreme Court has refined that inquiry into four elements: the federal issue must be (1) necessarily raised, (2) actually disputed, (3) substantial, and (4) capable of resolution in federal court “without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).

The experimental-use question satisfies only the first two factors. It is not “substantial” in the *Grable* sense. The Supreme Court has long refused to open the “arising under” door merely because a federal issue appears in a state complaint. *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936); *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Patent questions that arise only as elements of a state malpractice claim do not implicate the validity or scope of any patent right and therefore lack the requisite substantiality. *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 384, 388 (5th Cir. 2008).

Even if the issue were substantial, federal adjudication would upset the congressionally struck balance. State courts routinely resolve malpractice actions that incidentally require application of federal law. *New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 270 Neb. 264, 702 N.W.2d 336 (2005); *E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 189 Cal. App. 4th 1140, 117 Cal. Rptr. 3d 516 (2010); *Roof Technical Servs., Inc. v. Hill*, 679 F. Supp. 2d 749, 753-54 (N.D. Tex. 2010); *Genelink Biosciences, Inc. v. Colby*, 722 F. Supp. 2d 592 (D.N.J. 2010); *Danner, Inc. v. Foley & Lardner, LLP*, 2010 WL 2608292, at *5 (D. Or. Mar. 15, 2010); *Anderson v. Johnson*, 2009 WL 2244622, at *3 (N.D. Ill. July 27, 2009). Removing every such case to federal court would “herald a potentially enormous shift of traditionally state cases into federal courts.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 319.

Because Minton’s claim fails the *Grable* test at the third and fourth steps, exclusive federal jurisdiction does not lie. The judgment of the Texas Supreme Court should be reversed.
