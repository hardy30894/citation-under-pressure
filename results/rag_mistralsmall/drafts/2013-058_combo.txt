**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE BANKRUPTCY CODE’S RETIREMENT-FUND EXEMPTIONS DO NOT REQUIRE THAT THE DEBTOR HERSELF BE THE RETIREMENT SAVINGS’ ORIGINAL ACCOUNT-HOLDER.**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code exempts “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” the enumerated sections of the Internal Revenue Code, including §408 (IRAs). 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA loses its exempt status because Heidi Heffron-Clark did not create the account for her own retirement. The statutory text contains no temporal or ownership limitation tying the exemption to the debtor’s status as the original retirement saver. *See generally, Bruning v. United States*, 376 U.S. 358, 361 (1964) (courts must give effect to the plain meaning of statutory text absent clear contrary indication).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against importing unwritten limitations into exemption statutes. In *Myers v. Matley*, 318 U.S. 622, 629 (1943), the Court rejected a construction of the Bankruptcy Act that would have required a homestead exemption to depend on the debtor’s pre-bankruptcy use of the property. The Court held that the exemption turned on the nature of the asset—not the debtor’s personal history with it. *Id.* at 630. Similarly, in *Kaufman v. Tredway*, 195 U.S. 271, 283 (1904), the Court refused to read a “good faith” requirement into the Bankruptcy Act that was not expressed in the statute, emphasizing that exemptions are creatures of legislative grace and must be construed strictly against the debtor and liberally in favor of creditors. *Id.* at 284.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *Bronx Brass Foundry, Inc. v. Irving Trust Co.*, 297 U.S. 230, 240 (1936)—a preference case—is misplaced. *Bronx Brass* involved the scope of a creditor’s right to withdraw a claim, not the meaning of an exemption. *Id.* at 238. The Court there cautioned against judicial enlargement of statutory rights, a principle that supports a narrow construction of exemptions, not the broad one the Seventh Circuit adopted. *Id.* at 240.

II. **THE EXEMPTION TURNS ON THE TAX-DEFERRAL STATUS OF THE FUND, NOT ITS CURRENT OWNER’S RETIREMENT INTENTIONS.**

The Seventh Circuit erred in distinguishing inherited IRAs from traditional IRAs based on distribution requirements. The Bankruptcy Code does not condition the exemption on the debtor’s ability to delay withdrawals until retirement. *See Lockwood v. Exchange Bank*, 190 U.S. 294, 308 (1903) (exemptions are defined by the statute’s categories, not equitable considerations of use). The Court has long held that exemptions are creatures of statute and must be applied as written. *See Schrader v. Manufacturers’ Nat. Bank of Chicago*, 133 U.S. 67, 75 (1890) (exemptions are limited to what the statute expressly includes).

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a debtor’s receipt of cash from a liquidated IRA is flawed. *See* Slip Op. at 6. In *Joint Industry Board of Electrical Industry v. United States*, 391 U.S. 224, 228 (1968), the Court distinguished between funds held in trust for employees’ future benefits and mere contractual claims, emphasizing that the exemption turns on the fund’s *status*, not its *source*. *Id.* at 230. An inherited IRA remains a tax-deferred retirement vehicle under §408, even if its distribution schedule differs from a traditional IRA. *See* 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C) (defining inherited IRAs as IRAs for tax purposes).

III. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S CONSTRUCTION WOULD CREATE AN UNWORKABLE REGIME WHERE RETIREMENT FUNDS LOSE EXEMPTION STATUS UPON INHERITANCE.**

The Seventh Circuit’s rule would inject uncertainty into bankruptcy proceedings. In *Boteler v. Ingels*, 308 U.S. 57, 61 (1939), the Court rejected a construction of the Bankruptcy Act that would have required courts to trace the *use* of funds through multiple transactions, warning that such inquiries breed litigation and defeat the Act’s remedial purpose. *Id.* at 60. The Seventh Circuit’s rule would require courts to determine whether funds in a debtor’s account were ever “retirement funds” in someone else’s hands—a speculative inquiry that would embroil bankruptcy proceedings in precisely the sort of tracing and historical analysis the Court has condemned. *See id.*

The Court has repeatedly held that exemptions must be predictable and administrable. In *McKee v. Paradise*, 299 U.S. 119, 125 (1936), the Court refused to recognize an equitable lien in bankruptcy, emphasizing that creditors are entitled to rely on the statutory scheme. *Id.* at 127. The Seventh Circuit’s rule would deny creditors that certainty, as they could never know whether inherited retirement funds would be exempt until a court parsed the decedent’s intent decades earlier.

IV. **THE FEDERAL EXEMPTIONS ARE DESIGNED TO PROTECT RETIREMENT SAVINGS, WHETHER HELD BY THE ORIGINAL SAVERS OR THEIR BENEFICIARIES.**

The Bankruptcy Code’s retirement-fund exemptions reflect Congress’s policy of encouraging retirement savings by shielding them from creditors. *See* 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The Seventh Circuit’s rule would frustrate that policy by penalizing beneficiaries for accepting retirement savings they did nothing to create. In *Ira M. Hedges*, 218 U.S. 264, 270 (1910), the Court refused to allow a party to avoid the consequences of a statutory scheme by recasting a transaction, emphasizing that form should not prevail over substance where Congress has spoken clearly. *Id.* at 271. Here, Congress has spoken clearly: funds in accounts tax-exempt under §408 are exempt in bankruptcy. The Seventh Circuit rewrote that command by adding a temporal limitation Congress did not enact.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Seventh Circuit’s judgment and hold that inherited IRAs qualify as “retirement funds” under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). The judgment below should be affirmed.
