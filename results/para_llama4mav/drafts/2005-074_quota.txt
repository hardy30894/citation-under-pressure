The reinstatement statute, Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date. Even if it did, its application to Fernandez-Vargas would be impermissibly retroactive.

First, the plain language of the statute and the relevant case law do not clearly indicate that Congress intended for the reinstatement statute to apply retroactively to pre-enactment re-entries. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), the Supreme Court established a two-step test for determining whether a statute applies retroactively. The first step is to determine whether Congress has clearly indicated that the statute should be applied retroactively. If not, the second step is to assess whether the statute has an impermissible retroactive effect. The Court has since reaffirmed that "a requirement that Congress first make its intention clear helps ensure that Congress itself has determined that the benefits of retroactivity outweigh the potential for disruption or unfairness." Id. at 268. Here, the statute's language does not explicitly state that it applies to pre-enactment re-entries.

Furthermore, applying the reinstatement statute to Fernandez-Vargas would have a retroactive effect, as it would attach new legal consequences to his pre-enactment re-entry. In INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), the Court held that a statute cannot be applied retroactively if it would "impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party's liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed." Id. at 321 (quoting Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 280). Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States in 1982 and lived here for nearly two decades before the reinstatement statute was enacted. Applying the statute to him would impair his rights and impose new liabilities for past conduct.

Additionally, the Tenth Circuit's holding that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" is not supported by the case law. In St. Cyr, the Court recognized that aliens who had pleaded guilty to certain crimes had a reasonable expectation that they would be eligible for discretionary relief under the INA. Similarly, Fernandez-Vargas had a reasonable expectation that he could adjust his status upon marrying a U.S. citizen. The Court's decision in Fernandez-Antonia v. Gonzales, 442 F.3d 420 (6th Cir. 2006), which held that the reinstatement statute did not apply retroactively to an alien who re-entered before its effective date, supports this argument.

The government's argument that Fernandez-Vargas' continued presence in the United States after the statute's effective date somehow cures any retroactivity concerns is unpersuasive. As the Court held in Republic of Austria v. Altmann, 541 U.S. 677 (2004), "a statute does not operate 'retrospectively' merely because it is applied in a case arising from conduct antedating the statute's enactment." Id. at 696 (quoting Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-70) (internal quotation marks omitted). However, the conduct at issue here is Fernandez-Vargas' re-entry, which occurred before the statute's enactment.

Other circuits have considered similar issues. For example, in Arevalo v. Ashcroft, 344 F.3d 1 (1st Cir. 2003), the court discussed the implications of retroactivity in the context of immigration law, emphasizing the importance of clear congressional intent. Similarly, in Ixcot v. Holder, 646 F.3d 1202 (9th Cir. 2011), the Ninth Circuit examined the retroactive application of immigration statutes, highlighting the need to consider the potential for disruption or unfairness.

In conclusion, the reinstatement statute should not be applied to Fernandez-Vargas because it would be impermissibly retroactive. The statute's language and the relevant case law, including Landgraf and St. Cyr, support this argument. The Tenth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and Fernandez-Vargas' petition for review should be granted.
