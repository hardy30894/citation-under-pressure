**Argument**

I. **Section 1333(b) does not extend Longshore Act coverage to land-based injuries untethered to the outer Continental Shelf itself.**

The statute extends benefits only for disability or death “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That language is best read to require a geographically anchored injury connected to shelf operations, not a free-ranging causation test divorced from the Shelf’s situs. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act was enacted as a property-and-sovereignty measure, directed at the submerged lands beyond state jurisdiction. *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 27–39 (1947); *United States v. Texas*, 339 U.S. 707, 716–17 (1950). Congress legislated against that jurisdictional backdrop, and nothing in the statute suggests it meant to export shelf coverage to any landward accident merely because the employee’s work had some relationship to offshore production.

The Supreme Court’s pre-1970 decisions construing federal maritime compensation statutes confirm that coverage ordinarily turns on the place of injury, not simply on the place where employment happens to be centered. In *Victory Carriers, Inc. v. Law*, 404 U.S. 202 (1972), the Court later emphasized that traditional maritime remedies were historically tied to location. But well before 1970, the Court had already treated admiralty coverage as territorially limited, not all-purpose. *The Plymouth*, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 20, 35–36 (1865), held that maritime jurisdiction depended on the locality of the wrong. Likewise, *London Guarantee & Accident Co. v. Industrial Accident Commission*, 279 U.S. 109, 120–22 (1929), recognized that the federal scheme did not reach land-based injuries merely because they were connected to maritime work.

Here, Mr. Valladolid was killed on the grounds of an onshore oil-processing facility, after his work had been ferried ashore, trucked inland, and deposited for later sorting. That is not an injury occurring on the Outer Continental Shelf, nor an injury on property that Congress placed within the federal maritime compensation system. To read § 1333(b) otherwise would collapse the distinction between offshore operations and land-based industrial accidents.

II. **The statutory phrase “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” does not erase the geographic limit imposed by Congress.**

Petitioner relies on causation, but causation alone is not the statutory standard. Congress did not say “arising out of employment related to shelf operations.” It said “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The repeated reference to “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” shows that Congress was identifying a place-based category of work, not creating a universal compensatory regime for every downstream consequence of offshore production.

The Court’s pre-1970 cases construing federal compensation statutes counsel against that expansive reading. In *Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc.*, 314 U.S. 244, 246–47 (1941), the Court construed the Longshore Act by reference to its maritime situs and maritime character. In *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565, 568–69 (1944), the Court reiterated that coverage depended on the statutory locality. And in *Calbeck v. Travelers Insurance Co.*, 370 U.S. 114, 124–27 (1962), the Court explained that Congress had dealt with the problem of uncertain coverage by extending the Longshore Act to specified waters, not to all injuries with a maritime connection. Those decisions demonstrate that when Congress wants to expand compensation coverage, it does so by defining a situs or locality. It did not do so here for onshore injuries.

III. **The 1970 amendment history cannot be used to rewrite the statute beyond its text.**

Even if legislative purpose is consulted, pre-1970 authorities warn against stretching statutory coverage by policy preference. In *Crawford v. Shaw, Kemp, & Co.*, 270 U.S. 431, 436–37 (1926), the Court held that compensation statutes must be applied according to the coverage Congress actually enacted. And in *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 52–54 (1932), the Court recognized that federal compensation schemes operate within defined jurisdictional limits. Those limits matter here. Congress could have extended benefits to any injury causally related to shelf operations, wherever occurring. It did not.

Respondent’s position therefore preserves the text Congress chose, the territorial structure of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, and the settled pre-1970 principle that federal compensation coverage is not enlarged by judicial invention. Mr. Valladolid’s fatal accident occurred on land, at an onshore processing facility, and outside the class of injuries Congress placed within § 1333(b). The judgment denying OCSLA benefits should be affirmed.
