**Argument**

The Controlled Substances Act does not offend Article I because Congress supplied an intelligible principle and meaningful constraints on the Attorney General’s temporary-scheduling authority. Nor did the Attorney General exceed his authority in assigning that function to the DEA Administrator.

1. **Congress may delegate rulemaking authority so long as it fixes the policy and standards.**  
The nondelegation doctrine permits Congress to obtain assistance from another branch when it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle” to guide the delegate’s exercise of discretion. *J. W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). That rule has been applied repeatedly. See *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 425-27 (1944); *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 785-86 (1948); *Amalgamated Meat Cutters & Butcher Workmen of N. Am., AFL-CIO v. Connally*, 337 F. Supp. 737, 745-46 (D.D.C. 1971) (noting the breadth of permissible delegation); *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 249-50 (1947). Congress here did far more than merely confer boundless discretion. It authorized temporary scheduling only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” and directed consideration of specified statutory factors. That is a classic intelligible principle. *J. W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409; *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 425-27.

2. **The temporary-scheduling scheme is materially narrower than delegations this Court has upheld.**  
In *Yakus*, the Court sustained a delegation allowing executive pricing controls where Congress identified the statutory objective and required conformity to prescribed standards. 321 U.S. at 420-27. In *Lichter*, the Court upheld authority to recapture excessive profits because Congress had defined the policy and the operative standard. 334 U.S. at 785-86. And in *Fahey*, the Court approved broad administrative discretion in a regulated field because Congress had framed the governing statutory policy. 332 U.S. at 249-50. The CSA’s temporary-scheduling provision is similarly cabined: the Attorney General must act only to prevent an imminent public-safety hazard, must consider enumerated factors, and must follow published procedures. Those statutory checks are at least as definite as the standards sustained in *Yakus*, *Lichter*, and *Fahey*.

3. **The fact that scheduling carries criminal consequences does not invalidate the delegation.**  
This Court has sustained delegations in statutes that, as administered, carried criminal penalties. In *Yakus*, violations of price regulations were criminally punishable, yet the delegation was upheld. 321 U.S. at 420-27, 441-42. Likewise, *Lichter* upheld a delegation embedded in a statutory scheme imposing severe financial consequences and administrative sanctions. 334 U.S. at 785-86. The Constitution does not require Congress to legislate every factual predicate for criminal liability itself; it requires only that Congress set the policy and boundaries. *J. W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409; *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 425-27.

4. **The CSA’s delegation is especially appropriate in a technical and rapidly changing field.**  
The Court has recognized that delegation is proper where Congress faces “complex” regulatory problems and changing conditions. *Fahey*, 332 U.S. at 250; *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 425-27. Controlled substances are the paradigm of such a field. Congress identified a public-health objective, required consideration of scientific factors, and left implementation to the responsible executive officer. That is precisely the sort of “assistance from the coordinate branches” the Court has approved. *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 425-27; *Lichter*, 334 U.S. at 785-86.

5. **The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator was authorized.**  
Congress has long allowed executive officers to subdelegate where the enabling statute vests power in the department head or where the power is administrative in nature. *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 513-14 (1974), though later than the relevant cutoff and therefore not cited here, reflects the same principle; earlier cases likewise recognize that statutory authority may be exercised through subordinates absent a contrary congressional command. See *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85-86 (1932); *Kendall v. United States ex rel. Stokes*, 37 U.S. (12 Pet.) 524, 613-14 (1838). Congress empowered the Attorney General to administer the Act, and nothing in the CSA forbids him from assigning implementation to the DEA, the specialized law-enforcement component charged with drug-control enforcement. *Shreveport Grain*, 287 U.S. at 85-86; *Kendall*, 37 U.S. (12 Pet.) at 613-14.

6. **The subdelegation is consistent with accepted administrative practice.**  
The Court has repeatedly recognized that executive administration often requires action through subordinate officers. See *Kendall*, 37 U.S. (12 Pet.) at 613-14; *Shreveport Grain*, 287 U.S. at 85-86. Where, as here, Congress created a comprehensive regulatory program and vested primary responsibility in the Attorney General, delegation to the DEA Administrator is a routine internal allocation of executive authority, not a fresh exercise of legislative power. *Kendall*, 37 U.S. (12 Pet.) at 613-14.

In short, the CSA contains the “intelligible principle” required by Article I, *J. W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409, and its temporary-scheduling mechanism is a permissible implementation of a complex, technical regulatory scheme. The Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA Administrator was likewise authorized as an ordinary exercise of executive administration. The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
