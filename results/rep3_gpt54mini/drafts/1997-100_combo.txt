**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause forbids a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal, and it applies to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 794-96 (1969); *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That protection is not confined to the guilt phase. When a sentencing proceeding “has the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence,” the Clause bars a second attempt after the prosecution has failed to prove the necessary facts. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896); *United States v. Evans*, 213 U.S. 297, 302-03 (1909).

Here, California chose to litigate the truth of a prior-conviction allegation in a bifurcated proceeding, with proof required beyond a reasonable doubt. Having elected a trial-like process, the State may not get a second bite at the apple after failing to carry its burden. The relevant precedents treat such factfinding as the kind of adjudication that, once resolved for the defendant, cannot be reopened. *Sealfon v. United States*, 332 U.S. 575, 579-80 (1948); *Coffey v. United States*, 116 U.S. 436, 442-43 (1886).

The historical core of double jeopardy is the finality of an adverse determination in a criminal case. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 173-74 (1873), held that once punishment has been imposed and satisfied, the court may not increase it. *In re Bradley*, 318 U.S. 50, 52-53 (1943), reaffirmed that principle. And where the government has already had a full opportunity to prove its case, a second proceeding to supply missing proof is exactly what the Clause forbids. *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 128-29 (1904); *United States v. Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. 85, 87-88 (1916).

California relies on the label “sentencing,” but labels do not control. This Court has repeatedly looked to substance, not form. In *Coffey*, 116 U.S. at 443, the Court treated a forfeiture proceeding as barred because it was effectively a second attempt to litigate the same factual issue. In *Sealfon*, 332 U.S. at 578-80, the Court likewise held that a prior acquittal precluded relitigation of facts essential to a later prosecution. Those cases reflect the settled rule that the government cannot relitigate a factual determination once the defendant has prevailed.

That rule is especially strong where the proceeding is adversarial, structured, and outcome-determinative. The prior-conviction allegation here was not a discretionary background inquiry; it was a distinct factual issue that increased Monge’s punishment. California required formal proof, a contested hearing, and a judicial finding of truth. That is materially different from the broad discretionary sentencing considered in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246-51 (1949). *Williams* approved reliance on out-of-court information at sentencing, but it did not authorize repeated litigation of a discrete aggravating fact after the prosecution failed to prove it.

Nor does *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 17-18 (1919), help the State. *Stroud* involved a new jury verdict after a reversed conviction, not a second opportunity to prove a sentencing enhancement after evidentiary insufficiency. And *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. at 173-74, confirms that once the lawful proceeding has reached its end, the Constitution forbids a renewed imposition of greater punishment.

The same principle applies here with even greater force because the allegation is “prior conviction” based and the State had every opportunity to marshal documentary proof at the first hearing. A retrial would not correct a mere procedural defect; it would permit the prosecution to fill a substantive evidentiary gap. Double jeopardy does not allow the government to retry a defendant until it finally proves what it failed to prove the first time. *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 128-29; *Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. at 87-88; *Sealfon*, 332 U.S. at 579-80.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed. When California elected to make the prior-conviction allegation a formal, trial-like issue, the Double Jeopardy Clause attached to that adjudication. After the State failed to prove the allegation, it was constitutionally barred from trying again. *Benton*, 395 U.S. at 794-96; *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717.
