**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford Motor Company’s extensive in-state activities—advertising, selling parts, and servicing vehicles—satisfy that standard, even though the specific vehicle at issue was not originally sold in Montana. Ford’s contacts are not random; they represent purposeful availment of the Montana market, and the claims directly relate to those contacts.

**I. Ford’s Continuous Corporate Activities in Montana Satisfy the Minimum Contacts Test**

A state may subject a foreign corporation to personal jurisdiction when the corporation’s activities in the forum are “continuous and systematic” and the suit does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *Perkins v. Benguet Consol. Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437, 445 (1952) (quoting *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945)). In *Perkins*, this Court held that a corporation’s “continuous corporate activities” within the forum—even when the cause of action did not arise from those activities—supported jurisdiction. *Id.* at 447–48. Here, Ford’s Montana operations are far more extensive: it maintains dealerships, advertises nationally and locally, sells replacement parts, and provides warranty services. These contacts are not isolated; they constitute a deliberate effort to serve the Montana market.

**II. A Corporation That Does Business in a State Consents to Suit There**

This Court has long recognized that a corporation “desirous of doing business in a State other than that in which they have their domicil, are generally required to have an agent therein to receive service of process for them.” *Provident Sav. Life Assurance Soc’y v. Ford*, 114 U.S. 635, 641 (1885). By choosing to sell parts and advertise in Montana, Ford has accepted the privilege of doing business there and, with it, the obligation to defend suits arising from its products. *See also Western Loan & Sav. Co. v. Butte & Boston Consol. Mining Co.*, 210 U.S. 368, 373 (1908) (defendant waives personal privilege by appearing on the merits). Ford cannot enjoy the benefits of the Montana market while avoiding the burdens of litigation there.

**III. The Claims “Relate To” Ford’s Montana Contacts**

The “arise out of or relate to” standard does not require a strict causal link between the defendant’s forum contacts and the plaintiff’s injury. Rather, it is satisfied when the contacts form a “connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” Here, Ford’s nationwide distribution system—including its Montana operations—is the very mechanism that placed the defective Explorer in the state. The vehicle was designed, marketed, and sold as part of a national enterprise that Ford deliberately extended into Montana. The claim thus “relates to” Ford’s in-state activities because those activities are part of the same stream of commerce that brought the vehicle to the forum.

**IV. Precedent Confirms That a State May Exercise Jurisdiction Over Nonresident Defendants Whose Products Cause Injury Within the State**

This Court has consistently upheld state statutes that subject nonresident motorists to jurisdiction for accidents occurring within the state. *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352, 356 (1927) (upholding Massachusetts statute); *Olberding v. Illinois Cent. R.R.*, 346 U.S. 338, 341 (1953) (“No question is or can now be raised against the constitutionality of the Kentucky statute to secure the presence of an out-of-state motorist in the state courts to respond to damages.”). The same principle applies to manufacturers: a state may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident whose product causes injury in the forum, even if the product was not originally sold there. *See Minnesota Commercial Men’s Ass’n v. Benn*, 261 U.S. 140, 144 (1923) (examining whether the defendant had “done business” in the forum to support jurisdiction). Ford’s extensive Montana contacts far exceed the single act of driving a car through a state that supported jurisdiction in *Hess* and *Olberding*.

**V. Ford’s Argument Would Undermine Longstanding Principles of Fairness**

Ford contends that jurisdiction is improper because none of its Montana contacts caused the accident. But that argument ignores the relational nature of specific jurisdiction. The relevant inquiry is whether the defendant’s contacts with the forum are such that it is “reasonable and just” to require it to defend there. *Perkins*, 342 U.S. at 445. Ford’s nationwide marketing and sales create a foreseeable risk that its vehicles will be driven in Montana. Having profited from the Montana market, Ford cannot now claim surprise at being sued there. *See Clark v. Wells*, 203 U.S. 164, 170 (1906) (attachment jurisdiction proper where property is seized within the state); *Metzger Motor Car Co. v. Parrott*, 233 U.S. 36, 40 (1914) (upholding owner liability statute for negligent operation of vehicle). The Due Process Clause does not require a direct causal chain; it requires a “relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation.” That relationship exists here.

**CONCLUSION**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford’s purposeful contacts with Montana—advertising, selling parts, and servicing vehicles—establish specific personal jurisdiction. The judgment should be affirmed.
