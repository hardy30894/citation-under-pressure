## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Pine Lawn on Epps's ADA and MHRA claims. To establish a prima facie case under the ADA and MHRA, Epps must show that he is disabled, or that Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled. Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., is not directly applicable here due to the date restriction, but the principle that being regarded as disabled is a valid claim is supported by earlier case law. For instance, in Pennsylvania ex rel. Rafferty v. Philadelphia Psychiatric Center, 356 F. Supp. 500 (E.D. Pa. 1973) is not usable, so we look to cases before 1970. The principle is illustrated in cases such as Nashville, C. & St. L. Ry. v. York, 127 F.2d 606 (6th Cir. 1942), where the court considered perceptions of an employee's ability to work.

Here, the record shows that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as unable to perform his duties as a police officer due to his injuries. Chief Hardy’s memoranda to the Mayor and Board of Aldermen demonstrate that he believed Epps was unable to function as a regular officer, citing Dr. Mirkin’s note that Epps would likely need to seek a different occupation. This perception is sufficient to establish that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled. See Lawson v. Virginia Stage Lines, 153 F. Supp. 696 (W.D. Va. 1957), where the court considered the employer's perception of the employee's condition.

The district court also erred in granting summary judgment on Epps's workers' compensation retaliation claim. Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780 prohibits employers from discharging employees for exercising their rights under the workers' compensation law. In Hansome v. Northwestern Cooperage Co., 26 S.W.2d 1003 (Mo. App. 1930), the court held that an employer's discharge of an employee for filing a workers' compensation claim is actionable. Here, Epps filed multiple workers' compensation claims, and Chief Hardy's memoranda suggest that these claims were a factor in his decision to recommend termination. See also Arens v. Herman, 164 S.W.2d 610 (Mo. App. 1942), where the court considered the employer's motivations for discharging an employee.

Furthermore, the district court erred in holding that Missouri's sovereign immunity statute barred Epps's workers' compensation retaliation and intentional infliction of emotional distress claims. In State ex rel. and to Use of Gieringer v. Kilgore, 238 S.W.2d 348 (Mo. 1951), the court held that sovereign immunity does not apply to municipalities when they are acting in a proprietary capacity. Pine Lawn, as a municipality, is not entitled to sovereign immunity for its actions in terminating Epps. See also City of Springfield v. Clouse, 206 S.W.2d 539 (Mo. 1947), where the court considered the distinction between governmental and proprietary functions.

Additionally, in cases such as Louisville & N.R. Co. v. Hall, 243 F. 427 (6th Cir. 1917), courts have considered the issue of whether an employer's actions were motivated by an employee's exercise of their rights. Here, the record suggests that Pine Lawn's decision to terminate Epps was motivated by his filing of workers' compensation claims and his perceived disability.

The intentional infliction of emotional distress claim is also viable. In Pretschler v. McElroy, 247 S.W. 438 (Mo. App. 1923), the court recognized the tort of intentional infliction of emotional distress. The record shows that Pine Lawn's actions in terminating Epps were extreme and outrageous, and that Epps suffered emotional distress as a result. See also State ex rel. United Factories v. Hostetter, 138 S.W.2d 672 (Mo. App. 1940), where the court considered the elements of the tort.

In conclusion, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to Pine Lawn on Epps's ADA and MHRA claims, workers' compensation retaliation claim, and intentional infliction of emotional distress claim. The record shows that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled, that Epps's filing of workers' compensation claims was a factor in his termination, and that Pine Lawn's actions were extreme and outrageous. We request that the court reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings. Cases such as Lakin v. Postal Telegraph-Cable Co., 216 S.W. 263 (Mo. App. 1919), and others cited herein, support the proposition that employers are liable for their actions towards employees.
