**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens, Like Petitioner, Who Re-entered Before IIRIRA's Effective Date**

Congress is presumed not to intend statutes to operate retroactively unless it says so with unmistakable clarity. As this Court explained in *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), "the presumption against retroactive legislation is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence," *id.* at 265, and a court applying a new provision to preexisting conduct must ask "whether the new provision attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." *Id.* at 270. That presumption controls here. Nothing in the text of § 241(a)(5) states that Congress intended it to reach aliens whose illegal re-entry predated IIRIRA's 1997 effective date. Where, as here, "the statute contains no such express command," *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280, courts must determine whether applying the new rule "would impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed." *Id.* at 278.

Two courts of appeals have already applied this principle to § 241(a)(5) itself and concluded that it does not reach pre-enactment re-entries. In *Castro-Cortez v. INS*, 239 F.3d 1037 (9th Cir. 2001), the Ninth Circuit held that the reinstatement statute could not be applied to aliens who re-entered before IIRIRA because doing so would attach new and harsher consequences—elimination of all relief from removal—to conduct that occurred under a different legal regime. *Id.* at 1044-45. The Sixth Circuit reached the identical conclusion in *Bejjani v. INS*, 271 F.3d 670 (6th Cir. 2001), holding that "Congress did not clearly express its intent that § 241(a)(5) apply retroactively" to re-entries predating the statute. *Id.* at 687-89. These decisions correctly apply *Landgraf*'s command that ambiguity be resolved against retroactive operation.

That conclusion is reinforced by the settled canon that immigration statutes imposing harsh consequences on aliens are construed narrowly. As this Court held in *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6 (1948), "we resolve the doubts in favor of that construction because deportation is a drastic measure." *Id.* at 10. Section 241(a)(5)'s elimination of any possibility of relief—including adjustment of status based on marriage to a United States citizen—is exactly the kind of severe consequence that *Fong Haw Tan* instructs courts to avoid imposing absent clear congressional direction. No such direction exists here.

**II. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Petitioner's Pre-1997 Re-entry Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

Even if the statutory text were ambiguous as to temporal reach, applying § 241(a)(5) to Mr. Fernandez-Vargas would violate the retroactivity principles this Court articulated in *Landgraf* and *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001). In *St. Cyr*, the Court held that a statute eliminating discretionary relief from deportation could not be applied to aliens who had relied on the availability of that relief when they pleaded guilty to removable offenses, because doing so would "attach[] a new disability, in respect to transactions or considerations already past." *Id.* at 321 (quoting *Society for Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler*, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (No. 13,156) (CC NH 1814) (Story, J.)). The Court emphasized that "the potential for unfairness in the retroactive application of [the new provision] to people who entered into plea agreements ... is significant," *id.* at 323, and that Congress's failure to speak clearly counseled against retroactive effect.

The same reasoning governs here with even greater force. Mr. Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States in 1982 and lived here continuously for nineteen years, married a United States citizen, and applied for adjustment of status—all in reliance on an immigration regime that did not categorically bar such relief for aliens with prior deportation orders. Applying § 241(a)(5) retroactively strips him of relief he could have sought under the law as it existed when he re-entered and throughout most of his residence here. That is precisely the kind of "new disability" attached to "completed" conduct that *Landgraf* and *St. Cyr* forbid absent an unambiguous statutory command. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70; *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321.

This Court's decision in *Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343 (1999), confirms that retroactivity analysis focuses on whether the statute "would impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party's liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed." *Id.* at 358 (quoting *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280). Petitioner's continued residence, marriage, and pursuit of adjustment of status were transactions completed—or substantially undertaken—before § 241(a)(5)'s enactment. Retroactive application increases the legal consequences of his 1982 re-entry decades after the fact, foreclosing relief that was legally available when he acted.

Finally, the presumption against retroactivity applies with particular force given the statutory canon recognized in *Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital*, 488 U.S. 204 (1988), that "congressional enactments ... will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result." *Id.* at 208. Section 241(a)(5) contains no such language. Because Congress did not clearly command retroactive application, and because such application would impair rights and reliance interests petitioner possessed under prior law, the Tenth Circuit's judgment should be reversed.
