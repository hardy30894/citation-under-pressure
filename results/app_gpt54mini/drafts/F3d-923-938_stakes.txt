## ARGUMENT

### I. The Tax Court Applied an Overly Formalistic Standard; the Record Shows Bona Fide Debt Running Directly to Meruelo.

Section 1366 permits an S corporation shareholder to deduct losses to the extent of the shareholder’s basis in stock and in “any indebtedness of the S corporation to the shareholder.” 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1)(B). The Treasury regulation governing that provision requires “bona fide indebtedness of the S corporation that runs directly to the shareholder,” and it further provides that indebtedness need not arise from a formal note so long as the shareholder can show a genuine debtor-creditor relationship. Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The Eleventh Circuit has recognized that Subchapter S is designed as a pass-through regime, with items of income and loss attributed to shareholders, and that the basis limitation governs only the extent to which those losses may be absorbed. Bufferd v. Commissioner, 506 U.S. 523, 525 (1993).

Here, the Tax Court erred by refusing to treat the net intercompany advances as debt to Meruelo, even though the contemporaneous books, annual netting, and tax reporting all reflected that the affiliate transfers were being treated as shareholder-level financing of Merco. Meruelo did not merely stand at the edge of a chain of entity-to-entity transfers. He controlled the flow of funds through entities in which he held substantial interests, he caused the advances to be made to Merco, and his accountant consistently netted those advances and allocated the resulting indebtedness to him. That is the hallmark of indebtedness running directly to the shareholder: the shareholder is the economic source of the funds and the corporation is the ultimate obligor for the benefit of the shareholder’s investment.

The Tax Court’s contrary conclusion cannot be squared with the substance-over-form principles that govern tax law. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that tax consequences depend on the actual economic relationships, not merely labels. See Commissioner v. Court Holding Co., 324 U.S. 331, 334 (1945); Gregory v. Helvering, 293 U.S. 465, 469-70 (1935). And in the basis context, the inquiry focuses on whether the taxpayer made an actual economic outlay that left him poorer in a meaningful sense. Under that standard, Meruelo’s position is substantially stronger than the Tax Court acknowledged: funds were advanced through entities he owned, recorded as receivables and payables, netted annually, and ultimately treated on the returns as shareholder debt. Those facts demonstrate more than bookkeeping convenience; they show a genuine financing arrangement.

### II. The Tax Court’s Reliance on the Interposed Entities Conflicts with the Economic-Substance of the Transactions.

The Tax Court treated the fact that the advances traveled through affiliates as dispositive. But courts have long rejected formal interposition where the shareholder is the true source of the capital and the transaction is intended to function as shareholder financing. In Selfe v. United States, 778 F.2d 769, 772-74 (11th Cir. 1985), this Court held that a taxpayer may establish basis where a lender’s advance to the corporation is, in substance, a loan to the shareholder who in turn finances the corporation. The key question is whether the shareholder “incurred a real economic detriment” and whether the corporation’s obligation is, in substance, to the shareholder. Id. at 774.

That is exactly what the record establishes here. Meruelo personally arranged the financing structure; his accountant annually translated the intercompany balances into a shareholder loan; and Merco’s books consistently reflected net accounts payable attributable to the related-party transfers. The Commissioner’s focus on the entities as independent obligors ignores the practical reality that Meruelo bore the economic burden of the advances. The Tax Court’s rule would elevate form over substance and penalize taxpayers who use multiple entities for legitimate business reasons, even when the economics are indistinguishable from a direct shareholder loan.

### III. Meruelo Satisfied the “Actual Economic Outlay” Requirement.

This Court has described the basis inquiry as requiring an “actual economic outlay” that leaves the taxpayer “poorer in a material sense.” Under that standard, basis exists where the shareholder has, in substance, put his money at risk for the corporation’s benefit. See Selfe, 778 F.2d at 772-74. Meruelo did so. He personally borrowed funds to support Merco’s acquisition, caused those funds to be routed through an entity he owned, and sustained a net $9 million-plus balance of advances for Merco’s benefit over the relevant years. The fact that the advance structure was implemented through related entities does not erase the economic outlay; it merely describes the conduit through which Meruelo financed Merco.

The Commissioner may argue that the debt formally ran between Merco and the affiliates, not Meruelo. But this Court has already recognized that formal labels do not control where the taxpayer is the true economic actor and the corporate advance is, in substance, shareholder debt. Selfe, 778 F.2d at 774. The Tax Court’s contrary approach effectively creates a categorical bar to basis through related entities, a rule neither the statute nor the regulation imposes.

Because Meruelo established bona fide indebtedness running directly to him, or at minimum raised facts sufficient to satisfy the actual economic outlay standard, the disallowance of the loss deduction should be reversed.
