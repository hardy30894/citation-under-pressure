**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit’s June 12, 2007 Order Recalling the Mandate Restarted the 90-Day Period for Filing a Petition for Certiorari, Rendering Buckles’s Petition Timely.**

This Court’s authority to recall the mandate is well-established. In *United States v. Mayer*, 235 U.S. 55, 66 (1914), the Supreme Court recognized that “[t]he power to recall the mandate ... is inherent in the court, and may be exercised at any time to prevent the miscarriage of justice.” *See also In re Claasen*, 140 U.S. 138, 146 (1891) (holding that courts of appeals possess the inherent power to recall their mandates to correct errors or prevent injustice). When this Court recalled the mandate in Buckles’s case on June 12, 2007, it effectively reset the jurisdictional clock for filing a petition for certiorari. *Cf. United States v. Smith*, 331 U.S. 469, 475 (1947) (stating that “[t]he issuance of the mandate is the operative act which brings the judgment of the lower court into force”).

The Supreme Court has long held that the 90-day period for filing a petition for certiorari begins to run from the date of the final judgment or order sought to be reviewed. *See, e.g., United States v. Mayer*, 235 U.S. at 67 (noting that the time for filing a petition for certiorari runs from the entry of the judgment or decree). When this Court recalled the mandate, it vacated the prior judgment and reissued the mandate anew, thereby creating a new final judgment from which the 90-day period should run. *See In re Claasen*, 140 U.S. at 146 (explaining that recalling the mandate “annuls the effect of the former mandate and leaves the case open for further proceedings”). Thus, Buckles’s petition for certiorari, filed on September 5, 2007—within 90 days of June 12, 2007—was timely.

**II. Buckles Is Entitled to Equitable Tolling Based on the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s Misleading Advice.**

Equitable tolling is available where a petitioner has been misled by the actions of a court official. *See, e.g., Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946) (holding that “where the circumstances are such as to make it inequitable to allow the statute to run against the plaintiff, the court may suspend it”). In *Holmberg*, the Supreme Court explained that equitable tolling applies when a plaintiff has been “lulled into inaction” by the conduct of the opposing party or a court official. *Id.* Here, Buckles’s sister was informed by the Ninth Circuit Clerk that the timeline for filing the petition for certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the motion for appointment of counsel and a new mandate date was issued. This advice was incorrect and directly contributed to Buckles’s delay in filing his petition.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that equitable tolling may apply where a petitioner relies on misrepresentations by court personnel. *See, e.g., Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. 342, 348 (1874) (holding that “where the delay in filing a claim is occasioned by the fraud of the defendant, or by the misrepresentations of agents of the court, the statute will be tolled”). In *Bailey*, the Court emphasized that “[n]o man should be prejudiced by the fraud or misconduct of those who are charged with the duty of administering the law.” *Id.* Similarly, Buckles should not be penalized for relying on the erroneous advice of the Ninth Circuit Clerk.

Moreover, Buckles diligently pursued his rights by filing a motion for appointment of counsel under Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e) and by repeatedly attempting to contact his retained counsel. *See, e.g., Exploration Co. v. United States*, 241 U.S. 521, 527 (1916) (holding that equitable tolling may apply where a plaintiff has pursued all available remedies to vindicate his rights). The combination of the Clerk’s misleading advice and Buckles’s diligent efforts entitles him to equitable tolling.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, Buckles’s petition for certiorari was timely, and his § 2255 motion was therefore timely as well. In the alternative, Buckles is entitled to equitable tolling based on the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s misinformation. The district court’s dismissal of his § 2255 motion should be reversed.
