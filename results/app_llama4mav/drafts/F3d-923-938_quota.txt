## Argument

The Tax Court erred in disallowing Meruelo's claimed deduction for his share of Merco's losses. The court's ruling that Meruelo failed to establish a bona fide indebtedness of $9 million running directly to him is contrary to established law.

Under Subchapter S of the Internal Revenue Code, a shareholder's ability to deduct his proportionate share of a corporation's net operating losses is limited by the sum of his basis in his stock and the corporate indebtedness to him. 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1). To establish basis in corporate indebtedness, a shareholder must demonstrate that the corporation owes a "bona fide indebtedness" to him. Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i).

The key issue here is whether the monetary transfers between Merco and its affiliates, which were later reclassified as loans from Meruelo to Merco, established a bona fide indebtedness running directly to Meruelo. The Tax Court held that they did not, but this ruling is inconsistent with the principles established in relevant case law.

In Underwood v. Commissioner, 535 F.2d 309 (5th Cir. 1976), the court held that a shareholder's basis in an S corporation's indebtedness can be established through a series of transactions involving related entities. Similarly, in Yates v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 2001-280, the Tax Court recognized that a shareholder can establish basis in an S corporation's indebtedness through a "constructive loan" arrangement involving related entities.

The facts of this case demonstrate that Meruelo made an "actual economic outlay" toward the debt, as required by the regulation. Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). Meruelo obtained a personal loan to fund his share of the deposit for the condominium complex, and he transferred the proceeds to Akoya, which in turn transferred the funds to Merco. This transaction established a bona fide indebtedness running directly to Meruelo, as the court held in Selfe v. United States, 778 F.2d 769 (11th Cir. 1985).

Furthermore, the Tax Court's ruling is inconsistent with the principles established in cases such as Estate of Leavitt v. Commissioner, 875 F.2d 420 (4th Cir. 1989), and Gilday v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 1982-242. In these cases, the courts recognized that a shareholder can establish basis in an S corporation's indebtedness through a variety of arrangements, including loans made through related entities.

The Commissioner's argument that the debt ran from Merco to the other entities, rather than to Meruelo, is unpersuasive. As the court held in Culnen v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 2000-139, the substance of the transaction, rather than its form, determines whether a bona fide indebtedness exists. In this case, the substance of the transactions between Merco and its affiliates was to establish a bona fide indebtedness running directly to Meruelo.

The Tax Court's reliance on cases such as O'Halloran v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 2000-298, is misplaced. In O'Halloran, the court held that a shareholder failed to establish basis in an S corporation's indebtedness because the loan was not made directly to the shareholder. In contrast, Meruelo made an actual economic outlay toward the debt through his personal loan and transfer of funds to Akoya.

In summary, the Tax Court erred in disallowing Meruelo's claimed deduction for his share of Merco's losses. The court's ruling is inconsistent with established law, including Underwood v. Commissioner, 535 F.2d 309 (5th Cir. 1976), Selfe v. United States, 778 F.2d 769 (11th Cir. 1985), and Estate of Leavitt v. Commissioner, 875 F.2d 420 (4th Cir. 1989). Meruelo established a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him through the monetary transfers between Merco and its affiliates, and he made an actual economic outlay toward the debt. Therefore, the court should reverse the Tax Court's ruling and allow Meruelo's claimed deduction.
