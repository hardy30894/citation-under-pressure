The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from an insanity defense. The Arizona Supreme Court correctly held that Arizona law permits expert testimony on mental disease or defect to be considered only on the issue of insanity, not on the issue of mens rea.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that states have considerable latitude in defining the elements of crimes and the defenses thereto. See Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 201-02 (1977); see also Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 535-36 (1968) (plurality opinion). In Patterson, the Court upheld a New York statute that placed the burden of proving extreme emotional disturbance on the defendant, even though the prosecution was required to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt. 432 U.S. at 205-06. Similarly, Arizona's law limiting the use of expert testimony on mental disease or defect to the insanity defense does not relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove mens rea. See State v. Mott, 931 P.2d 1046, 1051 (Ariz. 1997).

The Court has also recognized that the Constitution does not require a state to adopt a particular insanity test or defense. See Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 437, 449-50 (1992); see also Clark v. Arizona, 548 U.S. 735, 752 (2006) (noting that "the insanity rule... represents a moral judgment"). Arizona's insanity defense, which requires a defendant to prove by clear and convincing evidence that he did not know the wrongfulness of his actions, is a reasonable formulation of the defense. See Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-502(A); see also State v. Schantz, 403 P.2d 521, 524 (Ariz. 1965).

Furthermore, the Court has held that a state's rule limiting the admissibility of certain evidence does not necessarily violate due process. See Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37, 42-43 (1996) (plurality opinion). In Egelhoff, the Court upheld a Montana statute that prohibited defendants from introducing evidence of voluntary intoxication to negate mens rea. Id. at 56. Similarly, Arizona's rule limiting the use of expert testimony on mental disease or defect to the insanity defense is a reasonable evidentiary rule that does not infringe on the defendant's due process rights.

The lower courts have also recognized that a state's rule limiting the use of expert testimony on mental disease or defect to the insanity defense is constitutional. See, e.g., Finger v. State, 27 P.3d 66, 84-85 (Nev. 2001); see also State v. Korell, 690 P.2d 992, 999-1000 (Mont. 1984). These cases demonstrate that Arizona's law is not an outlier and is consistent with the majority of jurisdictions.

In this case, the trial court correctly applied Arizona law by limiting the use of expert testimony on Clark's mental state to his insanity defense. The prosecution was still required to prove mens rea beyond a reasonable doubt, and Clark was free to present other evidence to rebut the prosecution's case. See State v. Schantz, 403 P.2d at 524. The Arizona Supreme Court correctly upheld Clark's conviction, and this Court should affirm that decision.

In conclusion, the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from an insanity defense. Arizona's law limiting the use of such evidence to the insanity defense is a reasonable formulation of the defense and does not infringe on the defendant's due process rights. This Court should affirm the Arizona Supreme Court's decision upholding Clark's conviction.
