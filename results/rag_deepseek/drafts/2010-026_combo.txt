**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by expanding Exemption 2 of the Freedom of Information Act beyond its statutory text and this Court’s settled precedent. The court held that documents relating to the effects of explosions are exempt from disclosure because they are “predominantly” for internal use and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation. That holding cannot be squared with the plain language of 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2), which exempts only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” The court’s “predominantly internal” standard reads the word “solely” out of the statute and contravenes this Court’s instruction that FOIA exemptions “are to be interpreted narrowly.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976).

**I. The Statutory Text and This Court’s Precedent Foreclose the Ninth Circuit’s Expansive Reading of Exemption 2**

FOIA reflects “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 360-61. The nine exemptions are “explicitly exclusive.” *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989). Exemption 2 is narrow: it covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The word “solely” is dispositive. It limits the exemption to matters that are exclusively about internal personnel rules and practices—not matters that are merely “predominantly” internal.

This Court has long recognized that statutory language must be given its ordinary meaning. *United States v. Alger*, 152 U.S. 384, 394 (1894) (holding that when “the meaning of the statute, as applied to these cases, appears to this court to be perfectly clear, no practice inconsistent with that meaning can have any effect”). The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard is inconsistent with the clear meaning of “solely.” A document that is “predominantly” internal is not “solely” internal. The court’s standard thus rewrites the statute.

In *Rose*, this Court considered Exemption 2 and held that it covers only “minor employment matters” and “rules and practices” that are “of no genuine and significant public interest.” 425 U.S. at 363, 369. The Court emphasized that the exemption does not apply to matters that “affect the public in a substantial way.” *Id.* at 369. The ESQD arc maps at issue here—which identify the blast ranges of explosive ordnance stored near Puget Sound communities—plainly affect the public in a substantial way. They are not “minor employment matters.” They are safety data of profound public concern.

**II. The Ninth Circuit’s “Predominantly Internal” Standard Has No Basis in This Court’s Precedent**

The Ninth Circuit adopted the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc). But *Crooker* was decided after the events in this case and, more importantly, after this Court’s decision in *Rose*. The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Crooker* is misplaced because *Crooker* itself acknowledged that Exemption 2’s text requires a “solely” internal connection. *Id.* at 1056-57. The D.C. Circuit’s substitution of “predominantly” for “solely” is a judicial amendment that this Court should reject.

This Court has never endorsed a “predominantly internal” standard. To the contrary, in *Rose*, the Court rejected the argument that Exemption 2 could be used to withhold documents that are “related to” internal personnel rules and practices in a broad sense. 425 U.S. at 363-64. The Court instead required a “solely” internal relationship. *Id.* at 364. The Ninth Circuit’s standard is thus inconsistent with *Rose*.

**III. The ESQD Arc Maps Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices”**

Even under a generous reading of Exemption 2, the ESQD arc maps do not qualify. These maps are technical data about the effects of explosions. They are not “rules” or “practices” governing agency personnel. They are not akin to parking regulations, lunch-hour policies, or sick-leave procedures—the “minor employment matters” that Exemption 2 was designed to protect. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 363.

This Court has held that Exemption 2 does not apply to documents that “relate to the public at large.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. The ESQD arc maps relate directly to the safety of the public living near Naval Magazine Indian Island. They are not internal personnel documents; they are external safety data. As the dissent below recognized, the Navy’s own affidavits describe the maps as “a long term planning tool” for designing storage facilities—not as personnel rules or practices. *Milner v. U.S. Dep’t of Navy*, 575 F.3d 959, 970 (9th Cir. 2009) (Kleinfeld, J., dissenting).

**IV. The Risk of Circumvention Does Not Justify Expanding Exemption 2**

The Ninth Circuit held that the ESQD arc maps could be withheld because their disclosure “may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. But *Rose* addressed the risk of circumvention only in the context of materials that are *already* within Exemption 2’s scope. *Id.* at 369-70. The risk of circumvention is not an independent basis for exemption; it is a factor to consider *after* determining that the documents are “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” The Ninth Circuit inverted this analysis, using the risk of circumvention to expand the exemption’s scope.

This Court has repeatedly warned against expanding FOIA exemptions beyond their text. *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151; *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. The risk of circumvention cannot transform safety data into internal personnel rules. If Congress had intended to exempt all documents whose disclosure might enable circumvention of agency regulation, it would have said so. It did not.

**V. The Navy’s National Security Concerns Are Better Addressed Through Other Exemptions**

The Navy argues that disclosure of the ESQD arc maps could threaten national security. But Congress has provided specific exemptions for such concerns. Exemption 1 covers classified documents. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1). Exemption 7(F) covers law enforcement records that “could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(F). The Navy did not classify the ESQD arc maps, and the dissent below concluded that they were not compiled for law enforcement purposes. *Milner*, 575 F.3d at 970-71 (Kleinfeld, J., dissenting). The Navy cannot use Exemption 2 as a catch-all for documents that do not fit within the exemptions Congress actually enacted.

This Court has held that FOIA exemptions “must be narrowly construed.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 undermines that principle and invites agencies to withhold any document that is “predominantly” internal and whose disclosure might “circumvent” agency regulation. That standard is unbounded and inconsistent with FOIA’s purpose of “full agency disclosure.” *Id.* at 360.

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit erred by adopting a “predominantly internal” standard that reads the word “solely” out of Exemption 2. The ESQD arc maps are not “internal personnel rules and practices.” They are safety data of significant public interest. This Court should reverse the judgment below and order disclosure of the requested documents.
