**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that the Leader2Leader® product, as publicly used and on sale prior to December 10, 2002, anticipated the asserted claims of the '761 patent under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). The jury’s verdict of invalidity must be reversed because the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Leader, fails to establish that the Leader2Leader® product disclosed every element of the asserted claims in a manner that would have enabled a person of ordinary skill in the art to practice the invention without undue experimentation. The district court’s legal standard for anticipation was flawed, and the factual findings supporting the verdict are not supported by substantial evidence. This Court should reverse.

**I. Anticipation Requires Disclosure of Every Claimed Element in a Single Prior Art Reference**

Under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b), a patent claim is invalid if the invention was “in public use, on sale, or otherwise available to the public” more than one year before the patent’s filing date. *Brenner v. Manson*, 383 U.S. 519, 526 (1966). However, “the mere fact that a product was publicly used or sold does not automatically render a patent invalid.” *In re Brouwer*, 42 F.3d 1378, 1381 (Fed. Cir. 1994). To establish anticipation, the prior art must disclose every element of the claimed invention, either expressly or inherently, such that a person of ordinary skill in the art could practice the invention without undue experimentation. *Pfizer Inc. v. Apotex Inc.*, 424 F.3d 1324, 1330 (Fed. Cir. 2005). “Anticipation is not established by a mere showing that the prior art and the claimed invention are similar or even substantially similar.” *In re O’Farrell*, 853 F.2d 894, 897 (Fed. Cir. 1988).

Here, the asserted claims—such as Claim 9—require a *computer-implemented method* that includes *dynamically associating metadata with data*, *tracking movement of the user from one user environment to a second*, and *dynamically updating the stored metadata* with the new environment. The district court erred in treating the Leader2Leader® product as a single, fully disclosed prior art reference without requiring Facebook to prove that each of these elements was present in the product as of December 10, 2002.

**II. The Evidence Does Not Support a Finding That the Leader2Leader® Product Disclosed the “Dynamic” Metadata Updating Required by Claim 9**

The crux of the asserted claims lies in the *dynamic* updating of metadata in response to user movement between environments. The '761 patent emphasizes that the metadata “automatically follow” the user as they move, *id.* col. 7, ll. 46–49, and that the system “dynamically updates” the metadata to reflect new contexts. This is not a mere static tagging system. The term “dynamically” implies real-time or automatic adjustment based on user behavior, not manual or delayed updates.

Facebook failed to present any evidence that the Leader2Leader® product *dynamically* updated metadata when a user moved between environments. The white paper from January 2002 describes a “Big Board” and “Input & Display Collaboration Devices” that include a “Browser,” but it does not describe any mechanism for *automatically* updating metadata when a user transitions from one environment to another. The only testimony on this point came from Michael McKibben, who testified that the *Digital Leaderboard® engine*—the underlying system—was the subject of the patent claims, but he did not testify that it performed *dynamic* metadata updates in the manner claimed. *J.A. 25585–86*. Without such testimony, and without any technical documentation or source code demonstrating the dynamic behavior, the jury could not reasonably conclude that the prior art disclosed this essential element.

This failure is fatal. As this Court has held, “a prior art reference must disclose every limitation of the claim, either expressly or inherently.” *In re Schreiber*, 128 F.3d 1473, 1477 (Fed. Cir. 1997). The mere existence of a system that allows users to access data via a browser does not equate to disclosure of a system that *dynamically updates metadata* in response to user movement. *See also* *In re NTP, Inc.*, 654 F.3d 1268, 1280 (Fed. Cir. 2011) (anticipation requires disclosure of all claim elements, not just functional similarities).

**III. The District Court Misapplied the “Enablement” Standard and Failed to Require Substantial Evidence**

The district court applied an erroneous legal standard by treating the Leader2Leader® product as inherently disclosing the claimed invention simply because it was “operational” and “fully developed.” *Leader Techs., Inc. v. Facebook, Inc.*, 770 F.Supp.2d 686, 717 (D. Del. 2011). But the mere fact that a product is “operational” does not mean it discloses the claimed invention. *See* *In re O’Farrell*, 853 F.2d at 897 (“The fact that a product is commercially available does not mean that it discloses the claimed invention.”). The burden is on Facebook to prove that the prior art *disclosed* each claim element, not merely that a product existed.

Moreover, the jury’s verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. *See* *In re Tominaga*, 696 F.3d 1374, 1380 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (a verdict must be supported by substantial evidence). Here, the only evidence of the Leader2Leader® product’s functionality came from a single white paper and general testimony. No source code, no technical specifications, no user manuals, and no expert testimony from Facebook’s own experts established that the system performed *dynamic* metadata updates. The absence of such evidence renders the verdict legally insufficient.
