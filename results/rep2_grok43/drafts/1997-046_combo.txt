ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause affords no protection to clemency proceedings. The power to grant reprieves and pardons “is an act of grace” that “proceeds from the executive” and “is not subject to the control or direction of the legislature.” United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160-61 (1833). Because the authority rests solely with the executive, “the courts have no power to control” its exercise. Ex parte Wells, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 307, 315 (1855). Clemency therefore creates no life or liberty interest enforceable under the Fifth or Fourteenth Amendments. See Davidson v. New Orleans, 96 U.S. 97, 104 (1878) (due process “is not meant to interfere with the power of the State to protect the lives, liberty, and property of its citizens” through traditional executive functions).

The same principle precludes any claim that clemency forms part of the adjudicative process. “The Constitution of the United States, as originally adopted, does not require a State to provide for an appeal.” McKane v. Durston, 153 U.S. 684, 687 (1894). When a State elects to confer an additional remedy after judgment, that remedy remains “a matter of grace” and does not import the procedural safeguards that attach to trial and direct review. See Ex parte Parks, 93 U.S. 18, 22 (1876). Consequently, the mere existence of a clemency procedure cannot transform an executive act into a continuation of the criminal prosecution.

The Fifth Amendment likewise supplies no basis for relief. The privilege against self-incrimination “is limited to criminal cases” and “does not extend to protect a witness from being compelled to give testimony which may tend to disgrace him.” Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 598 (1896). An inmate who elects to request an interview “voluntarily waives” any claim that the proceeding compels incriminating answers. See Burdick v. United States, 236 U.S. 79, 94 (1915). Because participation is optional, the State imposes no “compulsory process” within the meaning of the Amendment. Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 91 (1908).

Even if statements made during a voluntary interview could later be used, the Constitution does not forbid their admission when the witness “has not been compelled to testify.” Counselman v. Hitchcock, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892). The regulation at issue leaves the inmate free to decline the interview or to remain silent during it. No unconstitutional condition therefore arises.

Finally, the Due Process Clause itself imposes no requirement that the executive furnish reasons or procedures for the exercise of clemency. “The law does not require the State to give any reasons for its action.” See Murray’s Lessee v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 272, 280 (1856). Because the challenged procedures confer only a discretionary opportunity for mercy, they violate neither the Fourteenth nor the Fifth Amendment.
