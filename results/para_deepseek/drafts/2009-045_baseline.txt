**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a reasonable attorneys’ fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes, but only in the rare and exceptional case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence that the quality of service was superior to what one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984). This Court has never held otherwise.

**I. THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT PERMITS ENHANCEMENTS IN RARE AND EXCEPTIONAL CASES.**

In *Blum*, this Court expressly rejected the argument that an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988. *Id.* at 901. The Court stated that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Id.* (quoting *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 435 (1983)). The Court explained that an upward adjustment may be justified in the “rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged” and resulted in “exceptional” success. *Id.* at 899.

In *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986) (“*Delaware Valley I*”), this Court reaffirmed that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible . . . in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” *Id.* at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901). Although the Court stated that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” it left open the possibility of an extraordinary case. *Id.* at 566 (emphasis added). The Court did not overrule *Blum*; it cited *Blum* with approval. *Id.* at 564, 568.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY EXERCISED ITS DISCRETION.**

The district court found that this case was the rare and exceptional case warranting an enhancement. The court made detailed findings, supported by specific evidence in the record, that counsel’s performance was “superb” and “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The court relied on its “own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case,” including the court’s “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge.” *Id.* at 1286, 1290.

The district judge is in the best position to evaluate trial-level advocacy and to identify when that advocacy is exceptional. The court’s observations were not improper “testimony,” as petitioners suggest, but rather permissible explanations for the enhancement, as *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* require. The court’s findings are entitled to deference and should be reviewed only for abuse of discretion. *See Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 437.

**III. EVERY CIRCUIT TO CONSIDER THE ISSUE AGREES.**

There is no circuit split on this issue. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits all agree that a district court may provide an enhancement for exceptional performance and results in rare and exceptional cases. *See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). This uniform consensus reflects the sound principle that district courts must retain discretion to ensure that fee awards are truly reasonable in the rare case where the lodestar does not fully compensate counsel for exceptional performance and results.

**IV. PETITIONERS’ ARGUMENTS ARE WITHOUT MERIT.**

Petitioners argue that enhancements for quality of performance and results obtained are never permissible. But this Court has repeatedly rejected that categorical position. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 901; *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565. Petitioners also argue that the district court’s reliance on its own observations violated due process. But the court’s findings were based on its firsthand observation of counsel’s performance throughout the litigation—a factor that this Court has recognized as relevant. *See Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 437. The court did not “testify”; it explained its reasoning, as required by *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I*.

**CONCLUSION**

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The district court properly exercised its discretion to enhance the lodestar in this rare and exceptional case, supported by specific evidence and detailed findings. This Court’s precedents permit such enhancements, and every circuit to consider the issue agrees.
