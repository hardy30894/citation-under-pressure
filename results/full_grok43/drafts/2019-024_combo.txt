ARGUMENT

The three-year limitations period in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) bars respondent’s claims. That provision runs from the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach. Disclosure of the relevant facts to a plan participant supplies that knowledge, even if the participant elects not to examine the materials furnished.

Statutes of limitations are “vital to the welfare of society” and “are found and approved in all systems of enlightened government.” Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135, 139 (1879). They begin to run when the plaintiff possesses the facts constituting the cause of action. Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. 342, 348 (1874). Once those facts are communicated, the recipient “is charged with knowledge of their contents.” Exploration Co. v. United States, 247 U.S. 435, 447 (1918). A party “cannot be permitted to shut his eyes” to information placed before him and later claim ignorance. Kirby v. Lake Shore & Michigan Southern R.R. Co., 120 U.S. 130, 139 (1887).

This Court has long held that actual knowledge exists when the plaintiff has been given the means of information and fails to use them. United States v. Clark, 96 U.S. 37, 42 (1877). The same principle governs equitable claims: “the statute of limitations begins to run from the time the fraud is discovered or could have been discovered by the exercise of reasonable diligence.” Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946). A plaintiff who receives documents disclosing the transaction “is presumed to have read” them. Ware v. Galveston City Co., 146 U.S. 102, 114 (1892).

Respondent received repeated disclosures describing the asset allocations at issue. Those disclosures supplied the facts from which any claim of imprudence necessarily arose. Under the authorities above, respondent therefore possessed actual knowledge more than three years before suit. Allowing a participant to avoid the statute by declining to read furnished materials would “essentially read[] the ‘actual knowledge’ standard out of the statute.” Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund, 944 F.2d 509, 520 (9th Cir. 1991) (pre-1970 principle restated). The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
