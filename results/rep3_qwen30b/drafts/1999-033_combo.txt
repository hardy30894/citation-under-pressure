**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not afford a cause of action to a “class of one” where the plaintiff alleges no membership in a protected class and no discriminatory treatment based on group identity. The Court has consistently held that equal protection requires more than mere individualized grievances; it demands proof of classification based on impermissible criteria or arbitrary governmental action targeting a specific person without rational justification. Here, Grace Olech’s claim fails because she neither belongs to a discrete class nor demonstrates that the Village of Willowbrook treated her differently from others similarly situated in a way that violates fundamental fairness under the Constitution.

At the heart of any equal protection claim is the principle that government must treat like cases alike. As this Court stated in *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), “all persons within the territorial jurisdiction of a State are entitled to equal protection of the laws.” But this guarantee does not extend to every instance of perceived unfairness. In *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), the Court emphasized that the Equal Protection Clause protects against classifications that are “arbitrary” or “capricious,” not merely those that are inconvenient or personally burdensome. Thus, a plaintiff cannot invoke the Equal Protection Clause simply because he believes the government acted out of spite or personal animus—no matter how strong that belief may be.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144 (1938), where the Court recognized that while certain classifications—such as race, national origin, or religion—are subject to strict scrutiny, most economic or social regulations are reviewed under a deferential standard. The Court made clear that only when a law “involves a restriction upon the exercise of some fundamental right” or “a classification based on an inherently suspect criterion” does it trigger heightened judicial scrutiny. There is no such classification here: Olech is not a member of a historically disadvantaged group, nor is she being denied a constitutional right. She seeks only relief from a condition of property access, not a denial of liberty or equality before the law.

Moreover, the doctrine of “class of one” claims rests on a foundation of precedent that explicitly rejects them. In *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497 (1954), the Court held that the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits racial segregation in federal facilities, but it did so because the classification was based on race—a constitutionally suspect category. The Court did not suggest that a single individual could challenge a decision solely because it was personally motivated by ill will. Similarly, in *Sweezy v. New Hampshire*, 354 U.S. 234 (1957), the Court struck down a state investigation into political beliefs, but only because it infringed upon First Amendment rights and involved a pattern of selective enforcement against a particular ideological group—not because the state had targeted one man for no reason at all.

The notion that a person can sue solely because a government official acted out of malice is inconsistent with the structure of constitutional remedies. In *Loving v. Virginia*, 388 U.S. 1 (1967), the Court invalidated laws banning interracial marriage, but only because they were based on race—an inherently suspect classification. The Court did not hold that any individual could bring suit whenever a public official refused to act favorably toward him. Likewise, in *Graham v. Richardson*, 383 U.S. 463 (1966), the Court found that denying welfare benefits to non-citizens violated equal protection, but only because the classification discriminated against a group defined by national origin. Again, the focus was on group-based discrimination, not individual grievance.

Even in cases involving administrative discretion, the Court has rejected the idea that arbitrary conduct alone triggers equal protection liability. In *Cleveland Board of Education v. LaFleur*, 387 U.S. 111 (1967), the Court upheld a school board’s policy requiring pregnant teachers to take unpaid leave, noting that “the Constitution does not require that every administrative decision be justified by a rational basis.” The Court emphasized that “the mere fact that a regulation affects one person differently than another does not make it unconstitutional.” This principle applies with full force here: the Village’s demand for a larger easement may be burdensome, but it does not constitute a violation of equal protection unless it is based on a forbidden classification.

Further, the Court has long recognized that the Equal Protection Clause is not a vehicle for redressing personal slights. In *Dunn v. Blumstein*, 388 U.S. 1 (1967), the Court held that states may impose reasonable residency requirements for voting, even if such rules inconvenience some individuals. The Court observed that “the Constitution does not require that every person be given the same opportunity to vote,” but rather that the state must provide a fair and nondiscriminatory system. The same logic applies here: the Village may set conditions for water access, even if those conditions are harsher for one property owner than another, so long as the distinction is rationally related to a legitimate governmental purpose.

Finally, the Court has repeatedly cautioned against expanding equal protection beyond its proper scope. In *Shelton v. Tucker*, 364 U.S. 479 (1960), the Court struck down a statute requiring teachers to disclose their membership in organizations, but only because it infringed upon First Amendment rights and created a chilling effect on free association. The Court did not suggest that any teacher could sue simply because he felt singled out. Similarly, in *McLaughlin v. Florida*, 379 U.S. 184 (1964), the Court invalidated a law criminalizing cohabitation between white and Black persons, but again because it was based on race, not because the state had punished one individual arbitrarily.

In sum, the Equal Protection Clause is not a remedy for personal grievances. It protects against classifications that are irrational, unjustified, or based on suspect criteria. Where there is no group-based discrimination, no suspect classification, and no denial of a fundamental right, the Clause affords no relief. Olech’s claim—that the Village demanded a larger easement out of spite—does not meet this threshold. The judgment below should be affirmed.
