**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court’s Denial of Winbush’s Motion for a Fingerprint Expert and Continuance Violated His Right to Due Process and to Present a Defense**

The district court’s refusal to permit Winbush to obtain a fingerprint expert—and its concomitant denial of a short continuance—deprived him of a fundamentally fair trial. The court’s ruling left the government’s expert testimony on fingerprint evidence wholly unrebutted, in a case where the absence of fingerprints was central to the defense. This was an abuse of discretion and a denial of due process.

**A. The Governing Standard: Denial of a Continuance Violates Due Process When It Is Arbitrary and Prejudicial**

A trial court’s discretion to deny a continuance is not unlimited. As this Court has long recognized, “there are no mechanical tests for deciding when a denial of a continuance is so arbitrary as to violate due process. The answer must be found in the circumstances present in every case, particularly in the reasons presented to the trial judge at the time the request is denied.” *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964). A “myopic insistence upon expeditiousness” in the face of a legitimate need for more time can itself be a denial of due process. *Id.* at 589–90.

Here, the district court’s denial was arbitrary. Winbush moved for a fingerprint expert only six days before trial, but the case had been pending for nearly three years, and the government had disclosed its own fingerprint expert only a month earlier. The court gave no consideration to the substance of Winbush’s request, instead dismissing it as “woefully late.” That is precisely the kind of reflexive, mechanical response that *Ungar* forbids. The court never weighed the prejudice to Winbush against the minimal delay required to secure an expert.

**B. The Denial Deprived Winbush of the Basic Tools of an Adequate Defense**

Due process requires that a criminal defendant be afforded a meaningful opportunity to defend. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 68–69 (1932) (“The right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel.”). The state may not deny a defendant “the basic tools of an adequate defense.” *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 19 (1956). While *Griffin* concerned appellate transcripts, its principle extends to expert assistance where the defendant’s only means of rebutting the government’s scientific evidence is through an expert of his own.

The government planned to call a fingerprint expert to testify “about the difficulties in obtaining latent fingerprints from different materials and surfaces.” That testimony was designed to explain away the absence of fingerprints on the gun and drugs—evidence that was central to the government’s case. Without a defense expert, Winbush had no way to challenge the government’s scientific narrative. The subject of fingerprint recovery is “beyond the ken of the average layman,” *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923), and thus required expert explanation. By denying Winbush any expert, the court effectively allowed the government to present its scientific evidence as unchallenged truth.

**C. The Denial Violated Winbush’s Right to Compulsory Process and to Present a Defense**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to present witnesses in his defense. *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967) (“The right to offer the testimony of witnesses, and to compel their attendance, if necessary, is in plain terms the right to present a defense.”). That right is meaningless if the defendant is denied the means to obtain a witness whose testimony is essential. A defense expert is no less a witness than a fact witness. The district court’s ruling effectively foreclosed Winbush from presenting any expert testimony, while the government presented its own. This one-sided presentation denied Winbush a fair trial. *See In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. 257, 273 (1948) (“A person’s right to reasonable notice of a charge against him, and an opportunity to be heard in his defense—a right to his day in court—are basic in our system of jurisprudence.”).

**D. The Error Was Not Harmless**

The government’s case was not overwhelming. The gun and drugs were recovered from locations Winbush had fled, but no fingerprints were found on them. The government’s expert was called to explain that absence. A defense expert could have testified that the absence of fingerprints is inconsistent with Winbush having handled the items, or that the government’s recovery methods were inadequate. Such testimony could have created reasonable doubt. The denial of that testimony was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967).

**E. The Government’s Reliance on Unrebutted Expert Testimony Compounds the Due Process Violation**

Due process is violated when the state secures a conviction through fundamentally unfair means. *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935); *Pyle v. Kansas*, 317 U.S. 213, 216 (1942). Here, the government did not introduce false testimony, but it did introduce expert testimony that Winbush was powerless to rebut. The result is the same: a trial that was not a reliable adversarial testing of the evidence. The district court’s refusal to grant a brief continuance to allow Winbush to obtain an expert was an abuse of discretion that infected the entire trial.

For these reasons, Winbush’s conviction must be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
