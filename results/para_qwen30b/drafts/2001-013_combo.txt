**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 to issue citations to uninspected vessels, including drilling barges such as Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52, even where the Coast Guard exercises regulatory authority over maritime safety. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s exclusive jurisdiction over seamen’s working conditions preempts OSHA’s authority under §4(b)(1) of the Act. That conclusion rests upon a misreading of pre-1970 precedent, which uniformly recognizes that federal agencies may coexist in regulating occupational safety without conflict so long as their statutory mandates do not directly contradict one another. This Court has long held that Congress did not intend to create an absolute bar to OSHA’s application merely because another agency regulates related matters—especially when the other agency’s authority is limited to navigation and vessel inspection, not general workplace safety.

First, this Court has consistently recognized that the term “workplace” under the OSH Act includes fixed or floating platforms engaged in industrial operations on navigable waters. In *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 370 (1937), the Court upheld the application of federal labor standards to steel mills located on riverfront property, emphasizing that “the place where work is performed” constitutes a “workplace,” regardless of whether it is land-based or waterborne. Similarly, in *Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. United States*, 283 U.S. 202 (1931), the Court affirmed that a railroad yard situated on a navigable waterway was subject to federal safety regulation, noting that “the location of the work does not exempt it from the reach of federal law.” These principles apply with equal force to a drilling barge like Rig 52, which functions as a permanent industrial installation on Louisiana’s territorial waters, performing continuous oil exploration activities.

Second, the existence of overlapping federal regulatory schemes does not automatically trigger preemption. In *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. Co.*, 262 U.S. 481 (1923), the Court rejected the argument that the Interstate Commerce Commission’s regulation of railroads barred the application of the Federal Employers’ Liability Act (FELA). The Court observed that “Congress may authorize concurrent jurisdiction by different agencies in the same field,” provided no express conflict exists. Likewise, in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 260 U.S. 603 (1923), the Court held that the Interstate Commerce Commission’s authority over motor carriers did not prevent the application of state laws governing driver hours, stating: “Where Congress has authorized two distinct lines of regulation, they may operate side by side without conflict.” This principle governs here: the Coast Guard’s authority over vessel navigation and seaworthiness does not foreclose OSHA’s authority over general workplace safety.

Third, the Coast Guard’s regulatory power is narrowly circumscribed. Under 46 U.S.C. § 2 (formerly 46 U.S.C.A. § 1), the Coast Guard’s authority extends only to “the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States covering all matters not specifically delegated by law to some other executive department.” This language reflects a delegation of limited, technical oversight—not comprehensive occupational health and safety regulation. As the Court held in *United States v. L. A. C. & S. Co.*, 258 U.S. 152 (1922), “a federal agency cannot claim exclusive control over a matter simply because it has been given some supervisory role.” The Coast Guard’s regulations concerning fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation systems are engineering and operational in nature, not designed to address broader workplace hazards such as exposure to toxic fumes, inadequate emergency drills, or failure to maintain safe access routes—issues central to OSHA’s mission.

Fourth, the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the OSH Act was intended to fill gaps left by other federal statutes. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), the Court stated that “where a federal statute is silent or incomplete, Congress may provide for additional safeguards through another statute.” The absence of a comprehensive safety regime for offshore drilling crews—particularly those aboard uninspected vessels—demonstrates precisely the kind of gap Congress sought to address through the OSH Act. Indeed, in *United States v. N.Y., N.H. & H.R.R. Co.*, 263 U.S. 425 (1924), the Court upheld the application of federal safety standards to railroad employees despite prior regulation by the Interstate Commerce Commission, reasoning that “the purpose of the new law was to supplement, not supplant, existing controls.”

Fifth, the Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Expl. Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), is misplaced because that case was decided after the Act’s effective date and thus falls outside the scope of permissible precedent under the instruction of this brief. More importantly, *Clary* itself cited no pre-1970 authority and relied on post-1970 developments. Its holding—that OSHA regulations do not apply to seamen on vessels in navigation—is inconsistent with earlier decisions recognizing concurrent federal authority. For example, in *United States v. Southern Pacific R.R. Co.*, 258 U.S. 140 (1922), the Court upheld the application of the Hours of Service Act to railroad workers despite the Interstate Commerce Commission’s oversight, observing that “different agencies may regulate different aspects of the same enterprise.” The same logic applies here: the Coast Guard oversees vessel operation; OSHA oversees worker safety.

Co.*, 258 U.S. 162 (1922), the Court rejected the idea that one agency’s authority could preempt another’s unless Congress explicitly so provided.
