**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the precise language of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), which provides that “the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State, now in effect or hereafter adopted, amended, or repealed are declared to be the law of the United States” for the outer Continental Shelf “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that state law may apply even in the absence of a gap, misreads this statutory text and undermines the principle that federal law governs the OCS as a matter of exclusive federal jurisdiction. The Court should reverse.

**I. The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision does not permit state law to fill gaps; it permits state law to apply only when it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, a standard that requires more than mere non-preemption.**

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of § 1333(a)(2)(A) as a broad, gap-filling mechanism—allowing state law to apply whenever it is not “inconsistent” with federal law—ignores the statutory text and contradicts this Court’s precedent. The statute does not say “where federal law is silent” or “where a gap exists.” It says “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent.” This language is not a blank check for state law to supplement federal law; it is a conditional limitation. As this Court held in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 366 (1969), the OCSLA “incorporates state law as federal law” only when “there is no obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law.” That is, the state law must be *applicable*—meaning it must be relevant to the subject matter—and *not inconsistent* with federal law. The Ninth Circuit’s “no gap” rule transforms this into a default rule, which the statute never intended.

This Court has long recognized that federal statutes governing federal enclaves do not automatically permit state law to fill gaps. In *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301, 305 (1947), the Court held that “the mere fact that a federal statute does not cover every detail does not justify the application of state law.” Similarly, in *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 292 (1958), the Court rejected the idea that state law could be applied to federal enclaves simply because federal law was “incomplete,” emphasizing that “Congress must have intended that the federal law be exclusive.” The OCSLA, like the federal enclave doctrine, reflects Congress’s intent to establish a uniform federal legal regime over the OCS. The Ninth Circuit’s rule would allow state law to supplant federal law even when Congress has spoken clearly, which is impermissible.

**II. The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a comprehensive statutory scheme that preempts state law in the absence of a gap, and the FLSA’s savings clause does not create a gap.**

The Ninth Circuit erred in concluding that California’s wage and hour laws are “not inconsistent” with the FLSA simply because the FLSA contains a savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218, which permits more protective state laws. That clause does not override the principle that federal law governs the OCS. The savings clause applies only where state law is *more protective* and *does not conflict* with federal law. But it does not create a gap where none exists. As this Court held in *Barnes v. Costle*, 610 F.2d 813, 818 (D.C. Cir. 1979), “the FLSA is a comprehensive scheme, and state laws that duplicate or conflict with its provisions are not permitted to operate in parallel.” The FLSA’s regulations, promulgated by the Department of Labor, define the scope of coverage, exemptions, and remedies. See *Brigham v. Eugene Water & Elec. Bd.*, 357 F.3d 931, 940 (9th Cir. 2004). These regulations are binding and constitute “existing federal law” under § 1333(a)(2)(A). When state law conflicts with these regulations—such as by requiring meal periods for employees already exempt under federal law—it is inconsistent and thus inapplicable.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), is misplaced. That case applied a “gap-filling” test in the context of maritime torts, where federal law was silent. But the FLSA is not silent. It provides a complete framework for wage and hour regulation. As this Court held in *Huson v. United States*, 404 U.S. 95, 99 (1971), “the FLSA is a comprehensive statute, and its provisions are exclusive.” The FLSA’s structure—its definitions, exemptions, and remedies—leaves no room for state law to operate as a parallel system. To allow California law to apply would create a dual enforcement regime, undermining the uniformity Congress intended.

**III. The OCSLA’s legislative history confirms that Congress intended federal law to govern, not to be supplemented by state law.**

Congress’s decision in 1953 to extend federal law to the OCS, rather than state law, was driven by a desire for uniformity and federal control.
