**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford's exclusion of the Good News Club constituted permissible subject-matter discrimination in a limited public forum.**

A school district may "reserve its forum for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics," *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995), and may exclude speech that falls outside the forum's defined boundaries, *Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983). Milford's Community Use Policy opened school facilities for "social, civic and recreational meetings" and "other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community," but expressly barred "use for religious purposes." The policy therefore created a limited public forum whose subject-matter boundaries excluded religious instruction and worship.

The Club's proposed meetings consisted of prayer, memorization of scripture, Bible stories, and invitations to accept Jesus as personal Savior. These activities are "the equivalent of religious worship" rather than discussion of secular subjects from a religious perspective. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) (distinguishing worship from protected speech). Because the Club sought access for a use the forum was never opened to accommodate, Milford's denial was content-based subject-matter discrimination, not viewpoint discrimination. *See Bronx Household of Faith v. Community Sch. Dist. No. 10*, 127 F.3d 207, 215 (2d Cir. 1997).

**II. The Club's activities were not merely a religious viewpoint on an otherwise permissible topic.**

Viewpoint discrimination occurs only when the government targets "particular views taken by speakers on a subject." *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829. Milford permitted other organizations to address moral and character development. It did not, however, permit any group to conduct religious instruction or prayer. The distinction rests on the nature of the speech itself, not on hostility to the Club's religious perspective. *See Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (permitting religious viewpoints on child-rearing while excluding worship). The Club's meetings crossed the line from viewpoint to a distinct subject—religious worship and indoctrination—properly excluded from the forum.

**III. Milford's Establishment Clause concerns independently justify the exclusion.**

Even if the exclusion implicated free-speech interests, Milford's interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation supplies a compelling justification. *See Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 837 (recognizing that "the interest of the State in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation 'may be a compelling' one"). Permitting the Club's meetings would have placed Milford in the position of hosting weekly religious instruction and prayer for elementary-school children on school property immediately after the school day. Such direct governmental facilitation of religious worship raises the very concerns the Establishment Clause guards against. Milford therefore acted within constitutional bounds by maintaining the forum's secular limits.
