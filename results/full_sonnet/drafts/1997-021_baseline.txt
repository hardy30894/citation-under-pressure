# ARGUMENT

## I. THE BOARD'S "GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT" STANDARD, AS APPLIED, IS ARBITRARY AND INCONSISTENT WITH THE ACT

This Court has long recognized that an incumbent union enjoys only a *rebuttable* presumption of majority support. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). The presumption exists to promote stability in bargaining relationships, not to insulate unions from employee sentiment. An employer may rebut that presumption—and thereby justify withdrawal of recognition, an RM election, or an employee poll—through "objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status." *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990).

The Board's insistence on applying the *identical* evidentiary standard to polling as it applies to outright withdrawal of recognition renders the polling option illusory. As the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have each recognized, if an employer already possesses evidence "sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition from the union," it has no need to poll at all. *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984). Polling serves a distinct and valuable function: it allows an employer who harbors genuine uncertainty to obtain reliable information *before* taking the drastic step of severing the bargaining relationship. The Sixth Circuit correctly held "that an employer may poll its employees to determine their union sentiment if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient in itself to justify withdrawal." *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). The Ninth Circuit reached the same conclusion, holding that polling is "a useful and legitimate tool for the employer who is in sincere doubt of the union's majority status." *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1299. The Fifth Circuit likewise permits polling on "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support (even if that evidence is not sufficient by itself to justify withdrawal)." *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981).

The Board's collapsing of these distinct standards cannot be squared with its own stated policy preferences. The Board itself has acknowledged that RM elections—not polls—are its preferred mechanism for testing union support, describing polling as "potentially disruptive and unsettling." *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-62 (1989). Yet the Board applies the same threshold showing to polling as to RM elections and to unilateral withdrawal—the very course the Board considers least desirable. This scheme is internally incoherent: it "seems inconsistent for the Board to say that RM elections are the preferred method for testing employee support of the union, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061, and yet maintain the same evidentiary standard for allowing polling." An agency's interpretation of its governing statute, even one entitled to deference, cannot stand when it is arbitrary on its own terms. *See Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983) (agency action is unlawful if the agency "entirely failed to consider an important aspect of the problem").

## II. THE BOARD'S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED A GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

Even under the Board's own stringent standard, the record compels the conclusion that Allentown Mack possessed the requisite objective doubt. Judicial review of Board factual findings requires that they be supported by "substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole." *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). That standard is not satisfied here.

The Board arbitrarily discounted probative evidence of union disaffection. Employees Ridgick and Marsh both made statements during job interviews expressing dissatisfaction with the union, yet the Board disregarded these statements as "equivocal" or as mere dissatisfaction with representation rather than opposition to it. But an employee's spontaneous expression that he was "not getting his money's worth" from union dues, considered together with the totality of the workforce's statements, is precisely the kind of objective indicator this Court contemplated in *Fall River*. The Board compounded this error by refusing to credit the reports of employees Bloch and Mohr—including a union steward—that the night shift and broader unit did not support the union, on the rationale that such secondhand reports were unreliable. Yet the Board has elsewhere accepted comparably informal evidence when it suited the union's position.

Considered against the backdrop that this was a newly-formed successor employer with a workforce reconstituted from a much larger predecessor unit—32 employees drawn from a prior unit of 45—the foundations for a continuing presumption of majority support were substantially weaker than in the ordinary case. *Cf. Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 41-43 (successor employer inherits bargaining obligation, but circumstances of successorship remain relevant). As the Sixth Circuit held on comparable facts, "we find no substantial evidence to support the Board's conclusion that the Company did not have a good faith doubt as to the Union's majority status." *Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 868. The same conclusion is compelled here, particularly where the subsequent poll itself—conducted under a neutral third party with full procedural safeguards—confirmed the very doubt that Allentown Mack asserted, with employees voting 19 to 13 against representation.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
