**ARGUMENT**

**I. The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts law as a valid content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation.**

The Massachusetts statute regulates only where speech may occur near reproductive health care facilities; it does not target speech because of its message. In *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), the Court upheld a similar law restricting certain approaches within 100 feet of clinic entrances, explaining that the law was content neutral because it regulated conduct without reference to the topic of discussion. *Id.* at 719–25. The same is true here. The Massachusetts law applies to all persons entering or remaining within the buffer zone, subject to limited exemptions tied to facility access and public safety, not viewpoint. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 176–81 (1st Cir. 2009); *McCullen v. Coakley*, 573 U.S. 464, 481–83 (2014).

The First Amendment permits reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions in traditional public forums so long as they are content neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983); *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). Massachusetts easily satisfies that test. The Commonwealth has a significant interest in ensuring safe and unimpeded access to health care facilities and in protecting patients from obstruction, delay, and harassment. *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western New York*, 519 U.S. 357, 376–77 (1997); *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715–16. This Court has repeatedly recognized that the State may impose buffer zones to prevent congestion and preserve access where targeted counseling has impeded clinic operations. *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 380; *Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 768–70 (1994).

The law is also narrowly tailored. Narrow tailoring does not require the least restrictive means, *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798–99, but it does require a fit that does not burden substantially more speech than necessary. The First Circuit correctly concluded that the record showed persistent obstruction, crowding, and interference at clinic entrances, and that the legislature reasonably chose a fixed buffer zone to address those harms. *McCullen*, 571 F.3d at 180–81. That judgment is entitled to respect. This Court has upheld buffer-zone laws where they were designed to prevent interference with access and preserve public order. *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 380; *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768–70.

Nor did the statute foreclose ample alternatives. Petitioners remained free to speak, leaflet, pray, display signs, and counsel outside the zone. The First Amendment does not guarantee communicators their preferred distance or method of persuasion. *Heffron v. Int’l Soc’y for Krishna Consciousness, Inc.*, 452 U.S. 640, 654–55 (1981); *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 802. The First Circuit correctly held that these alternatives were adequate even if not ideal. *McCullen*, 571 F.3d at 182–84.

**II. If *Hill v. Colorado* is implicated, it should be reaffirmed; at minimum, it should not be overruled here.**

Petitioners ask the Court to limit or overrule *Hill*. That request should be rejected. *Hill* has been part of the Court’s First Amendment doctrine for nearly a quarter century, and stare decisis carries special force where a precedent has structured reliance and been repeatedly invoked in later cases. *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827–28 (1991); *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 212 (1984). The Court has cited *Hill* approvingly in the clinic-access context. *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 376–77. And *McCullen* itself did not overrule *Hill*; it distinguished the Massachusetts law on other grounds. *McCullen*, 573 U.S. at 486–97.

Overruling *Hill* would also disrupt the settled distinction between content-neutral access regulations and content-based restrictions on speech. This Court has repeatedly explained that the First Amendment permits regulation of the place and manner of speech in sensitive public forums when the rule is justified without reference to the content of expression. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791; *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719–25. Petitioners’ theory would unsettle that line and undermine longstanding access protections for persons seeking medical treatment. Nothing in the record warrants that result.

The judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
