**ARGUMENT**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). That statutory command satisfies § 1255(a)’s requirement that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” The Third Circuit’s contrary holding rests on an artificial distinction between “admission” and “status” that the text, structure, and purpose of the INA do not support.

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). When Congress later conferred lawful nonimmigrant status on TPS recipients, it necessarily supplied the inspection and authorization that the definition requires. See I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (“where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another . . . it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally”). Section 1255(k)(1) confirms the point by permitting adjustment when an alien is “present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission,” a phrase that § 1254a(f)(4) satisfies once lawful nonimmigrant status is conferred.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on Hanif v. Attorney General, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), and Taveras v. Attorney General, 731 F.3d 281 (3d Cir. 2013), is misplaced. Those decisions addressed adjustment of status itself, not the distinct question whether a subsequent grant of lawful nonimmigrant status constitutes the functional equivalent of admission for § 1255 purposes. Nothing in either opinion suggests that Congress cannot equate the two concepts when it chooses to do so.

Statutory structure reinforces the textual command. Congress created explicit exceptions to the admission requirement for special immigrants and certain visa applicants, 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i), but supplied no such exception for TPS recipients. Instead, it chose the broader language of § 1254a(f)(4). That choice is presumed deliberate. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. at 432. Reading § 1254a(f)(4) to satisfy § 1255(a) also avoids rendering § 1254a(h)(2)’s supermajority requirement superfluous, for it leaves Congress free to enact special relief while still permitting ordinary adjustment for those already present under TPS. See Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004).

The purpose of TPS—to permit qualifying nationals to remain lawfully without fear of removal—would be frustrated if the very status Congress conferred could never lead to permanent residence. The Third Circuit’s rule forces TPS recipients who entered without inspection to depart and reenter, exposing them to the very dangers TPS was enacted to avoid. Nothing in the statute compels that result.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
