## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because Harold Wasek presented sufficient evidence from which a reasonable jury could find that the harassment he endured occurred “because of . . . sex” within the meaning of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), and the analogous Michigan Elliot-Larsen Civil Rights Act, Mich. Comp. Laws § 37.2202. The court improperly required Wasek to prove that his tormentor’s conduct was motivated by sexual desire, ignoring that the undisputed sexual nature of the touching and comments constitutes direct evidence of gender-based animus.

### A. The Sexual Conduct Itself Establishes That the Harassment Was Because of Sex

Title VII prohibits an employer from discriminating “against any individual with respect to his compensation, terms, conditions, or privileges of employment, because of such individual’s . . . sex.” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1). The plain language of the statute does not require a plaintiff to show that the harasser acted out of sexual desire. Rather, discrimination “because of sex” occurs whenever an employee is subjected to adverse treatment that would not have occurred but for his gender. *Rosenfeld v. Southern Pac. Co.*, 293 F. Supp. 1219, 1223 (C.D. Cal. 1968) (holding that “sex” discrimination includes any differential treatment based on gender, not merely that motivated by sexual attraction). Here, the record is replete with conduct that is inherently sex-based: Paul Ottobre grabbed Wasek’s buttocks, poked him in the rear with a hammer handle and a sucker rod, and made repeated sexual comments such as “you’ve got a pretty mouth” and “you know you like it sweetheart.” R. 26-1 at 54–55. These acts are not gender-neutral; they are explicitly sexual in nature and were directed at Wasek because he is a man. A reasonable jury could conclude that Ottobre would not have engaged in such conduct toward a female coworker, or that the conduct was designed to demean Wasek based on his gender. The district court’s insistence on a separate showing of “sexual desire” or “gender stereotyping” imposes a requirement that the statute does not contain.

### B. The District Court Improperly Resolved Disputed Facts Against Wasek

Summary judgment is appropriate only when “there is no genuine issue as to any material fact.” *Poller v. Columbia Broad. Sys., Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 467 (1962). In evaluating the motion, the court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. *United States v. Diebold, Inc.*, 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962) (per curiam). The district court here disregarded Wasek’s testimony that Ottobre’s harassment was relentless, that his supervisor Pat Tripp laughed at the incidents and threatened Wasek with a “starvation schedule” if he complained, and that the Director of Operations Dick Smillie responded to Wasek’s complaint by telling him to “just kick [Ottobre’s] ass.” R. 26-1 at 56. These facts, taken in Wasek’s favor, create a genuine dispute as to whether the harassment was motivated by Wasek’s gender. The court erred by substituting its own judgment for that of the jury.

### C. The Michigan Elliot-Larsen Civil Rights Act Is Coextensive with Title VII

Michigan’s Elliot-Larsen Civil Rights Act prohibits discrimination “because of . . . sex” in employment. Mich. Comp. Laws § 37.2202. The Michigan Supreme Court has consistently interpreted the Act in harmony with federal Title VII precedent. Because Wasek has raised a triable issue under Title VII, his state-law claim likewise survives summary judgment.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
