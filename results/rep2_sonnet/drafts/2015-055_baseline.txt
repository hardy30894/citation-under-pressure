# ARGUMENT

## THE RULE ANNOUNCED IN JOHNSON V. UNITED STATES APPLIES RETROACTIVELY TO CASES ON COLLATERAL REVIEW

### A. Johnson Announced a New Substantive Rule of Constitutional Law

In *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), this Court established that new constitutional rules of criminal procedure generally do not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review, subject to two exceptions. The first exception applies to rules that are substantive rather than procedural—rules that "alter the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes." *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). This Court has consistently held that such substantive rules "must be applied retroactively." *Id.*

*Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), announced precisely this type of substantive rule. The Court held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) "violates the Constitution's guarantee of due process" because it is unconstitutionally vague. *Id.* at 2557. Critically, this vagueness holding did not merely alter the procedure by which courts identify violent felonies—it eliminated an entire category of predicate offenses from ACCA's reach. As this Court explained, invalidating the residual clause means that "the Government must fail" in seeking enhanced sentences based on that clause. *Id.* at 2557.

This substantive/procedural distinction is dispositive. In *Summerlin*, this Court held that a rule is substantive, and therefore retroactive, when it "narrow[s] the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms" or "place[s] particular conduct or persons covered by the statute beyond the State's power to punish." 542 U.S. at 351-52 (internal quotation marks omitted). Johnson does exactly this: by striking the residual clause, it removes an entire category of offenses—those previously classified as violent felonies only through the now-invalid "conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another" language—from ACCA's ambit altogether. A defendant like Mr. Welch, whose sentencing enhancement depended on the residual clause, is now serving a sentence for conduct that Congress lacked constitutional authority to punish in that manner.

This case is squarely analogous to *United States v. United States Coin & Currency*, 401 U.S. 715 (1971), and later substantive-rule cases where this Court has held that decisions narrowing the substantive reach of a criminal statute apply retroactively because "there is no way to avoid" retroactive application "when...the conduct at issue is constitutionally immune from punishment." *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620 (1998) (quoting *Davis v. United States*, 417 U.S. 333, 346 (1974)).

### B. Johnson Is Not a Procedural Rule Governing the Manner of Determining Facts

The Eleventh Circuit's implicit treatment of Johnson as merely procedural—a new method for evaluating prior convictions—misapprehends the nature of the holding. This Court's decision in *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part), which this Court adopted in *Teague*, distinguished between rules that regulate "the manner of determining the defendant's culpability" and rules that alter "the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes." *Summerlin*, 542 U.S. at 353. Johnson falls in the latter category. It does not instruct courts to use a different method for determining whether prior convictions qualify as violent felonies; it holds that an entire clause defining what qualifies as a violent felony is void.

This distinction mirrors that drawn in *Bailey v. United States*, 516 U.S. 137 (1995), and subsequent cases holding that decisions narrowing the substantive scope of criminal statutes—as opposed to decisions announcing new procedural safeguards like *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002)—apply retroactively. Indeed, this Court has already indicated that vagueness holdings striking down criminal statutes as unconstitutional are substantive. In *United States v. Davis*, this Court's predecessor decisions establish that a conviction or sentence obtained under a statute later held void for vagueness cannot stand, because the government "is without power to punish" conduct under an unconstitutionally vague law. *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 353 (1964).

### C. Denying Retroactive Application Would Perpetuate Unconstitutional Sentences

The consequences of the Eleventh Circuit's approach are severe: petitioners like Mr. Welch remain incarcerated under sentences imposed pursuant to a clause this Court has declared void for vagueness. This Court has recognized that "[t]here is no principled way to distinguish a rule that places certain conduct beyond the State's power to punish at all from one that simply makes illegal conduct a basis for enhanced punishment." *Summerlin*, 542 U.S. at 354 n.4. Mr. Welch's fifteen-year mandatory minimum sentence rests on a predicate finding—that his Florida robbery conviction qualified as a violent felony under the residual clause—that Johnson has rendered constitutionally infirm.

To deny retroactivity here would mean that similarly situated defendants receive dramatically different treatment based solely on the fortuity of when their cases became final, notwithstanding that all are serving sentences premised on the same unconstitutional statutory provision. This result is precisely what the substantive-rule exception to *Teague* was designed to prevent.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that Johnson announced a substantive rule of constitutional law that applies retroactively to cases on collateral review, and should reverse the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit.
