**ARGUMENT**

The district court abused its discretion in certifying the class. Rule 23(b)(3) demands that questions common to the class predominate, which in turn requires admissible evidence that damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 351 (2011). The court failed to conduct the “rigorous analysis” mandated by that standard. *Id.* at 351 (quoting *Gen. Tel. Co. of Sw. v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982)).

A district court may not certify a class on the basis of an expert model that fails to isolate the sole remaining theory of liability. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 316 (3d Cir. 2008). Plaintiffs’ damages methodology measured harm from four distinct theories, only one of which survived the court’s narrowing order. Because the model did not exclude the effects of the rejected theories, it supplied no evidence that damages could be proven on a class-wide basis through common proof. *See Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997) (predominance “tests whether proposed classes are sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation”).

The court’s acceptance of that model rested on clearly erroneous findings. A finding is clearly erroneous when the reviewing court is “left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). The record contained no evidence that the overbuilder-deterrence theory alone produced a uniform, measurable overcharge across the Philadelphia DMA. *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573-74 (1985). Instead, the model assumed class-wide impact without demonstrating that the remaining theory generated damages capable of common quantification.

Nor may a court rely on inadmissible expert testimony to satisfy predominance. *See Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 597 (1993). The district court admitted the model despite its mismatch with the narrowed liability theory, violating the requirement that certification rest on evidence admissible at trial. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 178 (1974) (Rule 23 determinations must be based on findings supported by the record).

Finally, the court’s geographic-market ruling compounded the error. Predominance cannot be established when the relevant market is defined at a level that precludes common proof of injury. *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 294, 336-37 (1962). The DMA-wide definition adopted here lacked evidentiary support once the model’s over-inclusiveness was recognized.

Because the record contains no admissible evidence that damages are measurable on a class-wide basis under the sole remaining theory, the certification order must be reversed.
