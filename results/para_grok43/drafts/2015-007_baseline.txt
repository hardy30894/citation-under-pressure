ARGUMENT

I. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Comports with the Sixth Amendment.

Ring v. Arizona held that, under the Sixth Amendment, “[b]ecause Arizona’s enumerated aggravating factors operate as ‘the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense,’ the Sixth Amendment requires that they be found by a jury.” 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002) (quoting Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 494 n.19 (2000)). Florida’s scheme satisfies this requirement. The jury must find the existence of at least one statutory aggravating circumstance beyond a reasonable doubt before it may recommend death. See § 921.141(2), Fla. Stat. (2012). The trial judge then independently determines whether the aggravating circumstances outweigh the mitigating circumstances and imposes sentence. Id. § 921.141(3). This structure ensures that the factual findings necessary to expose the defendant to a capital sentence are made by the jury, as Ring demands.

The Sixth Amendment does not further require that the jury’s recommendation be unanimous. The Court has never held that unanimity is an element of the jury-trial guarantee in capital cases. See Apodaca v. Oregon, 406 U.S. 404, 411-12 (1972) (plurality opinion) (non-unanimous verdicts permissible in state criminal trials). Florida’s provision for a majority recommendation therefore raises no constitutional infirmity.

II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Determination of Mental Retardation.

Atkins v. Virginia established that the Eighth Amendment prohibits execution of intellectually disabled offenders, but left “to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002) (quoting Ford v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399, 416 (1986)). Florida has done so by requiring the trial court to determine, after an evidentiary hearing, whether the defendant has proven intellectual disability by clear and convincing evidence. See § 921.137(4), Fla. Stat. (2012); Nixon v. State, 2 So. 3d 137, 145 (Fla. 2009). Nothing in Atkins or subsequent decisions mandates that this determination be made by a jury. The Eighth Amendment’s concern is that intellectually disabled offenders not be executed; it does not prescribe the identity of the decision-maker.

III. Florida’s Scheme Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment’s Prohibition on Cruel and Unusual Punishments.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “death is different.” Ring, 536 U.S. at 605-06. Yet the Eighth Amendment does not require every procedural safeguard that might be imagined. Florida’s requirement of a jury finding of an aggravating circumstance, followed by independent judicial sentencing, provides the “heightened reliability demanded by the Eighth Amendment.” Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). The absence of jury unanimity or jury adjudication of intellectual disability does not render the scheme so arbitrary or unreliable as to violate the Constitution.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
