**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in refusing to clarify the jury’s inconsistent damage awards and in entering judgment that Unit recovered nothing. When a verdict is ambiguous or internally inconsistent, the trial court has a duty to resolve the ambiguity before the jury is discharged. See *Diamond Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Zinke & Trumbo, Ltd.*, 791 F.2d 1416, 1428 (10th Cir. 1986) (trial court may and should seek clarification of an ambiguous verdict before discharge); *McDonnell v. Timmerman*, 530 F.2d 1386, 1389 (10th Cir. 1976) (same); *Lavoie v. Pacific Press & Shear Co.*, 975 F.2d 48, 54 (2d Cir. 1992) (district court should not speculate about jury intent where verdict is unclear). Here, the jury returned a verdict on Form 1 awarding Unit $70,768.02 on its breach claim and a separate Form 3 set-off award to Enron of $86,494.30. Those two findings created at least an ambiguity as to whether the jury intended to offset one award against the other or to find separate, duplicative components of damages. The district court’s refusal to ask the jury to clarify that ambiguity was error.

Rule 49 and Rule 51 do not permit a court to guess at the jury’s intent when the verdict forms reasonably support more than one interpretation. See *Fed. R. Civ. P.* 49; *Diamond Shamrock*, 791 F.2d at 1428. The very purpose of seeking clarification is to avoid precisely what occurred here: the court “interpreted” the verdict by subtracting the set-off from the damages award and then entered judgment for zero. But courts may not rewrite a jury’s verdict. *McDonnell*, 530 F.2d at 1389; *Dreiling v. Peugeot Motors of America, Inc.*, 539 F.2d 531, 543 (10th Cir. 1976) (where verdict is uncertain, it should be clarified rather than reinterpreted by the court).

The district court’s interpretation also conflicted with the jury instructions and with ordinary contract-damages principles. A prevailing plaintiff in a breach-of-contract case is entitled to recover expectation damages proven at trial, subject to any properly found offset, but the offset must be applied only once. See *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* § 347 (1981); *ZIS Co. v. Department of Defense*, 56 F.3d 72, 74 (10th Cir. 1995) (damages in contract cases are intended to place the injured party in the position it would have occupied absent breach). The jury was told it could award Unit the unpaid invoices if it found full performance, or unpaid invoices less repair costs if it found substantial performance. Nothing in the instructions authorized the jury to reduce Unit’s award on Form 1 and then separately award the same repair costs to Enron as a set-off on Form 3. Because the verdict forms and instructions allowed an obvious duplication problem, clarification was required. See *United States v. Morris*, 612 F.2d 483, 488 (10th Cir. 1979) (where verdict form may create confusion, court must ensure jury’s intent is clear).

Unit preserved this issue. Although Rule 51 requires a timely and specific objection to instructional error, it does not bar review where the complaint is that the court failed to resolve an ambiguous verdict before discharge. See *Neu v. Grant*, 548 F.2d 281, 287 (10th Cir. 1977); *Merritt v. Dillard Paper Co.*, 120 F.3d 1181, 1186 (11th Cir. 1997) (issue preserved where party timely raised concern before jury discharged). Unit promptly asked the court, before discharge, to question the jury to clarify the damage awards. That was enough. See *Diamond Shamrock*, 791 F.2d at 1428; *McDonnell*, 530 F.2d at 1389. The court’s refusal foreclosed the only practical method of discovering the jury’s actual intent.

Nor can the judgment be sustained as a harmless error. Where an ambiguous verdict leaves the reviewing court unable to tell whether the plaintiff was awarded a net recovery or was denied damages altogether, prejudice is inherent. See *Kuehne & Nagel (AG & Co.) v. Geosource, Inc.*, 874 F.2d 283, 288 (5th Cir. 1989) (ambiguous verdict affecting damages cannot be left to judicial speculation); *Wheeler v. John Deere Co.*, 862 F.2d 1404, 1411 (10th Cir. 1988) (new trial warranted where verdict inconsistency prevents reliable judgment). A jury that awarded Unit $70,768.02 on its contract claim may well have intended that amount to represent the net amount recoverable after accounting for Enron’s repair costs. The district court had no basis to assume otherwise. See *Dopp v. Pritzker*, 38 F.3d 1239, 1245 (10th Cir. 1994) (court may not speculate about jury reasoning).

The proper remedy is reversal and remand for clarification or, at minimum, a new trial on damages. See *Diamond Shamrock*, 791 F.2d at 1428; *Wheeler*, 862 F.2d at 1411. Because the court entered judgment inconsistent with the jury’s own findings and without first resolving the ambiguity, Unit was denied the benefit of the verdict it actually received.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for entry of a proper damages judgment or for a new trial on damages.
