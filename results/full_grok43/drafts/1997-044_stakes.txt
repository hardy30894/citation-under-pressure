**ARGUMENT**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only “any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent obtained by” confines the exception to the value actually procured through the fraud. Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 282 n.2 (1991). Treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are not money obtained by fraud; they are a statutory penalty imposed after the fact. In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir. 1992). Only the $31,382.50 in excess rent satisfies the statutory limitation.

The Code’s “fresh start” policy reinforces this textual limit. Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). Exceptions to discharge are construed narrowly, Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915), and apply solely to “honest but unfortunate debtor[s],” Grogan, 498 U.S. at 286-87. A debtor who has already made full restitution of the fraudulently obtained sum has satisfied the only debt Congress placed beyond discharge.

The contrary view—that any judgment traceable to fraudulent conduct is nondischargeable—would erase the “to the extent obtained by” qualifier and convert every statutory multiplier into a nondischargeable debt. That reading finds no support in the statute’s text or in this Court’s precedents interpreting parallel exceptions. See, e.g., Kawaauhau v. Geiger, 523 U.S. 57, 62 (1998) (requiring deliberate or intentional injury for § 523(a)(6)).

Because the $94,147.50 treble-damages award exceeds the sum actually obtained by fraud, it is dischargeable under Chapter 7.
