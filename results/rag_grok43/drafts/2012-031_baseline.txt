**ARGUMENT**

The district court abused its discretion by certifying the class without requiring plaintiffs to demonstrate by a preponderance of admissible evidence that damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement. Rule 23(b)(3) demands that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This Court has instructed that certification requires a “rigorous analysis” that may “entail some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff’s underlying claim.” Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 131 S. Ct. 2541, 2551 (2011). The Third Circuit’s own precedent confirms that the analysis must extend to damages: a plaintiff must show “a common methodology to quantify damages on a class-wide basis.” In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig., 552 F.3d 305, 311 (3d Cir. 2008).

The district court failed to apply this standard. It accepted Dr. McClave’s regression model even though the model measured damages flowing from four distinct theories of antitrust impact, only one of which survived the court’s own narrowing order. The model therefore calculated overcharges attributable to conduct the court had excluded from the case. Because the model could not isolate damages caused by the single remaining theory—deterrence of overbuilders—it supplied no evidence that damages were measurable on a class-wide basis.

A finding that common evidence can prove class-wide impact does not relieve plaintiffs of the separate obligation to prove that damages are likewise measurable by common evidence. Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 311. The district court’s contrary conclusion rested on the erroneous premise that any plausible damages methodology satisfies predominance. That premise conflicts with the requirement that the methodology “actually measure damages that result from the wrong.” The court’s acceptance of an overinclusive model therefore constituted an abuse of discretion.

The error is not harmless. Without a damages model tied to the sole remaining theory of liability, individual questions of damages would predominate, defeating certification under Rule 23(b)(3). The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
