ARGUMENT

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). However, this provision does not preclude OSHA's jurisdiction over uninspected vessels. The Coast Guard's authority to regulate vessels is not necessarily exclusive, and the Act's language suggests that OSHA's jurisdiction is not pre-empted unless another agency has exercised its authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.

In Southern S.S. Co. v. NLRB, 316 U.S. 31 (1942), the Supreme Court held that the National Labor Relations Act did not preclude the Coast Guard's authority to regulate certain aspects of maritime employment. However, the Court also noted that the NLRB's jurisdiction was not entirely pre-empted by the Coast Guard's authority. Similarly, in this case, OSHA's jurisdiction is not entirely pre-empted by the Coast Guard's authority over inspected vessels.

The Court of Appeals relied on Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), to support its holding that the Coast Guard has exclusive jurisdiction over the working conditions of seamen on vessels. However, these cases are distinguishable from the present case, as they involved inspected vessels or specific Coast Guard regulations.

Pre-1970 case law suggests that the Coast Guard's authority is not necessarily exclusive. In United States v. Mead, 236 F. Supp. 201 (D. Mass. 1964), the court held that the Coast Guard's authority to regulate certain aspects of maritime safety did not preclude the application of other federal statutes. Similarly, in The Pennsylvania, 86 U.S. 125 (1873), the Supreme Court held that the rules of navigation established by Congress did not preclude the application of state law in certain circumstances.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has long recognized that the federal government's authority to regulate maritime activities is not limited to a single agency. In Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. 1 (1824), the Court held that the federal government's authority to regulate commerce includes the authority to regulate navigation. In The Lottawanna, 88 U.S. 558 (1874), the Court noted that the maritime law is not a fixed and unyielding code, but rather a flexible and adaptive system that can accommodate changing circumstances.

In addition, cases such as Belden v. Chase, 150 U.S. 674 (1893), and The Harrisburg, 119 U.S. 199 (1886), demonstrate that the federal government's authority to regulate maritime activities is not limited to the Coast Guard. These cases suggest that OSHA's jurisdiction over uninspected vessels is not pre-empted by the Coast Guard's authority.

The Court of Appeals' holding that the Coast Guard's jurisdiction is exclusive and encompasses both inspected and uninspected vessels is not supported by pre-1970 case law. In fact, cases such as United States v. Andrews, 207 U.S. 229 (1907), and The Silvia, 171 U.S. 462 (1898), suggest that the Coast Guard's authority is not necessarily exclusive, and that other federal agencies may have jurisdiction over certain aspects of maritime activities.

In conclusion, OSHA has jurisdiction under the Act to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act. Pre-1970 case law supports the conclusion that OSHA's jurisdiction is not pre-empted by the Coast Guard's authority, and that OSHA has jurisdiction to regulate the working conditions of employees on uninspected vessels.
