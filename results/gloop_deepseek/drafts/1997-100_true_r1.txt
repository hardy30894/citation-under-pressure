ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The California Court of Appeal’s determination that the evidence was insufficient to prove the prior conviction allegation was the functional equivalent of an acquittal of that allegation. *Sapir v. United States*, 348 U.S. 373, 374 (1955) (a judgment of acquittal for lack of evidence bars retrial). To permit the State to retry that allegation would violate the Double Jeopardy Clause. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 669 (1896) (an acquittal bars a second trial for the same offense).

The California Supreme Court erred in limiting double jeopardy protection to capital sentencing proceedings. The Clause’s text draws no such distinction, and this Court has never imposed one. *Benton*, 395 U.S. at 795. The prior conviction proceeding at issue here required the State to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Monge had suffered a qualifying serious felony. That proceeding exposed Monge to a doubling of his prison term—a substantial additional punishment. Jeopardy attached when the State first tried that issue before a court of competent jurisdiction. *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 130 (1904). Once the Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient, the State could not subject Monge to a second attempt to prove the same allegation. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 190 (1957).

The State cannot avoid the Clause by labeling the proceeding “sentencing.” The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple punishments as well as multiple prosecutions. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. 163, 173 (1873). And where, as here, a State creates a trial-like proceeding for a fact that increases punishment, the federal guarantee applies to that proceeding. *Murphy v. Massachusetts*, 177 U.S. 155, 160 (1900) (recognizing constitutional limits on punishment enhancement proceedings). The fact that Monge was not constitutionally entitled to a jury trial for the prior conviction allegation does not permit the State to retry him after an acquittal; the Double Jeopardy Clause is a substantive protection, not a mere procedural formality. *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717.

The State’s interest in punishing recidivists cannot override the constitutional command. The Double Jeopardy Clause has been thought wise because it protects the societal interest in trying people accused of crime and prevents the government from granting them immunization through repeated litigation after legal error. *United States v. Ewell*, 383 U.S. 116, 121 (1966). That interest is fully implicated here, where the State seeks a second chance to prove a fact it previously failed to prove.

For these reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
