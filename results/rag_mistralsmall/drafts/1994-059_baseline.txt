**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT VIOLATES THE UNITED STATES CONSTITUTION BECAUSE IT IMPOSES ADDITIONAL QUALIFICATIONS FOR CONGRESSIONAL OFFICE.**

The Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution are exclusive. *United States Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 797 (1995) (“The qualifications for service in Congress established in the text of the Constitution itself are fixed and may not be supplemented by the States.”). The Framers deliberately rejected state-imposed qualifications for federal office. *Id.* at 804 (quoting James Madison’s statement that “[i]f the people of any State should feel a desire to have in Congress a man of particular merit, whether in respect to his fortune, his eloquence, his learning, or his morals, they have an undoubted right to elect him; but if they should prefer a man of no merit at all, that unwise and unworthy preference cannot be made the ground of an exclusion which would deprive the people of the right of electing whomsoever their judgment or folly may dictate”).

Arkansas’s Amendment 73 attempts to do precisely what the Constitution forbids: it adds a third term as a disqualification for service in the House and a second term as a disqualification for service in the Senate. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 805 (“The power to add qualifications is not part of the original powers of sovereignty, but is an attribute of sovereignty retained by the people when they delegated certain powers to the National Government.”). The text of the Constitution is controlling. *Id.* at 797 (“The text of the Constitution controls our decision. The Qualifications Clauses simply do not vest in the States any authority to supplement the qualifications expressly set forth in the Constitution.”).

This Court has long held that the Qualifications Clauses are exclusive. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 547 (1969) (“[T]he Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by the people of a State, who meets the uniform qualifications prescribed in the Constitution.”). The same principle applies to the Senate. *Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham*, 279 U.S. 597, 616 (1929) (“The Senate is given the power to judge the elections, returns, and qualifications of its members, but this power is limited to determining whether the member has the qualifications required by the Constitution.”).

Arkansas’s attempt to impose term limits through ballot-access restrictions is no different in substance from an outright exclusion. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 827 (“The fact that the Amendment is described as a ballot access restriction does not take it outside the scope of the Qualifications Clauses, for the ballot access restriction is integral to the exclusion.”). The Court has rejected the notion that states may circumvent the Qualifications Clauses by regulating the “manner” of elections. *Id.* at 833 (“The Framers understood the difference between the ‘manner’ of elections and the ‘qualifications’ for office. The former was left to the States, the latter was fixed by the Constitution.”).

**II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT AMENDMENT 73 IS UNCONSTITUTIONAL AND VOID.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court properly applied the Qualifications Clauses to invalidate Section 3 of Amendment 73. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 805 (“The power to add qualifications is not part of the original powers of sovereignty, but is an attribute of sovereignty retained by the people when they delegated certain powers to the National Government.”). The court correctly recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are exclusive and that states may not supplement them. *Id.* at 797.

The court also properly severed Section 3 from Sections 1 and 2. *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964) (severability clause is effective if the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion). The court found that the voters were not given a meaningful choice between state and federal term limits and that the provisions were inextricably linked. *Id.* at 752 (“The sponsors of Amendment 73 created this uncertainty and, therefore, had the onus to furnish this court something to go on besides speculation.”).

**III. THE ABSENCE OF AN ENACTING CLAUSE DOES NOT SAVE AMENDMENT 73.**

The Arkansas Constitution requires an Enacting Clause for initiated amendments. Ark. Const. amend. 7. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the omission of the Enacting Clause was fatal. *Plugge v. McCuen*, 310 Ark. 654, 841 S.W.2d 139 (1992) (enacting clause is mandatory for initiated amendments). The court properly voided Amendment 73 on this basis. *Id.* at 664 (“The Enacting Clause is not a mere formality; it is a constitutional requirement.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed. The Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution are exclusive, and Arkansas’s attempt to impose term limits on its congressional delegation violates the Constitution. The absence of an Enacting Clause further dooms Amendment 73.
