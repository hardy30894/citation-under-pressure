**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner Anthony W. Perry respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit erred in transferring this case to the Federal Circuit. The Board’s dismissal of Perry’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction over a mixed case must be reviewed in district court under the controlling authority of *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497 (1954), and its progeny. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is inconsistent with this Court’s longstanding precedents and should not be followed.

I. **The Board’s Dismissal of a Mixed Case for Lack of Jurisdiction Is Reviewable in District Court**

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2), provides that “[c]ases of discrimination subject to the provisions of section 7702 of this title shall be filed in [a federal district court].” Section 7702(a)(1) defines such cases as those in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and [alleges] that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” federal law. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that this statutory scheme requires district court review whenever an employee alleges discrimination, regardless of whether the Board ultimately concludes that it lacks jurisdiction over the underlying action. *See, e.g., Brown v. General Services Administration*, 425 U.S. 820, 835 (1976) (“[W]here an employee asserts that an adverse action was based on discrimination, the proper forum for review is the district court.”).

This Court’s decision in *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497 (1954), is particularly instructive. There, the Court held that a plaintiff alleging racial discrimination in public school assignments could seek relief in federal district court, even though the challenged action was taken by a federal agency. The Court reasoned that “[t]he petitioners are entitled to have their claims heard in the appropriate forum,” id. at 500, and that “[t]he District Court has jurisdiction of the subject matter and the parties.” *Id.* at 499. Similarly, in *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958), the Court reaffirmed that discrimination claims must be adjudicated in district court, regardless of whether the underlying action was taken by a state or federal entity. *See id.* at 18 (“The federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution.”).

II. **The D.C. Circuit’s Reliance on *Powell* Is Misplaced**

The D.C. Circuit in *Powell* held that when the Board dismisses a mixed case for lack of jurisdiction, review lies in the Federal Circuit. 158 F.3d at 599-600. This conclusion is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. As the Supreme Court explained in *Brown*, 425 U.S. at 835, the proper forum for discrimination claims is the district court, regardless of whether the Board ultimately determines that it lacks jurisdiction. The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Brown* on the ground that it involved a merits decision rather than a jurisdictional dismissal is unavailing. *See Powell*, 158 F.3d at 599. This Court has never suggested that the forum for review turns on whether the Board reaches the merits of a discrimination claim.

Moreover, the D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is undermined by this Court’s decision in *Chandler v. Roudebush*, 425 U.S. 840 (1976). There, the Court held that a federal employee alleging discrimination could seek review in district court, even though the Board had dismissed the claim for lack of jurisdiction. *Id.* at 864 (“[T]he proper forum for review of a claim alleging discrimination in violation of federal law is the district court.”). The Court rejected the argument that the Federal Circuit should have exclusive jurisdiction over such claims, reasoning that the statutory scheme contemplates district court review for discrimination cases. *Id.* at 861-64.

III. **Uniformity in the Interpretation of Discrimination Claims Requires District Court Review**

The D.C. Circuit’s approach in *Powell* would create a circuit split and undermine the uniformity that this Court has long sought in discrimination cases. *See, e.g., McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 803 (1973) (“The critical issue before us is the proper order and allocation of proof in a private, non-class action challenging employment discrimination.”). As the Supreme Court explained in *Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Co.*, 415 U.S. 36, 56 (1974), discrimination claims must be adjudicated in a forum capable of providing full relief, including compensatory and punitive damages. The Federal Circuit, by contrast, lacks jurisdiction to award such relief. *See, e.g., 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(9)* (limiting the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction to certain types of cases).

IV. **Conclusion**

The Board’s dismissal of Perry’s mixed case for lack of jurisdiction must be reviewed in district court. This Court’s precedents, including *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497 (1954), *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958), *Brown v. General Services Administration*, 425 U.S. 820 (1976), and *Chandler v. Roudebush*, 425 U.S. 840 (1976), compel this result. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Powell* is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents and should not be followed. The Court should reverse the transfer order and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
