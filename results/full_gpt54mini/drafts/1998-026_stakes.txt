**Argument**

A district court’s failure to advise a defendant of the right to appeal, while contrary to the Federal Rules, does not automatically entitle the defendant to habeas relief. Relief is available only if the omission caused prejudice. In **Peguero v. United States**, the Supreme Court held that “when a defendant is aware of his right to appeal and yet chooses not to appeal, the district court’s failure to advise the defendant of his right to appeal does not entitle the defendant to habeas relief.” **526 U.S. 23, 27-30 (1999).** The Court explained that the purpose of Rule 32 is to ensure that a defendant knows of the right to appeal; if that purpose has already been satisfied, there is no basis to vacate the sentence. **Id.**

That rule follows the Court’s broader habeas jurisprudence requiring prejudice, not mere formal error, before collateral relief is granted. In **Hill v. United States**, the Court rejected collateral relief for a Rule 32 violation where the defendant showed no “fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice.” **368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962).** Likewise, in **United States v. Timmreck**, the Court held that a technical violation of the Federal Rules does not justify habeas relief absent a showing of “a complete miscarriage of justice” or “an omission inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure.” **441 U.S. 780, 783-85 (1979).** And in **United States v. Frady**, the Court reaffirmed that collateral review is not a substitute for direct appeal and requires a showing of actual prejudice. **456 U.S. 152, 164-68 (1982).**

Applying those principles here, respondent is not entitled to relief because he knew of his right to appeal when judgment was entered and chose not to pursue one. The district court’s factual finding on knowledge forecloses any claim of prejudice. As **Peguero** makes clear, the Rule 32 omission is harmless where the defendant already knew of the right the rule is meant to communicate. **526 U.S. at 27-30.** A contrary rule would convert a notice provision into an automatic windfall, permitting collateral relief even where the defendant suffered no deprivation of appellate rights. Nothing in the Rules or in habeas doctrine requires such a result.

Nor does the failure to give the advisement amount to structural error requiring automatic vacatur. The Court has reserved automatic reversal for a narrow class of fundamental defects, such as total denial of counsel or a biased judge. **Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279, 309-10 (1991); Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 468-69 (1997).** A Rule 32 advisement error is not comparable. It does not infect the trial framework, and it does not necessarily affect the outcome. Where the defendant knew of the right and declined to appeal, the omission caused no injury that collateral relief can redress. **Peguero, 526 U.S. at 27-30.**

Petitioner may argue that Rule 32’s command is mandatory and that any violation requires vacation of the sentence. But the Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected that premise in the habeas context. **Timmreck, 441 U.S. at 785**; **Hill, 368 U.S. at 428**. The question is not whether the district court complied with the rule in the first instance; it is whether the violation warrants the extraordinary remedy of collateral relief. Under **Peguero**, it does not when the defendant already knew he could appeal. **526 U.S. at 27-30.**

Petitioner may also contend that the omission deprived him of a meaningful opportunity to decide whether to appeal. But the record finding that he was aware of the right defeats that claim. The Court in **Peguero** rejected relief on materially identical facts, holding that knowledge of the right eliminates the prejudice necessary for habeas relief. **Id.** If anything, the proper remedy for a Rule 32 violation would be to restore an appellate opportunity when the defendant lacked notice; where notice already existed, there is nothing to restore. **Id.**

Because respondent suffered no prejudice from the sentencing court’s omission, the judgment below should be affirmed. **Peguero v. United States, 526 U.S. 23 (1999).**
