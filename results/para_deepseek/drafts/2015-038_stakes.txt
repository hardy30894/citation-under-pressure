**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 103(c) of ANILCA Does Not Strip the National Park Service of Authority to Regulate Non-Federal Lands Within Conservation System Units.**

The plain text of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (“ANILCA”) § 103(c), 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c), provides: “Only those public lands within the boundaries of any conservation system unit which are public lands as defined in this Act shall be deemed to be included in such unit.” The statute defines “public lands” to exclude lands owned by the State of Alaska. 16 U.S.C. § 3102(2). But the limitation operates solely on the inclusion of *lands* within a unit; it does not restrict the National Park Service’s (“NPS”) regulatory jurisdiction over the unit’s boundaries as a whole. As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, the hovercraft ban—36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e)—prohibits operation of hovercraft on “all lands and waters administered by the National Park Service,” not merely on “public lands” as defined by ANILCA. Because the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve is a unit of the National Park System, the regulation applies uniformly throughout its boundaries, including state-owned submerged lands and waters.

This reading respects the statutory structure. Section 103(c) exists to prevent state lands from being *considered part* of a unit for purposes of federal ownership or withdrawal, but it does not divest NPS of its pre-existing authority under the National Park Service Organic Act, 16 U.S.C. § 1 (now 54 U.S.C. § 100751), which empowers the Secretary to “promulgate and enforce regulations” for “the proper use, management, government, and protection” of park units. *See United States v. Brown*, 552 F.2d 817, 821 (8th Cir. 1977) (upholding NPS enforcement on private inholdings). ANILCA’s own savings clause reinforces this conclusion: “Nothing in [ANILCA] shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. The Organic Act is such an “existing la[w],” and no provision of ANILca specifically repeals NPS’s regulatory power over non-federal lands. Congress knows how to limit agency jurisdiction when it intends to; for example, ANILCA § 103(c) itself speaks only to deemed inclusion of lands, not to regulatory reach. *See Barnhart v. Peabody Coal Co.*, 537 U.S. 149, 163 (2003) (courts presume Congress “acts intentionally and purposely when it includes particular language in one section but omits it in another”).

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation also harmonizes with the purpose of ANILCA, which was to preserve large conservation systems while respecting state and private ownership. Congress balanced these interests by limiting the *land base* of units, not the NPS’s ability to enforce uniform regulations needed to protect park resources and visitors. The hovercraft ban—designed to prevent noise, erosion, and wildlife disturbance—applies to the preserve as a whole. Allowing operators to ignore the ban simply because the water is state-owned would undermine the very conservation purposes ANILCA sought to achieve. *Cf. Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 540 (1976) (recognizing federal power to protect public lands and resources).

**II. Even If the Statute Is Ambiguous, the Agency’s Reasonable Interpretation Is Entitled to Deference.**

Should this Court find ambiguity in Section 103(c), the NPS’s consistent interpretation that it retains regulatory authority over non-federal lands within park units is reasonable and entitled to deference under *Chevron, U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984). The agency has applied park regulations to state-owned lands and waters within Alaskan units for decades, a practice Congress has not disturbed. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 94-1569, at 24 (1976) (rejecting earlier attempts to limit NPS regulatory reach). The Ninth Circuit correctly found that “NPS’s current view comports with the text of the statute.” *Sturgeon v. Masica*, No. 3:11-CV-0183-HRH, 2013 WL 5888230, at *8 (D. Alaska Oct. 30, 2013), *aff’d sub nom. Sturgeon v. Frost*, 800 F.3d 1093 (9th Cir. 2015). Deference is especially appropriate given the agency’s expertise in managing complex conservation units and the need for uniform regulation to protect resource values.

**III. The Hovercraft Ban Is a Valid Exercise of NPS Authority.**

Because ANILCA does not limit NPS’s regulatory jurisdiction to federal lands, the hovercraft ban—a content-neutral, uniform regulation—applies to Sturgeon’s use of his craft on the Nation River within the preserve. The regulation is consistent with the Organic Act and the preservation mandate of the Park System. The Ninth Circuit’s judgment should be affirmed.
