# ARGUMENT

## Due Process Permits Montana to Exercise Specific Jurisdiction over Ford Because Ford's Continuous, Purposeful Activities in Montana Are Sufficiently Related to Gullett's Claims.

### A. Due process requires only that the defendant's forum contacts make the suit fair, not that the specific unit sold trace a direct causal path to the forum.

The touchstone of specific jurisdiction has always been fairness, not mechanical causation. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), holds that due process demands only "minimum contacts" with the forum such that maintaining suit there does not offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *Id.* at 316. The Court explained that "to the extent that a corporation exercises the privilege of conducting activities within a state, it enjoys the benefits and protection of the laws of that state," and may therefore be called to answer for "obligations" connected to those activities. *Id.* at 319. Nothing in *International Shoe* requires that the very unit of a mass-produced product first enter the forum by sale there; it requires only that the defendant's forum activities be of the kind and character that generate the litigation.

This Court's decision in *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352 (1927), confirms the point. There the Court sustained jurisdiction over a nonresident motorist based on his use of the forum's highways—the very instrumentality that caused the plaintiff's injury—even though the defendant had no other presence in the state. *Id.* at 356. Ford's vehicles, like the automobile in *Hess*, travel and are driven on Montana's highways as a direct consequence of Ford's nationwide distribution scheme; Ford cannot disclaim the consequences of that scheme merely because a dealer intermediary, rather than Ford itself, placed this particular vehicle into the stream of commerce leading to Montana.

### B. Ford purposefully availed itself of the Montana market for the very category of product that injured Gullett.

*Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235 (1958), requires "some act by which the defendant purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State." *Id.* at 253. Ford satisfies that standard many times over: it advertises, markets, sells, and services Explorers—the exact model at issue—throughout Montana, and supplies replacement parts, including tires, to Montana dealers and consumers. That Ford's contacts need not be tied to the single originating sale is settled by *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220 (1957), which held that "it is sufficient for purposes of due process that the suit was based on a contract which had substantial connection with that State," even though the defendant's only forum contact was a single mailed policy. *Id.* at 223. Ford's Montana contacts, involving continuous sales and servicing of thousands of Explorers, are vastly more substantial than the single contract in *McGee*. Likewise, in *Travelers Health Ass'n v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643 (1950), the Court upheld state authority over a foreign association that solicited business by mail in the forum, reasoning that such purposeful solicitation of the local market justified the exercise of jurisdiction over disputes arising from that market activity. *Id.* at 649.

### C. Jurisdiction over manufacturers whose products enter a state through an established distribution network and there cause injury has long been recognized as consistent with due process.

State courts applying *International Shoe* have consistently upheld jurisdiction over manufacturers whose products, though not sold directly in the forum by the manufacturer, reach the forum through a foreseeable distribution chain and injure a forum resident. In *Gray v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp.*, 22 Ill. 2d 432, 176 N.E.2d 761 (1961), the Illinois Supreme Court held that a valve manufacturer that sold no product directly in Illinois was nonetheless subject to jurisdiction there because it placed its product into the stream of commerce with the expectation that it would be incorporated into products sold nationally, including in Illinois. *Id.* at 442. The Washington Supreme Court reached the same conclusion in *Tyee Construction Co. v. Dulien Steel Products, Inc.*, 62 Wash. 2d 106, 381 P.2d 245 (1963), and the Illinois Supreme Court similarly sustained jurisdiction in *Nelson v. Miller*, 11 Ill. 2d 378, 143 N.E.2d 673 (1957), emphasizing that a defendant who purposefully exploits a forum's market cannot escape accountability for injuries that market activity generates. The California Supreme Court likewise upheld jurisdiction over an out-of-state manufacturer in *Buckeye Boiler Co. v. Superior Court*, 71 Cal. 2d 893, 458 P.2d 57 (1969), on the ground that a manufacturer deriving benefit from a state's consumer market must accept the corresponding burden of defending suits there for injuries its products cause in that market. Ford's position is indistinguishable: it deliberately cultivated the Montana market for Explorers and their component parts, and it cannot now disclaim responsibility for the predictable consequence—an Explorer failing on a Montana highway.

### D. Montana's interest in providing a forum for its injured citizen further supports jurisdiction.

Finally, *Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437 (1952), confirms that fairness to both parties, and the forum's own interest in adjudicating harms to its residents, are central to the due process calculus. *Id.* at 445–46. Montana has a manifest interest in providing its own courts to redress the death of one of its citizens caused by a product Ford chose to market pervasively within the State. Because Ford's Montana contacts are extensive, purposeful, and directly related to the category of product and risk at issue, the exercise of specific jurisdiction here comports fully with the due process principles established in *International Shoe* and its progeny.
