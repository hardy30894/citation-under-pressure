## Argument

### I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on the ADA and MHRA Claims Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Exist as to Whether Pine Lawn Regarded Epps as Disabled.

The Americans with Disabilities Act prohibits discrimination “against a qualified individual with a disability because of the disability of such individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a). A plaintiff may establish a “disability” by showing that the employer “regarded” him as having an impairment that substantially limits a major life activity. 42 U.S.C. § 12102(2)(C); *Murphy v. United Parcel Service, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 516, 521-22 (1999). The Missouri Human Rights Act is analyzed under the same framework. *Hill v. St. Louis Univ.*, 123 S.W.3d 246, 249 (Mo. Ct. App. 2003).

Here, the record contains ample evidence that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as substantially limited in the major life activity of working. Chief Hardy’s September 29, 1999 memorandum relied on Dr. Mirkin’s note that Epps “would very likely need to seek a different type of occupation,” and cited the “extensive amount of sick time” and “number of workers’ compensation claims” to conclude that Epps was “unable to perform his duties.” His March 17, 2000 memorandum reiterated that Dr. Mirkin concluded Epps “will never be able to function as a regular officer.” The City then terminated Epps because it believed he could “never” perform the essential duties of a police officer—bending, squatting, running.

A person is regarded as substantially limited in working when the employer believes the individual is “significantly restricted in the ability to perform either a class of jobs or a broad range of jobs in various classes.” *Wooten v. Farmland Foods*, 58 F.3d 382, 386 (8th Cir. 1995); *see also* 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(j)(3)(i). The evidence that Hardy and the City thought Epps could never be a “regular officer” and must seek a “different occupation” creates a genuine issue whether the City regarded him as precluded from an entire class of law-enforcement jobs, not just his particular position. *Cf. Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 471, 491-92 (1999) (rejecting “regarded as” claim where employer excluded plaintiff from only a single job). The district court therefore erred in granting summary judgment on these claims.

### II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on the Workers’ Compensation Retaliation Claim Because Sovereign Immunity Does Not Bar Claims Under Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780.

Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780 provides that “[n]o employer … shall discharge … any employee because of the employee’s exercise of his or her rights” under the workers’ compensation law. This statute creates a separate, statutory cause of action that is not subject to the general sovereign immunity defense available to public entities. *Griffin v. City of St. Louis*, 843 S.W.2d 393, 395-96 (Mo. Ct. App. 1992) (holding that § 287.780 “creates a distinct statutory tort” and that “sovereign immunity does not bar a claim brought pursuant to § 287.780”). See also *Brown v. City of Ellisville*, 921 S.W.2d 155, 156-57 (Mo. Ct. App. 1996) (same). The district court’s reliance on the general sovereign immunity statute, Mo. Rev. Stat. § 537.600, was misplaced. Epps alleged that Pine Lawn terminated him because he filed multiple workers’ compensation claims. The timing of the termination—shortly after his second claim and after Hardy’s memoranda referencing the “number of workers’ compensation claims”—raises a triable issue of retaliatory motive. Summary judgment on this count should be reversed.

### Conclusion

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s grant of summary judgment on the ADA, MHRA, and workers’ compensation retaliation claims, and remand for further proceedings.
