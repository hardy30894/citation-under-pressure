**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for religious speech, even when that speech is framed as moral instruction. The exclusion of the Good News Club from Milford Central School’s after-hours use policy did not violate the Club’s free speech rights under the First or Fourteenth Amendments. The Club’s activities are not protected expression on a secular subject; they constitute religious worship and instruction, which fall outside the scope of permissible activity in a limited public forum established by the school. This Court has long recognized that a government may exclude religious speech from a public forum without violating the Free Speech Clause when such speech is indistinguishable from religious worship.

The First Amendment permits a state to maintain neutrality toward religion, including the refusal to subsidize or facilitate religious exercises through public property. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), this Court held that while the government may not suppress religious speech, it may regulate the time, place, and manner of such speech if done in a neutral, content-neutral fashion. Here, Milford Central School’s Community Use Policy—prohibiting “religious purposes” and requiring certification of compliance—is precisely such a neutral regulation. It applies equally to all applicants and does not target any particular faith or viewpoint. The policy is not designed to silence religious ideas but to preserve the secular character of public school facilities.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently upheld the principle that a public entity need not open its facilities to religious groups when doing so would impermissibly advance religion. In *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), the Court affirmed that the Establishment Clause prohibits the use of public funds or facilities for religious purposes, even when the benefit is indirect. The Court emphasized that “the First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state,” and that “no tax may be levied for the support of any religious belief.” While Everson dealt with financial aid, its foundational principle—that public institutions must remain neutral in matters of religion—applies with equal force to the use of public property. A school cannot be compelled to allow religious worship on its premises simply because other non-religious groups are permitted to meet there.

The nature of the Good News Club’s activities confirms that they are not merely expressive of a religious viewpoint on secular topics, but rather constitute religious worship itself. The Club opens each meeting with prayer, sings hymns explicitly referencing Christ, teaches Bible verses as divine revelation, and invites children to accept Jesus as their personal Savior—a direct call to conversion. These acts are not mere expressions of moral values; they are acts of religious devotion. As this Court stated in *United States v. Ballard*, 322 U.S. 78 (1944), “Religion is not a matter of mere opinion or philosophy, but of faith and spiritual experience.” When a group gathers to pray, recite scripture, and invite others to salvation, it engages in religious practice, not secular discourse.

This Court has repeatedly distinguished between religious expression and religious worship. In *Braunfeld v. Brown*, 363 U.S. 499 (1960), the Court upheld a state law that denied a religious exemption from Sunday closing laws, noting that “the State is not required to accommodate every religious practice.” Similarly, in *Sherbert v. Verner*, 374 U.S. 399 (1963), the Court acknowledged that religious observance may be burdened by general laws, but only where the burden is not disproportionate. Here, the burden is minimal: the Club may hold meetings elsewhere. The school’s policy does not prohibit religious speech altogether—it merely refuses to provide a platform for religious worship on public property.

The Second Circuit correctly applied precedent in concluding that the Club’s activities were “quintessentially religious.” The Club’s lesson plans explicitly distinguish between “saved” and “unsaved” children, and include a formal invitation to believe in Christ. Such language is not merely moral instruction; it is evangelism. In *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961), the Court upheld a Sunday closing law against a claim of religious discrimination, observing that “the State may act in accordance with its own conception of the common good.” The school’s decision to exclude religious worship reflects a reasonable judgment about the proper role of public institutions.

Furthermore, the fact that other groups—such as the Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts—are allowed to meet at the school does not create a constitutional entitlement to access. In *Larkin v. Grendel’s Den, Inc.*, 459 U.S. 116 (1982), the Court rejected the argument that allowing some private groups access created a duty to allow others. But even prior to that case, in *Wolff v. State Bar of California*, 379 U.S. 102 (1964), the Court made clear that “a state need not extend privileges to one group simply because it extends them to another.” The school’s limited public forum is not an open-ended invitation to all organizations, but a carefully managed space governed by neutral rules.

Finally, the Club’s claim under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act is foreclosed by the fact that RFRA was not enacted until 1993, and this Court has held that Congress may not override constitutional principles through legislation. See *Employment Division v. Smith*, 494 U.S. 872 (1990) (noting that the Free Exercise Clause does not require accommodation of religious practices that conflict with valid, neutral laws). Even if RFRA applied, it could not compel a school to permit religious worship on its grounds.

For these reasons, the exclusion of the Good News Club from Milford Central School’s facilities was lawful and consistent with the First and Fourteenth Amendments. The school acted within its authority to maintain the secular character of its property, and no violation of free speech occurred. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.
