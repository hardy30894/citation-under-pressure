**ARGUMENT**

The decision below cannot be reconciled with this Court’s longstanding habeas jurisprudence. A procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot serve as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of a separate habeas claim. To hold otherwise would eviscerate the finality of state-court judgments and undermine the exhaustion requirement that has governed federal habeas review for decades.

**I. A Procedurally Defaulted Claim Cannot Itself Constitute “Cause”**

This Court has consistently held that a federal habeas petitioner must demonstrate that he has exhausted all available state remedies before seeking relief. *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116–17 (1944). Where a petitioner has failed to comply with state procedural rules, federal review is barred unless he can show both “cause” for the default and “prejudice” resulting therefrom. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 486–87 (1953); *Daniels v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 486–87 (1953). The “cause” requirement demands that the petitioner identify some external impediment—not merely another procedurally defaulted claim—that prevented compliance with the state’s rules. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438–39 (1963).

Here, Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim was itself procedurally defaulted when the Ohio Court of Appeals dismissed his application to reopen as untimely. That default cannot supply “cause” for the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. A defaulted claim is, by definition, one that the state courts have declined to consider on the merits. To allow such a claim to excuse a separate default would permit a petitioner to circumvent state procedural rules by simply alleging ineffective assistance—even where that allegation itself was never properly presented. This Court has never sanctioned such a bootstrap. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963) (emphasizing that federal courts must respect state procedural determinations).

**II. The Exhaustion Requirement Precludes Reliance on a Defaulted Claim**

The exhaustion doctrine, rooted in comity and federalism, requires that each claim be fairly presented to the state courts before it may be raised in a federal habeas petition. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 203–04 (1950). A claim that has been procedurally defaulted in state court is not “exhausted” in any meaningful sense; the state has been denied the opportunity to correct its own errors. *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. at 117. Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was never considered on the merits by the Ohio courts because it was untimely. That claim therefore cannot serve as a vehicle to revive his sufficiency claim, which was likewise defaulted.

This Court has long recognized that the right to effective assistance of counsel, while fundamental, must be asserted in a timely manner. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932); *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464–65 (1938). A petitioner who fails to raise an ineffective-assistance claim in accordance with state procedural rules cannot later rely on that claim to excuse another default. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 70 (1942) (holding that the right to effective counsel is not self-executing and must be preserved through proper objection). To hold otherwise would encourage piecemeal litigation and reward noncompliance with state rules.

**III. The Sixth Circuit’s Approach Conflicts with Settled Precedent**

The court below held that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could serve as cause regardless of whether that claim itself was defaulted. That holding cannot be squared with *Fay v. Noia*, which held that a petitioner who “deliberately bypasses” state procedures forfeits federal review. 372 U.S. at 438–39. Carpenter’s failure to timely file his application to reopen was not the result of any external impediment; it was a simple failure to comply with Ohio’s procedural rules. Under *Fay*, that failure bars reliance on the ineffective-assistance claim as cause.

Moreover, this Court has consistently required that a petitioner demonstrate “cause” for each defaulted claim individually. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. at 486–87. A defaulted claim cannot provide cause for another default because it is itself a claim that the state has been denied the opportunity to adjudicate. *Daniels v. Allen*, 344 U.S. at 487. The Sixth Circuit’s rule would allow a petitioner to circumvent this requirement by simply alleging ineffective assistance—even where that allegation is itself procedurally barred.

**IV. Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. A procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. This Court’s decisions in *Ex parte Hawk*, *Brown v. Allen*, *Daniels v. Allen*, *Fay v. Noia*, *Townsend v. Sain*, *Darr v. Burford*, *Powell v. Alabama*, *Johnson v. Zerbst*, and *Glasser v. United States* compel that result.
