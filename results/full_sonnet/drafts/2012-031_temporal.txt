**ARGUMENT**

**I. Rule 23(b)(3) Certification Requires Proof, Not Speculation, That Common Evidence Can Establish Class-Wide Damages.**

A class may be certified under Rule 23(b)(3) only where the proponent demonstrates, through admissible evidence, that common questions predominate over individual ones. This Court has long insisted that a party invoking a procedural device affecting the substantive rights of absent parties must make an evidentiary showing, not rest on assertion. In *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 370 F.2d 119 (2d Cir. 1966) (*Eisen I*), the Second Circuit held that a district court could not resolve the propriety of class treatment without examining whether the mechanics of adjudicating the claims—including calculation of relief—could actually be managed on a common basis. When the case returned on the question of damages methodology, the same court in *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 391 F.2d 555 (2d Cir. 1968) (*Eisen II*), scrutinized the proposed method for computing class-wide recovery and required that any mechanism for quantifying damages be practically and evidentially sound before the class device could go forward. The lesson of both decisions is the same: a court abdicates its responsibility if it certifies a class on the assumption that damages will somehow be provable in common, rather than on record evidence that they can be.

That principle tracks the general rule that findings supporting a significant judicial determination must have record support. As this Court explained in *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), a factual determination cannot stand where the reviewing court is "left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." A predominance finding resting on an admittedly flawed or overinclusive damages model is exactly such a mistake—it is not a permissible view of the evidence, because it is not tied to evidence of the claim actually certified for trial.

**II. Class-Wide Antitrust Damages Must Be Measured by a Methodology Tied to the Specific Theory of Violation Found, Not to Theories the Court Has Rejected.**

Antitrust damages are recoverable only for injury flowing from the violation actually proven. In *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100, 114 n.9 (1969), this Court reaffirmed that a plaintiff's injury must be "of the type that the statute was intended to forestall," and that damages must be causally connected to the specific unlawful conduct at issue—not to conduct that has been alleged but not accepted as a basis for liability. Similarly, in *Hanover Shoe, Inc. v. United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 392 U.S. 481, 489 (1968), the Court examined with care whether the damages methodology corresponded to the precise economic effect of the antitrust violation established, rather than to a broader universe of alleged harms.

These decisions confirm a basic evidentiary principle: a model that computes damages by reference to multiple theories of anticompetitive conduct—several of which the court has rejected as unsupported by common proof—cannot satisfy the predominance requirement merely because the same model happens to produce a class-wide number. The model must be capable of isolating damages attributable to the one theory the court has approved for class treatment.

**III. Although Antitrust Damages Need Not Be Proven with Mathematical Certainty, They Must Rest on a Rational, Case-Specific Evidentiary Foundation.**

This Court has never excused antitrust plaintiffs from grounding damages in the facts of the violation proven, even while relaxing the degree of arithmetical precision required. In *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264-65 (1946), the Court permitted a "just and reasonable estimate" of damages "based on relevant data," but only because the estimate was drawn from evidence of the actual injury the defendant's proven conduct caused. *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931), likewise required that damages be "traced to some definite cause," even as it tolerated some uncertainty in extent. And in *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927), the Court insisted that damages evidence supply "a basis for a reasonable estimate" that was not "speculative or illusory."

None of these decisions countenances a model that computes an aggregate damages figure by reference to conduct and theories the trial court has expressly excluded from the case. A method untethered to the certified theory of liability is not a "reasonable estimate" of the injury proven—it is speculation dressed as common proof.

**IV. The Damages Model Approved Below Fails This Standard.**

The District Court narrowed the case to a single theory of antitrust impact—deterrence of overbuilder entry—yet accepted a regression model designed to calculate damages flowing from four distinct theories of harm, only one of which survived. Nothing in the record isolates damages attributable to the overbuilder theory alone. Under *Zenith Radio*, *Hanover Shoe*, *Bigelow*, and *Story Parchment*, that failure is fatal: a model that cannot distinguish harm caused by the accepted theory from harm attributed to rejected theories supplies no rational, evidentiary basis for class-wide damages. Following *Eisen I* and *Eisen II*, the Court of Appeals was obligated to determine whether the proposed mechanism for calculating relief was evidentially sound before permitting the class device to proceed. It did not do so. The judgment below should be reversed.
