**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit’s Order Recalling the Mandate Restarted the 90-Day Certiorari Period**

The district court erred in holding that Buckles’s certiorari petition was untimely. Under Supreme Court Rule 13(3), the time to file a certiorari petition runs from “the date of the denial of rehearing” only if the lower court “appropriately entertains an untimely petition for rehearing.” But here, the Ninth Circuit did more: it *recalled its mandate* to consider Buckles’s motion for appointment of counsel. That recall effectively vacated the prior mandate and reset the clock. *See United States v. Kellington*, 217 F.3d 1084, 1098 (9th Cir. 2000) (recall of mandate “restores the jurisdiction of this court” and “vacates the prior mandate”). When the mandate reissued on June 20, 2007, the 90-day period began anew. Buckles filed his certiorari petition on September 5, 2007—within 90 days of June 20. *See Sup. Ct. R. 13(1)*.

The Supreme Court has recognized that a lower court’s order can affect the certiorari deadline. *In re McDonald*, 489 U.S. 180, 183 (1989) (per curiam) (time runs from “final action” of lower court). Here, the Ninth Circuit’s June 12 order denying the motion but recalling the mandate was the final action. *Cf. United States v. Houser*, 804 F.2d 565, 568 (9th Cir. 1986) (recall of mandate “reopens the case”). Because the mandate was recalled, the original mandate was no longer operative, and the certiorari clock should have started from the reissuance. *See also United States v. LaBonte*, 520 U.S. 751, 757 (1997) (deadlines are “strictly construed” but subject to court-ordered modifications). The district court’s contrary conclusion conflicts with *Kellington* and *Houser*.

**II. Alternatively, Equitable Tolling Applies Due to Attorney Misconduct and Clerk Misinformation**

Even if the certiorari clock was not restarted, Buckles is entitled to equitable tolling. The Supreme Court has held that equitable tolling is available in § 2255 proceedings when “extraordinary circumstances” prevented a timely filing. *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010). Buckles faced two such circumstances.

First, his retained counsel abandoned him in violation of Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e), which imposes a duty to assist in filing a motion for appointment of counsel. Counsel’s failure to respond to Buckles’s repeated requests constitutes “attorney misconduct” that warrants tolling. *See Maples v. Thomas*, 565 U.S. 266, 281 (2012) (attorney abandonment is an “extraordinary circumstance”); *Lott v. Mueller*, 304 F.3d 814, 821 (9th Cir. 2002) (tolling for attorney’s failure to file). Buckles diligently pursued his rights by mailing a letter to counsel and the Clerk on the 21st day, and later filing a pro se motion. *See Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 418 (2005) (tolling requires diligence).

Second, the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office provided misinformation. Buckles’s sister was told that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” Reliance on such official misinformation is a classic ground for equitable tolling. *See Pliler v. Ford*, 542 U.S. 225, 231 (2004) (court’s misleading advice can justify tolling); *United States v. Battles*, 362 F.3d 1195, 1197 (9th Cir. 2004) (tolling for “affirmative misconduct” by government). Buckles reasonably relied on that statement, believing he had 90 days from the recalled mandate. *See also Irwin v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs*, 498 U.S. 89, 96 (1990) (tolling when “claimant has been induced or tricked by his adversary’s misconduct”).

The district court failed to consider these circumstances. Because Buckles exercised diligence and faced extraordinary obstacles, equitable tolling should apply. *See Holland*, 560 U.S. at 653; *Ford v. Pliler*, 590 F.3d 782, 789 (9th Cir. 2009) (tolling for “unique circumstances” including court error). The judgment must be reversed.
