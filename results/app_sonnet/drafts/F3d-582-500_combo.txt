ARGUMENT

I. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because the Record Contains Genuine Disputes of Material Fact Concerning Erdman’s Hours of Service.

A movant is entitled to summary judgment only where the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, admits of no genuine dispute as to any material fact. See McTernan v. City of York, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009). That standard is not new; it traces to this Court’s longstanding obligation to draw every reasonable inference in the nonmovant’s favor. In United States v. Diebold, Inc., 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962), the Supreme Court held that “the inferences to be drawn from the underlying facts . . . must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion.” Likewise, in Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962), the Court cautioned that summary judgment must be used sparingly where “motive and intent play leading roles” and the relevant proof lies largely in the hands of the party seeking judgment—precisely the posture here, where Nationwide alone possesses the timekeeping records bearing on Erdman’s hours and the true reason for her discharge. And in Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944), the Court made clear that a district court may not resolve disputed factual inferences on paper simply because it finds one party’s account more persuasive.

The District Court disregarded these principles when it concluded, as a matter of law, that Erdman had not worked the 1,250 hours necessary to qualify as an “eligible employee.” The record—including Sarno’s testimony that she routinely approved Erdman’s extra hours, Erdman’s unanswered 2002 email to Getgen seeking confirmation that she could continue accruing “comp” time, and Getgen’s own concession in January 2003 that Erdman’s time calculations were correct—creates, at minimum, a genuine dispute as to how many compensable hours Erdman actually worked. That dispute cannot be resolved on summary judgment.

II. Nationwide’s Own Recordkeeping Deficiencies and Ambivalent Policies Cannot Be Used to Defeat Erdman’s Eligibility.

The FMLA, like the Fair Labor Standards Act which it complements, is remedial legislation designed to protect employees, and its hours-of-service requirement should not be construed to erect an “impossible hurdle” against an employee whose employer’s own conduct created the very ambiguity now invoked against her. Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687 (1946) (holding that “the remedial nature of this statute and the great public policy which it embodies militate against making that burden an impossible hurdle for the employee”). In Mt. Clemens Pottery, the Supreme Court held that where an employer’s records are inaccurate or inadequate, the employee may satisfy her burden through reasonable inference from the best available evidence, shifting to the employer the burden of coming forward with evidence of the precise amount of work performed. Id. at 687–88. Nationwide, which alternately authorized, ignored, and then abruptly forbade Erdman’s accrual of “comp” time, cannot now exploit the very inconsistency of its own supervisory instructions to deny her eligibility.

This result is consistent with the broader principle, announced in Tennessee Coal, Iron & R.R. Co. v. Muscoda Local No. 123, 321 U.S. 590 (1944), that provisions of remedial labor legislation defining compensable “work” must be interpreted broadly to serve their protective purpose, and with A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945), where the Court held that any exception to such “humanitarian and remedial legislation must . . . be narrowly construed.” The 1,250-hour threshold operates as a qualifying condition, not an exemption favoring employers, but the same interpretive caution applies: courts should not construe ambiguous hour calculations to strip employees of statutory protection where the employer itself sowed the confusion. See also Overnight Motor Transportation Co. v. Missel, 316 U.S. 572, 577 (1942) (recognizing that provisions governing compensable hours must be applied in a manner consistent with the statute’s remedial purpose).

III. Nationwide Should Be Estopped from Asserting an Eligibility Defense It Induced Through Its Own Representations.

Independent of the hours dispute, Nationwide is estopped from denying Erdman’s FMLA eligibility. A Nationwide human resources employee told Erdman, upon receipt of her leave request, that she saw “no problems” with it. Having induced Erdman to believe her request was valid, Nationwide cannot now disclaim the very eligibility it implicitly confirmed. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a party may be estopped from asserting a defense where its own conduct caused the opposing party’s detrimental reliance. See Glus v. Brooklyn Eastern District Terminal, 359 U.S. 231, 232–33 (1959) (explaining that it is “deeply rooted in our jurisprudence” that “no man may take advantage of his own wrong”). Nationwide’s shifting positions—permitting “comp” time, then forbidding it weeks before terminating Erdman, then assuring her that FMLA leave posed “no problems”—present exactly the sort of self-created ambiguity that equitable estoppel exists to prevent a party from exploiting.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the District Court’s grant of summary judgment on Erdman’s FMLA claims should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
