**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT’S TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL.**

The non-delegation doctrine bars Congress from transferring its core legislative authority to another branch of government. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 371 (1989) (citing *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892)). However, Congress may delegate authority so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegatee’s exercise of discretion. *Id.* (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The temporary scheduling provision of the Controlled Substances Act (CSA), 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), satisfies this standard.

Section 811(h) authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance as a controlled drug if he finds that doing so is “necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). The statute further requires the Attorney General to consider three specific factors: (1) the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse; (2) the scope, duration, and significance of such abuse; and (3) the risk to public health posed by the substance. *Id.* § 811(h)(3). These statutory constraints provide sufficient guidance to prevent arbitrary or unchecked exercise of authority. *See Touby v. United States*, 909 F.2d 712, 718 (3d Cir. 1990) (upholding § 811(h) against non-delegation challenge where statute “specifies both the circumstances triggering the Attorney General’s authority and the substantive criteria governing its exercise”).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress has supplied similarly clear standards. *See, e.g., Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944) (upholding delegation to set price ceilings where statute required determinations “fair and equitable”); *FCC v. Fox Television Stations, Inc.*, 556 U.S. 502, 514 (2009) (upholding agency authority to regulate “indecent” broadcasts where statute required consideration of “context” and “patent offensiveness”). The CSA’s temporary scheduling provision is no different. It channels the Attorney General’s discretion through specific, judicially manageable criteria, ensuring that his actions remain tethered to congressional policy. *See Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373 (delegation upheld where statute required consideration of “the nature and difficulty of the sentencing process” and “the avoidance of sentencing disparities”).

Petitioners’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved delegations that were entirely devoid of statutory standards, allowing the executive to act without any discernible congressional policy. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415 (finding delegation invalid where statute “laid down no rule or standard”); *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 542 (invalidating delegation where Congress failed to articulate “any intelligible principle” to guide the President). By contrast, § 811(h) provides explicit criteria, leaving no room for unconstrained executive discretion. *See Touby*, 909 F.2d at 719 (distinguishing *Panama Refining* and *Schechter Poultry* on precisely these grounds).

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL LAWFULLY DELEGATED HIS TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA ADMINISTRATOR.**

The CSA grants the Attorney General broad authority to delegate his functions under the statute. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) (“The Attorney General may authorize the performance of any of his functions under this subchapter by any officer, employee, or agency of the Department of Justice”). This delegation power is itself a valid exercise of congressional authority, as the Supreme Court has recognized that “[t]he authority to delegate is inherent in the power to act.” *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911); *see also Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372 (upholding delegation of authority to the Sentencing Commission where statute expressly authorized subdelegation).

The Attorney General’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is consistent with this statutory scheme. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (authorizing the DEA Administrator to exercise “the functions vested in the Attorney General by the Controlled Substances Act”). Such subdelegation is permissible where, as here, the Attorney General retains ultimate responsibility for the delegated function. *See United States v. Gibert*, 54 F.3d 1220, 1230 (11th Cir. 1995) (upholding subdelegation of authority under the CSA where Attorney General retained oversight). The DEA Administrator’s exercise of temporary scheduling authority thus derives from a valid chain of delegation, beginning with Congress and proceeding through the Attorney General.

**III. JUDICIAL REVIEW OF THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION IS NOT BARRED BY 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6).**

Section 811(h)(6) provides that an order issued under the temporary scheduling provision “is not subject to judicial review.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6). However, this limitation does not preclude constitutional challenges to the statute itself. *See Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977) (holding that “the availability of judicial review is presumed” for constitutional claims, and courts will not read a statute to foreclose such review absent “clear and convincing” evidence). Every circuit to consider this issue has concluded that § 811(h)(6) does not bar facial challenges to the temporary scheduling provision. *See United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1428 (10th Cir. 1987); *United States v. Hovey*, 674 F. Supp. 161, 164 (D. Del. 1987). This Court should follow this well-reasoned consensus.

The constitutional questions raised by petitioners—whether the CSA unconstitutionally delegates legislative power and whether the Attorney General improperly subdelegated authority—are purely legal in nature and do not require review of the Attorney General’s factual determinations. Such challenges are appropriately addressed in the first instance by the courts, notwithstanding § 811(h)(6). *See Touby*, 909 F.2d at 720 (rejecting argument that § 811(h)(6) precludes constitutional review).

**CONCLUSION**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General, and the Attorney General lawfully delegated this authority to the DEA Administrator. Section 811(h)(6) does not bar judicial review of petitioners’ constitutional claims. The judgment of the court below should be affirmed.
