ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. Under settled admiralty principles, a shipowner owes longshoremen a duty to exercise reasonable care in the management of the vessel and its appurtenances when those operations remain under the crew’s control. The Osceola, 189 U.S. 158, 175 (1903); Carlisle Packing Co. v. Sandanger, 259 U.S. 255, 259 (1922). That duty extends to equipment such as the fixed snatch blocks whose positioning the crew alone was authorized to alter. Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co., 321 U.S. 96, 99 (1944).

The record shows the blocks were left in the horizontal position on cargo after the crew completed hatch-cover operations. This created an obstruction that Serbin could neither safely work around nor ignore without halting cargo flow. Where a shipowner’s servants have created a hazardous condition in an area under their active direction, liability for resulting injury follows. Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn, 346 U.S. 406, 413 (1953); Alaska S.S. Co. v. Petterson, 347 U.S. 396, 397 (1954).

The crew’s exclusive responsibility for the blocks distinguishes this case from situations in which longshoremen assume control of the gear. Crumady v. The Joachim Hendrik Fisser, 358 U.S. 423, 427 (1959). Serbin’s prior occasional assistance in moving blocks does not relieve the owner of its non-delegable obligation to maintain the vessel in a condition permitting safe cargo operations. Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc., 362 U.S. 539, 543 (1960).

Genuine issues therefore remain whether the crew’s failure to restore the blocks to the vertical position constituted negligence that proximately caused Serbin’s fall. Gutierrez v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 373 U.S. 206, 213 (1963); see also The Arizona v. Anelich, 298 U.S. 110, 121 (1936); Jamison v. Encarnacion, 281 U.S. 635, 639 (1930). Summary judgment was accordingly improper.
