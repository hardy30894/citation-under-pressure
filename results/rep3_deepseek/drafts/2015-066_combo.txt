The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is a fundamental right, essential to a fair trial. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 68–69 (1932) (“The right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel.”). In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963), this Court held that “lawyers in criminal courts are necessities, not luxuries,” and that the right to appointed counsel is fundamental and obligatory on the States. The same Amendment binds the federal government directly. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 463 (1938). Mr. Bryant’s federal prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) therefore had to comply with the Sixth Amendment.

The government sought to prove an element of that federal offense using two prior tribal court convictions obtained without any right to counsel. This Court has squarely held that an uncounseled conviction cannot be used to support guilt or enhance punishment. In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967), the Court reversed a recidivist conviction based on a prior uncounseled conviction, stating: “To permit a conviction obtained in violation of *Gideon v. Wainwright* to be used against a person either to support guilt or enhance punishment for another offense is to erode the principle of that case.” That is precisely what occurred here. The tribal court convictions were not merely sentencing considerations; they were used to establish the “at least 2 separate prior occasions” element of the federal felony. Under *Burgett*, that use “to support guilt” is forbidden.

The government may argue that tribal courts are separate sovereigns not subject to the Sixth Amendment, and that the tribal convictions are therefore not “obtained in violation of *Gideon*.” But that argument misapprehends the nature of the Sixth Amendment claim. The violation is not in the tribal court; it is in the federal courtroom. The federal government may not rely on a presumptively unreliable conviction to satisfy its burden of proof. The Sixth Amendment’s concern is reliability. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464. A conviction entered without counsel is inherently unreliable, whether entered by a state court or a tribal court. The Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. § 1302, does not provide the full panoply of Sixth Amendment protections, including appointed counsel for all indigent defendants. The absence of counsel in the tribal proceedings creates exactly the same risk of an erroneous conviction that *Gideon* and *Johnson* sought to eliminate. The federal government cannot bootstrap such a judgment into a new felony conviction.

The traditional recognition of recidivist statutes does not help the government. In *Moore v. Missouri*, 159 U.S. 673 (1895), and *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616 (1912), this Court upheld enhanced penalties for repeat offenders, but in each case the predicate convictions were presumed valid. These decisions do not authorize the use of convictions that would not satisfy the Sixth Amendment’s fundamental fairness requirement. *McDonald v. Massachusetts*, 180 U.S. 311 (1901), likewise assumed that the prior convictions were valid and did not involve any challenge to the absence of counsel. The government’s position would transform these recidivist statutes into engines for compounding an earlier constitutional violation, which *Burgett* forbids.

The right to counsel is not limited to formal criminal trials. In *In re Gault*, 387 U.S. 1, 36 (1967), this Court held that the right to counsel attaches in juvenile delinquency proceedings because the result may be a deprivation of liberty. The same logic applies a fortiori here. The prior uncounseled tribal court convictions are being used to create a federal felony punishable by up to five years’ imprisonment. That is a far greater deprivation of liberty than the juvenile commitment at stake in *Gault*. To allow the government to manufacture this federal penalty out of unreliable, uncounseled tribal judgments would eviscerate the Sixth Amendment’s core guarantee.

The government’s solution is simple: if it wishes to use tribal court convictions as predicates for federal felony charges, it may require that tribal courts provide counsel, or it may limit the predicate statute to convictions obtained with counsel. What it cannot do is use uncounseled convictions as an essential element of a federal crime. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the district court should have dismissed the indictment. This Court should affirm that judgment.
