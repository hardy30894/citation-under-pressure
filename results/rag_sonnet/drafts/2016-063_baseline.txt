# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of the Civil Service Reform Act Directs Mixed Cases—Including Jurisdictional Dismissals of Mixed Cases—to District Court

The Civil Service Reform Act establishes a single, coherent path for judicial review of "mixed cases," defined as cases in which a federal employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board" and "alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" enumerated federal statutes. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). This Court in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), held that "two sections of the CSRA, read naturally, direct employees like Kloeckner to district court." *Id.* at 41. The statute draws no distinction between the procedural posture in which the Board disposes of such a case. Rather, it asks only whether the employee's case is a mixed case—one involving an "action which the employee ... may appeal to the" Board and an allegation of discrimination. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A)–(B).

Perry's case is a mixed case under this definition. He alleged that his suspension and retirement resulted from disability discrimination and retaliation, and that the settlement compelling those outcomes was the product of coercion tied to that discrimination. The Board's dismissal for lack of jurisdiction does not remove the case from the statutory category of "mixed cases"; it simply reflects the Board's conclusion, after considering the discrimination-related coercion allegations, that Perry's retirement was voluntary. That determination goes to the merits of whether Perry was, in fact, subjected to an adverse action tainted by discrimination—precisely the kind of inquiry Congress assigned to district courts in mixed cases.

## II. *Kloeckner* Forecloses Any Distinction Between Procedural and Jurisdictional Dismissals for Purposes of Determining the Proper Forum

The D.C. Circuit below concluded that *Kloeckner* addressed only "procedural" dismissals and left undisturbed the rule of *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), that "jurisdictional" dismissals go to the Federal Circuit. That distinction cannot be squared with *Kloeckner*'s reasoning or its result.

*Kloeckner* did not hinge its holding on the label attached to the Board's dismissal. The Court framed the controlling inquiry as whether the case, considered as a whole, was a "mixed case"—one "appealable to the MSPB" and alleging discrimination. 568 U.S. at 41. As the Court explained, "[n]o one here contests that Kloeckner brought a mixed case—that she was affected by an action (i.e., removal) appealable to the MSPB and that she alleged discrimination." *Id.* The Court granted certiorari "to resolve a Circuit split on whether an employee seeking judicial review should proceed in the Federal Circuit or in a district court when the MSPB has dismissed her mixed case on procedural grounds," *id.*, but nothing in its analysis suggests that the same statutory text yields a different forum when the Board instead concludes that it lacks jurisdiction because the underlying action was voluntary.

The voluntariness inquiry that dooms jurisdiction in cases like Perry's is not a threshold, forum-defining question separate from discrimination; it is often inseparable from the discrimination allegation itself. Perry's claim was that he was coerced into settlement because of disability discrimination and retaliation for protected activity. The Board's rejection of that coercion claim is a rejection, on the merits, of the very discrimination theory that made this a mixed case in the first place. Treating that determination as jurisdictional rather than procedural elevates form over substance and revives exactly the kind of forum-shopping confusion that *Kloeckner* sought to eliminate by directing that mixed cases—full stop—belong in district court.

The D.C. Circuit's reliance on the fact that the Board can excuse untimeliness under 5 C.F.R. § 1201.22(c) but cannot waive true jurisdictional defects does not justify a different forum rule. The employee's need for a single, predictable route to judicial review does not depend on whether the agency later characterizes its ruling as jurisdictional or procedural. Employees cannot be expected to guess, before the Board rules, which label its eventual decision will bear, and then select a forum accordingly. Congress did not condition the forum for judicial review on the Board's post hoc characterization of its own disposition; it conditioned it on the nature of the case as pleaded—an action appealable to the Board (which the Board's later erroneous jurisdictional ruling does not change) coupled with a discrimination allegation.

## III. Uniformity and Practical Considerations Confirm That District Court Review Is Correct

Judicial economy is not served by shunting the same category of claims—settlement agreements challenged as products of discrimination or retaliation—into two different courts depending on an unpredictable, after-the-fact characterization by the Board. The whole thrust of *Kloeckner* was to give mixed-case litigants "one, and only one" route to review, avoiding exactly the kind of fractured, forum-dependent litigation that arises when courts must first sort dismissals into procedural and jurisdictional bins before knowing where an appeal belongs.

Nor is there any sound reason to worry that district courts are less competent than the Federal Circuit to police the boundaries of the Board's own jurisdiction. District courts routinely determine whether they possess jurisdiction over a claim as an antecedent matter to reaching the merits, and they are equally capable of assessing whether an employee's retirement or settlement was the product of unlawful coercion tied to discrimination—an inquiry that substantially overlaps with, and often is indistinguishable from, the merits of the discrimination claim itself.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that judicial review of a Board decision dismissing a mixed case for lack of jurisdiction lies in district court, and should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals.
