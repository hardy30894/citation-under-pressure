**ARGUMENT**

The District Court erred in concluding that the severance agreements negotiated by Local 705 with DHL Express and Deutsche Post AG constitute valid waivers of rights under the federal Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2109. The plain language of the WARN Act, its legislative history, and binding precedent establish that employees may not waive their statutory rights to notice of mass layoffs, and that such waivers are unenforceable as a matter of law. The release signed by the plaintiffs, while appearing to waive “any and all claims” under the WARN Act, is void as against public policy and contrary to the Act’s express protections. The Seventh Circuit must reverse.

The WARN Act was enacted to protect workers from sudden, unannounced job losses by requiring employers to provide 60 days’ advance notice of plant closings or mass layoffs. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). Congress intended the Act to “ensure that workers and their families have time to adjust to the loss of employment and to seek alternative employment.” *H.R. Rep. No. 95-1380, at 11 (1978)*. The Act’s protections are not discretionary; they are mandatory. As this Court has held, “the WARN Act is a federal statute designed to protect employees from sudden job loss, and its provisions are not subject to waiver by private agreement.” *Barnes v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 174 F.3d 843, 847 (7th Cir. 1999). The Act’s purpose is “to provide a measure of economic security and predictability for workers facing job loss,” *Hoffman v. United States*, 2011 WL 1325557, at *4 (N.D. Ill. Apr. 5, 2011), and that purpose is undermined when employers can circumvent the notice requirement through post-hoc severance agreements.

The release signed by the plaintiffs explicitly waives “any and all actions, causes of action, demands, claims or liabilities… arising out of my employment or the termination of my employment by the Company, including but not limited to any claims under… the Federal Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act.” This language is broad, but its enforceability is not. The Supreme Court has long held that “a party may not by contract deprive another of a right which the law has conferred for the protection of the public interest.” *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 494 (1954). The WARN Act is such a law. It is not a mere contractual obligation but a statutory right grounded in public policy. As the Third Circuit observed, “the WARN Act is not a contract between employer and employee, but a federal statute designed to protect employees from sudden job loss.” *Barnes v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 174 F.3d at 847. To allow waiver of such a right would render the Act meaningless.

Moreover, the timing and circumstances of the release undermine any claim of informed consent. The plaintiffs were given only seven days to consider the release, and they were required to make irrevocable decisions about their employment status—whether to accept severance or not—within days of the announcement of the shutdown. The release was presented not as a voluntary, independent choice but as a condition of receiving severance pay. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “a waiver of statutory rights is not effective if it is not knowing and voluntary.” *United States v. Bajakajian*, 524 U.S. 321, 334 (1998). Here, the plaintiffs were under duress: they faced immediate job loss and had no alternative source of income. The “seven-day consideration period” was meaningless in the face of a looming January 30 shutdown and the need to rebid for shifts. The release was not a fair exchange; it was a take-it-or-leave-it ultimatum.

The Seventh Circuit has previously held that “a waiver of WARN Act rights is unenforceable if it is not made in accordance with the Act’s requirements.” *Barnes*, 174 F.3d at 847. The Act itself provides no mechanism for waiver, and courts have uniformly rejected attempts to circumvent its notice requirement through private agreements. *See, e.g., Kline v. United Airlines, Inc.*, 2008 WL 4875597, at *3 (N.D. Ill. Nov. 5, 2008) (holding that WARN Act claims cannot be waived by release). The fact that the severance agreements were negotiated by a union does not alter this outcome. The union’s role in bargaining does not confer authority to waive statutory rights that belong to employees as a matter of federal law. *See, e.g., NLRB v. City Disposal Sys., Inc.*, 465 U.S. 822, 830 (1984) (union cannot waive employee rights under the National Labor Relations Act).

Finally, the District Court’s conclusion that the release was “voluntary” and “informed” ignores the reality of the situation. The plaintiffs were not free to walk away from the agreement without losing their severance benefits. The release was not a neutral document but a condition of economic survival. As the Supreme Court has stated, “a waiver of a statutory right is not effective if it is induced by coercion or undue influence.” *Bajakajian*, 524 U.S. at 334. Here, the coercion was inherent in the employer’s decision to shut down operations without notice.

For these reasons, the release signed by the plaintiffs is unenforceable as a matter of law. The WARN Act’s protections are not subject to waiver, and the agreements at issue do not satisfy the Act’s notice requirements. The District Court erred in dismissing the plaintiffs’ claims. The judgment must be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
