ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING AMERICAN STEAMSHIP’S EXPERT TESTIMONY ON THE CAUSE OF THE LINE’S PARTING.

Federal Rule of Evidence 702 permits expert testimony only where “scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge will assist the trier of fact.” This Court’s gatekeeping obligation, articulated in Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579 (1993), requires that expert opinion be grounded in reliable methodology and actually assist the jury beyond its own common understanding. Where an opinion amounts to nothing more than speculation dressed in professional credentials, it fails Rule 702’s threshold requirement.

The Supreme Court long ago recognized the corollary principle in the maritime context: expert testimony is improper where the jury is equally capable of drawing its own conclusions from the facts. Salem v. United States Lines Co., 370 U.S. 31, 35 (1962) (“There is no reason to believe that the jurors ... were incapable of comprehending” the matter without expert assistance). Here, American Steamship’s expert did not apply specialized engineering or metallurgical analysis to the manilla line; he merely speculated, from the fact that the torch had been used nearby, that heat caused the parting. This was not knowledge derived from expertise—it was an inference any layperson could draw, or fail to draw, from the same facts already before the jury. Because the witness’s opinion did not rest on specialized knowledge but on the same evidence available to the jury, admitting it violated Rule 702 and Daubert’s reliability requirement, and requires a new trial untainted by this improper opinion.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON UNSEAWORTHINESS.

The shipowner’s duty to furnish a seaworthy vessel is, as the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized, “a species of liability without fault.” Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc., 362 U.S. 539, 549 (1960). The duty extends to every appurtenance of the vessel, including its rigging and equipment, and is breached whenever equipment furnished for a seaman’s use is not reasonably fit for its intended purpose. Id. at 550. Unseaworthiness focuses not on the shipowner’s conduct but on the condition of the vessel and its gear. Usner v. Luckenbach Overseas Corp., 400 U.S. 494, 499 (1971) (“unseaworthiness is a condition of the vessel”).

The record leaves no genuine dispute that the line supporting Cook’s bos’n’s chair was not reasonably fit for its purpose. The line was old manilla rope that had been left rigged and exposed through prior sailing seasons, subjected to repeated wetting and freezing, and parted under ordinary use while lowering a seaman to a dock—precisely the use for which it was intended. That the line failed under foreseeable conditions is itself evidence of its unfitness. Because reasonable jurors could reach only one conclusion—that the vessel’s gear was unseaworthy—the district court erred in submitting that issue to the jury rather than granting judgment as a matter of law under Fed. R. Civ. P. 50.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON COMPARATIVE NEGLIGENCE.

The Jones Act incorporates the FELA standard of causation, under which an employer’s negligence renders it liable if that negligence “played any part, even the slightest, in producing the injury.” Rogers v. Missouri Pacific R.R. Co., 352 U.S. 500, 506 (1957). This “featherweight” causation standard applies equally to the question of a seaman’s own comparative fault, but comparative negligence still requires that the seaman’s conduct fall below the standard of care a reasonable seaman would exercise under like circumstances. See Rogers, 352 U.S. at 508. Cook was directed to work under emergency conditions—ice, sub-zero temperatures, and a compressed rigging window imposed by the master’s own order—using standard deicing methods common to the trade. On this record, no reasonable jury could find that Cook’s use of a propane torch, sanctioned by shipboard practice and necessitated by the ship’s own equipment failures, constituted negligence chargeable to him. The district court therefore erred in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on this issue as well, and any verdict apportioning fault to Cook cannot stand.

IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK’S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS.

Evidence is relevant only if it has “any tendency to make the existence of any fact ... more or less probable,” Fed. R. Evid. 401, and even relevant evidence must be excluded where its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. Fed. R. Evid. 403. Courts assessing such prejudice must be mindful that jurors may draw impermissible character inferences from evidence of a party’s personal habits unrelated to the accident. See United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45, 54–55 (1984) (recognizing the trial court’s duty to weigh probative value against prejudicial impact). Cook’s alcohol-related medical history bore no connection to the parting of the line, the rigging of the boom, or the events of December 26, 1990. Its sole function was to invite the jury to view Cook as an unreliable or blameworthy figure, unrelated to any issue the jury was asked to decide. Admission of this evidence was error, and combined with the errors set forth above, deprived Cook of a fair trial.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment below should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial free of these errors.
