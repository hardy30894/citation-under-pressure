**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Controlled Substances Act’s Delegation to the Attorney General Satisfies the Intelligible Principle Test.**

The nondelegation doctrine does not forbid Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches, so long as Congress lays down “by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Supreme Court has upheld every congressional delegation of power presented to it since 1935, even where Congress provided negligible standards. *See, e.g., Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 424-27 (1944) (upholding delegation to fix maximum commodity prices); *Flemming v. Florida Citrus Exchange*, 358 U.S. 153, 162-63 (1959) (upholding delegation to Secretary to prohibit harmful color additives).

The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), provides multiple intelligible principles. First, the Attorney General may temporarily schedule a substance only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” *Id.* § 811(h)(1). Second, he must consider three specific factors: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health. *Id.* § 811(h)(3). Third, the Attorney General must publish notice in the Federal Register and transmit his proposal to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose comments must be taken into account. *Id.* § 811(h)(4). Fourth, temporary scheduling is limited to one year, with a possible six-month extension. *Id.* § 811(h)(2). These constraints far exceed the “intelligible principle” standard.

The Toubys’ argument that a stricter standard applies when the delegatee may create crimes is unavailing. In *Yakus*, the Court upheld a delegation that authorized criminal penalties for violating price-control regulations. 321 U.S. at 444-46. And in *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521-22 (1911), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to make rules governing national forests, the violation of which was a crime. The Court explained that “Congress may delegate to an administrative body the power to make regulations” and that “the authority to make administrative rules is not a delegation of legislative power.” *Id.* at 521.

**II. The Attorney General Properly Subdelegated Temporary Scheduling Authority to the DEA.**

Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Controlled Substances Act. Section 871(a) provides that the Attorney General “may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This broad grant of authority permits subdelegation of the temporary scheduling power.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the power to delegate” is “implied” when necessary to carry out statutory duties. *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 364 (1942). Here, Congress expressly granted the power. The Attorney General exercised that power by promulgating 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which assigns to the DEA Administrator the functions vested in the Attorney General by the Controlled Substances Act. This regulation is a valid exercise of the Attorney General’s statutory authority.

The Toubys cannot show that Congress intended to withhold the power to subdelegate temporary scheduling. Unlike the subpoena power at issue in *Cudahy*, where Congress had “specifically authorized delegation” in some statutes but not others, 315 U.S. at 365, here Congress broadly authorized delegation of “any” function. The temporary scheduling provision contains no language restricting subdelegation. Where Congress has not expressly limited delegation, courts presume the agency head may delegate. *See, e.g., Wichita Railroad & Light Co. v. Public Utilities Commission*, 260 U.S. 48, 58-59 (1922) (upholding delegation where legislature “enjoin[ed] upon [the agency] a certain course of procedure and certain rules of decision”).

**III. The Temporary Scheduling Provision Does Not Violate the Separation of Powers.**

The Toubys’ facial challenge fails because Congress retained substantial control over the scheduling process. Congress defined the five schedules, specified the criteria for placement, and limited temporary scheduling to one year. The Attorney General’s role is “subordinate” to Congress’s policy choices. *See District of Columbia v. John R. Thompson Co.*, 346 U.S. 100, 113-14 (1953) (upholding delegation where Congress “prescribe[d] the standards” and the delegatee “act[ed] in subordination” to them).

Moreover, the regulation of dangerous drugs is a field where “well-known and generally acceptable standards” exist. *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). The Supreme Court has recognized that the “police power to regulate the administration, sale, prescription and use of dangerous and habit-forming drugs” is “so manifest in the interest of the public health and welfare” that it is “too firmly established to be successfully called in question.” *Minnesota ex rel. Whipple v. Martinson*, 256 U.S. 41, 45 (1921). Congress acted well within its constitutional authority in delegating temporary scheduling power to the Attorney General, who in turn properly subdelegated that power to the DEA.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
