# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS OF CHAPTER 93-121 ARE UNCONSTITUTIONALLY OVERBROAD

The First Amendment protects the right to petition government, and lobbying is the modern embodiment of that right. *See Eastern R.R. Presidents Conference v. Noerr Motor Freight, Inc.*, 365 U.S. 127, 137-38 (1961). While the State may require some disclosure to inform the public about efforts to influence legislation, *see United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 625-26 (1954), the disclosure regime Florida has enacted sweeps far beyond any legitimate informational interest and chills protected advocacy.

Chapter 93-121 compels lobbyists to disclose not merely the fact and general subject of their advocacy, but granular expenditure data across open-ended categories including "food and beverages, entertainment, research, communication, media advertising, publications, travel, and lodging." Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a). This is not the narrow "who is being hired, who is paying, and how much" disclosure the Supreme Court sanctioned in *Harriss*. 347 U.S. at 625. It requires lobbyists to itemize the texture of their associational and expressive activity—effectively logging every meal, every conversation, every research memorandum prepared in the course of political advocacy.

A statute is facially overbroad when "a substantial number of its applications are unconstitutional, judged in relation to the statute's plainly legitimate sweep." *Washington State Grange v. Washington State Republican Party*, 552 U.S. 442, 449 n.6 (2008) (citing *New York v. Ferber*, 458 U.S. 747, 769-71 (1982)). This Court's own recitation of the governing standard, *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987), does not foreclose facial review—it merely raises the bar. Appellant meets that bar here because the overwhelming majority of expenditures the statute forces into public disclosure bear no relationship to preventing corruption or its appearance, the interest *Harriss* found sufficient to justify lobbying regulation. 347 U.S. at 625.

The category-based disclosure requirement is particularly infirm because it is non-exclusive; lobbyists cannot know in advance whether a given expenditure falls within a category the State will later deem reportable. This vagueness, layered atop the compelled-disclosure burden, creates exactly the chilling effect condemned in *Talley v. California*, 362 U.S. 60, 64 (1960), where the Court struck down an ordinance requiring identification on handbills because "identification and fear of reprisal might deter perfectly peaceful discussions." The same dynamic operates here: lobbyists, uncertain whether their advocacy-related expenditures must be disclosed, will simply curtail the advocacy rather than risk civil sanction. That is precisely the "unattractive set of options"—cease protected activity or risk penalty—that renders this controversy ripe. *See Abbott Lab. v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 152-53 (1967).

The administrative advisory-opinion procedure, Fla. Stat. § 11.045(4), does not cure the defect. A speaker should not be required to seek the government's blessing before engaging in core political speech. *See Freedman v. Maryland*, 380 U.S. 51, 58-59 (1965) (condemning schemes that place the burden of instituting proceedings on the speaker). The very existence of an advisory mechanism confirms that the statute's boundaries are unclear to the persons it regulates—a structural admission of overbreadth.

## II. THE CONTINGENCY-FEE BAN VIOLATES THE FIRST AMENDMENT

Florida Statute § 11.047 flatly forbids lobbyists from accepting compensation contingent on legislative or executive success. This Court's opinion below acknowledges that it upholds the ban only because it considers itself bound by "old pronouncements of the Supreme Court," even while recognizing that "more recent cases suggest that the Supreme Court might someday reach a result contrary to the one we reach today." That candid acknowledgment is itself grounds for reversal, or at minimum for this Court to recognize that the doctrinal foundation for the ban has eroded to the point of collapse.

The ban traces to *Trist v. Child*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 441 (1875), and *Marshall v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co.*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 314 (1854), decisions resting on a common-law conception of lobbying as inherently corrupt—a conception the Court has since abandoned. In *Noerr*, the Court held that "the right of petition is one of the freedoms protected by the Bill of Rights," and refused to treat lobbying, even vigorous and self-interested lobbying, as beyond the Constitution's protection. 365 U.S. at 138. Contingency compensation is simply the economic structure through which many citizens exercise that right; lawyers routinely take contingency fees for pursuing relief in court, and no one doubts that litigation is petitioning activity. There is no principled basis, post-*Noerr*, for treating a contingent fee paid to a lobbyist as inherently more corrupting than one paid to a litigator.

More recent compelled-speech and unconstitutional-conditions jurisprudence confirms the ban cannot survive independent scrutiny. In *Riley v. National Federation of the Blind*, 487 U.S. 781, 795 (1988), the Court struck down a percentage-based fee restriction on charitable solicitors, holding that the State could not "presume" that high fees signal fraud and regulate on that unsupported presumption alone. The same infirmity taints § 11.047: Florida presumes contingent compensation breeds corrupt influence without any tailored showing that it does so more than flat fees, retainer arrangements, or in-house employment—all of which remain untouched by the statute.

This Court is right that it "lack[s] the power to overrule" *Trist* and *Marshall* outright; that prerogative belongs to the Supreme Court alone. *See Rodriguez de Quijas v. Shearson/American Express, Inc.*, 490 U.S. 477, 484 (1989) (lower courts must follow directly applicable Supreme Court precedent until the Court itself overrules it). But this Court can and should hold that *Trist* and *Marshall*, decided on due-process and public-policy grounds a century before *Noerr* recognized lobbying as protected First Amendment petitioning, do not control a First Amendment challenge never presented to the *Trist* Court. On that narrower ground, reversal is warranted without any need to anticipate whether the Supreme Court will one day overrule its own precedent.
