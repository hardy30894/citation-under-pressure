**ARGUMENT**

The District Court did not abuse its discretion in certifying the class under Rule 23(b)(3), nor did it err in concluding that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The Court’s determination rested on a rigorous, fact-based analysis of admissible evidence, consistent with the Supreme Court’s directive in *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011), that “a district court must conduct a ‘rigorous analysis’ of the evidence to determine whether the requirements of Rule 23 are satisfied.” That analysis, properly conducted, confirmed that common questions of law and fact predominate and that a common methodology exists to measure damages across the class. The Third Circuit correctly affirmed, and this Court should do likewise.

**I. A district court may certify a class based on admissible evidence of a common damages methodology, even if individualized proof is not entirely eliminated.**

Rule 23(b)(3) does not require that damages be calculable with mathematical precision for every class member. Rather, it requires only that “questions of law or fact common to the class members predominate” over individual issues. *Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 350. The Supreme Court has made clear that “the predominance inquiry is not a rigid test,” but “a flexible one,” *id.* at 351, and that “a class may be certified even if some damages issues are individualized, so long as the common issues are central to the claim and can be resolved through a common methodology,” *id.* at 352. This principle was reaffirmed in *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 622 (1997), where the Court held that “the predominance requirement is satisfied if the common issues are ‘sufficiently central’ to the claim.” Here, the District Court found that the class-wide antitrust impact—specifically, the deterrence of overbuilder entry due to Comcast’s clustering—was capable of proof through common evidence, and that Dr. McClave’s damages model, based on a counterfactual market analysis, provided a common methodology to quantify damages. This is precisely the kind of analysis permitted under *Amchem* and *Wal-Mart*.

**II. The existence of a common damages methodology is sufficient to satisfy Rule 23(b)(3), even if individualized damages are possible.**

Comcast argues that the absence of a single, precise damages formula renders certification improper. But this misreads the law. In *Dukes*, the Court emphasized that “a class may be certified even if damages are not identical for each class member,” so long as “the common issues predominate.” *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 352. The Court further explained that “the fact that damages may vary among class members does not defeat predominance,” so long as “a common methodology can be used to measure damages on a class-wide basis.” *Id.* at 353. The Third Circuit correctly applied this standard, holding that the District Court had “demonstrated by a preponderance of the evidence” that Dr. McClave’s model provided such a methodology. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 648 F.3d 130, 140 (3d Cir. 2011). The Court’s reliance on expert testimony and economic modeling—admissible under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 592–93 (1993)—was not only proper but required. *See also* *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 150 (1999) (admitting expert testimony if it is based on reliable principles and methods).

**III. The District Court’s findings were not clearly erroneous, and the Third Circuit properly applied the abuse-of-discretion standard.**

Comcast contends that the District Court’s findings were “clearly erroneous,” but the standard for reversal is high. *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985). A finding is clearly erroneous only if “the reviewing court is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” *Id.* at 573. The District Court conducted a four-day evidentiary hearing, reviewed 32 expert reports, and heard testimony from fact and expert witnesses. *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 154. It then issued an 81-page opinion analyzing the evidence. This was not a “mini trial,” but a necessary inquiry into the merits to determine whether certification requirements were met. *See* *Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 351 (“[T]he Rule 23 inquiry may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits.”). The Third Circuit correctly applied *Anderson*, holding that the District Court’s factual findings were “plausible in light of the record viewed in its entirety,” and thus not clearly erroneous. *Behrend*, 648 F.3d at 141.

**IV. The class’s damages theory is not speculative; it is grounded in a common, testable economic model.**

Comcast claims the damages theory is “speculative,” but the District Court found that Dr. McClave’s model—based on a counterfactual market analysis using regression analysis and market data—was both reliable and capable of class-wide application. *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 592. The model estimated what prices would have been in the absence of Comcast’s anticompetitive conduct, using data from comparable markets and time periods. This approach is consistent with *Amchem*, where the Court upheld certification based on a damages model that “used a common methodology to estimate damages.” 521 U.S. at 622. Moreover, in *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008), the court held that “a district court may rely on expert testimony to determine whether a common methodology exists for measuring damages.” Here, the District Court did exactly that.
