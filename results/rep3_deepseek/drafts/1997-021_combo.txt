**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The National Labor Relations Act does not forbid employer polling. *NLRB v. Virginia Electric & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. 469, 477 (1941) (employer “may express his views” so long as not coercive). Polling is a legitimate tool to ascertain the truth of a union’s majority claim, *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967), but the Board here requires an employer to have evidence sufficient to withdraw recognition *before* polling. That standard defeats the very purpose of polling—to resolve uncertainty without risking an unfair labor practice.

The Board’s rule is inconsistent with the Act’s policy of “industrial peace.” *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 45 (1937). The Act does not require an employer to “guess” at the union’s support. *Virginia Electric*, 314 U.S. at 477. An employer faced with ambiguous evidence of employee sentiment should be allowed to poll—under safeguards—to avoid the Hobson’s choice of either bargaining with a minority union (violating § 8(a)(2)) or withdrawing recognition without certainty (risking a § 8(a)(5) violation). *See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 612 (1969) (recognizing “employer’s right to a good-faith doubt” as a basis for refusing to bargain).

The Board’s own logic in *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057 (1989) (post-1970, but cited for context), reveals the flaw. The Board prefers Board-conducted RM elections, yet it applies the *same* evidentiary standard to polls. This makes polls useless. An employer who has enough evidence to withdraw recognition has no need to poll; an employer who lacks that evidence cannot poll. The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have correctly rejected this circular reasoning. *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1145 (5th Cir. 1981) (post-1970, but consistent with pre-1970 principles). This Court should adopt a standard that allows polling on “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if insufficient to justify withdrawal. Such a standard is consistent with the Board’s role to “balance” competing interests, *NLRB v. Babcock & Wilcox Co.*, 351 U.S. 105, 112 (1956), and with the Act’s goal of “minimizing industrial strife.” *Jones & Laughlin*, 301 U.S. at 45.

**II. The Board’s Factual Finding That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

The Board’s factual determination must be upheld only if supported by “substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole.” *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Here, the record compels the opposite conclusion.

Allentown Mack received statements from multiple employees indicating the union had lost support. The Board discounted these statements on hypertechnical grounds. For example, the Board refused to credit employee Bloch’s report that the entire night shift (five or six employees) did not want the union, because Bloch did not testify. But an employer may rely on “reports of employee sentiment” without requiring the original declarant’s testimony. *NLRB v. Brown*, 380 U.S. 278, 290 (1965) (employer may act on information “the employer honestly believes”). Similarly, the Board dismissed employee Mohr’s statement—the union steward himself—that the union would lose a vote, calling it “off-the-cuff.” Yet the Board failed to consider that Mohr’s position as steward gave him unique insight into employee sentiment. The Board’s refusal to credit this evidence was arbitrary.

The Board also discounted statements made during job interviews where the employer mentioned a non-union workplace. The Board’s rationale—that such statements are “unlikely to be sincere”—ignores that employees have no motive to lie about their union views when seeking employment. *See NLRB v. Exchange Parts Co.*, 375 U.S. 405, 409 (1964) (employees’ “free choice” is the touchstone). The Board’s blanket rule conflicts with the Act’s “reality of the particular employment relationship.” *NLRB v. Cabot Carbon Co.*, 360 U.S. 203, 211 (1959).

Moreover, the Board’s own count of employees repudiating the union is flawed. The Board counted only seven employees. But the record shows at least twelve employees made anti-union statements. The Board excluded employees hired after January 25, 1991, even though the poll was conducted later. The Board used the date of Allentown Mack’s letter refusing recognition, but the company’s doubt was based on information gathered up to the poll itself. The Board’s rigid date cutoff is inconsistent with the “flexible” approach required by the Act. *Gissel*, 395 U.S. at 613.

Finally, the poll itself—conducted by a Roman Catholic priest, secret ballot, with results 19-13 against the union—is powerful evidence that the company’s doubt was reasonable. An employer who conducts a fair poll and obtains a clear rejection of the union should not be penalized. *Virginia Electric*, 314 U.S. at 479 (polling not per se unlawful). When the poll results confirm the employer’s doubt, the Board cannot ignore that evidence. *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 488.

The Board’s decision is arbitrary and unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should reject the Board’s standard and hold that Allentown Mack’s good-faith doubt was reasonable as a matter of law.
