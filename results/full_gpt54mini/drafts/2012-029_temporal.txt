The federal courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction merely because a state-law case requires some analysis of patent law. Section 1338(a) reaches only those cases “arising under” the patent laws, and the Supreme Court has long held that a suit does not so arise unless the patent question is essential to the plaintiff’s own statement of his claim. *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912); *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936). As *Gully* explained, federal jurisdiction cannot be found by “a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside.” 299 U.S. at 117-18.

That rule controls here. Minton’s malpractice claim sounds in state tort law. His right to relief arises from the attorney’s alleged breach of duty, not from patent law itself. The patent issue enters only as an evidentiary step in the “case within a case” showing that the alleged negligence caused a loss. A state claim of this kind does not become a patent case simply because proving causation requires reference to patent doctrine. *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569; *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117-18. The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned that a case does not arise under federal law unless the federal issue appears in the plaintiff’s well-pleaded statement of his own cause of action, not in a defense or in a collateral question. *Louisville & Nashville R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152-53 (1908).

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach cannot stand. Under its rule, any malpractice claim connected to patent litigation would be swept into exclusive federal jurisdiction merely because the plaintiff must prove that the underlying patent case would have been won. That would make § 1338(a) far broader than Congress or this Court has ever allowed. Jurisdiction does not turn on whether a federal issue is “necessary” in some abstract sense, but whether the plaintiff’s own cause of action is created by federal law or necessarily depends on a substantial federal question. *American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916); *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569.

This Court’s decisions confirm the point. In *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180, 199-201 (1921), federal jurisdiction existed because the validity of federal bonds was an essential element of the plaintiff’s claim for equitable relief. But *Smith* was the exceptional case, not the rule. Later decisions narrowed that path and insisted that federal jurisdiction be found only where the federal issue is truly embedded in the cause of action itself. *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117-18; *T.B. Harms Co. v. Eliscu*, 339 F.2d 823, 828-29 (2d Cir. 1964) (Friendly, J.) (distinguishing between cases arising under federal law and cases merely requiring reference to federal law). A legal-malpractice action like this one falls on the wrong side of that line.

Exclusive federal jurisdiction is especially inappropriate because it would displace state authority over the regulation of attorneys and the adjudication of legal-malpractice claims. State courts have long exercised primary responsibility over such claims, and nothing in the patent laws suggests Congress meant to oust them whenever patent issues are part of the proof. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against expanding federal jurisdiction in a way that disturbs the federal-state balance. *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117-18; *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569.

Nor is there any need for exclusivity to protect patent uniformity. A state court deciding whether counsel negligently failed to assert a patent defense does not adjudicate patent validity for all purposes; it decides only whether the client can prove malpractice. That limited, backward-looking inquiry does not create a patent-rights regime different from the federal one. A state court can apply patent principles incidentally, just as state courts routinely apply federal standards in other contexts without surrendering jurisdiction. *Smith*, 255 U.S. at 199-201; *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117-18.

The better reading of § 1338(a) is therefore the narrower one long reflected in this Court’s precedents: federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction only when the plaintiff’s own claim arises under patent law, not when patent law is merely an ingredient in a state-law malpractice case. *American Well Works*, 241 U.S. at 260; *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569; *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117-18. Because Minton’s claim is for attorney negligence under state law, the Texas courts had jurisdiction, and the judgment dismissing for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction should be reversed.
