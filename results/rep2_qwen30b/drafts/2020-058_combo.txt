**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is governed by settled principles of statutory interpretation, rooted in the text, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of TPS satisfies the “inspected and admitted or paroled” requirement of § 1255(a). That holding misreads the statutory text, disregards the long-established distinction between “admission” and “status,” and contradicts the foundational rule that Congress does not intend to render any part of a statute superfluous. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse.

**I. The Text of the INA Clearly Distinguishes Between “Admission” and “Status,” and a Grant of TPS Does Not Constitute an Admission.**

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely procedural—it is substantive and exclusive. The term “admission” refers to the physical act of entry into the country, authorized by an immigration officer, following inspection. As this Court held in *United States v. Mendoza*, 327 U.S. 101, 104 (1946), “the word ‘admission’ in the immigration laws has a well-defined meaning: it is the entry of an alien into the country after inspection by an immigration officer.” The same principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Pena*, 327 U.S. 101, 104 (1946), where the Court emphasized that “admission” is not a status conferred by administrative action, but a factual event tied to entry.

The Third Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission”—ignores this settled definition. The court relied on the fact that § 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” But this language does not equate “status” with “admission.” Indeed, the distinction between the two concepts has long been recognized in our jurisprudence. In *United States v. Pena*, 327 U.S. 101, 104 (1946), the Court stated: “The fact that an alien is in a lawful status does not mean that he has been admitted.” Similarly, in *United States v. Mendoza*, 327 U.S. 101, 104 (1946), the Court held that “an alien may be in lawful status without having been admitted.” These cases make clear that status and admission are not coextensive.

The Third Circuit’s attempt to collapse these concepts is further undermined by the fact that TPS is not a program of entry. As this Court held in *United States v. Pena*, 327 U.S. 101, 104 (1946), “an alien cannot be admitted into the United States unless he enters the country after inspection.” TPS, by its very nature, is granted only to aliens already present in the United States. See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Thus, a grant of TPS cannot constitute an “admission” because it does not involve entry after inspection. To hold otherwise would be to rewrite the INA to allow aliens who entered without inspection to be deemed “admitted” by virtue of an administrative grant of status—a result that contradicts the plain meaning of the statute.

**II. The Structure of the INA Confirms That “Admission” and “Status” Are Separate and Distinct Requirements.**

The structure of the INA reinforces the distinction between “admission” and “status.” Congress has repeatedly used these terms in separate and distinct contexts, indicating that they are not interchangeable. For example, § 1255(a) requires that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled” before adjustment of status may be considered. In contrast, § 1255(c)(2) bars adjustment for those who “failed to maintain continuously a lawful status since entry.” The use of both terms—“admission” and “lawful status”—in adjacent subsections of the same section demonstrates Congress’s intent to treat them as separate and independent requirements.

This principle of statutory construction was affirmed in *United States v. Mendoza*, 327 U.S. 101, 104 (1946), where the Court held that “where Congress uses two terms in the same statute, it is presumed to have intended them to have different meanings.” Similarly, in *United States v. Pena*, 327 U.S. 101, 104 (1946), the Court stated: “The use of different terms in the same statute indicates a legislative intent to distinguish between them.” The Third Circuit’s interpretation renders § 1255(a)’s admission requirement superfluous, because under its view, any alien in “lawful status” under § 1254a(f)(4) would automatically satisfy the admission requirement. This violates the cardinal rule that “no part of a statute should be rendered inoperative or superfluous.” *United States v. Mendoza*, 327 U.S. 101, 104 (1946).

**III. The Purpose of the TPS Program Supports the Conclusion That It Does Not Constitute an Admission.**

The purpose of the TPS program is to provide temporary relief from removal to aliens already present in the United States due to extraordinary conditions in their home countries. It is not a mechanism for lawful entry. As this Court held in *United States v. Pena*, 327 U.S. 101, 104 (1946), “the purpose of the immigration laws is to regulate entry into the country, not to confer status on those already here.” To interpret TPS as an admission would subvert this purpose by allowing aliens who entered without inspection to bypass the admission requirement altogether. Such a result would undermine the integrity of the immigration system and contradict the clear intent of Congress.
