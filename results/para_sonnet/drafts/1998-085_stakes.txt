**ARGUMENT**

**Punitive Damages Under 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) Require a Showing of Egregious Misconduct, Not Merely an Adverse Employment Decision Later Found Discriminatory**

**I. The Statute's "Malice or Reckless Indifference" Standard Was Drawn From Common-Law Punitive-Damages Principles That Reserve Such Awards for Especially Reprehensible Conduct.**

Section 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only where a plaintiff "demonstrates that the [employer] engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That language does not appear in a vacuum. Congress borrowed it from this Court's articulation of the punitive-damages standard under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 in *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), where the Court held "that a jury may be permitted to assess punitive damages in an action under § 1983 when the defendant's conduct is shown to be motivated by evil motive or intent, or when it involves reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others." *Id.* at 56.

*Smith* did not hold that any intentional violation of a federal right supports punitive damages. It described a standard borrowed from the common law of torts, under which punitive damages have traditionally been confined to conduct that is outrageous or egregious—not the ordinary case of an employer choosing one qualified candidate over another based on an impermissible criterion. The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) (1979) reflects this same tradition, explaining that punitive damages are reserved "for conduct that is outrageous, because of the defendant's evil motive or his reckless indifference to the rights of others." Congress legislated against that backdrop, and there is no indication it meant to sever the "malice or reckless indifference" language from the body of law that gave it content.

**II. Collapsing the Liability and Punitive-Damages Inquiries Would Erase the Distinction the 1991 Act Draws Between Compensatory and Punitive Relief.**

Title VII liability, as this case illustrates, may rest entirely on a jury's rejection of an employer's proffered nondiscriminatory explanation. This Court has held that "no additional proof of discrimination is required" once a plaintiff's prima facie case survives and the employer's explanation is disbelieved. *St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993). That standard, sensible for liability, cannot simultaneously serve as the standard for punitive damages without erasing the very distinction Congress drew when it created a separate damages remedy in the Civil Rights Act of 1991. If every plaintiff who prevails on liability by discrediting the employer's explanation may also obtain punitive damages, then punitive damages become the rule rather than the exception—precisely the outcome the "malice or reckless indifference" language was designed to prevent. Congress capped punitive damages under § 1981a(b)(3) at amounts ranging from $50,000 to $300,000 depending on employer size, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3), a structure that confirms Congress viewed punitive liability as an extraordinary remedy for extraordinary conduct, not an automatic adjunct to a jury's liability finding.

**III. This Court's Due Process Precedents Confirm That Punitive Awards Must Track the Reprehensibility of the Defendant's Conduct.**

This Court has repeatedly instructed that "the most important indicium of the reasonableness of a punitive damages award is the degree of reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct." *BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 575 (1996). In *Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Haslip*, 499 U.S. 1 (1991), the Court recognized punitive damages as "quasi-criminal" in character, *id.* at 19, warranting safeguards against arbitrary or disproportionate awards untethered to genuinely culpable conduct. These due process concerns apply with full force to Title VII, where liability may be established through inference and pretext rather than direct evidence of animus, and where employers of every size and sophistication face exposure. Reading § 1981a(b)(1) to require a showing of egregious conduct, independent of the bare finding of intentional discrimination, honors these constitutional guideposts by ensuring that punitive exposure corresponds to conduct genuinely deserving of punishment and deterrence—not to close factual disputes about an employer's business judgment.

**IV. The Legislative History Confirms That Congress Intended Punitive Damages to Remain an Extraordinary Remedy.**

The interpretive memorandum submitted by Senator Dole and his co-sponsors during the 1991 Act's passage explained that punitive damages would be available only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). That understanding is consistent with the pre-1991 landscape under § 1981, where courts—applying the *Smith v. Wade* standard to closely analogous facts—had likewise required conduct substantially more blameworthy than the intent necessary to establish liability itself. There is no sound reason to read § 1981a's parallel "malice or reckless indifference" language more permissively than the common-law standard from which it was drawn, or than Congress's own sponsors described it.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, an award of punitive damages under Title VII should require proof that the employer's conduct was egregious or outrageous, independent of—and in addition to—the state-of-mind showing of malice or reckless indifference. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
