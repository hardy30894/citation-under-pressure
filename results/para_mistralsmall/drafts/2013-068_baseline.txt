**ARGUMENT**

**I. The First Circuit Correctly Upheld the Massachusetts Buffer-Zone Law as a Content-Neutral, Narrowly Tailored Time, Place, and Manner Regulation Consistent with the First Amendment.**

The Massachusetts statute at issue—Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½—is a facially content-neutral regulation of speech that satisfies intermediate scrutiny under *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781 (1989). The First Circuit correctly applied this framework in affirming the law’s constitutionality. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I)*, 571 F.3d 167, 176–81 (1st Cir. 2009), *aff’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds*, 134 S. Ct. 2518 (2014).

A. The Law Is Content-Neutral and Viewpoint-Neutral.

The statute does not target speech based on its message, subject matter, or viewpoint. *See Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 720 (2000) (upholding a similar buffer-zone law as content-neutral). It applies equally to all individuals—regardless of their message—who seek to engage others within the 35-foot buffer zone. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 177. The law’s exemptions—covering patients, employees, and municipal agents—are defined by function, not by the content of speech. *See id.* at 178. This neutrality distinguishes the law from impermissible content-based restrictions, which are subject to strict scrutiny. *See United States v. Playboy Entm’t Group, Inc.*, 529 U.S. 803, 813 (2000).

B. The Law Is Narrowly Tailored to Serve Significant Governmental Interests.

The Commonwealth has a substantial and well-documented interest in protecting patients and clinic employees from harassment, intimidation, and interference as they access reproductive healthcare facilities. *See Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 767–68 (1994) (upholding a 36-foot buffer zone as serving the state’s interest in public safety and order). The Massachusetts legislature found that prior laws were insufficient to address persistent disruptions, including physical blockades and confrontational tactics. *See* 2007 Mass. Acts ch. 171, § 1. The 35-foot buffer zone directly advances this interest by ensuring safe access without criminalizing speech itself. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715 (recognizing the state’s interest in protecting patients from unwanted approaches).

The law is narrowly tailored because it leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. Petitioners may speak, distribute literature, display signs, and engage in prayer outside the buffer zone. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181. The record confirms that petitioners have successfully communicated with patients before they enter the zone. *See id.* at 180–81. The First Circuit correctly rejected the claim that the law is overbroad, as it does not prohibit speech but merely regulates its location. *See id.* at 181–82.

C. The Law Does Not Violate the First Amendment as Applied to the Challenged Clinics.

The First Circuit properly applied *Ward* in evaluating the law’s application to the Boston, Worcester, and Springfield clinics. *See id.* at 181–84. The court found that adequate alternative channels remained, including oral speech, signage, and literature distribution outside the buffer zone. *See id.* The record supports this conclusion. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen III)*, 844 F. Supp. 2d 206, 213–25 (D. Mass. 2012). The law does not prevent petitioners from conveying their message; it merely requires them to do so at a reasonable distance from clinic entrances. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 720.

**II. *Hill v. Colorado* Remains Good Law and Should Not Be Overruled.**

The petitioners urge this Court to limit or overrule *Hill*, but that decision remains controlling precedent. *Hill* upheld a 100-foot buffer zone around healthcare facilities, finding it a valid time, place, and manner regulation. 530 U.S. at 720. The Court rejected the argument that the law was a content-based restriction on speech, emphasizing its neutrality and tailoring to the state’s interest in public safety. *Id.* at 715–20.

The Massachusetts law is materially indistinguishable from the Colorado statute. Both are content-neutral, narrowly tailored regulations that leave open ample alternative channels for speech. *Compare id.* at 715–20, *with McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 176–81. The First Circuit correctly relied on *Hill* in upholding the Massachusetts law. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181–82. This Court should reaffirm *Hill* and decline to overrule it.

**III. The First Circuit Properly Applied the Law of the Case Doctrine.**

The petitioners’ attempt to relitigate the facial constitutionality of the law is barred by the law of the case doctrine. *See United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011). The First Circuit previously upheld the law in *McCullen I*, and the petitioners have not shown a dramatic change in controlling legal authority or a miscarriage of justice. *See id.* at 14. Their reliance on *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), and other cases is misplaced, as those decisions addressed entirely different factual and legal contexts. *See Matthews*, 643 F.3d at 14.

**Conclusion**

The Massachusetts buffer-zone law is a constitutional, content-neutral regulation that serves significant governmental interests without suppressing speech. The First Circuit correctly upheld it under *Ward* and *Hill*, and this Court should affirm.
