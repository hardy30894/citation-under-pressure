I. The District Court Erred in Denying Habeas Relief on Exhaustion Grounds.

Federal habeas jurisdiction is not defeated by failure to exhaust tribal remedies where the record shows no deliberate bypass. Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 426 (1963) (exhaustion rests on comity, not jurisdiction). The requirement is one of comity, not an inflexible rule. Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944); Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950); Ex parte Royall, 117 U.S. 241, 251 (1886). Selam presented his compulsory-process claim to the tribal court of appeals; the confrontation claim, though first raised in federal court, concerns the same trials and witnesses. Under these circumstances, the district court should have reached the merits rather than finding waiver.

II. Selam Was Denied Rights Secured by the Indian Civil Rights Act.

The Confrontation Clause guarantees that the accused shall enjoy the right to be confronted with the witnesses against him. Pointer v. Texas, 380 U.S. 400, 403 (1965). In the second trial the five-year-old complainant did not appear; her statements were introduced through four witnesses. A witness is not unavailable for confrontation purposes unless the prosecution has made a good-faith effort to secure his presence. Barber v. Page, 390 U.S. 719, 724-25 (1968). No such effort appears on this record.

The right to compulsory process likewise forbids arbitrary denial of the defendant’s ability to present witnesses in his favor. Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967). Although Selam supplied names, the tribal court took no steps to compel attendance after he declined to assist counsel further. A waiver of constitutional rights must be knowing and intelligent. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). The record contains no such waiver.

Because the district court declined to examine these claims on the merits, the judgment should be reversed and the petition remanded for consideration under the standards set forth above.
