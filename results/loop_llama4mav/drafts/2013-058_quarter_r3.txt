The Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under Section 522 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code. The plain meaning of the statute, combined with a long line of judicial precedent, supports this conclusion.

The Bankruptcy Code exempts certain retirement funds from the bankruptcy estate. 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C). The key issue is whether an inherited IRA constitutes "retirement funds" for the beneficiary. As the Seventh Circuit correctly observed, an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund for the beneficiary because it is not held for their retirement.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that exemptions in bankruptcy law should be narrowly construed. In Re Irwin, 95 F.2d 925 (2d Cir. 1938), the court applied a strict interpretation to exemptions under the Bankruptcy Code. See also Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181 (1902); Bank of Minden v. Clement, 256 U.S. 126 (1921).

In Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, the Supreme Court noted that the Bankruptcy Code is designed to provide a fair and equitable distribution of a debtor's assets among creditors. The Court stated that the purpose of the Bankruptcy Act is "to convert the assets of the bankrupt into cash for distribution among creditors, and then to relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness and permit him to start afresh." 186 U.S. at 186.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is consistent with this long line of precedent. By holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt from the bankruptcy estate, the court ensured that the Bankruptcy Code is applied in a fair and equitable manner. The decision should be affirmed.

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker is supported by a long line of judicial precedent and is consistent with the plain meaning of the Bankruptcy Code. The decision should be affirmed, and inherited IRAs should not be exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy.
