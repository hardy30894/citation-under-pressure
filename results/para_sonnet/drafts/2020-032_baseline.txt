# ARGUMENT

## A Claim for Nominal Damages, Standing Alone, Cannot Prevent Mootness Once the Challenged Policy Is Repealed

Article III confines the federal judicial power to actual, live "Cases" and "Controversies." *Lewis v. Continental Bank Corp.*, 494 U.S. 472, 477 (1990). A case becomes moot when "the issues presented are no longer 'live'" or the parties "lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome." *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). Because GGC voluntarily repealed both the Speech Zone Policy and the relevant Student Code provision before judgment, Petitioners' request for declaratory and injunctive relief is indisputably moot—a point Petitioners did not even contest below. The sole question is whether a bare, unadorned demand for nominal damages, untethered to any compensable injury, can keep this case alive.

It cannot. As the Eleventh Circuit correctly held below, and as the en banc court held in *Flanigan's Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017), "a claim for nominal damages, by itself, cannot save a case from mootness" once the challenged conduct has ceased. This rule flows directly from the constitutional definition of standing and mootness as requiring "a personal stake in the outcome" that a favorable judgment will actually redress. *City of Los Angeles v. Lyons*, 461 U.S. 95, 101 (1983). Nominal damages are, by definition, "a trifling sum awarded when a plaintiff establishes that he has suffered a violation of some legal right, but cannot show that this violation caused him actual injury." *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266–67 (1978) (Marshall, J., concurring in part). Because they compensate nothing, a bare prayer for nominal damages is not the kind of concrete stake that keeps a controversy "live" once the predicate conduct has stopped.

Respondents recognize that in *Carey v. Piphus*, this Court held that nominal damages are available to vindicate a violation of procedural due process even absent proof of actual injury, and that plaintiffs "may be able to establish neither actual injury entitled to be compensated by compensatory damages nor a colorable claim for equitable relief" and yet still recover a nominal sum. *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266–67. But *Carey* addressed the *availability* of nominal damages as a remedy for a completed constitutional violation already before the court on the merits; it did not address whether a mere request for such damages suffices, standing alone, to keep an otherwise dead controversy alive under Article III. The Eleventh Circuit's holding in *Flanigan's*—and the decision below—do not deny that nominal damages may be recoverable in an appropriate case. They hold only that a plaintiff cannot manufacture a live controversy solely by appending a request for a nominal sum to claims for injunctive and declaratory relief that have themselves become moot through intervening voluntary cessation of the challenged conduct.

This limitation follows naturally from *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83 (1998), which reaffirmed that redressability is a core component of the case-or-controversy requirement, and that a plaintiff must demonstrate that the relief sought will remedy an actual, particularized injury, not merely vindicate an abstract interest in the correct application of law. See *id.* at 107 ("relief that does not remedy the injury suffered cannot bootstrap a plaintiff into federal court"). A one-dollar award for a policy that no longer exists redresses nothing; it is not compensation, and it does not restrain any ongoing or threatened conduct. To hold otherwise would permit every plaintiff who ever suffered a constitutional grievance, however fleeting, to keep a federal case alive in perpetuity by the simple incantation of the words "nominal damages," regardless of whether the defendant has fully and voluntarily remedied the underlying policy. That result cannot be squared with the "case or controversy" limitation that "subsists through all stages of federal judicial proceedings." *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 102 (quoting *Lewis*, 494 U.S. at 477).

Nor is this a case in which the voluntary-cessation doctrine preserves the controversy. Under that doctrine, a defendant's voluntary cessation of challenged conduct moots a case only if it is "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000). The district court found, and Petitioners did not challenge on appeal, that GGC had "unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies and there is no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them." That finding satisfies *Laidlaw*'s demanding standard and forecloses any suggestion that GGC's revision was a litigation gambit designed to evade review.

Petitioners' remaining argument—that their complaint should be construed to seek compensatory, not merely nominal, damages—was correctly rejected below. As the Eleventh Circuit explained, compensatory damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 must rest on "actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the 'abstract value' of the constitutional rights that may have been violated." *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000); see also *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 308 (1986) (damages under § 1983 "may not be based on the abstract 'value' or 'importance' of constitutional rights"). Petitioners' amended complaint never identified any actual injury—no lost wages, no expenditure, no diagnosed harm—and their belated, appellate assertions of humiliation and travel costs cannot substitute for well-pleaded allegations. The district court therefore correctly read the complaint, as a whole and in the light most favorable to Petitioners, to seek only nominal relief. See *Chaparro v. Carnival Corp.*, 693 F.3d 1333, 1335 (11th Cir. 2012).

Finally, the district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to treat an informal request embedded in a response brief as a motion for leave to amend. This Court has long required that requests to amend be presented in a proper motion specifying the substance of the proposed amendment, not merely raised as an aside in opposition papers. See *Foman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962) (leave to amend should be freely given "when justice so requires," but the request must be properly presented and its basis articulated). Petitioners never did so, and the court left the door open for them to seek amendment through the proper procedural vehicle. They simply chose not to.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
