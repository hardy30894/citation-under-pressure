The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are designed to give debtors a fresh start, but this protection is not available to those who commit fraud. The question presented here is whether individuals who commit fraud can discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded, under the Bankruptcy Code. The answer is no.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." The plain language of this provision indicates that debts resulting from fraud are nondischargeable. The Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit correctly held that the debt owed by Edward S. Cohen to Hilda de la Cruz and other tenants is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) because it was obtained through Cohen's actual fraud.

The elements required to establish nondischargeability under § 523(a)(2)(A) are well-established. A creditor must show that the debtor obtained money, property, or services through a material misrepresentation, that the debtor knew the representation was false or made with gross recklessness as to its truth, that the debtor intended to deceive the creditor, that the creditor reasonably relied on the debtor's false representations, and that the creditor sustained a loss and damages as a proximate result of the debtor's materially false representations (In re Poskanzer, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992)).

Cohen's actions meet these elements. By charging rents in excess of the amounts permitted under the Hoboken Rent Control ordinance, Cohen made an implicit representation that the rents were lawful. His failure to inform the tenants of the rent control limits and his failure to comply with the ordinance demonstrate a reckless disregard for the truth, satisfying the knowledge and intent elements (In re Cohn, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118-19 (3d Cir.1995); In re Woolley, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr.E.D.Va.1991)).

The award of treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is also nondischargeable. The Eleventh Circuit's decision in In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir.1993), is persuasive on this point. The court held that punitive damages are not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) because they are part of the debt incurred through the debtor's fraudulent conduct. Similarly, the treble damages awarded in this case are a direct result of Cohen's fraud and are therefore nondischargeable.

The "fresh start" policy of the Bankruptcy Code does not protect debtors who commit fraud. As the Supreme Court noted in Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991), the Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are intended to benefit the "honest but unfortunate debtor." Cohen's actions were not those of an honest debtor, and he should not be permitted to discharge the debts resulting from his fraud.

In conclusion, the debts resulting from Cohen's fraud, including the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
