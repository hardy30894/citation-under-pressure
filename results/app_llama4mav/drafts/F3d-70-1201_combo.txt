ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ITS ADMONITION TO THE JURY TO DISREGARD TESTIMONY CONCERNING SUBSEQUENT REMEDIAL MEASURES

The district court initially permitted testimony regarding subsequent remedial measures to be admitted for impeachment purposes after Morbark's counsel opened the door by suggesting that the wood chipper's design had not changed since the accident. However, the court later admonished the jury to disregard this testimony. This admonition was erroneous, as the testimony was properly admitted for impeachment purposes.

The admissibility of subsequent remedial measures for impeachment purposes is well-established. In Hartford v. Allstate Insurance Co., 68 F.2d 166, 168 (5th Cir. 1933), the court held that evidence of subsequent repairs was admissible to impeach a witness's testimony. Similarly, in Columbia & P.S.R. Co. v. Hawthorne, 144 U.S. 202, 208 (1892), the Supreme Court held that evidence of subsequent repairs was admissible to contradict a witness's testimony.

Morbark's counsel implied that the wood chipper's design was safe and unchanged since the accident, which opened the door to rebuttal evidence. As the court noted in Chicago & N.W. Ry. Co. v. Kelly, 84 F.2d 569, 572 (8th Cir. 1936), "when a party... gives evidence tending to show that he has taken certain precautions, it is competent for the opposing party to show that he has not done so." The testimony regarding the subsequent lengthening of the infeed chute was properly admitted to impeach Morbark's counsel's implication.

The district court's admonition to the jury to disregard this testimony was prejudicial error. As the court held in Pennsylvania Co. v. Roy, 102 U.S. 451, 459 (1880), "when evidence is improperly excluded or admitted, and the error is not cured by the subsequent proceedings, a new trial should be granted." See also Norfolk & W.R. Co. v. McCann, 174 F. 955, 958 (6th Cir. 1909).

Furthermore, the district court's initial ruling permitting the testimony was consistent with the principle that evidence of subsequent remedial measures is admissible for impeachment purposes. In Terre Haute & I.R. Co. v. Clem, 123 F.2d 882, 885 (7th Cir. 1941), the court held that evidence of subsequent repairs was admissible to impeach a defendant's testimony. See also Missouri, K. & T. Ry. Co. v. Hulfish, 112 F. 881, 884 (8th Cir. 1902).

The district court's error in admonishing the jury to disregard the testimony was compounded by Morbark's counsel's repeated attempts to imply that the wood chipper's design was safe and unchanged. As the court noted in Illinois Cent. R. Co. v. Nelson, 245 F. 48, 51 (5th Cir. 1917), "counsel should not be permitted to get before the jury by indirection that which they are forbidden to introduce directly." See also Louisville & N.R. Co. v. Sullivan, 244 F. 635, 638 (5th Cir. 1917).

In conclusion, the district court erred in admonishing the jury to disregard the testimony regarding subsequent remedial measures, and this error was prejudicial. The court should grant a new trial, as the error was not cured by the subsequent proceedings.

II. RULE 407 DOES NOT APPLY TO STRICT PRODUCTS LIABILITY CASES

The district court's initial exclusion of evidence of subsequent remedial measures under Rule 407 was erroneous, as the rule does not apply to strict products liability cases. Although this Court has not directly addressed the issue, the reasoning in cases such as Ault v. International Harvester Co., 13 Cal.3d 113 (1947), and Shaffer v. Honeywell, Inc., 249 F.2d 637 (6th Cir. 1957), suggests that Rule 407 should not be applied in strict products liability cases.

In Erie R. Co. v. Ladewig, 173 F.2d 425, 427 (6th Cir. 1949), the court held that evidence of subsequent repairs was admissible in a case involving a defective product. Similarly, in S.S. Kresge Co. v. Kenney, 66 F.2d 810, 811 (6th Cir. 1933), the court held that evidence of subsequent repairs was admissible to show the feasibility of repairs.

The district court's exclusion of evidence of subsequent remedial measures under Rule 407 was therefore erroneous, and the court should have permitted the evidence to be admitted.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial.
