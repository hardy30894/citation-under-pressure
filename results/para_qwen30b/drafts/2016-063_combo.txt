**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board decision dismissing an employment discrimination case on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to interpret the statutory framework governing judicial review of federal employee appeals. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in this case, which transfers the matter to the Federal Circuit based on its precedent in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is fundamentally at odds with the text, structure, and settled precedent of the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978. The Court must reverse, for the D.C. Circuit’s conclusion rests on a strained reading of *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S.Ct. 596, 184 L.Ed.2d 433 (2012), and a failure to recognize that *Kloeckner* did not overrule, nor even address, the jurisdictional distinction that has long governed the review of MSPB decisions.

The statutory text of 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A) provides that judicial review of a “mixed case”—one in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and [alleges] that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” federal law—“shall be filed in district court.” This provision establishes a clear and exclusive forum for such claims. The key phrase is “may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board,” which refers to the *subject matter jurisdiction* of the Board, not the procedural posture of the appeal. When the Board dismisses a case for lack of jurisdiction, it is not merely rejecting a procedural defect—it is declaring that the action in question is not one that the Board may hear. That determination is not a mere procedural hurdle; it is a threshold judgment on the Board’s own authority.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* to uphold Federal Circuit review in the face of a jurisdictional dismissal is misplaced. *Powell* did not stand for the proposition that all pre-merits dismissals are reviewable only in the Federal Circuit. Rather, *Powell* held that when the Board determines that an employee’s action was voluntary—thereby removing it from the Board’s jurisdiction under 5 U.S.C. § 7512(1)—the case is not a “mixed case” within the meaning of § 7702(a)(1)(A), because the employee was not “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” 158 F.3d at 599. The Court in *Powell* did not purport to define the scope of judicial review; it merely applied the statutory language to a case in which the Board had concluded that the employee’s separation was voluntary and thus not appealable. The Court’s reasoning was grounded in the premise that the Board’s jurisdictional determination was dispositive of whether the case qualified as a “mixed case” under § 7702.

This Court has long recognized that the distinction between jurisdictional and procedural dismissals is not merely technical but substantive. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 510 (1940), the Court held that “a court’s jurisdiction is not to be defeated by a mere irregularity in procedure.” Similarly, in *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 312 U.S. 176, 180 (1941), the Court emphasized that “the power of a court to act is not dependent upon the form of the proceeding, but upon the nature of the matter before it.” These principles apply with equal force to administrative tribunals. When the Board determines that it lacks jurisdiction over an action because it was voluntary, it is not merely applying a procedural rule—it is making a determination that the action is not one that the Board may review. That determination is not a mere procedural bar; it is a jurisdictional one.

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* on the ground that it involved a “procedural” dismissal while this case involves a “jurisdictional” one is not only unsupported by the record but contradicts the very logic of *Kloeckner*. In *Kloeckner*, the Supreme Court held that when the Board dismisses a mixed case on a procedural ground—such as untimeliness—judicial review lies in district court. 133 S.Ct. at 604. The Court did so because, in its view, the employee had “been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB,” and thus the case qualified as a “mixed case” under § 7702(a)(1)(A), regardless of the procedural defect. The Court did not say that all dismissals—whether procedural or jurisdictional—must be reviewed in district court. It said only that when the Board dismisses a case that is otherwise a mixed case on a procedural ground, the employee may seek review in district court. The Court’s opinion repeatedly emphasized that the employee had “brought a mixed case”—that is, she was affected by an action appealable to the Board. Id. at 604.

This distinction is critical. In *Kloeckner*, the Board did not question whether the action was appealable; it only questioned whether the appeal was timely. In this case, the Board concluded that the action—retirement and suspension—was not appealable because it was voluntary. That is a jurisdictional determination, not a procedural one. The Board’s conclusion that the action was voluntary is not a mere procedural defect; it is a determination that the action falls outside the Board’s jurisdiction altogether. As the Court held in *United States v. McQuade*, 309 U.S. 515, 518 (1940), “a court cannot act where it has no jurisdiction.” The same principle applies to the MSPB.
