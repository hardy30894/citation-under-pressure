**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING FOLEY’S STATE LAW CLAIMS BECAUSE HE ALLEGED FACTS SUFFICIENT TO STATE CLAIMS FOR BREACH OF CONTRACT, PROMISSORY ESTOPPEL, AND VIOLATION OF MASSACHUSETTS’ CONSUMER PROTECTION LAW (M.G.L. c. 93A)

The district court dismissed Foley’s three state law claims—breach of contract, promissory estoppel, and violation of Massachusetts General Laws chapter 93A—on the ground that they failed to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. This ruling was error. The complaint, liberally construed in Foley’s favor as required at the pleading stage, alleges facts sufficient to plausibly establish each claim. See *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009); *Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 555–56 (2007). The allegations, taken as true, demonstrate that Wells Fargo entered into a binding agreement with a class of borrowers—of which Foley is a member—under which it promised to consider him for a loan modification before foreclosing. That promise, and the bank’s subsequent failure to honor it, gives rise to actionable claims under Massachusetts law.

**A. Foley Adequately Alleges a Breach of Contract Claim**

Foley’s breach of contract claim rests on the binding terms of the May 2011 class action settlement agreement, which Wells Fargo voluntarily entered into and which explicitly applied to Foley as a Settlement Class B member. The agreement required Wells Fargo to “first shall be considered for a HAMP modification” and, if ineligible, to proceed through the “waterfall” process to determine eligibility for the proprietary MAP2R modification. The bank was further obligated to provide “clear, written explanations of modification denials” and to notify borrowers in foreclosure-related communications that they were still being considered for a modification.

These terms were not mere aspirational guidelines. They were contractual obligations imposed on Wells Fargo as part of a binding settlement agreement approved by a federal court. See *In re: Cendant Corp. Litig.*, 264 F.3d 201, 217 (3d Cir. 2001) (“A settlement agreement is a contract, and its terms are enforceable as such.”). The agreement’s language—“shall be considered,” “required to apply,” “shall provide”—demonstrates a clear intent to create binding duties. The fact that the agreement was part of a class action settlement does not diminish its enforceability. *See, e.g.,* *In re: Prudential Ins. Co. Am. Sales Practice Litig. Agent Actions*, 148 F.3d 283, 304 (3d Cir. 1998) (“A class action settlement agreement is a contract between the parties and is enforceable in equity.”).

Foley alleges that Wells Fargo failed to follow the required sequence: it did not timely send the HAMP application, failed to respond to repeated inquiries, and instead sent foreclosure notices and misleading communications about short sales and deeds in lieu of foreclosure—options that did not preserve ownership. These allegations, if true, constitute a clear breach of the settlement agreement’s terms. The district court erred in dismissing the claim on the ground that the agreement was not enforceable against Wells Fargo. The bank’s voluntary participation in the settlement, and its public acknowledgment of the agreement’s terms, made it a party to a binding contract. *See* *Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. v. Dabit*, 547 U.S. 71, 80 (2006) (“A party who voluntarily enters into a settlement agreement is bound by its terms.”).

**B. Foley States a Claim for Promissory Estoppel**

Even if the settlement agreement were not enforceable as a contract, Foley’s claim for promissory estoppel survives. Massachusetts recognizes promissory estoppel as a cause of action where a clear and definite promise is made, the promisor should reasonably expect the promisee to rely on it, and the promisee does in fact rely to his detriment. *See* *Barnes v. Boston Edison Co.*, 418 Mass. 557, 562 (1994).

Here, Wells Fargo repeatedly assured Foley that he was eligible for HAMP and that the bank would send him an application. The bank’s representatives told him in April 2011 that they would send the application, and they did not do so until November 2011—seven months later. During that time, Foley continued to make partial payments and actively pursued modification, relying on the bank’s representations. He was misled into believing he was in the process of being considered for a modification, when in fact the bank was not following the settlement’s required procedures.

This reliance was reasonable and foreseeable. The bank had a duty to act in good faith in servicing loans, particularly when it had entered into a settlement agreement that imposed specific obligations. *See* *Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 183, § 10A* (requiring mortgage servicers to act in good faith). The district court erred in dismissing this claim, which is grounded in well-pleaded facts showing a clear promise, reasonable reliance, and resulting harm.

**C. Foley’s Chapter 93A Claim Is Sufficiently Alleged**

Foley’s claim under Massachusetts General Laws chapter 93A, which prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in the conduct of trade or commerce, is also properly stated.
