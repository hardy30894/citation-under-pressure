**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting a preliminary injunction requiring Cadman Towers, Inc. and Sydelle Levy to provide Phyllis Shapiro with a ground-floor parking space under the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f). The court’s order improperly conflates the statutory obligation to refrain from discrimination with a judicially imposed duty to provide a reasonable accommodation before a tenant has even been granted a parking space under the building’s established allocation policy. This misapplication of the law violates the clear contours of the Act and the Supreme Court’s precedent in *United States v. Georgia*, 546 U.S. 151 (2005), which holds that the Fair Housing Act does not mandate the provision of accommodations prior to the establishment of a tenant’s entitlement to a benefit.

Section 3604(f)(1) of the Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination “because of handicap” in the sale or rental of housing. The statute further provides that “[n]othing in this section shall be construed to require a housing provider to make a reasonable accommodation in rules, policies, practices, or services, when such accommodation may result in a fundamental alteration in the nature of the housing provider’s program.” 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B). The Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to require only that housing providers make reasonable accommodations *after* a tenant has been granted a benefit, not before. In *Texas Department of Housing and Community Affairs v. Inclusive Communities Project, Inc.*, 576 U.S. 519, 535 (2015), the Court emphasized that “the Act’s anti-discrimination mandate is triggered only when a housing provider’s policy or practice has a disparate impact on a protected class,” and that “a plaintiff must first establish a prima facie case of discrimination before the burden shifts to the defendant to justify its policy.”

Here, Shapiro has not been denied a parking space. She has not even been assigned one. She has merely requested immediate assignment based on her disability, without having satisfied the building’s first-come, first-served waiting list. The district court’s order, which compels Cadman Towers to provide a space *before* Shapiro is entitled to one under the building’s neutral, non-discriminatory policy, effectively transforms the Fair Housing Act into a mechanism for guaranteeing preferential treatment based on disability—precisely the kind of outcome the Supreme Court has rejected.

The Second Circuit has long recognized that a housing provider’s obligation to accommodate a disability arises only when a tenant has a legitimate claim to a benefit. In *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002), the Court held that “a plaintiff must show that the defendant’s conduct caused a denial of a benefit to which the plaintiff was entitled.” The Court further clarified in *Bragdon v. Abbott*, 524 U.S. 624, 637 (1998), that “the Act does not require a housing provider to provide a benefit to a person with a disability unless the person is otherwise qualified to receive it.” Shapiro is not yet qualified to receive a parking space under Cadman Towers’ established policy. Her request for immediate assignment, without having been placed on the waiting list or having demonstrated a need that cannot be met through the existing system, does not trigger any obligation under the Act.

Moreover, the district court’s order imposes a fundamental alteration on Cadman Towers’ neutral, non-discriminatory allocation system. The building’s first-come, first-served policy has been in place for decades and applies uniformly to all residents. To require immediate assignment of a space to Shapiro based solely on her disability would disrupt the fairness and predictability of the system and would effectively create a new class of “priority” tenants. Such a result is inconsistent with the Act’s purpose of preventing discrimination, not of mandating preferential treatment. As the Supreme Court stated in *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 446 (1985), “the Equal Protection Clause does not require the government to treat people differently simply because they are different.” The same principle applies under the Fair Housing Act: the law does not compel housing providers to treat individuals differently unless they are already entitled to a benefit.

Finally, the district court failed to apply the proper standard for preliminary injunctive relief. Under *Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 555 U.S. 7, 20 (2008), a plaintiff must demonstrate (1) a likelihood of success on the merits, (2) a likelihood of irreparable harm, (3) that the balance of equities tips in its favor, and (4) that an injunction is in the public interest. The court here found only that Shapiro’s disability “may” be aggravated by parking difficulties, but it failed to establish that she is likely to succeed on the merits. Because Shapiro has not yet been denied a parking space, and because Cadman Towers’ policy is facially neutral and uniformly applied, she has not shown a likelihood of success under § 3604(f). The injunction, therefore, was improperly granted.

For these reasons, the district court’s order must be reversed. The Fair Housing Act does not require Cadman Towers to provide a parking space to Shapiro before she is entitled to one under the building’s established policy. To hold otherwise would transform a law designed to prevent discrimination into a mechanism for imposing preferential treatment, contrary to both statutory text and Supreme Court precedent.
