**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Enhancing the Lodestar Based on Exceptional Performance and Results.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s award of attorneys’ fees, including a $4.5 million enhancement, because the lodestar calculation did not fully account for the extraordinary quality of counsel’s representation and the exceptional results achieved. *See Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008). This Court has long recognized that district courts possess broad discretion in determining reasonable attorneys’ fees under fee-shifting statutes, and such awards are reviewed only for abuse of discretion. *See Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 896 (1984); *Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air v. Pennsylvania (“Delaware Valley I”)*, 478 U.S. 546, 561 (1986).

The district court’s enhancement was fully consistent with this precedent. It expressly found that counsel’s performance was “superb,” the litigation was “long and difficult,” and the results obtained were “exceptional.” *Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). This Court has repeatedly held that such findings justify an upward adjustment in the rare case where the lodestar fails to reflect the true value of the services rendered. *See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000). The Eleventh Circuit properly deferred to these factual determinations, as it must. *See Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 437 (1983) (“The district court has discretion in determining the fee question, and its decision should not be disturbed unless it is clearly erroneous.”).

**II. The Supreme Court Has Authorized Enhancements in Exceptional Cases.**

Contrary to Petitioners’ contention, the Supreme Court has never foreclosed the possibility of enhancing the lodestar based on superior performance and exceptional results. In *Blum*, the Court held that the lodestar is presumed reasonable but left open the door for enhancements in “rare” and “exceptional” cases supported by specific evidence. 465 U.S. at 899. The Court reiterated this principle in *Delaware Valley I*, where it expressly declined to overrule *Blum* and reaffirmed that enhancements remain permissible in appropriate circumstances. 478 U.S. at 565-68.

Petitioners misread *Delaware Valley I* as categorically barring enhancements. To the contrary, the Court explicitly stated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” *Id.* at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901). The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied this standard, and its decision is fully consistent with controlling Supreme Court authority.

**III. The District Court’s Findings Were Supported by the Record and Did Not Violate Due Process.**

Petitioners argue that the district court’s findings amounted to impermissible ex parte testimony. This contention is meritless. The district court’s observations were based on its firsthand experience observing counsel throughout the litigation and its familiarity with prevailing market rates. *See Kenny A. III*, 454 F. Supp. 2d at 1286, 1290. Such observations are not only permissible but expected, as district courts are uniquely positioned to evaluate the quality of advocacy. *See, e.g., Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999) (“The district court is in the best position to evaluate trial-level advocacy.”).

Moreover, the district court’s findings were not arbitrary. It identified specific examples of counsel’s exceptional work, including the complexity of the litigation, the skill required to achieve the settlement, and the long-term benefits to the class. These findings were amply supported by the record and provided a sufficient basis for the enhancement. *See Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899 (requiring “specific evidence” of superior performance and exceptional results).

**IV. Every Circuit to Address This Issue Has Permitted Enhancements in Exceptional Cases.**

Petitioners’ claim of a circuit split is unsupported. Every circuit to consider the issue—including the Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits—has held that enhancements are permissible in rare and exceptional cases. *See, e.g., Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with this consensus.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in enhancing the lodestar based on counsel’s exceptional performance and the extraordinary results achieved. The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed this award, and this Court should do likewise.
