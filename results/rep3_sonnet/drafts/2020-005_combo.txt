# ARGUMENT

## I. Due Process Requires Only That the Defendant's Forum Contacts Be Sufficiently Related to the Litigation, Not That They Be the Historical Cause of the Plaintiff's Injury.

The touchstone of specific jurisdiction has never been a strict causal test. In *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), this Court held that a corporation which "exercises the privilege of conducting activities within a state" thereby "enjoys the benefits and protection of the laws of that state," and that "the exercise of that privilege may give rise to obligations." *Id.* at 319. The Court asked only whether the defendant's obligations "arose out of those very activities," *id.* at 320, and whether maintenance of the suit would offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *Id.* at 316. That standard is relational, not causal: it examines the *relationship* between the forum contacts and the litigation, not whether the particular contact was the proximate cause of the plaintiff's injury.

This Court confirmed that relational, non-mechanical approach in *Travelers Health Ass'n v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643 (1950), where it held that "the amount and kind of activities which must be done within a state in order to make it reasonable and just to subject a foreign corporation to the jurisdiction of that state" cannot be reduced to a formula, but "must be determined in each case." *Id.* at 649. And in *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220 (1957), the Court sustained jurisdiction over an out-of-state insurer where the only contact was a single contract with a forum resident, reasoning that "[i]t is sufficient for purposes of due process that the suit was based on a contract which had substantial connection with that State." *Id.* at 223. Nothing in *McGee* required that the specific contract clause sued upon be the historical cause of the contact; it was enough that a substantial connection existed between the defendant's forum activity and the litigation.

## II. Ford's Continuous, Deliberate Marketing, Sale, and Servicing of Explorer Vehicles in Montana Establish the Requisite Relationship to Gullett's Claims.

Ford is not a stranger to Montana. Ford has for decades advertised, distributed, sold, and serviced Ford Explorers—the very model at issue—throughout the State, through an extensive network of Montana dealerships. This is not the "unilateral activity of those who claim some relationship with a nonresident defendant" that this Court condemned in *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). It is Ford's own conduct, and Ford has "purposefully avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State," *id.*, by cultivating a market for Explorers among Montana residents such as Gullett, who was killed by an alleged defect in precisely the kind of vehicle Ford placed and supported there.

*Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437 (1952), confirms that a defendant's continuous and systematic forum activity may support jurisdiction over claims not literally traceable to a single transaction. There, this Court upheld jurisdiction over a Philippine mining company sued in Ohio on causes of action having "no relation" to the company's wartime Ohio operations, because those operations were themselves so substantial. *Id.* at 447–48. If general activity untethered to the claim can ground jurisdiction under *Perkins*, then Ford's activity—marketing and servicing the identical product line whose alleged defect injured a Montana resident in Montana—easily satisfies the more demanding, but still relational, "relate to" standard for specific jurisdiction.

The stream-of-commerce reasoning of *Gray v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp.*, 22 Ill. 2d 432, 176 N.E.2d 761 (1961), is instructive. There the Illinois Supreme Court held that a manufacturer who places its product into commerce with the expectation that it will be used or sold in other states may be subject to jurisdiction where the product causes injury, notwithstanding that the manufacturer's direct sale occurred elsewhere. Ford's conduct is even more deliberate here: Ford did not merely place vehicles into an undifferentiated national market—it specifically cultivated Montana as a market for Explorers through direct advertising and dealer sales within the State.

Finally, *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352 (1927), demonstrates that a State's interest in redressing injuries occurring on its own highways, caused by vehicles operating there, is itself a legitimate basis for jurisdiction consistent with due process. This Court upheld Massachusetts's implied-consent statute because the State's "power to regulate the use of its highways" and to protect its citizens from the "dangerous instrumentality" of automobiles justified subjecting nonresident motorists to suit. *Id.* at 356. Montana's interest is identical: Gullett died on a Montana highway because of an alleged defect in a vehicle that Ford marketed and serviced there.

## III. Fair Play and Substantial Justice Confirm the Reasonableness of Montana's Exercise of Jurisdiction.

As this Court held in *Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457 (1940), due process demands only that jurisdiction not offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *Id.* at 463. Montana has a manifest interest, recognized in *Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714 (1878), in adjudicating injuries to its citizens occurring within its borders. Ford, having chosen to do business there, cannot claim surprise or unfairness in defending claims arising from the very type of product it sold and serviced in that State.

The Montana Supreme Court's judgment should be affirmed.
