**ARGUMENT**

**I. A reviewing court may not consider extra-record evidence to assess the impact of plain error.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly confined its prejudice analysis to the trial record. In United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), the Supreme Court explained that plain-error review is “narrow” and “limited,” id. at 732, and that the fourth Olano factor—whether the error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings,” id. at 736 (internal quotation marks omitted)—must be assessed “in light of the entire record,” id. at 735. The Court has since reiterated that appellate courts “must not look to extra-record facts” when conducting this inquiry. United States v. Marcus, 560 U.S. 258, 265 (2010) (citing Olano, 507 U.S. at 735–36).

The Eleventh Circuit adhered to this principle. It examined the trial transcript, the jury instructions, and the parties’ arguments to determine whether the government’s failure to prove—and the court’s failure to instruct on—Greer’s knowledge of his status affected the trial’s fairness or integrity. That approach comports with Olano’s command that reviewing courts “cannot correct error that was not called to the district court’s attention unless [it] is plain and [it] affects substantial rights.” 507 U.S. at 731 (internal quotation marks omitted).

**II. The Eleventh Circuit did not err in concluding that Greer failed to show prejudice.**

Under Olano, Greer bore the burden to demonstrate that the errors “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” Id. at 734. The Eleventh Circuit found that Greer could not carry this burden because the record contained no evidence that he lacked knowledge of his felon status. The court properly relied on the trial record—including Greer’s prior convictions and the plea colloquy—to conclude that any error was harmless. See United States v. Veltmann, 6 F.3d 1483, 1487 (11th Cir. 1993) (holding that a defendant’s prior felony convictions supported the conclusion that a Rehaif error was harmless).

Greer’s reliance on United States v. Davis, 984 F.3d 1054 (11th Cir. 2021), is misplaced. In Davis, the court considered the defendant’s prior convictions to assess prejudice under Rehaif, but it did so based on the trial record, not extra-record evidence. Id. at 1060–61. The Eleventh Circuit’s approach here was consistent with Davis and with Olano’s requirement that prejudice be evaluated in light of the record as a whole.

**III. The Eleventh Circuit’s analysis did not undermine the fairness or integrity of judicial proceedings.**

The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that the errors did not “seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings,” Olano, 507 U.S. at 736, is supported by the record. The court did not rely on improper factors or engage in speculation. Instead, it carefully reviewed the trial transcript and the parties’ arguments to determine whether the errors had a prejudicial impact. This approach ensures that plain-error review remains “limited” and “narrow,” id. at 732, as the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
