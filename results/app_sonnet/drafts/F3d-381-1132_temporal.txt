ARGUMENT

I. STANDARD OF REVIEW

Summary judgment is appropriate only where there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact. Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944) (summary judgment cannot be sustained where inferences from the undisputed facts remain contested). Because the Court of Federal Claims resolved a legal question—whether the Government's conduct effected a physical taking—de novo review is appropriate.

II. THE GOVERNMENT'S LEASE OF EXCESS FIBER-OPTIC CAPACITY TO A PRIVATE TELECOMMUNICATIONS COMPANY EXCEEDS THE SCOPE OF THE 1953 EASEMENT AND EFFECTS A NEW PHYSICAL OCCUPATION OF TUTHILL'S LAND

A. A Physical Occupation for Purposes Beyond the Scope of the Government's Property Interest Is a Taking of a Distinct Property Right

The Court of Federal Claims held that "the scope of the easement is not relevant in this court because it cannot give rise to a physical taking." Tuthill, slip op. at 6. That holding cannot be reconciled with this Court's precedent recognizing that a landowner's right to exclude is itself a protected property interest, separate from the soil. In United States v. General Motors Corp., 323 U.S. 373, 378 (1945), the Supreme Court explained that the group of rights comprising property "may be composed of many strands," and that among the most valuable of these is "the right to exclude others." When the Government authorizes a use of land that exceeds what the landowner conveyed, it does not merely breach a contract—it seizes a stick from that bundle of rights that was never granted.

The 1953 easement conveyed to the BPA the right to erect and maintain "signal lines ... necessary in connection" with the power lines. It did not convey the right to construct a commercial telecommunications network for lease to unrelated third parties. When BPA installed fiber-optic cable far exceeding its own operational needs and then leased the surplus capacity to a private telecommunications company, it physically occupied Tuthill's land for a purpose wholly outside the four corners of the granted interest. That occupation—by a stranger to the easement, for a commercial purpose Tuthill never authorized—is indistinguishable from a new physical invasion of the servient estate.

B. Physical Invasion of Land for a Purpose Outside a Government Interest Has Long Been Treated as a Per Se Taking

This Court's predecessors have consistently held that any physical invasion of land, however slight, effects a taking if it destroys or appropriates the owner's beneficial use. In Pumpelly v. Green Bay Co., 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 166, 181 (1871), the Supreme Court held that a permanent flooding of land was a taking because "there is no difference, in effect, between a permanent condition of things by which the land is rendered unfit for use, ... and a direct proceeding to take it." So too here: BPA's decision to permit a private company to string its commercial telecommunications traffic across Tuthill's easement rendered the land subject to a use, and a occupier, entirely foreign to the original grant.

The physical-invasion doctrine does not require that the Government's own agents personally trespass; it is enough that the Government causes or permits an invasion. In Portsmouth Harbor Land & Hotel Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 327, 329–30 (1922), the Court held that repeated firing of projectiles across claimant's land, even absent any physical entry upon the soil, constituted a taking because the Government's conduct made "a permanent oppression" of the land unlawful. Likewise, in Peabody v. United States, 231 U.S. 530, 538 (1913), the Court recognized that where Government activity so burdens land as to work "a practical ouster of the possession," a taking occurs even though the Government never sets foot upon the property. And in United States v. Causby, 328 U.S. 256, 261 (1946), the Court held that when Government-authorized flights over land were "so low and so frequent as to be a direct and immediate interference with the enjoyment and use of the land, there is a taking," explaining further that "it is the owner's loss, not the taker's gain, which is the measure of the value of the property taken." 328 U.S. at 261. BPA's authorization of a private company's continuous, income-generating use of Tuthill's land is no less an "interference with the enjoyment and use of the land" than the low-altitude overflights condemned in Causby.

C. The Government Cannot Immunize an Unauthorized Occupation Merely Because It Also Occupies the Land for a Legitimate Purpose

The Government contends that because it lawfully installed fiber-optic cable within the scope of the easement, any subsequent lease of unused capacity works no "additional physical intrusion." But this Court's antecedents have rejected the notion that a taking is measured solely by whether new wires or structures are added to the land. In United States v. Dickinson, 331 U.S. 745, 748 (1947), the Supreme Court explained that "[p]roperty is taken in the constitutional sense when inroads are made upon an owner's use of it to an extent that ... a servitude has been acquired." The servitude the Government imposed here—commercial telecommunications transmission for a private carrier's profit—was never acquired by agreement, and Tuthill has received no compensation for it.

III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Court of Federal Claims erred in holding that the scope of the 1953 easement was categorically irrelevant to the physical-takings analysis. Tuthill respectfully requests that this Court reverse the grant of summary judgment to the Government, vacate the denial of Tuthill's cross-motion, and remand for further proceedings on the question of just compensation.
