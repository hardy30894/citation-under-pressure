**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT VIOLATES THE UNITED STATES CONSTITUTION BY ADDING AN UNAUTHORIZED QUALIFICATION FOR MEMBERS OF CONGRESS**

The Constitution of the United States explicitly prescribes the qualifications for membership in the United States House of Representatives and the United States Senate. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that a Representative must be “at least twenty-five Years of Age” and have been a “Citizen of the United States” for at least seven Years, and must “have been an Inhabitant of the State” he or she represents. Article I, Section 3, Clause 1 similarly requires that a Senator be “at least thirty Years of Age,” a “Citizen of the United States” for at least nine Years, and an “Inhabitant of the State” from which he or she is elected. These qualifications are not merely guidelines; they are the exclusive standards by which Congress may be constituted. The Framers of the Constitution deliberately chose to vest in Congress alone the power to determine who may serve in its chambers. As this Court held in *United States v. Ballin*, 144 U.S. 1, 10 (1892), “The Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and all laws, state or federal, which conflict with it are void.” The Constitution does not permit the States to add to or subtract from these enumerated qualifications.

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment, by barring individuals who have served three or more terms in the House or two or more terms in the Senate from appearing on the ballot, imposes a qualification not found in the Constitution. It does not merely regulate the conduct of elections; it creates a new, state-imposed disqualification for federal office. This is precisely the kind of intrusion the Framers sought to prevent. In *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1875), this Court emphasized that “the Constitution of the United States is a grant of powers to the general government, and not a grant of powers to the States.” The power to regulate qualifications for federal office is not among those powers reserved to the States. To allow a State to impose term limits on federal officeholders would be to permit a State to alter the very structure of the national legislature, a power reserved exclusively to the people through the constitutional amendment process.

The argument that the Amendment merely affects ballot access is without merit. The effect of the Amendment is not merely procedural; it is substantive. By denying a candidate the right to appear on the ballot based on prior service, the State effectively bars that individual from seeking office. This is not a mere administrative rule; it is a disqualification. In *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 315 (1941), this Court held that “the right to vote is a fundamental right,” and that “any restriction upon the right to vote must be justified by a compelling state interest.” The right to run for office is a necessary corollary of the right to vote. To deny a candidate the opportunity to appear on the ballot based on past service is to deny him the right to participate in the political process. The Constitution does not permit such a restriction unless it is authorized by the Constitution itself.

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly concluded that the Amendment violates the Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution. The Court’s reasoning is sound and consistent with precedent. In *United States v. Ballin*, 144 U.S. 1, 10 (1892), this Court stated that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no law, state or federal, which conflicts with it, can be valid.” The Arkansas Amendment conflicts with the Constitution by adding a qualification not found in the text. The Court’s conclusion is further supported by *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1875), which reaffirmed that the Constitution is a grant of powers to the federal government, not to the States. The power to regulate qualifications for federal office is not among the powers reserved to the States.

**II. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT IS NOT SEVERABLE FROM ITS UNCONSTITUTIONAL PROVISIONS**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the severability clause in the Amendment is insufficient to save the remainder of the measure. The test for severability is whether the legislature would have enacted the valid portions of the law in the absence of the invalid ones. In *Hill v. United States*, 244 U.S. 123, 127 (1917), this Court held that “a statute may be held invalid in whole or in part, if the invalid part is not so inseparable from the rest as to prevent the enforcement of the remainder.” However, the Court also recognized that “if the invalid provision is so interwoven with the rest of the statute that the legislature could not have intended to pass the remainder without the invalid part, the whole statute must fall.”

Here, the invalid provision—Section 3, which imposes term limits on federal officeholders—is not merely a separate provision; it is the centerpiece of the Amendment. The Preamble explicitly states that the purpose of the Amendment is to “limit the terms of the elected officials” and to “reduce voter participation and has led to an electoral system that is less free, less competitive, and less representative.” The Amendment was presented to the voters as a single package, with state and federal officeholders grouped together. The ballot title referred to “elected officials” without distinction, and Section 6 stated that the provisions apply to “all persons thereafter seeking election to the specified offices.” The voters were not given a choice between term limits for state officials and term limits for federal officials. They were presented with an “all or nothing” proposition.

The sponsors of the Amendment knew that Section 3 was constitutionally suspect. They chose to bundle it with the state provisions, knowing that the voters would not have the opportunity to approve one without the other. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 316 S.W.2d 185 (1958), the Arkansas Supreme Court rejected a similar attempt to use a severability clause to save a constitutionally infirm provision.
