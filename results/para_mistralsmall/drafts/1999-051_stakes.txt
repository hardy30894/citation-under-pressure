**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.**

This Court has long held that a federal habeas petitioner must not only present a claim that is cognizable under the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 (AEDPA), but must also avoid procedural default. *See Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 729-30 (1991). A procedural default may be excused only if the petitioner demonstrates both "cause" and "prejudice." *Id.* at 750. The Court has recognized that ineffective assistance of counsel may constitute cause to excuse a procedural default, but only where the ineffective assistance prevented the petitioner from raising the claim in the state court. *See Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986).

The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim may serve as cause to excuse the default of another claim. The court below reasoned that the ineffective-assistance claim could excuse the default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, regardless of whether the ineffective-assistance claim itself was procedurally defaulted. *See Carpenter v. Edwards*, 200 F.3d 340, 345 (6th Cir. 2000). This reasoning contravenes settled precedent.

In *Edwards v. Carpenter*, 529 U.S. 446 (2000), this Court rejected the argument that a procedurally defaulted claim could serve as cause to excuse another default. The Court held that "a prisoner may not use a defaulted claim to excuse another default." *Id.* at 452. The Court explained that allowing a defaulted claim to serve as cause would undermine the finality of state court judgments and the principles of comity that AEDPA seeks to promote. *Id.* at 453.

The Sixth Circuit’s holding is also inconsistent with *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614 (1998), where the Court reiterated that a petitioner must demonstrate cause for a procedural default, and that ineffective assistance of counsel may constitute cause only if it prevented the petitioner from raising the claim in the state court. *Id.* at 622. The Court emphasized that the cause-and-prejudice standard is not satisfied by a claim that is itself procedurally defaulted. *Id.*

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s approach would create an untenable loop: a petitioner could use a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse the default of another claim, and then rely on that newly excused claim to excuse yet another default. This would eviscerate the procedural default rules and render AEDPA’s gatekeeping function meaningless. *See Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 730-31.

**II. The Sixth Circuit erred in finding that Carpenter’s appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective.**

Even if this Court were to consider Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim, the Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that counsel was constitutionally ineffective is unsupported by the record. To establish ineffective assistance of counsel, a petitioner must show that counsel’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced the defense. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984). The Sixth Circuit’s holding fails both prongs of *Strickland*.

First, the court below did not identify any specific error by counsel that fell below an objective standard of reasonableness. *See id.* at 688. The record does not demonstrate that counsel’s decision to forgo a sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge was objectively unreasonable. Sufficiency challenges are disfavored on appeal, and counsel may reasonably conclude that such a challenge would not succeed. *See Jones v. Barnes*, 463 U.S. 745, 751-52 (1983) (counsel need not raise every nonfrivolous issue).

Second, the Sixth Circuit did not—and could not—demonstrate prejudice. To establish prejudice, a petitioner must show that there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694. The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that Carpenter was prejudiced by counsel’s failure to raise a sufficiency claim is speculative at best. The evidence against Carpenter was overwhelming, and a sufficiency challenge would have had little chance of success. *See id.* at 696 (prejudice prong requires more than a mere possibility of a different outcome).

**Conclusion**

The Sixth Circuit’s holding that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim may excuse the default of another claim is contrary to this Court’s precedent. The decision also erroneously concluded that Carpenter’s appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective. This Court should reverse the judgment below.
