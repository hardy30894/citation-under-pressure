**ARGUMENT**

The District Court’s admission of improper expert and lay opinion testimony from government witnesses—Assistant District Attorneys (ADAs) and a police lieutenant—constituted reversible error under Federal Rule of Evidence 701 and 702, as well as well-established principles of due process. These witnesses were permitted to testify as to the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, to offer legal conclusions about the existence of probable cause, and to interpret evidence that was within the jury’s capacity to assess. Such testimony violated the fundamental rule that “a witness may not vouch for the truth of another witness’s testimony,” *United States v. Hines*, 555 F.3d 102, 108 (2d Cir. 2009), and that “a lay witness may not testify as to the legal significance of facts,” *United States v. D’Amato*, 923 F.2d 1004, 1008 (2d Cir. 1991). The cumulative effect of these errors was so prejudicial that the jury verdict cannot stand.

First, the ADAs and the police lieutenant were allowed to testify that Ramos and Rivera were “credible,” “truthful,” and “consistent,” despite having no personal knowledge of the events beyond what was reported to them. *See United States v. Candelaria*, 878 F.2d 608, 612 (2d Cir. 1989) (holding that “a witness may not express a personal belief in the truth of another’s testimony” because such testimony “invites the jury to rely on the witness’s authority rather than its own judgment”). The ADAs, in particular, were permitted to state that “based on my experience,” the officers’ version of events “made sense” and “was consistent with how police officers typically act.” This testimony was not merely opinion—it was a direct endorsement of the officers’ credibility, which is a matter for the jury alone to assess. *See United States v. Mendoza*, 733 F.3d 444, 451 (2d Cir. 2013) (“The jury, not the prosecution, is the trier of fact and must determine the credibility of witnesses.”).

Second, the ADAs and the lieutenant offered legal conclusions about probable cause, a question of law that is properly reserved for the court. *See Fed. R. Evid. 704(a)*. The ADAs testified that “there was probable cause to arrest” Cameron and Higgenbottom, and that the officers “had a reasonable basis” for their actions. The lieutenant stated that “the officers were acting lawfully” and that “the arrest was justified.” These statements were not based on personal observation but on the prosecution’s interpretation of the facts. *See United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 517 (1995) (“The determination of whether probable cause exists is a legal question for the court, not a factual one for the jury.”). Allowing government agents to instruct the jury on the legal sufficiency of probable cause effectively transformed the jury into a rubber stamp for the prosecution’s theory of the case.

Third, the witnesses interpreted evidence that was equally accessible to the jury. The ADAs and the lieutenant repeatedly commented on the significance of photographs, statements, and the timing of events, asserting that certain evidence “supported” the officers’ version or “undermined” the Appellants’ claims. *See United States v. Maldonado*, 943 F.2d 128, 132 (2d Cir. 1991) (“A witness may not interpret evidence for the jury when the jury is capable of drawing its own inferences.”). The jury was thus deprived of its essential role as the sole arbiter of fact.

These errors were not harmless. The credibility of Ramos and Rivera and the existence of probable cause were the two most contested issues in the case. The jury was presented with a narrative in which government officials—ADAs and a police lieutenant—vouched for the officers’ truthfulness and declared their actions lawful. This created an impermissible appearance of official endorsement that undermined the fairness of the trial. *See United States v. Hines*, 555 F.3d at 108 (“When the government’s own agents testify as to the credibility of law enforcement officers, the risk of prejudice is particularly acute.”). The District Court’s failure to intervene was a clear abuse of discretion.

Moreover, the Appellants’ motion for judgment as a matter of law was properly denied because the photographs, while suggestive, did not “blatantly contradict” the Appellees’ version of events to the extent that no reasonable jury could accept it. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 251 (1986) (“A jury may not be directed to return a verdict for one party if there is any evidence in the record from which a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the other party.”). The images show Cameron near the police car, but do not definitively establish whether she reached into the vehicle. Thus, the issue was properly submitted to the jury.

Finally, the District Court erred in failing to instruct the jury on two critical points. First, under New York law, the offense of *obstructing governmental administration* (Penal Law § 190.20) requires that the officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. *See People v. Johnson*, 89 N.Y.2d 100, 105 (1996). The jury should have been instructed that Higgenbottom could not be found guilty unless the officers were lawfully arresting Cameron at the time. Second, the Appellants’ evidence—particularly the lack of criminal charges against Higgenbottom and the officers’ failure to file a report—was sufficient to permit a jury to infer malice. *See *Barnes v. City of New York*, 574 F.3d 122, 130 (2d Cir. 2009) (“Malice may be inferred from the absence of probable cause and the absence of any legitimate justification for the arrest.”). A punitive damages instruction was therefore warranted.

For these reasons, the verdict must be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial, with proper instructions and exclusion of the improper testimony.
