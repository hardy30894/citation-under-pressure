**ARGUMENT**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. Under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), new constitutional rules are generally inapplicable to final convictions, but two exceptions exist. *Id.* at 310. The first exception covers rules that place “certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” *Id.* (quoting *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667, 692 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part)). The second covers watershed rules of criminal procedure. *Id.* at 311.

*Johnson* falls within the first exception. It held that the ACCA residual clause, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is unconstitutionally vague because it requires courts to assess “the hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version” of an offense. 576 U.S. at 597. The decision therefore “narrow[ed] the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms,” *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351 (2004), and “prohibit[ed] a certain category of punishment for a class of defendants because of their status or offense,” *Saffle v. Parks*, 494 U.S. 484, 495 (1990). After *Johnson*, defendants whose prior convictions qualified only under the residual clause are no longer subject to ACCA’s fifteen-year mandatory minimum. *See also Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620–21 (1998) (holding that a decision narrowing the elements of a federal offense applies retroactively on collateral review).

This Court has repeatedly confirmed that substantive rules altering the range of conduct or the class of persons subject to punishment are retroactive. *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190, 206 (2016); *Chaidez v. United States*, 568 U.S. 342, 347–48 (2013); *Whorton v. Bockting*, 549 U.S. 406, 416 (2007). *Johnson* is such a rule: it “rest[ed] on the premise that the government has no power to impose the challenged punishment,” *Montgomery*, 577 U.S. at 201, rather than on procedural accuracy.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that *Johnson* is merely procedural—misapplies *Teague*. Procedural rules “regulate only the manner of determining the defendant’s culpability,” *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353, whereas *Johnson* eliminated an entire category of predicate offenses. Because Welch’s Florida robbery conviction was valid only under the now-invalid residual clause, his ACCA sentence exceeds the statutory maximum authorized by his remaining convictions. The judgment should be reversed.
