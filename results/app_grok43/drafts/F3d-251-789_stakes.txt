The district court’s finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention of Danelsy is clearly erroneous. Findings of fact are reviewed for clear error, and reversal is required when “the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” Anderson v. Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985).

Under the Hague Convention, implemented by the International Child Abduction Remedies Act, 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601-11610, a respondent who asserts consent as an affirmative defense must prove it by a preponderance of the evidence. 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(2)(B). Consent must be “clear and unequivocal.” See Mozes v. Mozes, 239 F.3d 1067, 1076 (9th Cir. 2001) (requiring “clear and unequivocal” evidence of consent or acquiescence to defeat return).

The record contains no such evidence. Gonzalez-Caballero’s accounts uniformly describe only a temporary or conditional arrangement tied to her own immigration. She relinquished Danelsy’s documents solely to facilitate travel while she completed paperwork through her sister in Georgia. The day after departure she contacted Panamanian authorities and, within weeks, the Office of the President, radio stations, and the Children’s Commission. These prompt, repeated efforts are incompatible with any understanding that Mena could retain Danelsy permanently.

Mena’s contrary testimony—that Gonzalez-Caballero agreed Danelsy “would have a better life in the United States”—does not establish the required clear and unequivocal consent. Even credited, that statement reflects only a conditional hope premised on Gonzalez-Caballero’s eventual relocation, not an agreement to permanent separation. Because the district court’s consent finding rests on evidence that, taken as a whole, leaves a definite conviction of mistake, it must be reversed and the petition granted.
