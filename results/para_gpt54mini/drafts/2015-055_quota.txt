Johnson’s rule applies retroactively because it is a new substantive rule that altered “the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes,” not a mere procedural regulation of how courts adjudicate ACCA cases. Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). In Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989), the Court held that new substantive rules apply on collateral review, and subsequent cases have repeatedly treated rules narrowing the scope of a criminal statute as retroactive. See Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 620-21 (1998); Welch v. United States, 578 U.S. 120, 129-31 (2016).

Johnson did more than announce a new interpretive gloss. It held that ACCA’s residual clause, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is void for vagueness because it “fails to give ordinary people fair notice of the conduct it punishes” and “invites arbitrary enforcement by judges.” Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591, 595, 597 (2015). The Court invalidated a clause that had authorized enhanced punishment for a category of defendants. Once that clause fell, those sentenced under it no longer remained within ACCA’s lawful reach. That is the hallmark of a substantive rule. Schriro, 542 U.S. at 351-52; Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 U.S. 190, 200-01 (2016).

The government may argue that Johnson is procedural because it concerns judicial methodology. But this Court has rejected the notion that a rule is procedural merely because it affects the process by which a court reaches a sentence. In Welch, the Court held that Johnson “changed the substantive reach of the Armed Career Criminal Act, altering ‘the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.’” 578 U.S. at 129 (quoting Schriro, 542 U.S. at 353). The Court explained that Johnson “affected the reach of the underlying statute rather than the judicial procedures by which the statute is applied.” Welch, 578 U.S. at 130. That is enough to make it retroactive on collateral review. Id. at 129-31.

The retroactivity result follows from basic Teague principles. New rules of criminal procedure generally do not apply retroactively, but substantive rules do because they “necessarily carry a significant risk that a defendant faces a punishment that the law cannot impose upon him.” Montgomery, 577 U.S. at 200; see also Teague, 489 U.S. at 311. Johnson fits comfortably within that category: it narrowed ACCA by eliminating one of the three clauses defining “violent felony,” and defendants whose sentences depended on that clause were sentenced under an unconstitutional expansion of the statute. Johnson, 576 U.S. at 606; Welch, 578 U.S. at 129-30.

Nor does the fact that Johnson announced its rule in the context of a vagueness challenge make it nonretroactive. This Court has long applied retroactively decisions that narrow the scope of a criminal law or invalidate the legal basis for punishment. See Bousley, 523 U.S. at 620-21 (applying retroactively a narrowing interpretation of a federal criminal statute); Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1974) (permitting collateral relief where later decision showed conviction rested on conduct Congress had not made criminal). Johnson likewise “announced a substantive rule that has retroactive effect in cases on collateral review.” Welch, 578 U.S. at 135.

Welch is therefore entitled to resentencing. His ACCA sentence depended on the residual clause, and Johnson rendered that clause void. Johnson, 576 U.S. at 606; Welch, 578 U.S. at 135. Because “a conviction or sentence imposed in violation of the Constitution or laws of the United States” may be vacated on collateral review, 28 U.S.C. § 2255(a), the judgment below cannot stand once Johnson is given its retroactive effect. See Reed v. Ross, 468 U.S. 1, 11-12 (1984); Davis, 417 U.S. at 346-47.

The Eleventh Circuit should therefore reverse and hold that Johnson applies retroactively to Welch’s collateral challenge. The Supreme Court’s decisions in Johnson, Welch, Schriro, Montgomery, Teague, Bousley, Davis, and Reed compel that result.
