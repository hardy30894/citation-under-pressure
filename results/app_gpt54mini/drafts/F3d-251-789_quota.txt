**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s denial of Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero’s petition should be reversed because the record does not support a finding that she *consented* to the removal and retention of Danelsy within the meaning of the Hague Convention and the International Child Abduction Remedies Act (“ICARA”), 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601-11610. Under the Convention, removal is wrongful only when it breaches the left-behind parent’s custody rights and no Convention defense applies. *Abbott v. Abbott*, 560 U.S. 1, 8-10 (2010); *Mozes v. Mozes*, 239 F.3d 1067, 1070-71 (9th Cir. 2001). Although the district court’s factual findings are reviewed for clear error, whether those facts establish consent is a legal question informed by the Convention’s narrowly construed defenses. *Friedrich v. Friedrich*, 78 F.3d 1060, 1069 (6th Cir. 1996); *Baxter v. Baxter*, 423 F.3d 363, 371-72 (3d Cir. 2005).

Consent under Article 13(a) must be established by the respondent, and the defense is limited to the petitioner’s subjective intent at the time of departure. *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371-72; *In re Lozano*, 809 F. Supp. 2d 197, 206-07 (S.D.N.Y. 2011), aff’d, 697 F.3d 41 (2d Cir. 2012). Courts distinguish true consent from later acquiescence, which the Convention treats separately. *Friedrich*, 78 F.3d at 1069-70; *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371. The defense cannot be premised on ambiguous, conditional, or temporary permission; it requires a “positive and unequivocal” manifestation that the child may be removed or retained. *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371; *In re Koc*, 181 F. Supp. 2d 136, 152-53 (E.D.N.Y. 2001). That standard is especially important because the Convention is designed to deter unilateral removals and restore the status quo pending custody litigation. *Abbott*, 560 U.S. at 20; *Mozes*, 239 F.3d at 1070.

Measured against those authorities, the evidence here falls far short. Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero’s account was that she agreed, at most, to a temporary trip so that Danelsy could travel while immigration arrangements were pursued. That is not consent to removal and retention; it is permission for a limited purpose. *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371-72. Even if the district court credited Mr. Mena’s version of events, the record shows only a disputed, informal discussion in a hotel, followed by Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero’s immediate efforts to locate and recover her daughter through police, child-protection agencies, the office of the President, and the Children’s Commission. Such prompt and repeated efforts are the opposite of consent. *Friedrich*, 78 F.3d at 1069-70; *Gonzalez v. Gutierrez*, 311 F.3d 942, 949-50 (9th Cir. 2002).

The district court appears to have relied heavily on the fact that Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero gave Mr. Mena Danelsy’s documents and accompanied him to the airport. But those acts do not establish consent to permanent retention. The surrender of travel papers may be consistent with permission for a short trip, particularly where the parties are also discussing immigration assistance and reunion in the United States. *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371-72; *Silverman v. Silverman*, 338 F.3d 886, 900-01 (8th Cir. 2003). Likewise, facilitating departure procedures does not amount to an agreement that the child need not be returned. *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 372; *Mendez Lynch v. Mendez Lynch*, 220 F. Supp. 2d 1347, 1363-64 (M.D. Fla. 2002). The Convention requires more than cooperation in travel arrangements; it requires proof that the petitioner agreed to the relocation as a final, not provisional, change in custody. *Mozes*, 239 F.3d at 1071-72.

Nor can any finding of consent be reconciled with Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero’s prompt resort to authorities once Mr. Mena failed to call upon arrival. A parent who immediately seeks police and governmental intervention has not manifested the sort of knowing and settled waiver that Article 13(a) requires. *Friedrich*, 78 F.3d at 1069-70; *Sonderup v. Tondelli*, 2001 (1) SA 1171 (CC) at ¶¶ 26-27 (S. Afr.). To the extent the district court treated later statements about immigration or hopes of joining Danelsy in the United States as consent, those statements reflect only a desire to cooperate with a parent who had already departed with the child, not an agreement to cede custody. *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371-72; *Croll v. Croll*, 229 F.3d 133, 142-43 (2d Cir. 2000), superseded by statute on other grounds as stated in *Abbott*, 560 U.S. at 8.

Because the consent defense was not proved by a preponderance of the evidence, see 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(2)(B), the district court erred in denying return. The proper remedy under the Convention is prompt repatriation so the custody dispute may be decided in Panama, the child’s habitual residence. *Abbott*, 560 U.S. at 20; *Mozes*, 239 F.3d at 1070; *Gonzalez v. Gutierrez*, 311 F.3d at 951. This Court should reverse and remand with instructions to order Danelsy’s return to Panama.
