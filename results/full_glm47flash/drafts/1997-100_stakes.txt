**Argument**

**I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Applies to Sentencing Proceedings Involving Factual Findings.**

The Double Jeopardy Clause forbids a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal or conviction. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 188 (1957). It also forbids the imposition of a more severe sentence after an acquittal of an essential element of the offense. Id. When a sentencing proceeding involves factual determinations, it is a "trial" for Double Jeopardy purposes. *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981). Here, the determination of whether Monge suffered a prior serious felony conviction was a factual finding essential to his sentence. The California Supreme Court erred in treating this as a mere administrative check.

**II. *Bullington* and *Rumsey* Require Protection Against Retrial for the Same Facts.**

The Supreme Court has held that once a jury decides on a sentence, the State cannot retry the defendant on the same facts to impose a harsher sentence. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439. This rule applies to noncapital cases as well. *Rumsey v. Arizona*, 467 U.S. 650, 654 (1984). The California Supreme Court’s attempt to limit *Bullington* to capital cases is contrary to controlling precedent. *Rumsey* explicitly extended *Bullington*’s protections to noncapital sentencing. The Court in *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994), noted that *Bullington* was based on the unique circumstances of capital sentencing but did not limit its holding to capital cases.

**III. The State’s Procedural Distinctions Are Inadequate.**

The State argues that because California provides a statutory right to a jury trial for prior convictions, it may retry the defendant if the first jury fails to find the facts. This reasoning ignores the constitutional mandate of *Bullington*. The fact that a state provides a jury trial does not mean it can ignore the Double Jeopardy Clause when the jury’s verdict is insufficient. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 188.
