## ARGUMENT

The D.C. Circuit erred by holding that Guam’s 2004 consent decree with the Environmental Protection Agency triggered CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby barring Guam’s timely § 107 cost-recovery claim. The decision conflicts with this Court’s precedent and misapplies the statutory framework.

### I. The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not Resolve Guam’s Liability for a CERCLA “Response Action”

Section 113(f)(3)(B) permits contribution only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The 2004 decree resolved Guam’s liability under the Clean Water Act, not CERCLA. The decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the dump, and install a cover system to eliminate untreated leachate discharges. Consent Decree 5–12, J.A. 94–101. These obligations addressed Clean Water Act violations—discharges of pollutants without a permit—not the release of hazardous substances that CERCLA targets.

CERCLA defines “response action” as “removal” or “remedial action” relating to hazardous substances. 42 U.S.C. § 9601(25). While CERCLA includes “collection of leachate and runoff” as a remedial action, id. § 9601(24), that definition applies only when the leachate arises from hazardous substance contamination. Here, EPA’s complaint alleged only “discharges of untreated leachate” into waters of the United States without a permit. CWA Compl. ¶ 21, J.A. 85. It did not allege that the leachate contained hazardous substances. The decree thus resolved Clean Water Act liability, not CERCLA liability.

This Court has emphasized that CERCLA’s provisions must be interpreted according to their plain terms. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004). Section 113(f)(3)(B) requires a settlement that “resolves liability” for a “response action.” A settlement that resolves only non-CERCLA obligations does not satisfy that requirement. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that the decree’s cover system constituted a “response action” because CERCLA includes leachate collection—ignores the decree’s Clean Water Act foundation. The cover system was designed to stop unpermitted discharges, not to remediate hazardous substances.

Moreover, the decree expressly disclaimed liability: it was “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3, J.A. 92. A disclaimer of liability weighs against finding that the parties intended to resolve liability. *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015). The D.C. Circuit dismissed this disclaimer as insufficient, but it cannot be ignored when the decree’s substantive terms do not reference CERCLA liability at all. The decree resolved no CERCLA liability, and Guam never conceded it was a potentially responsible party under CERCLA.

### II. Even If Section 113(f) Were Triggered, Guam May Still Pursue a Section 107 Cost-Recovery Claim

This Court held in *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 139 (2007), that a potentially responsible party may bring a § 107 cost-recovery claim even if it could also bring a § 113 contribution claim. Section 107 and § 113 provide “two potentially overlapping” remedies. *Id.* at 138. The D.C. Circuit’s holding that § 113(f)(3)(B) is the exclusive remedy when a settlement triggers contribution rights conflicts with *Atlantic Research*. There, the Court rejected the argument that § 107 is unavailable to PRPs, explaining that nothing in CERCLA “purports to bar a PRP that has incurred cleanup costs from recovering those costs under § 107.” *Id.* at 139.

The D.C. Circuit relied on the principle that a party cannot use § 107 to circumvent § 113’s shorter statute of limitations. But *Atlantic Research* explicitly declined to decide whether a PRP that has been sued under § 107 or has settled under § 113 may still bring a § 107 claim. *Id.* at 139 n.6. The Court left open the possibility that § 107 remains available. The D.C. Circuit’s categorical rule—that any settlement triggering § 113(f)(3)(B) forecloses § 107—exceeds the statutory text and this Court’s guidance.

### III. The D.C. Circuit’s Decision Deprives Guam of Any Remedy

Guam closed the Ordot Dump in 2011 and sued in 2017—within the six-year statute of limitations for § 107 remedial actions. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B). The D.C. Circuit held that Guam’s § 113(f) claim expired in 2007, three years after the consent decree. By barring Guam’s § 107 claim, the decision leaves Guam with no recourse against the Navy, which operated the dump for decades and allegedly deposited munitions, DDT, and Agent Orange. Am. Compl. ¶ 11. This result contradicts CERCLA’s purpose of ensuring that “responsible parties foot the bill.” *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010). Congress did not intend to immunize the United States from liability for its own contamination simply because Guam entered a Clean Water Act settlement.

The D.C. Circuit’s judgment should be reversed.
