# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 107 Provides a Freestanding Cost-Recovery Remedy That Guam May Invoke Because It Never "Resolved" CERCLA Liability in a Qualifying Settlement

CERCLA establishes two independent remedial pathways. Section 107(a) allows a party that has itself incurred response costs to recover those costs from other responsible parties, while section 113(f) provides contribution to a party that has already paid for its share of another's liability. See 42 U.S.C. §§ 9607(a), 9613(f). This Court has recognized that these provisions "complement one another by providing causes of action to persons in different procedural circumstances." United States v. Atlantic Research Corp., 551 U.S. 128, 139 (2007). A party is relegated to section 113(f)'s contribution remedy—and its shorter three-year limitations period—only when it satisfies one of that section's specific triggers: an underlying civil action under section 106 or 107, CERCLA § 113(f)(1), or a settlement that "resolved its liability to the United States . . . for some or all of a response action," CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B).

Because section 113(f) is triggered only in defined circumstances, this Court in Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157 (2004), refused to read the contribution provision expansively where its textual conditions were not met, holding that a party may seek contribution under section 113(f)(1) only "during or following" a specified civil action. Id. at 165–67. The same textual discipline governs section 113(f)(3)(B): unless a settlement actually resolves CERCLA liability for a response action, the provision simply does not apply, and the party remains free to pursue the six-year cost-recovery remedy under section 107. CERCLA's core purpose—to "ensure that responsible parties foot the bill" for hazardous-waste cleanup, General Electric Co. v. Jackson, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010)—is served, not undermined, by allowing Guam access to section 107's fuller remedy where no qualifying settlement has actually resolved its CERCLA liability.

## II. The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not Resolve Guam's CERCLA Liability and Therefore Cannot Trigger Section 113(f)(3)(B)

The 2004 Consent Decree arose from an enforcement action brought exclusively under the Clean Water Act, 33 U.S.C. §§ 1251 et seq.—not CERCLA. EPA's complaint alleged that Guam violated the Clean Water Act by discharging pollutants without a permit, and it sought injunctive relief compelling Guam to submit plans and complete construction of a cover system. Nowhere does the Decree reference CERCLA, "response costs," or "response actions" as those terms are defined in 42 U.S.C. § 9601. Indeed, the Decree expressly disclaims any adjudication of liability, providing that it is entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." Consent Decree 3.

Other circuits have recognized that a settlement's failure to invoke CERCLA, or its express disclaimer of liability, weighs against finding that the agreement "resolved" liability within the meaning of section 113(f)(3)(B). See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (concluding a decree did not resolve liability where, among other things, "the plaintiff had not conceded the question of its liability"). While some settlements may impose CERCLA-like obligations sufficient to trigger contribution rights notwithstanding a disclaimer, see Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co., 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017); Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries, Inc., 937 F.3d 928, 931 (7th Cir. 2019), those cases involved settlements negotiated under CERCLA's own enforcement framework. Guam's Decree, by contrast, was negotiated entirely under the Clean Water Act's separate statutory scheme, aimed at permit compliance rather than resolution of hazardous-substance liability. EPA's own subsequent practice confirms the significance of this omission: EPA has since revised its model settlement language to include an express statement that a settlement "constitutes an administrative settlement for purposes of Section 113(f)(3)(B) of CERCLA." Florida Power Corp., 810 F.3d at 1009. That EPA found it necessary to add such language demonstrates that silence on this point—as in Guam's Decree—cannot be read to accomplish the same result.

## III. Barring Guam's Section 107 Claim Would Contravene CERCLA's Remedial Structure and Produce an Untenable Result

Treating an unrelated Clean Water Act consent decree as a CERCLA-liability-resolving settlement would produce the "untenable" result of extinguishing a cost-recovery claim before a party could even recognize that its contribution clock had begun to run. Guam neither knew nor could have known in 2004 that a Clean Water Act settlement, silent as to CERCLA, would later be construed to start a three-year contribution period that would expire in 2007—a decade before this suit and years before Guam had even completed remediation or ascertained the Navy's role in the contamination. Congress could not have intended CERCLA's contribution mechanism, designed to encourage settlement and cost-sharing, see Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009), to instead operate as a trap that forecloses recovery from the party that generated the hazardous contamination in the first place.

For these reasons, Guam's section 107 cost-recovery action remains timely, and the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
