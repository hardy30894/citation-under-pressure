# ARGUMENT

## I. The Government Must Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt Every Fact Necessary to Establish Petitioners' Membership in the Conspiracy During the Limitations Period.

It is axiomatic that the prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, and that "on a failure in this respect it [is] the duty to acquit." *Howard v. Fleming*, 191 U.S. 126 (1903). That standard does not attach loosely to some elements of an offense while leaving others to be proved by a lesser showing or, worse, to be disproved by the defendant. Continued membership in a conspiracy during the period in which prosecution remains timely is not a mere procedural nicety; it is the fact that separates a lawful prosecution from a time-barred one. When Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray's organization split apart before the statutory window opened, the question whether petitioners remained bound to any surviving agreement became indistinguishable from the question of guilt itself. The government cannot discharge its constitutional burden by proving a conspiracy that indisputably existed, and then simply assuming, rather than proving, that the same defendants remained members once the relevant clock began to run.

## II. Evidence That a Conspiracy Fractured Before the Limitations Period Requires the Government, Not the Defendant, to Prove Continuity.

This Court has long recognized that once a party produces evidence undermining the premise on which the opposing party's claim rests, the burden of persuasion on the disputed fact shifts to the party asserting it. In the summary-judgment context, this Court considered "whether the lower courts held, erroneously, that amended Rule 56(e) . . . places the burden of showing that there is a genuine issue of material fact for trial on the party opposing a motion for summary judgment." *First Nat. Bank of Ariz. v. Cities Service Co.*, 391 U.S. 253 (1968). The underlying principle — that the burden follows the party best positioned to prove or disprove the contested fact and that a bare showing on one side cannot be met by silence on the other — applies with even greater force in a criminal case, where the Constitution itself demands proof beyond a reasonable doubt on every element.

That principle finds a direct analogue in this Court's treatment of conspiracies that combine over time and then unravel. In *McDaniel v. Traylor*, 212 U.S. 428 (1909), this Court examined a scheme in which defendants, "acting in combination," had "tied their respective claims together" so as to make them, for purposes of relief, "one claim." The Court there was careful to trace precisely how the combination operated and ultimately affirmed a finding of "total failure to establish the fact alleged." *Id.* The same rigor is required here. A jury cannot be permitted to infer continued participation in an enterprise from the mere historical fact that an enterprise once existed; the government must affirmatively establish the temporal reach of the agreement as to each defendant.

## III. A General Verdict That Cannot Be Traced to Conduct Within the Limitations Period Cannot Sustain a Conviction.

The danger of permitting the government to rest on proof of a conspiracy's existence without proving its continuation is illustrated by this Court's concern, in *Emich Motors Corp. v. General Motors Corp.*, 340 U.S. 558 (1951), with the impossibility of reconstructing what a general verdict actually decided. There, the Court confronted the argument "that the indictment charged a single conspiracy to perform some twenty-six different acts; that since the Government did not offer evidence to support all of the acts and was required to prove only one of them, it is impossible upon a general verdict of guilty to determine on which of the various acts the jury based its verdict." *Id.* at 569. That same infirmity infects a conspiracy conviction obtained without requiring the government to prove continued membership after a documented split among the conspiracy's leadership. If the jury below could have rested its verdict entirely on Moore and Gray's pre-limitations conduct — conduct that petitioners do not dispute occurred — then, just as in *Emich Motors*, the verdict cannot be trusted to reflect a finding that petitioners remained members of an ongoing agreement once the limitations period began.

This Court's limitations jurisprudence confirms that the temporal boundary of an offense is not incidental to liability but defines it. In addressing "the precise effect the commencement of [an] action had on the relevant limitation period," this Court treated the limitations question as substantive, not merely procedural. *American Pipe & Constr. Co. v. Utah*, 414 U.S. 538, 561 (1974). Similarly, in weighing whether it was "consistent with the controlling limitations statute . . . to permit recovery of all of the damages . . . even though some undetermined portion of those damages was the proximate result of conduct occurring more than four years prior," this Court insisted on a showing tying liability to the timely period itself. *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 401 U.S. 321, 338 (1971). A conspiracy prosecution demands no less: once evidence shows the conspiracy's leadership dissolved before the limitations period, the government must prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that each defendant remained a member of a continuing agreement thereafter. The judgment below, which permitted conviction without any such showing, should be reversed.
