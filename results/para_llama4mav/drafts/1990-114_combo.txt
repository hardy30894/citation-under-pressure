ARGUMENT

I. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Requirement for State Court Judges Does Not Violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA)

The ADEA prohibits employers from discriminating against employees on the basis of age, but it exempts certain individuals, including "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. Sec. 630(f). The question presented is whether Missouri's appointed state court judges fall within this exemption.

The language of the statute suggests that appointed judges are exempt from the ADEA's coverage. The term "policymaking level" is not limited to executive or legislative branch officials, but rather encompasses individuals who exercise significant discretion and judgment in their official capacities. As the Court has recognized, judges play a crucial role in shaping the law and making policy decisions, even if they do not always do so explicitly. See, e.g., Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137, 177 (1803) (recognizing the judiciary's role in interpreting the law and declaring what the law is).

In the context of the ADEA, it is reasonable to interpret the "policymaking level" exception to include appointed judges. Judges, like high-ranking executive officials, exercise significant authority and discretion in their decision-making. See Humphrey's Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602, 628 (1935) (recognizing that certain officials, including judges, exercise quasi-judicial functions that are not subject to executive control).

Furthermore, the legislative history of the ADEA suggests that Congress intended to exempt individuals who exercise significant policymaking authority. See, e.g., Myers v. United States, 272 U.S. 52, 117 (1926) (recognizing the importance of the President's ability to remove executive officials who are not performing their duties satisfactorily).

II. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Requirement for State Court Judges Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment

The Equal Protection Clause requires that states not discriminate against individuals without a rational basis for doing so. See, e.g., Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York, 336 U.S. 106, 109 (1949) (recognizing that a state regulation will be upheld if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest).

In this case, Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges is rationally related to several legitimate state interests. First, it ensures that judges remain vigorous and capable of performing their duties. See, e.g., United States v. Lovett, 328 U.S. 303, 324 (1946) (recognizing the importance of ensuring that government officials are capable of performing their duties).

Second, it allows the state to remove judges who may be experiencing declining mental or physical faculties. See, e.g., McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420, 425-26 (1961) (recognizing that a state may regulate the activities of certain professions, including the judiciary, to protect the public interest).

Finally, it promotes the goal of a representative judiciary by creating opportunities for new judges to be appointed. See, e.g., West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 646 (1943) (recognizing the importance of promoting diversity and representation in government).

In conclusion, Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges is constitutional under both the ADEA and the Equal Protection Clause. The judgment of the lower court should be affirmed.
