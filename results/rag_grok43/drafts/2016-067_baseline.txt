**ARGUMENT**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates only “debt collector[s],” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6), a term the Act defines to reach persons who “regularly collect[] or attempt[] to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (quoting § 1692a(6)). Santander purchased the defaulted loans for its own account and thereafter sought to collect sums owed to itself. Because those sums were not “owed or due another,” Santander falls outside the statutory definition.

The Act’s text confirms the distinction. It separates “debt collector” from “creditor,” the latter being “any person … to whom a debt is owed.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). When a creditor collects its own debt, it does not act “for another.” Heintz, 514 U.S. at 293. The complaint alleges that CitiFinancial sold the loans to Santander on December 1, 2011, after which Santander collected for its own account. That transaction converted Santander from servicer to creditor; its subsequent efforts therefore lie beyond the Act’s reach.

Petitioners urge that default status at acquisition controls. The statute, however, keys liability to the identity of the person to whom the debt is owed at the time of collection, not to the debt’s status when acquired. See Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich, L.P.A., 559 U.S. 573, 576 (2010) (Act “regulates interactions between consumer debtors and ‘debt collector[s]’”). Nothing in the definitional text or in Heintz supports grafting an additional “default-at-purchase” test onto the phrase “owed or due another.”

The Act’s purpose reinforces the textual line. Congress sought to curb “abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). It did not impose the same constraints on creditors collecting their own obligations. Santander’s conduct after the purchase therefore cannot give rise to FDCPA liability.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
