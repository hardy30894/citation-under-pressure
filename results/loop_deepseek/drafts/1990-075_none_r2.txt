**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act satisfies the nondelegation doctrine by providing an intelligible principle that confines the Attorney General's discretion. Congress may lawfully obtain assistance from coordinate branches when it "lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This Court has repeatedly sustained delegations with standards far less specific than those Congress provided here.

**I. The Temporary Scheduling Provision Provides an Intelligible Principle.**

The Temporary Scheduling Provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), requires the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance only when necessary "to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety." This standard is both precise and demanding. This Court has upheld delegations with comparably general language. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 420 (1944), the Court sustained a delegation authorizing the Price Administrator to fix prices that would be "fair and equitable" and "effectuate the purposes of this Act." In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court upheld a delegation to the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to prescribe regulations for the "orderly and efficient" functioning of savings and loan associations. If "fair and equitable" and "orderly and efficient" satisfy the intelligible principle test, the stricter "imminent hazard to the public safety" standard indisputably satisfies it.

The Act further cabins the Attorney General by requiring consideration of three enumerated factors: the substance's "history and current pattern of abuse," the "scope, duration, and significance of abuse," and the "risk . . . to the public health." 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4)-(6). This Court has consistently held that requiring consideration of specified factors is a hallmark of a constitutional delegation. *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-79 (1948) (sustaining delegation where Congress directed consideration of "such factors as he may deem advisable"); *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952) (upholding delegation to Attorney General to deny bail in "his discretion" while considering enumerated standards).

The Temporary Scheduling Provision also contains a temporal limitation: temporary scheduling lasts only one year, with a possible six-month extension. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). This durational boundary distinguishes the provision from the unlimited delegations invalidated in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). In *Panama Refining*, Congress gave the President unfettered authority to prohibit interstate transportation of petroleum with "no standard" whatsoever. 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, Congress authorized the President to approve "codes of fair competition" with "no limitations" on the President's power to create crimes. 295 U.S. at 537-38. Here, by contrast, Congress defined the triggering hazard, prescribed the factors to be considered, and imposed a fixed expiration. No comparable abdication exists.

Congress's delegation is also supported by the complexity and rapidly changing nature of the regulated field. This Court has recognized that "in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives." That principle has deep roots. *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 216 (1943) (upholding broad delegation to the FCC because the "complex nature" of radio regulation required flexibility). The designer drug market evolves constantly, demanding quick law enforcement responses that Congress cannot itself provide. *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 432 (rapidly changing conditions support broader delegation).

**II. The Delegation of Criminal-Law-Making Authority Does Not Require a Stricter Standard.**

Petitioners argue that a more searching nondelegation analysis applies because temporary scheduling creates criminal prohibitions. This Court has never adopted such a rule. To the contrary, in *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to promulgate regulations that carried criminal penalties. The Court reasoned that Congress had "declared its will to withdraw [the lands] from settlement," and the Secretary could "fill up the details" consistent with that will. *Id.* at 517. The Temporary Scheduling Provision is far more concrete than the open-ended grant in *Grimaud*: the Secretary there had general authority to "make rules and regulations," while the Attorney General here must find an "imminent hazard" and consider enumerated factors.

*Schechter Poultry* and *Panama Refining* are not to the contrary. Both involved delegations with no intelligible principle at all. In *Schechter Poultry*, the President could "prescribe a code of fair competition" and violation of that code was a crime, but "the code" had no definition. 295 U.S. at 538. Here, the Attorney General's power is tightly circumscribed by statutory standards, procedural requirements, and a sunset provision. *Schechter Poultry* does not stand for the proposition that every criminal-law delegation must contain narrow and detailed definitions; it stands for the proposition that Congress cannot confer "uncontrolled power to make crime." *Id.* The Temporary Scheduling Provision confers no such uncontrolled power.

**III. The Attorney General Properly Subdelegated to the DEA.**

The Controlled Substances Act authorizes the Attorney General to "promulgate and enforce any rules, regulations, and procedures which he may deem necessary." 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This Court has long recognized that officers to whom executive power is delegated may subdelegate that power unless Congress expressly prohibits it. *United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc.*, 272 U.S. 1, 11 (1926) (upholding subdelegation by President of authority under Trading with the Enemy Act); *Runkle v. United States*, 122 U.S. 543, 557 (1887) (recognizing implied authority to subdelegate). Here, Congress vested the temporary scheduling power in the "Attorney General," not in a specific subordinate or bureau. The Attorney General's regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), subdelegating to the DEA Administrator, is a routine administrative arrangement.

Petitioners' contrary position would require every executive officer to personally exercise every power Congress confers. That proposition has no support in precedent. *Chemical Foundation*, 272 U.S. at 11; *Runkle*, 122 U.S. at 557. In a department as large and complex as the Department of Justice, such a rule would paralyze law enforcement. *Cf. Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496-97 (1904) (upholding subdelegation in the Treasury Department as consistent with congressional intent).

**IV. Conclusion**

The Temporary Scheduling Provision provides an intelligible principle—imminent hazard to public safety—reinforced by enumerated factors and a fixed duration. This satisfies the nondelegation doctrine as applied in *J.W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409; *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 420; *Fahey*, 332 U.S. at 250; *Lichter*, 334 U.S. at 778-79; *Carlson*, 342 U.S. at 542-44; *National Broadcasting Co.*, 319 U.S. at 216; *Grimaud*, 220 U.S. at 521; and *Buttfield*, 192 U.S. at 496-97. The Attorney General's subdelegation to the DEA is authorized under *Chemical Foundation*, 272 U.S. at 11, and *Runkle*, 122 U.S. at 557. The judgment below should be affirmed.
