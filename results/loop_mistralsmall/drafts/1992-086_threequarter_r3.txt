**ARGUMENT**

I. The School District’s facilities constitute a **public forum** open to all community groups, and the District may not exclude Lamb’s Chapel based on the religious content of its speech.

The First Amendment forbids government from “abridging the freedom of speech.” U.S. Const. amend. I. Where government property has been “long devoted to assembly and debate,” it becomes a **traditional public forum**, and any content-based exclusion must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling state interest. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515–16 (1939). Even where property is not a traditional public forum, if the government has “opened a forum for public discourse,” it may not “discriminate against speech on the basis of viewpoint.” *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45–46 (1983) (citing *Police Dep’t of Chicago v. Mosley*, 408 U.S. 92, 98–99 (1972)).

Here, the School District has opened its facilities to the general public for after-hours use, including lectures, films, and community events. *See* Brief of Appellant at 3–4. By permitting such use, the District has created a **limited public forum** open to all community groups. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267–68 (1981) (holding that a university’s policy of accommodating student meetings created a forum open to religious speech). The District’s blanket exclusion of religious groups based on the content of their speech is therefore unconstitutional. *See Mosley*, 408 U.S. at 95–96 (invalidating a ban on labor picketing while allowing other picketing).

II. The District’s policy violates the **Establishment Clause** by preferring secular speech over religious speech in a public forum.

The Establishment Clause prohibits government from “establish[ing] religion.” U.S. Const. amend. I. Where government creates a forum open to private speech, it may not “aid one religion … or prefer one religion over another.” *Everson v. Bd. of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 15 (1947). The District’s policy, which allows secular films and lectures but bars religious ones, impermissibly prefers secular speech over religious speech. *See McDaniel v. Paty*, 435 U.S. 618, 626–28 (1978) (invalidating a law that disqualified ministers from public office).

The District’s reliance on *Board of Educ. v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236 (1968), is misplaced. *Allen* upheld a program of lending secular textbooks to parochial schools, distinguishing between direct aid to religion and neutral programs available to all. *Id.* at 243–44. Unlike *Allen*, the District’s policy does not provide a neutral benefit; it excludes religious speech entirely from a public forum. *See Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612–13 (1971) (invalidating a program that subsidized parochial schools’ secular instruction).

III. The District’s policy is not saved by the **neutrality principle** because it is not viewpoint-neutral.

A regulation is viewpoint-neutral only if it “draws no distinctions based on the content of the message.” *Mosley*, 408 U.S. at 96. The District’s policy, which allows secular films but bars religious ones, is content-based and discriminates against religious speech. *See Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948) (invalidating a ban on religious amplification in a public park). The District cannot justify this discrimination by pointing to its general policy against religious use, because the policy is enforced selectively. *See Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 70 (1953) (invalidating a selective enforcement of a breach-of-peace statute against a religious group).

IV. The District’s policy violates the **Free Exercise Clause** by suppressing religious speech.

The Free Exercise Clause protects the right to worship “as [one’s] conscience dictates.” *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940). The District’s policy, which bars religious speech while allowing secular speech, imposes a penalty on the exercise of religion. *See Sherbert v. Verner*, 374 U.S. 398, 402–03 (1963) (invalidating a law that burdened religious practice). The District cannot justify this burden by pointing to its general policy, because the policy is not neutral and generally applicable. *See Fowler*, 345 U.S. at 70.

**Conclusion**

The District’s policy violates the First Amendment by excluding Lamb’s Chapel’s religious speech from a public forum. The judgment below should be reversed.
