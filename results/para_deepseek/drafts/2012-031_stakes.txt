**ARGUMENT**

The district court properly certified this class because it conducted a rigorous analysis and found, based on ample admissible evidence, that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis through common methodology. The question presented asks whether certification may occur without “adequate admissible evidence” on that point. Here, the district court had precisely that—four days of evidentiary hearings, 32 expert reports, deposition excerpts, and live testimony from fact and expert witnesses. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). The Third Circuit correctly affirmed, and this Court should do the same.

**I. Rule 23 Requires a “Rigorous Analysis,” Not a Mini-Trial, and the District Court Satisfied That Standard.**

This Court has made clear that a district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” to ensure that Rule 23’s requirements are met. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350–51 (2011). That analysis “may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits” but does not require a full merits trial. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 317 (3d Cir. 2008) (citing *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974)). The district court here followed that precise path. It held a four-day evidentiary hearing, reviewed extensive expert submissions, and issued an 81-page opinion explaining why the predominance requirement was satisfied. *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 154. That is the epitome of a “rigorous analysis.”

Comcast’s challenge to the adequacy of the evidence is really a challenge to the weight the district court gave to plaintiffs’ expert testimony. But the district court, as factfinder, “must be free to choose among competing expert opinions.” *Blue Bird Body Co. v. Alabama*, 573 F.2d 309, 317 (5th Cir. 1978). The court here did exactly that, crediting Dr. McClave’s methodology as “common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis.” *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 154. That finding is entitled to deference unless clearly erroneous, *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573–74 (1985), and Comcast offers no basis for overturning it.

**II. The District Court Had Adequate Admissible Evidence of a Common Methodology for Class-Wide Damages.**

The predominance requirement of Rule 23(b)(3) is satisfied when “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). A common methodology for calculating damages is one such common question. *Klay v. Humana, Inc.*, 382 F.3d 1241, 1259 (11th Cir. 2004). Here, plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. McClave, presented a regression model that estimated the overcharge to all class members attributable to Comcast’s anticompetitive conduct. The district court found that model “capable of proof at trial through evidence that is common to the class.” *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 154.

Comcast argues that the model was not “admissible” because it required assumptions about the relevant geographic market. But the district court already resolved that issue in plaintiffs’ favor, finding that the Philadelphia Designated Market Area was a permissible geographic market. *Id.* at 170. That finding is supported by the record and cannot be disturbed on appeal. *See Anderson*, 470 U.S. at 573–74.

Moreover, this Court has never held that a damages model must be airtight or unchallenged at the certification stage. To the contrary, “the fact that damages may have to be ascertained on an individual basis is not sufficient to defeat class certification” when a common methodology exists. *Blue Bird Body Co.*, 573 F.2d at 314. The district court’s finding that Dr. McClave’s methodology was common and admissible is therefore fully consistent with Rule 23.

**III. The District Court Properly Narrowed the Class’s Theory to One That Could Be Proved Through Common Evidence.**

The district court did not simply rubber-stamp certification. It actively managed the case by limiting proof of antitrust impact to a single theory: “that Comcast engaged in anticompetitive clustering conduct, the effect of which was to deter the entry of overbuilders in the Philadelphia DMA.” *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 191. That narrowing ensured that the class’s claims would be proved through common evidence, satisfying the predominance requirement. *See Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 352–53 (requiring commonality across the class). The Third Circuit correctly recognized that this approach was a permissible exercise of the district court’s discretion.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court had ample admissible evidence to certify the class, including expert testimony, reports, and a four-day hearing. Its rigorous analysis satisfied Rule 23, and the Third Circuit properly affirmed. This Court should affirm.
