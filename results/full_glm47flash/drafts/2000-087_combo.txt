**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Post-Removal-Period Statute Does Not Authorize Indefinite Detention**

The post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the ninety-day removal period. The Supreme Court has held that indefinite detention of aliens raises serious constitutional concerns. The power to expel is not the power to punish. *United States ex rel. Toth v. Quarles*, 350 U.S. 11, 16 (1955). Consequently, the duration of detention must be limited to what is reasonably necessary to effectuate removal. The Fifth Amendment is satisfied by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497, 499 (1954). The right to pursue a lawful calling is a fundamental right. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 354, 371 (1886). Therefore, the government cannot confine an alien indefinitely simply because removal is difficult.

The power to exclude or expel is a fundamental and acknowledged attribute of sovereignty, but it is not unlimited. *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy*, 338 U.S. 537, 546 (1950). The Constitution does not follow the flag. *United States ex rel. Johnson v. Eisentrager*, 339 U.S. 630, 638 (1950). The right to a fair hearing is a fundamental right. *Boutilier v. INS*, 383 U.S. 268, 273 (1966). The right to be heard is a fundamental right. *Johnson v. Mendoza*, 380 U.S. 228, 233 (1965). The writ of habeas corpus is a great and efficacious writ. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 401 (1963). Because the statute is intended to facilitate removal rather than punish, it must be read to contain an implicit “reasonable time” limitation. The detention of petitioner for over three years after the expiration of the removal period exceeds this limitation.
