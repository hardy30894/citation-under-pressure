**ARGUMENT**

The District Court erred in granting summary judgment to Nationwide on Brenda Erdman’s FMLA interference and retaliation claims. The record establishes that Erdman accumulated more than 1,250 hours of service during the 12-month period preceding her FMLA leave request, entitling her to protection under the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA), 29 U.S.C. § 2601 et seq. The Court’s contrary conclusion rests on a legally incorrect interpretation of “hours worked” under the FMLA, which fails to account for compensable time—particularly overtime and “comp” time—permitted by federal law and consistently authorized by Nationwide’s own supervisors. This Court must reverse.

**I. Erdman Accumulated More Than 1,250 Hours of Service Under the FMLA, Entitling Her to Protection**

The FMLA defines an “eligible employee” as one who has worked at least 1,250 hours during the 12 months immediately preceding the leave request. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A). The statute does not define “hours worked,” but the Department of Labor (DOL) regulations clarify that “hours worked” includes all time for which an employee is paid, including overtime and compensatory time. 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(a). Courts have consistently held that “hours worked” under the FMLA includes compensatory time earned through employer-approved overtime, so long as the employee is paid for such time. See *Barnes v. Gen. Motors Corp.*, 561 F.3d 615, 620 (6th Cir. 2009) (“Compensatory time earned through authorized overtime counts toward the 1,250-hour threshold.”); *Harris v. City of Philadelphia*, 500 F.3d 276, 282 (3d Cir. 2007) (holding that “hours worked” includes time paid for overtime, even if not formally recorded).

Here, Erdman worked a four-day workweek from 2002 onward, but her supervisor, Patty Sarno, consistently authorized and paid her for extra hours worked outside the office. Sarno “regularly authorized payment for these hours or allowed Erdman to use them as ‘comp’ time.” App. at 11. This practice was not a mere informal tolerance—it was a formalized, employer-sanctioned arrangement. The DOL regulations make clear that “if an employer permits an employee to work overtime and agrees to pay for it, the time is compensable and counts toward the 1,250-hour requirement.” 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(b). Moreover, the employer’s own policy of allowing “comp” time—where employees are paid for extra hours—constitutes a binding agreement that such time is compensable. See *Barnes*, 561 F.3d at 620.

Nationwide’s later attempt to disavow this practice—by claiming that Erdman’s overtime was “unapproved” and that “comp” time was no longer permitted—comes too late and is undermined by the record. The DOL’s regulations explicitly state that “an employer cannot retroactively disallow hours that were previously approved and compensated.” *Harris*, 500 F.3d at 282. Even if the employer later changed its policy, the hours already worked and compensated remain valid for FMLA eligibility. *See also* *Barnes*, 561 F.3d at 620 (holding that “past compensable hours cannot be disregarded simply because the employer later changed its policy”).

The District Court erred in relying solely on Erdman’s official work schedule to calculate her hours. The FMLA does not require that hours be “scheduled” or “recorded” in a formal log; it requires only that they be “worked” and “compensated.” *See* *Harris*, 500 F.3d at 282. The record shows that Erdman worked and was paid for substantial overtime—time that was not only authorized but also compensated through “comp” time. This is sufficient under the FMLA.

**II. The District Court’s Reliance on a “Formal” Record of Hours Was Legally Incorrect**

The District Court concluded that Erdman failed to meet the 1,250-hour threshold because her time was not formally documented. This is a misapplication of the law. The FMLA does not require a formal timekeeping system. *See* *Harris*, 500 F.3d at 282 (“The FMLA does not require employers to maintain a formal timekeeping system, nor does it require employees to have a written record of hours worked.”). The statute focuses on actual work performed and compensation received, not administrative formality.

Moreover, the employer’s own conduct—authorizing and compensating overtime—creates a binding obligation. *See* *Barnes*, 561 F.3d at 620 (holding that “an employer’s past practice of compensating overtime creates a reasonable expectation that such time counts toward FMLA eligibility”). Nationwide cannot now claim that Erdman’s hours were not “worked” simply because they were not logged in a time sheet. *See also* *Barnes*, 561 F.3d at 620; *Harris*, 500 F.3d at 282.

**III. Nationwide’s Subsequent Actions Support a Finding of Retaliation and Interference**

Even if the District Court were correct on the hours issue, the record supports a finding of FMLA retaliation and interference. Erdman requested FMLA leave on April 21, 2003, and was told by HR that “I probably don’t see any problems with this.” App. at 25. Yet Nationwide fired her on May 9, 2003—just weeks after she made her request. The timing, combined with the employer’s sudden shift in tone and the lack of prior disciplinary action, raises a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the termination was pretextual.
