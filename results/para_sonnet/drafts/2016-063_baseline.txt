# ARGUMENT

**A DECISION OF THE MERIT SYSTEMS PROTECTION BOARD DISMISSING A MIXED CASE FOR LACK OF JURISDICTION IS SUBJECT TO JUDICIAL REVIEW IN DISTRICT COURT**

## I. The Text of the Civil Service Reform Act Compels a Single Route of Review for Mixed Cases

The Civil Service Reform Act channels review of Board decisions to the Federal Circuit "[e]xcept as provided in paragraph (2) of this subsection." 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A). Paragraph (2) directs that "cases of discrimination subject to the provisions of section 7702" shall be filed in district court. *Id.* § 7703(b)(2). Section 7702, in turn, defines the relevant class of cases as those in which an employee "(A) has been affected by an action which the employee ... may appeal to the [Board], and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination." *Id.* § 7702(a)(1).

This Court construed that very language in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), holding that "Section 7702's mixed-case provisions" apply whenever an employee "brought a mixed case," meaning she "was affected by an action ... appealable to the MSPB and ... alleged discrimination." *Id.* at 44, 56. The Court held that "when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds, the employee is entitled to judicial review in district court—the same court in which she could seek review if the MSPB had rejected the discrimination claim on the merits." *Id.* at 44. Critically, *Kloeckner*'s reasoning was not limited by the type of threshold defect the Board identified. The Court explained that "Title 5 does not contemplate a scheme in which a district court and the Federal Circuit divvy up" mixed cases; instead, "the district court is the exclusive forum for judicial review" whenever an employee has raised a discrimination claim connected to an otherwise-appealable personnel action. *Id.* at 50.

The D.C. Circuit's distinction between "jurisdictional" and "procedural" dismissals finds no footing in this statutory text. Section 7702(a)(1)(A) asks only whether the employee "has been affected by an action which the employee ... may appeal to the [Board]"—a question resolved by the *nature of the action itself* (e.g., a removal, a suspension of more than fourteen days), not by whether the Board ultimately agreed that the employee met every precondition for adjudicating that appeal. Perry, like Kloeckner, was "affected by an action"—his suspension and retirement—that falls squarely within the categories of appealable actions enumerated in 5 U.S.C. §§ 7512–7513. That his settlement was later found voluntary no more removes his case from the statutory definition of a "mixed case" than Kloeckner's untimeliness removed hers.

## II. This Court's Reasoning in *Kloeckner* Forecloses a Jurisdictional/Procedural Distinction

*Kloeckner* rested on three pillars, none of which depends on the label attached to the Board's threshold ruling.

**First**, the Court emphasized simplicity and administrability: "Assuming the employee has alleged discrimination, the answer will always be the same: district court." 568 U.S. at 50. The Court expressly worried about creating a scheme that would force "requiring plaintiffs to shuttle between district court and the MSPB" or split their case across two forums depending on the Board's disposition. *Id.* A rule allocating review based on whether the Board dismissed for "jurisdiction" or "procedure" recreates exactly the bifurcation *Kloeckner* rejected—except now the line is drawn between two different labels for the identical judicial task: examining whether the underlying personnel action actually occurred and whether it was appealable.

**Second**, *Kloeckner* held that the *reason* for the Board's dismissal is irrelevant to the forum question. The Court stated that its holding governs "whether the MSPB decides a mixed case on procedural grounds or instead on the merits," 568 U.S. at 44, and nowhere suggested that a third category—jurisdictional dismissals—would be treated differently. Indeed, the Court framed the entire universe of non-merits dismissals as falling on the district-court side of the ledger, reasoning that Congress could not have intended "judicial review of the individual claims composing a mixed case" to "be split between two tribunals." *Id.* at 54.

**Third**, and most tellingly, this Court in *Kloeckner* addressed—and rejected—the very premise that voluntariness determinations belong in the Federal Circuit for uniformity's sake. The Court observed that federal district courts "routinely" adjudicate threshold questions about the scope of an agency's or tribunal's authority when those questions are enmeshed with the plaintiff's discrimination claim, and that nothing in the statute suggests Congress wanted to wall off such threshold rulings from the discrimination-focused review scheme it created. *Id.* at 50–56.

## III. Voluntariness Determinations Are Not Meaningfully Different from Timeliness Determinations

The D.C. Circuit sought to distinguish jurisdictional dismissals from procedural ones on the ground that the Board can "waive" a procedural defect but cannot waive a jurisdictional one. But this distinction collapses on examination of what "jurisdiction" means in this context. The Board's "jurisdiction" over a suspension or retirement turns entirely on whether the employee's acceptance of that personnel action was *voluntary*—a fact-intensive inquiry that, as this case illustrates, is frequently intertwined with the merits of the employee's discrimination and coercion claims. Perry's contention that his settlement was coerced by discriminatory pressure is not conceptually distinct from Kloeckner's contention that her removal was unlawful; both require adjudication of the employee's substantive claims to determine whether the Board could act.

Nothing in the Federal Circuit's specialized expertise over personnel law justifies assigning it exclusive authority to decide voluntariness where, as here, that determination is inseparable from allegations of discrimination-based coercion. To the contrary, *Kloeckner*'s core teaching—that discrimination claims arising from mixed cases belong in district court regardless of the procedural posture in which the Board disposed of them—applies with full force.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case should proceed in the United States District Court.
