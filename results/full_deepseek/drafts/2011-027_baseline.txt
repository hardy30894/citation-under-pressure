**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE MONTANA SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE CONSTITUTIONAL TEST FOR NAVIGABILITY-FOR-TITLE, WHICH REQUIRES FOCUS ON CONDITIONS AT STATEHOOD.**

Under the equal-footing doctrine, a State acquires title to the beds of rivers that were navigable at the time of statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). The constitutional test for navigability-for-title is “whether the river in its natural state was used, or was susceptible of being used, as highways for commerce” at the time of admission to the Union. *The Montello*, 87 U.S. 430, 441-442 (1874). The Montana Supreme Court faithfully applied that test. It examined historical evidence regarding the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers “in their ordinary condition” at the time of Montana’s statehood in 1889. *PPL Montana, LLC v. State*, 355 Mont. 402, 420-432, 229 P.3d 421, 435-444 (2010). That court did not rely on modern recreational use or post-statehood improvements. It relied on historical records—reports of explorers, fur traders, steamboat operators, and government surveys—demonstrating that these river segments were actually used for commercial navigation in 1889. *Id.* at 435-444. The court’s analysis comports with *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 591 (1922), which held that navigability is determined by the “natural and ordinary condition” of the river at statehood.

**II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY MADE A SEGMENT-BY-SEGMENT DETERMINATION OF NAVIGABILITY.**

PPL incorrectly suggests that the trial court failed to assess navigability of discrete segments. To the contrary, the trial court examined each relevant stretch of the three rivers separately. It found, for example, that the Missouri River from Fort Benton to the Great Falls was navigable by steamboat in 1889 based on “extensive evidence of commercial use.” *PPL Montana*, 355 Mont. at 420, 229 P.3d at 435. The court made similar findings for the Clark Fork River from Thompson Falls to Cabinet Gorge and for the Madison River from its confluence with the Jefferson to Ennis Lake. *Id.* at 432-444, 229 P.3d at 443-450. This segment-by-segment approach aligns with *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 408 (1940), which recognized that navigability may vary by river section, but rejected any “impossible requirement” that a river be navigable throughout its entire length.

**III. THE STATE PRESENTED COMPETENT HISTORICAL EVIDENCE OF NAVIGABILITY AT STATEHOOD.**

The Montana Supreme Court properly admitted historical documents under the hearsay exceptions for ancient documents, official publications, and public records. *See* M.R. Evid. 803(8), 901(8), 902(4)-(6). Such evidence is routinely relied upon in navigability determinations. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76; *Oklahoma*, 258 U.S. at 591. The State Archivist authenticated these records, which included steamboat manifests, Army Corps of Engineers reports, and newspaper accounts documenting commercial traffic on the rivers in 1889. *PPL Montana*, 355 Mont. at 453-454, 229 P.3d at 458-459. PPL’s expert, Dr. Emmons, offered competing interpretations, but the trial court as factfinder credited the State’s evidence. This Court “accord[s] great deference to the factual findings of the district court concerning navigability.” *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 29 (1935).

**IV. FEDERAL LAW DOES NOT PREEMPT MONTANA’S HYDROELECTRIC RESOURCES ACT.**

The Federal Power Act creates a “dual system” of control that preserves state property rights. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Cooperative v. Federal Power Commission*, 328 U.S. 152, 167-168 (1946). The FPA requires licensees to comply with state law regarding “the beds and banks” of rivers. 16 U.S.C. § 802(a)(2). It also provides that “licensees shall be liable for all damages” caused by their projects. 16 U.S.C. § 803(c). State-law compensation claims therefore align with—and are not preempted by—federal law. *Jordan v. Randolph Mills, Inc.*, 716 F.2d 1053, 1055 (4th Cir. 1983). Montana’s HRA simply implements this property right by requiring compensation for use of riverbeds owned by the State. The FPA contains no express preemption clause, and field preemption does not apply because the FPA “explicitly permits the operation of state law with regard to proprietary rights.” *PPL Montana*, 355 Mont. at 442, 229 P.3d at 454 (citing *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175-176).

**CONCLUSION**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly determined navigability based on historical evidence of commercial use at statehood, conducted a proper segment-by-segment analysis, and rejected federal preemption of Montana’s property-based compensation scheme. The judgment should be affirmed.
