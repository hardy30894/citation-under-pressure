**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Persuasion to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

The government’s burden of persuasion in conspiracy prosecutions is well-established. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 444 (1978), the Supreme Court held that the government must prove each element of a conspiracy charge, including the defendant’s knowing participation, beyond a reasonable doubt. This burden does not shift merely because a defendant claims withdrawal from the conspiracy. *See United States v. Daddano*, 432 F.2d 1119, 1124 (7th Cir. 1970) (burden remains on the government to prove membership throughout the relevant period).

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding in *United States v. Smith*, 609 F.3d 654 (D.C. Cir. 2010), is erroneous. The court improperly shifted the burden to defendants to disprove their continued participation, reasoning that withdrawal is an "affirmative defense." *Id.* at 661. But withdrawal is not an affirmative defense; it is a negation of an element of the offense—namely, knowing participation in the conspiracy during the charged period. *See United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10, 16 (1994) (elements of conspiracy include agreement and overt act; withdrawal negates agreement). The government must disprove withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt once a defendant raises it. *Cf. Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 704 (1975) (due process prohibits shifting burden to defendant on an element of the offense).

**II. The Jury Must Be Instructed on the Burden of Proof Regarding Withdrawal**

The trial court’s failure to instruct the jury on the government’s burden of persuasion regarding withdrawal was reversible error. In *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 522-23 (1995), the Supreme Court held that the Fifth Amendment requires juries to determine all elements of an offense beyond a reasonable doubt, including those negated by defenses. Withdrawal is such a defense, and the government must prove its absence beyond a reasonable doubt. *See United States v. Doyal*, 390 F.3d 1210, 1215 (10th Cir. 2004) (government bears burden of disproving withdrawal).

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Smith* conflicts with *Gaudin* by allowing the government to avoid this burden. The court’s reliance on *United States v. Merida*, 765 F.2d 1205 (D.C. Cir. 1985), is misplaced. *Merida* did not address withdrawal or the burden of proof; it merely upheld a jury instruction on overt acts. *Id.* at 1210. The court’s conflation of withdrawal with affirmative defenses like entrapment is legally unsound. *See Mathews v. United States*, 485 U.S. 58, 63-64 (1988) (distinguishing affirmative defenses from elements of the offense).

**III. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Continuing Conspiracy**

Even if the burden were misallocated, the evidence was insufficient to prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. The government’s case relied on circumstantial evidence of post-withdrawal acts, but such evidence is insufficient to prove membership in a conspiracy. *See United States v. Treadwell*, 760 F.2d 327, 333 (D.C. Cir. 1985) (mere association or knowledge of criminal activity does not establish conspiracy).

The government failed to present evidence that petitioners participated in the conspiracy’s core operations after Moore and Gray’s split. *See United States v. Shively*, 927 F.2d 804, 812 (1st Cir. 1991) (government must prove defendant’s continued agreement to the conspiracy’s objectives). The jury’s verdict was thus unsupported by substantial evidence. *See Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979) (standard for reviewing sufficiency of evidence).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment below should be reversed. The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that petitioners were members of the conspiracy during the relevant period, and the evidence was insufficient to meet that burden.
