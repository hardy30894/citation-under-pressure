# ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has long recognized that the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That protection is not a technical nicety; it is, as this Court explained in Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187 (1957), designed to ensure that "the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity." The California Supreme Court's holding that this fundamental guaranty simply does not apply to noncapital sentence-enhancement proceedings cannot be reconciled with this Court's double jeopardy jurisprudence, which has never confined the Clause's protection to the adjudication of guilt on the underlying substantive offense.

## I. A Finding That the Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove the Prior-Conviction Allegation Is the Functional Equivalent of an Acquittal and Forecloses Retrial.

The Court of Appeal held that the evidence introduced against Monge was insufficient to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that he had personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury in his prior offense. That determination is indistinguishable in substance from a judgment of acquittal, and this Court has consistently held that such judgments are final regardless of the correctness of the reasoning underlying them. In United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), this Court explained that a verdict of acquittal is final and cannot be reviewed, on error or otherwise, without putting the defendant twice in jeopardy and thereby violating the Constitution. The Court reaffirmed that principle in Fong Foo v. United States, 369 U.S. 141 (1962), holding that a judgment of acquittal bars further prosecution even where the trial court's ruling was erroneous as a matter of law. And in Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100 (1904), the Court made clear that the government may not obtain appellate correction of an acquittal precisely because doing so would subject the defendant to a second jeopardy for the same charge.

These cases rest on a simple but profound principle: once a tribunal charged with determining a factual predicate for punishment has found the proof wanting, the government does not get a second chance to marshal better evidence. See Hoag v. New Jersey, 356 U.S. 464 (1958) (recognizing that principles of collateral estoppel may bar relitigation of facts resolved in a defendant's favor in a prior proceeding). There is no principled reason why this rule should evaporate merely because the factual predicate at issue is a prior conviction used to enhance punishment rather than an element of the charged offense. The consequence to the defendant—years added to a term of imprisonment—is no less severe, and the government's incentive to relitigate a losing factual issue is no less real.

## II. The Multiple-Punishment Component of the Double Jeopardy Clause Independently Bars Retrial of the Enhancement Allegation.

The Double Jeopardy Clause does not merely bar successive prosecutions after acquittal; this Court has recognized that the guarantee also protects against multiple punishments for the same offense. See North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). This Court's decision in Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299 (1932), confirms that the Clause's concern with successive or duplicative punishment is not confined to the trial of the substantive offense; the Court there examined whether separate statutory provisions, applied to the same underlying conduct, could each support punishment, explaining that the test is "whether each provision requires proof of an additional fact which the other does not." Blockburger, 284 U.S. at 304. That same concern with repeated governmental attempts to secure enhanced punishment for the same alleged conduct is squarely implicated here. California's own procedures required the State to prove the enhancement allegation—confrontation, cross-examination, the reasonable-doubt standard, and the rules of evidence all applied. See Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967) (due process requires that a defendant subject to a factfinding proceeding materially affecting the length of confinement be afforded the right "to be confronted with witnesses against him [and] to cross-examine" them). Once the State attempted to prove the enhancement through such a trial-like proceeding and failed, permitting a second attempt exposes Monge to the very evil that the guarantee against successive jeopardy was designed to prevent.

## III. The Proceeding Below Bore the Hallmarks of a Trial and Therefore Falls Within the Core Protection of the Clause.

California itself has treated prior-conviction proceedings as trial-like adjudications, affording the reasonable-doubt standard, the rules of evidence, and confrontation rights. Having voluntarily structured its enhancement proceeding to mirror a trial on guilt or innocence in every respect but the label attached to it, California cannot now disclaim the double jeopardy consequences that attend such proceedings. This Court's teaching in Kepner, Ball, and Fong Foo does not turn on formal labels; it turns on whether the proceeding functions to determine, through an adversarial, evidence-based process, a fact that exposes the defendant to further jeopardy. The enhancement trial here did precisely that.

## CONCLUSION

Because the Court of Appeal's insufficiency ruling functions as an acquittal, and because the Double Jeopardy Clause's guarantees against successive prosecution and multiple punishment for the same offense are not confined to the adjudication of the substantive charge, this Court should hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation and reverse the judgment of the California Supreme Court.
