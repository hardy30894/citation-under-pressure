### Argument

The Due Process Clause does not require a trial court to apply a separate, heightened “reasoned choice” competency standard for guilty pleas or waiver of counsel. The governing constitutional inquiry is whether the defendant has the capacity to understand the proceedings and assist counsel; if that standard is met, the Constitution permits the plea or waiver unless the record otherwise shows the waiver was not knowing and voluntary. **Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162 (1975); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966).**

This Court has repeatedly framed competency in functional terms tied to understanding and assistance, not to a separately calibrated ability to make a “reasoned choice.” **Dusky, 362 U.S. at 402; Drope, 420 U.S. at 171-72.** In **Drope**, the Court explained that the inquiry is whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” **Drope, 420 U.S. at 171-72.** Nothing in that formulation creates a second, higher constitutional test for plea decisions.

Nor does **Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969),** support the Ninth Circuit’s heightened standard. Boykin held that a guilty plea must be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent because it waives several fundamental rights. **Boykin, 395 U.S. at 242-43.** But Boykin addressed the adequacy of the plea colloquy and the record of waiver; it did not redefine competency. Likewise, **Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742 (1970),** teaches that a plea is valid when it is entered “voluntarily” and with “sufficient awareness of the relevant circumstances and likely consequences.” **Brady, 397 U.S. at 748.** Brady confirms that the Constitution demands a knowing waiver, not a special “reasoned choice” competency inquiry.

The same is true for waiver of counsel. **Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938),** requires an “intelligent and competent” waiver, but it does not impose a heightened cognitive standard beyond competency and informed choice. **Johnson, 304 U.S. at 464.** And in **Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975),** the Court held that a defendant may proceed pro se after a knowing and voluntary waiver of counsel, even if the choice is unwise. **Faretta, 422 U.S. at 835.** The Constitution protects autonomy; it does not require the court to ensure the defendant makes the best or most rational tactical decision.

Most importantly, this Court later rejected the premise that competency to waive counsel or plead guilty is a distinct, heightened inquiry. In **Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993),** the Court held that the standard for competency to plead guilty and to waive the right to counsel is not higher than the standard for competency to stand trial. **Godinez, 509 U.S. at 398-400.** The Court explained that competency is a “single standard” and that the question whether a waiver is knowing and voluntary is analytically distinct from competency. **Id.** The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” requirement cannot be squared with that holding.

The court of appeals relied on decisions such as **Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973),** **Evans v. Raines, 705 F.2d 1479 (9th Cir. 1983),** **Evans v. Raines, 800 F.2d 884 (9th Cir. 1986),** and **Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853 (9th Cir. 1987),** but those cases cannot survive **Godinez** to the extent they impose a heightened competency threshold. Their “reasoned choice” language is inconsistent with this Court’s rejection of a special competency standard for plea and waiver decisions. **Godinez, 509 U.S. at 398-400.**

The proper constitutional safeguard is procedural: when the trial court has reason to doubt competency, it must inquire further. **Pate, 383 U.S. at 385-86; Drope, 420 U.S. at 180-81.** But that duty to investigate does not convert the substantive standard into a “reasoned choice” test. A defendant who is competent under **Dusky** and **Drope** may choose, however imprudently, to plead guilty or waive counsel. The Constitution requires competence, understanding, and voluntariness—not judicial second-guessing of the wisdom of the choice. **Johnson, 304 U.S. at 464; Faretta, 422 U.S. at 835; Brady, 397 U.S. at 748.**

Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed. The Due Process Clause requires application of the ordinary competency standard, not the Ninth Circuit’s heightened “reasoned choice” rule. **Godinez, 509 U.S. 389 (1993); Dusky, 362 U.S. 402; Drope, 420 U.S. 162; Pate, 383 U.S. 375.**
