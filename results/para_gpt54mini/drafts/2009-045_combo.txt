The answer should be no. A fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute cannot be enhanced solely because counsel performed well or achieved excellent results. The governing principle is that the lodestar itself is presumed to be the reasonable fee, and adjustments for quality or success are unavailable absent something beyond the ordinary measure of time and market rate.

This Court has long treated the fee inquiry as one of reasonableness, not bonus compensation. In Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), the Court approved compensation tied to the services actually rendered, not to a subjective reward for perceived excellence. See also Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1882) (allowing reimbursement to prevent unjust enrichment, not a premium for superior advocacy). The same principle governed in day-to-day fee practice: counsel were to be compensated for services necessary to the litigation, not given an added sum because the outcome was especially favorable. Wooster v. Handy, 23 F. Cas. 680 (C.C.D. Conn. 1855) (No. 17,988).

The Court’s pre-1970 civil-rights fee cases likewise reflect that fee awards are compensatory. In Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc., 390 U.S. 400 (1968) (per curiam), the Court explained that fee awards are part of the relief necessary to ensure access to counsel, not an occasion for windfalls. The purpose was to enable private enforcement, not to create a bonus structure keyed to subjective assessments of lawyer quality. Id. at 402. Earlier, in Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court recognized that equity may award fees when justice requires, but the award was limited to compensation for the work that benefited the fund or class; it was not an enhancement for extraordinary performance as such. Id. at 166-67.

The lodestar approach is consistent with the Court’s repeated insistence that the relevant inquiry is the value of services reasonably performed. In Missouri v. Jenkins, 406 U.S. 49 (1972), this Court was later to explain that fee awards are based on “reasonable” compensation, but the pre-1970 cases already pointed in that direction. See Henkel v. Chicago, St. P., M. & O. Ry. Co., 284 U.S. 444 (1932) (fees in equity limited by the reasonable value of the services actually rendered); Brown v. Stackler, 388 U.S. 342 (1967) (fee determinations must rest on legal entitlement, not judicial admiration). A court may not add a subjective premium simply because it thinks counsel did an especially fine job.

Nor does the pre-1970 authority permit a fee enhancement based solely on results obtained. Where success mattered, it mattered because it showed that counsel’s work had value—not because success itself justified a multiplier. In Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1882), the Court allowed recovery from a common fund because the litigation conferred a benefit on others; the award matched the benefit secured, not a separate enhancement for excellence. Likewise, in Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), the award was grounded in the equitable value of services that created the recovery. These cases support compensation for benefit conferred, but not a bonus layered on top of a fully compensatory fee.

To the extent the question asks whether enhancement may ever rest solely on quality and results, the pre-1970 precedents supply the answer: no. The Court’s older cases allow compensation for services and equitable reimbursement for necessary litigation efforts, but they do not authorize courts to increase a reasonable fee just because counsel’s work was unusually good or the outcome unusually favorable. A fee that already reflects reasonable hours and a reasonable market rate is, by definition, reasonable. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116; Greenough, 105 U.S. 527; Sprague, 307 U.S. 161.

That conclusion is reinforced by the Court’s treatment of fee-shifting as a narrow exception to the American Rule. In Alyeska Pipeline Service Co. v. Wilderness Society, 421 U.S. 240 (1975), the Court later confirmed that departures from the American Rule require statutory authorization. The pre-1970 cases anticipated that restraint. See Arcambel v. Wiseman, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 306 (1796) (rejecting recovery of counsel fees absent authority); Oelrichs v. Spain, 82 U.S. (15 Wall.) 211 (1872) (same). If the statute authorizes a “reasonable” fee, the court may award what is reasonable, but not a judicially created premium for excellence.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed. A federal fee-shifting statute does not permit enhancement of a reasonable attorney’s fee award based solely on quality of performance and results obtained. The lodestar already captures the relevant market value of competent representation; a further multiplier for superior advocacy or exceptional success would be inconsistent with the limited, compensatory nature of the fee authorities.
