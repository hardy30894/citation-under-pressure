**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates the civil and criminal laws of adjacent States only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” The text and structure of the Act, as construed by this Court, require a gap in federal coverage before state law may be adopted as surrogate federal law. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent”—contradicts the statute’s plain meaning and this Court’s precedents.

In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court explained that Congress enacted OCSLA because “federal law, because of its limited function, was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” Id. at 357 (quoting S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6 (1953)). The Act therefore borrowed state law “only to fill the substantial ‘gaps’ in the coverage of federal law.” Id. at 358. The Court reiterated that fixed platforms on the outer Continental Shelf are treated “as though they were federal enclaves in an upland State,” id. at 355, and that state law supplies the rule of decision solely where federal law is silent.

Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Department of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19 (1988), confirmed the same understanding. There the Court observed that OCSLA “established a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” by extending federal law and, where necessary, “the law of the adjacent States.” Id. at 27. The Court’s repeated emphasis on filling voids created by the limited reach of federal statutes is incompatible with the Ninth Circuit’s view that state law may operate concurrently whenever it is not expressly preempted.

The Fair Labor Standards Act supplies a comprehensive federal scheme governing wages and hours on the outer Continental Shelf. Its savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), preserves more protective state laws only within the territorial jurisdiction of the States; it does not authorize their extraterritorial application to federal enclaves. Because the FLSA addresses the precise subject matter of Newton’s claims, no gap exists for California law to fill. See Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners’ Mutual Insurance Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969) (holding that state law is assimilated under OCSLA only when “necessary to fill a significant void or gap”).

The Ninth Circuit’s test would convert OCSLA’s limited borrowing provision into a general choice-of-law rule that applies state law whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law. That reading erases the statutory qualifier “to the extent that they are applicable” and the historical purpose of supplying missing federal rules. It also conflicts with the principle, recognized in Rodrigue, that OCSLA does not “incorporate” state law wholesale but only “where necessary.” 395 U.S. at 358.

Accordingly, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
