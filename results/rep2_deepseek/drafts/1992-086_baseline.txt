ARGUMENT

The District violated the First Amendment by excluding Lamb’s Chapel from a limited public forum solely because of the religious viewpoint of its speech. The school’s facilities were open to a broad range of secular organizations for after-hours use, but the District categorically barred any group seeking to use the premises “for religious purposes.” That policy discriminates on the basis of viewpoint, not merely content, and cannot survive strict scrutiny.

I. The District Created a Limited Public Forum for Expressive Activities

Under this Court’s forum analysis, the government may designate public property as a limited public forum, opening it “for use by the public as a place for expressive activity.” *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983). Once a forum is opened, the State may impose reasonable, viewpoint-neutral restrictions on the time, place, and manner of speech, but it “may not discriminate against speech on the basis of its viewpoint.” *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). The District’s “Rules and Regulations for Community Use of School Facilities” permitted after-hours use by “community groups” for a wide array of purposes—social, civic, recreational, and educational. The record shows that the District allowed film screenings, lectures, musical performances, and other events by groups such as the Southern Harmonizers, a local theater group, and a lecture series on parapsychology. By consistently opening its facilities to such diverse secular speakers, the District created a limited public forum for expressive activities. Consequently, the District may exclude a speaker “only if the exclusion is ‘necessary to serve a compelling state interest’ and ‘narrowly drawn’ to achieve that interest.” *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45 (quoting *Carey v. Brown*, 447 U.S. 455, 461 (1980)).

II. The District’s Exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel Was Viewpoint Discrimination

The District did not merely exclude all religious speech; it singled out religious *viewpoints* on topics that were otherwise permitted. Lamb’s Chapel sought to show a film series on family values and child rearing—subjects that the District had allowed other groups to discuss. For example, the school hosted a lecture series on “Psychology and The Unknown” that touched on spiritual matters, and allowed a performance with religious overtones. The District’s rule, however, barred any use “for religious purposes,” which the District interpreted to exclude Lamb’s Chapel’s film because it was presented from a “Christian perspective.” This Court has held that “the First Amendment forbids the government to regulate speech in ways that favor some viewpoints or ideas at the expense of others.” *Members of City Council v. Taxpayers for Vincent*, 466 U.S. 789, 804 (1984). By permitting secular discussions of family life while prohibiting religiously motivated discussions of the same subject, the District engaged in precisely the kind of viewpoint discrimination that is “presumptively unconstitutional.” *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 382 (1992).

The Second Circuit erred in concluding that the District’s exclusion was viewpoint-neutral because it excluded all religious groups. But a rule that excludes all religious speech is not viewpoint-neutral when it allows secular speech on the same topics. “When the government targets not subject matter, but particular views taken by speakers on a subject, the violation of the First Amendment is all the more blatant.” *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). A ban on “religious purposes” is a ban on a particular perspective—the religious perspective—on otherwise permissible subjects. This Court’s decision in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), is directly on point. There, the Court held that a state university could not exclude student religious groups from a forum generally open to other student groups. The Court reasoned that the university’s “open forum” policy created a limited public forum, and that “the exclusion of several groups based on the content of their speech—the religious content—violates the First Amendment.” *Id.* at 269. The same principle applies here: the District’s forum was open to secular groups, and the exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel because its speech was religious was impermissible content discrimination. Moreover, this Court has since clarified that “discrimination against religious speech is not a permissible subject of the government’s ‘power to restrict speech.’” *Capitol Square Review & Advisory Bd. v. Pinette*, 515 U.S. 753, 760 (1995) (quoting *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 269).

III. The District’s Establishment Clause Concerns Do Not Justify the Exclusion

The District may argue that excluding religious speech was necessary to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. But this Court has repeatedly rejected that argument. In *Widmar*, the Court held that “an open forum in a public university does not confer any imprimatur of state approval on religious sects or practices.” 454 U.S. at 274. Similarly, in *Board of Education v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 250 (1990), the Court stated that “secondary school students are mature enough and are likely to understand that a school does not endorse or support student speech that it merely permits on a nondiscriminatory basis.” The same reasoning applies to community groups using school facilities after hours. The District’s fear of an Establishment Clause violation is not a compelling interest, and even if it were, the blanket exclusion of all religious speech is not narrowly tailored. The District could have used disclaimers or other less restrictive means to avoid any appearance of endorsement. Because the exclusion was viewpoint-based and not justified by a compelling interest, it violated the First Amendment.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
