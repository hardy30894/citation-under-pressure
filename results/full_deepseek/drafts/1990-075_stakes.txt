**ARGUMENT**

**I. Congress Provided an Intelligible Principle Guiding the Attorney General’s Temporary Scheduling Authority, Rendering the Delegation Constitutional.**

The nondelegation doctrine requires only that Congress “lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) satisfies this standard.

First, Congress specified the precise goal of temporary scheduling: to avoid “an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). Second, Congress enumerated three specific factors the Attorney General must consider: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health. *Id.* § 811(h)(3) (cross-referencing § 811(c)(4)–(6)). Third, Congress imposed procedural constraints: notice in the Federal Register, transmission to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, and consideration of the Secretary’s comments. *Id.* § 811(h)(4). Fourth, Congress limited the duration of temporary scheduling to one year, with a possible six-month extension. *Id.* § 811(h)(2). These constraints far exceed the “intelligible principle” standard, which the Court has upheld even where Congress provided “negligible standards.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373.

The temporary scheduling provision is analogous to the delegation upheld in *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), where Congress authorized the Price Administrator to fix commodity prices to “stabilize prices” and “prevent speculation.” *Id.* at 420–21. The Court held that the “standards with which the Administrator must comply are sufficiently definite and precise to enable Congress, the courts, and the public to ascertain whether the Administrator has conformed to those standards.” *Id.* at 425–26. Here, the CSA’s standards are equally definite: the Attorney General must find an “imminent hazard” based on three enumerated factors, and his order is limited in duration.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In those cases, Congress provided “no criterion to govern the President’s course.” *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415. Here, Congress supplied multiple criteria and procedural safeguards. Moreover, the Court has repeatedly rejected nondelegation challenges to statutes that authorize executive officials to define criminal conduct. *See, e.g., United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911) (upholding delegation to Secretary of Agriculture to make rules governing public lands, violation of which was a crime). The temporary scheduling power is no different.

**II. The Attorney Lawfully Subdelegated Temporary Scheduling Authority to the DEA Administrator.**

The CSA expressly authorizes the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions” to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General exercised that authority by regulation, assigning to the DEA Administrator “[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.” 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989). This subdelegation is valid because Congress intended the Attorney General to have broad administrative flexibility. *See United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 514 (1974) (recognizing that § 871(a) “authorizes the Attorney General to delegate any of his functions under the Act”). Petitioners’ contrary argument ignores the plain text of the statute and the Attorney General’s longstanding practice.

**III. The Temporary Scheduling Provision Does Not Violate Separation of Powers.**

The temporary scheduling power does not aggrandize executive power at Congress’s expense. Congress retained ultimate control by limiting the duration of temporary scheduling and requiring the Attorney General to follow permanent scheduling procedures for any substance to remain controlled beyond 18 months. *See* 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). This arrangement respects the constitutional balance. *See Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 393–94 (upholding delegation where Congress “retained sufficient authority” over the delegatee). The preclusion of judicial review of temporary scheduling orders, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6), does not render the delegation unconstitutional; it merely reflects Congress’s judgment that emergency scheduling decisions require speed and finality. Constitutional challenges to the delegation itself remain reviewable, as the Third Circuit correctly held.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
