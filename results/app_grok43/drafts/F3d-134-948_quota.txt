ARGUMENT

I. Exhaustion of tribal remedies is not a jurisdictional prerequisite to federal habeas relief under the Indian Civil Rights Act.

The district court erred in holding that Selam waived his confrontation claim. The ICRA expressly authorizes federal habeas corpus “to test the legality of his detention by order of an Indian tribe.” 25 U.S.C. § 1303. In Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 66 (1978), the Court recognized that this remedy is available without the limitations that ordinarily attend tribal sovereign immunity. National Farmers Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow Tribe of Indians, 471 U.S. 845, 856 (1985), and Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 16 (1987), require exhaustion only in civil actions challenging tribal-court jurisdiction; neither decision imposes a similar bar on criminal habeas petitions. Because the statute creates an independent federal remedy, Selam’s failure to present the confrontation issue to the tribal court of appeals did not constitute a waiver. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938); Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963); Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 78 (1977).

II. Selam was denied compulsory process and confrontation rights secured by the ICRA.

The tribal court’s refusal to secure the attendance of the witnesses Selam identified violated his right “to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967), holds that this guarantee is “a fundamental element of due process.” The same provision incorporates the confrontation right. Pointer v. Texas, 380 U.S. 400, 403 (1965). In the second trial the five-year-old complainant did not appear; her out-of-court statements were admitted through four witnesses without any opportunity for cross-examination. The resulting convictions rest on testimony that the Sixth Amendment, made applicable by the ICRA, forbids.

The district court’s judgment should be reversed.
