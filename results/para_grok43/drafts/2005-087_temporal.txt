ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not confer upon a defendant a right, independent of an insanity defense, to introduce expert evidence of mental condition for the purpose of negating the mens rea element of the charged offense. States retain authority to prescribe the elements of crimes and the defenses available to them, subject only to the requirement that proceedings conform to "fundamental principles of liberty and justice which lie at the base of all our civil and political institutions." Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934).

This Court has long recognized that a state may place upon the accused the burden of establishing insanity. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952), the Court upheld a statute requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, observing that "the burden of proof in such cases may constitutionally be placed on the defendant." The same principle permits a state to channel evidence of mental disease exclusively to that defense rather than permitting its use to rebut the prosecution's proof of intent. Once the state has defined the offense to require proof of a culpable mental state and has provided an affirmative defense of insanity, due process does not demand an additional avenue for the same evidence.

The common-law presumption of sanity reinforces this allocation. As stated in Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 486 (1895), "the government in a criminal prosecution is not required to prove affirmatively that the defendant was sane at the time of the commission of the act charged." The presumption operates until the defendant carries the burden assigned to the insanity defense. Allowing mental-state evidence to serve a second function would effectively shift to the prosecution the obligation to disprove every possible mental abnormality, a requirement unknown to the common law and not compelled by the Fourteenth Amendment.

The trial court's limitation of the expert testimony to the insanity claim therefore comported with established principles of due process. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
