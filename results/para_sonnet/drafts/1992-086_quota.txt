# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Engaged in Impermissible Viewpoint Discrimination When It Excluded Lamb's Chapel Solely Because Its Speech Was Religious.

The Center Moriches Union Free School District opened its facilities to a broad range of civic, social, and educational uses after school hours, thereby creating a forum for expressive activity. Once government designates property, even for limited purposes, as a forum for expressive activity, the First Amendment sharply constrains its power to pick and choose among speakers based on viewpoint. See *Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983) (recognizing that even in a limited public forum, the State "may not discriminate against speech on the basis of its viewpoint"). This Court has long held that "government has no power to restrict expression because of its message, its ideas, its subject matter, or its content." *Police Dep't of Chicago v. Mosley*, 408 U.S. 92, 95 (1972). That prohibition applies with special force when the excluded viewpoint happens to be a religious one, for "selective exclusions from a public forum may not be based on content alone." *Mosley*, 408 U.S. at 96.

The District's own rule, on its face, singles out religion: "The school premises shall not be used by any group for religious purposes." That is not a content-neutral time, place, or manner restriction of the sort permitted under *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45; it is a categorical bar on an entire viewpoint—the religious perspective on family and childrearing—while secular perspectives on the identical subject matter remain welcome. The film series at issue, "Turn Your Heart Toward Home," addressed family values and childrearing from a religious point of view. Had Lamb's Chapel proposed the identical topic from a secular psychological or sociological standpoint—precisely the kind of presentation the District permitted from the Center Moriches Free Public Library's lecture series on "Psychology and The Unknown"—access would not have been denied. That disparity is the essence of viewpoint discrimination condemned in *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985), which reaffirmed that "the First Amendment forbids the government to regulate speech in ways that favor some viewpoints or ideas at the expense of others."

## II. This Court's Decisions in Widmar and Mergens Confirm That a Limited Forum May Not Exclude Speech Merely Because It Is Religious.

The Second Circuit attempted to distinguish this case from *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), and *Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), on the ground that those cases involved students rather than outside community groups. That distinction does not diminish the controlling First Amendment principle those cases establish. In *Widmar*, this Court held that a state university could not deny a student religious group access to facilities generally available to other student organizations, explaining that "the University has opened its facilities for use by student groups" and that exclusion of religious speech from that forum constituted "a content-based exclusion of religious speech." *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 267–69. The Court squarely rejected the argument that a forum may be closed to religious viewpoints even while remaining open to comparable secular ones, because such exclusion "requires the University to justify its abridgment of respondents' First Amendment rights" under strict scrutiny. *Id.* at 269–70.

While *Mergens* rested on statutory grounds under the Equal Access Act, its reasoning illuminates the same constitutional principle: a school that opens its facilities to any noncurriculum-related use may not slam the door on religious groups without engaging in precisely the viewpoint discrimination the Speech Clause forbids. 496 U.S. at 247. The identity of the speaker—student or community pastor—does not alter the character of the forum once government has opened it to expressive activity on a given subject. The relevant inquiry under *Perry* and *Cornelius* focuses on the nature of the forum and the character of the restriction, not on whether the speaker holds a school identification card.

## III. Permitting Lamb's Chapel Equal Access Would Not Violate the Establishment Clause.

The District's asserted justification—that Establishment Clause concerns compelled exclusion—cannot withstand analysis under this Court's settled framework in *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612–13 (1971). Allowing Lamb's Chapel to use school facilities on the same terms as other community groups would have a plainly secular purpose: the evenhanded administration of a public forum. It would neither advance nor inhibit religion, since any benefit to Lamb's Chapel would be "no more than incidental," flowing from a neutral policy of access available to all groups regardless of viewpoint. See *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 16–18 (1947) (permitting neutral public benefits made generally available without regard to religion). Nor would it create the kind of "excessive entanglement" between church and state that concerned the Court in *Lemon*. To the contrary, refusing access solely because speech is religious reflects the very hostility toward religion that the Free Exercise Clause forbids. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940) (the Free Exercise Clause protects the freedom to communicate religious views "without unreasonable governmental interference").

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
