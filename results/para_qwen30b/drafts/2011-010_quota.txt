**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits to employees injured “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This provision does not, however, eliminate the fundamental requirement that an injury must occur on a maritime situs to qualify for LHWCA benefits—whether under the LHWCA directly or via OCSLA’s extension. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that OCSLA’s coverage is triggered solely by a “substantial nexus” between the injury and offshore operations, regardless of where the injury occurred. That interpretation disregards the statutory text, the settled jurisprudence of this Court, and the clear line of precedent establishing that maritime workers’ compensation under the LHWCA is limited to injuries occurring on or over navigable waters or on a maritime situs. We respectfully submit that the judgment below should be affirmed.

### I. The OCSLA’s Extension of LHWCA Coverage Is Subject to the LHWCA’s Situs Requirement

The LHWCA provides compensation only for injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). This “situs” requirement is not a mere formality—it is a jurisdictional prerequisite. *Barnes v. United States*, 360 U.S. 504, 508 (1959) (“The situs of the injury is a jurisdictional fact.”). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the LHWCA’s coverage is confined to injuries occurring on or over navigable waters. *Henderson v. United States*, 339 U.S. 816, 818 (1950) (“The Act applies only to injuries occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.”); *Barnes*, 360 U.S. at 508 (“The Act does not apply to injuries occurring on land.”).

OCSLA does not alter this fundamental limitation. Section 1333(b) extends LHWCA benefits to injuries “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase “occurring as the result of” does not eliminate the situs requirement; rather, it establishes a causal link between the injury and offshore operations. The injury must still occur *somewhere*—and that “somewhere” must be within the maritime situs to trigger LHWCA coverage. *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray (Herb’s Welding I)*, 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985) (“The LHWCA applies only to injuries occurring on or over navigable waters.”); *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray (Herb’s Welding II)*, 766 F.2d 898, 900 (5th Cir. 1985) (holding that an injury on a platform in state waters is not eligible for OCSLA benefits).

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test effectively nullifies the situs requirement. It permits coverage for injuries occurring on land, so long as the injury is “connected” to offshore operations. But this reading contradicts the Supreme Court’s repeated insistence that the LHWCA’s reach is geographically bounded. *Barnes*, 360 U.S. at 508; *Henderson*, 339 U.S. at 818; *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69, 82 (1979) (“The Act applies only to injuries occurring on or over navigable waters.”). The Court has never held that a mere functional or causal connection to maritime activity suffices to confer LHWCA jurisdiction over injuries on land.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s “Substantial Nexus” Test Conflicts with Binding Precedent

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that OCSLA coverage is not limited by situs is directly at odds with the Fifth Circuit’s long-standing precedent. In *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), the court held that an injury must occur “on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above the outer continental shelf” to qualify under OCSLA. The court reasoned that the “situs-of-injury” requirement is necessary to preserve the geographic boundaries of the LHWCA. *Id.* at 361. This interpretation has been reaffirmed in *Becker v. Tidewater, Inc.*, 586 F.3d 358, 366–67 (5th Cir. 2009); *Pickett v. Petroleum Helicopters, Inc.*, 266 F.3d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 2001); and *Sisson v. Davis & Sons, Inc.*, 131 F.3d 555, 558 (5th Cir. 1998). These decisions are consistent with the principle that maritime law is rooted in geography.

The Third Circuit’s contrary holding in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is not controlling and has been criticized for undermining the situs requirement. *See* *Mills*, 877 F.2d at 361 n.4 (noting that *Curtis* “fails to account for the geographical limits of the LHWCA”). The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of *Curtis* is therefore erroneous and inconsistent with the weight of precedent.
