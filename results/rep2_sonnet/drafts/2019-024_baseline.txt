# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF SECTION 1113(2) REQUIRES SUBJECTIVE AWARENESS, NOT CONSTRUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE, BUT DISCLOSURE OF INFORMATION IN COMPLIANCE WITH ERISA'S STATUTORY SCHEME CONSTITUTES ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE AS A MATTER OF LAW

Section 1113(2) bars suit unless commenced within "three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This Court has long instructed that statutory terms are given "their ordinary, contemporary, common meaning." *Perrin v. United States*, 444 U.S. 37, 42 (1979). The ordinary meaning of "actual knowledge" is possession of information—not a heightened mental state requiring conscious recollection or comprehension of legal significance.

Congress's 1987 decision to eliminate the separate "constructive knowledge" provision, which had triggered the limitations period when a report "from which [the plaintiff] could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge" was filed, confirms that Congress meant something distinct from mere availability of information. *See* Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. But the Ninth Circuit's holding goes too far in the opposite direction. That court held that Sulyma's own sworn denial that he *recalled* reviewing disclosures created a triable issue precluding summary judgment, even though Intel proved it disclosed the relevant information through Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and annual disclosures mandated by ERISA's disclosure regime. This holding effectively resurrects a subjective-recollection standard that reads "actual" to mean "actually remembered at time of deposition," a gloss nowhere found in the statute.

This Court's reasoning in *Merck & Co. v. Reynolds*, 559 U.S. 633 (2010), construing an analogous "actual knowledge" trigger in the securities-fraud context, supports Intel's position that actual knowledge is established once a fiduciary satisfies its disclosure obligations under the statute. There, the Court distinguished "actual knowledge" from constructive or inquiry notice, but nowhere suggested that a plaintiff could defeat actual knowledge merely by disclaiming memory of documents he was legally furnished and had every opportunity to read. Congress structured ERISA's disclosure requirements, 29 U.S.C. § 1024, precisely so that participants would possess the information necessary to police fiduciary conduct. Allowing plaintiffs to circumvent the limitations period through self-serving denials of recollection undermines that structure and the "reticulated" statutory balance this Court has recognized ERISA embodies. *See Mertens v. Hewitt Associates*, 508 U.S. 248, 251 (1993) (describing ERISA's "carefully crafted and detailed enforcement scheme").

## II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT'S RULE CONFLICTS WITH THE STRUCTURE AND PURPOSE OF ERISA'S TWO-TIERED LIMITATIONS SCHEME

Section 1113 establishes two limitations periods: six years running from "the date of the last action which constituted a part of the breach," and three years running from actual knowledge. 29 U.S.C. § 1113(1)–(2). This structure reflects Congress's judgment that a shorter period should apply once a plaintiff possesses the relevant facts, "regardless of whether the plaintiff knew that those facts amounted to a breach of the applicable duty." *Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma*, 909 F.3d 1069, 1073 (9th Cir. 2018) (Ikuta, J., dissenting from denial of rehearing). If plaintiffs may indefinitely extend the three-year period merely by asserting they do not "recall" reading disclosures, the three-year provision becomes a nullity whenever a fiduciary has complied with disclosure obligations, collapsing into the six-year period in every case. This Court has cautioned against interpretations that render statutory provisions superfluous. *See TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 31 (2001) ("It is a cardinal principle of statutory construction that a statute ought, upon the whole, to be so construed that, if it can be prevented, no clause, sentence, or word shall be superfluous.") (internal quotation marks omitted).

The Ninth Circuit itself acknowledged that its precedents created "confusion" regarding the actual-knowledge standard, citing conflicting district court applications. *Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1073. Yet rather than resolving that confusion by anchoring the standard in objective disclosure compliance, the panel adopted a rule under which "only a fact-finder could have determined that Sulyma had the requisite 'actual knowledge of the breach.'" *Id.* at 1077. That approach effectively guarantees that virtually every ERISA fiduciary-breach case will proceed to trial on the limitations question alone, contrary to Congress's evident intent to provide fiduciaries a meaningful three-year repose period once relevant facts are disclosed.

## III. WHERE A FIDUCIARY DISCLOSES ALL MATERIAL INFORMATION IN THE MANNER ERISA REQUIRES, THE PLAINTIFF'S UNILATERAL FAILURE TO READ THAT INFORMATION CANNOT DEFEAT SUMMARY JUDGMENT

This Court's decisions applying limitations periods elsewhere confirm that a party cannot manufacture a factual dispute simply by professing ignorance of information properly furnished to him. In *TRW*, the Court noted the importance of the discovery-based triggers Congress selects, distinguishing between periods that run from disclosure of relevant facts and those requiring subjective realization of wrongdoing. 534 U.S. at 27–28. Congress's use of "actual knowledge" in § 1113(2), following the deletion of the constructive-knowledge provision, indicates that Congress intended the "actual" modifier to exclude *inferred* or imputed knowledge from third parties or general availability of public information—not to permit plaintiffs to disclaim knowledge of documents Intel proves were transmitted directly to them as plan participants under mandatory ERISA disclosure provisions.

The record here establishes that Intel provided Sulyma, individually, with Fund Fact Sheets disclosing the precise asset allocations he now challenges, along with Summary Plan Descriptions and annual disclosures explaining the investment strategy. Under the Ninth Circuit's rule, this evidentiary showing is rendered irrelevant by Sulyma's litigation-driven declaration that he does not recall reviewing the documents. Nothing in the text of § 1113(2), or in this Court's construction of analogous statutes of limitations, supports allowing a plaintiff's self-serving amnesia to override documented compliance with statutory disclosure obligations.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
