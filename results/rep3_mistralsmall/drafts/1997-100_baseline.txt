**ARGUMENT**

I. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding when the initial finding was set aside for insufficient evidence.

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. The Supreme Court has long held that this Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court extended double jeopardy protections to capital sentencing proceedings, holding that a jury’s determination that the State had failed to prove the necessary aggravating factors beyond a reasonable doubt barred retrial of those factors. *Id.* at 446. The Court reasoned that because the capital sentencing proceeding “resembled a trial on guilt or innocence,” *id.* at 438, it triggered double jeopardy protections. *See also Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 210 (1984) (applying *Bullington* to bar retrial of aggravating factors when the initial finding was set aside for insufficient evidence).

The Court has since clarified that the critical inquiry is whether the sentencing proceeding “involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209–10. In *Monge v. California*, 524 U.S. 721 (1998), the Supreme Court addressed whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Court held that it does not, distinguishing capital sentencing proceedings based on their “unique circumstances.” *Id.* at 734. However, the Court did not disturb the principle that when a sentencing proceeding—capital or noncapital—resembles a trial on guilt or innocence, double jeopardy protections apply. *See id.* at 732–34.

Here, the California Court of Appeal correctly concluded that the prior conviction allegation proceeding resembled a trial on guilt or innocence and thus triggered double jeopardy protections. The proceeding was bifurcated, required proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and involved factual determinations about the defendant’s prior conviction. *See People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993) (holding that prior conviction allegations must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt). Under *Bullington* and *Rumsey*, these characteristics are sufficient to bring the proceeding within the ambit of the Double Jeopardy Clause. The California Supreme Court erred in concluding otherwise.

II. The Double Jeopardy Clause applies to prior conviction allegations because they are “the same offense” for double jeopardy purposes.

The Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits retrial for “the same offence.” U.S. Const. amend. V. The Supreme Court has held that “the same offence” includes sentencing enhancements that are based on prior convictions. *See Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 747 (1994) (holding that a prior conviction used for sentencing enhancement is an element of the offense for double jeopardy purposes). Here, the prior conviction allegation was an element of the enhanced sentence, making it “the same offence” for double jeopardy purposes. Thus, retrial of the prior conviction allegation after the initial finding was set aside for insufficient evidence violates the Double Jeopardy Clause.

III. The Court of Appeal correctly applied double jeopardy protections to the prior conviction allegation proceeding.

The California Court of Appeal recognized that the prior conviction allegation proceeding resembled a trial on guilt or innocence and thus triggered double jeopardy protections. *See Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438. The proceeding was bifurcated, required proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and involved factual determinations about the defendant’s prior conviction. *See Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th at 566. Under *Bullington* and *Rumsey*, these characteristics are sufficient to bring the proceeding within the ambit of the Double Jeopardy Clause. The California Supreme Court erred in concluding otherwise.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the Double Jeopardy Clause.
