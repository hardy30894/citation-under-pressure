## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Bryant's Claim to Enforce the Arbitration Award

The district court erred in dismissing Bryant's claim seeking enforcement of the arbitration award. As an employee covered by a collective bargaining agreement, Bryant has standing to seek enforcement of the arbitration award under Section 301 of the Labor Management Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. § 185. See Vaca v. Sipes, 386 U.S. 171, 183-84 (1967) (holding that an employee has standing to bring a claim to enforce an arbitration award under Section 301). The Supreme Court has consistently held that Section 301 confers federal jurisdiction over controversies involving collective bargaining agreements, including disputes over arbitration awards. See Textile Workers Union v. Lincoln Mills, 353 U.S. 448, 451 (1957). Therefore, the district court should have enforced the arbitration award in favor of Bryant.

### II. The District Court Erred in Failing to Recognize the Recent Supreme Court Clarification to the Proof Requirements Under the Burden-Shifting Standard in Title VII and Section 1981 Cases

The district court erred in failing to apply the correct burden-shifting standard in evaluating Bryant's Title VII and Section 1981 claims. The Supreme Court has clarified that a plaintiff need not present direct evidence of discrimination to establish a prima facie case under Title VII or Section 1981. See Desert Palace, Inc. v. Costa, 539 U.S. 90, 101-02 (2003) (holding that a plaintiff may establish a prima facie case of discrimination through circumstantial evidence). Under the burden-shifting framework established in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), a plaintiff must first establish a prima facie case of discrimination. If the plaintiff succeeds, the burden shifts to the defendant to articulate a legitimate, non-discriminatory reason for the adverse employment action. See id. at 802. The district court failed to properly apply this framework, and therefore, its grant of summary judgment was improper.

### III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because There Are Genuine Issues of Material Fact Still in Dispute

The district court erred in granting summary judgment in favor of Bell Atlantic because there are genuine issues of material fact still in dispute. Under Rule 56(c) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, summary judgment is only appropriate if there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 247 (1986). The record reveals that there are disputed facts regarding Bell Atlantic's motivations for terminating Bryant, including the company's refusal to accommodate Bryant's child care responsibilities and its disparate treatment of Bryant compared to other employees. See Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 147 (2000) (holding that a plaintiff may establish pretext by showing that the employer's proffered reason is false or that the employer has treated similarly situated employees more favorably). Therefore, the district court's grant of summary judgment was improper, and this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.
