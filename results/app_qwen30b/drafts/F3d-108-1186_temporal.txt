**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INTERPRETING THE JURY’S AMBIGUOUS VERDICT BY SUBTRACTING THE SET-OFF FROM THE DAMAGES AWARDED, CONTRARY TO THE CLEAR RULE THAT A JURY’S VERDICT MUST BE GIVEN EFFECT IF IT IS CONSISTENT WITH THE INSTRUCTIONS AND THE EVIDENCE.

The jury returned a verdict on Form 1 awarding Unit $70,768.02 in damages for breach of contract, and on Form 3 awarded Enron a set-off of $86,494.30. The district court, without seeking clarification, interpreted these verdicts as mutually exclusive and concluded that because the set-off exceeded the damages, Unit was entitled to no recovery. This interpretation is erroneous as a matter of law and violates the fundamental principle that a jury’s verdict must be given effect if it can be reasonably construed to be consistent with the instructions and the evidence.

Under the settled law of this Circuit, a jury’s verdict must be interpreted in a manner that gives effect to its intent, so long as that interpretation is consistent with the instructions and the evidence. *See United States v. Hines, 347 F.2d 577, 580 (10th Cir. 1965)*. Where a jury returns multiple verdict forms, the court must determine whether the verdicts can be reconciled in a way that reflects the jury’s intent. *See United States v. Hines, 347 F.2d at 580*. The court may not, as the district court did, mechanically subtract one award from another when the jury’s instructions permitted the possibility of a net recovery.

The jury was instructed that if it found Enron had breached the contract, it could award Unit damages either (1) in the full amount of the unpaid invoices, $157,262.36, if Unit fully performed, or (2) in the amount of the unpaid invoices less the cost to Enron of correcting defects, if Unit only substantially performed. The jury’s award of $70,768.02 on Form 1—less than the full invoice amount—demonstrates that it found Unit had not fully performed, but had substantially performed. The jury was further instructed that if it found Enron was entitled to a set-off, it should indicate that on Form 3. The jury did so, awarding Enron $86,494.30.

The critical point is that the jury was not instructed to subtract the set-off from the damages. Indeed, the instructions did not even suggest that the set-off could be applied against the damages in a single calculation. Instead, the jury was told to consider the set-off only if it found a breach. The jury’s award of $70,768.02 on Form 1, when compared to the $157,262.36 in unpaid invoices, clearly reflects a determination that Unit’s performance was defective and that the cost to Enron to correct the defects was $86,494.30—precisely the amount awarded on Form 3. The jury, in effect, applied the set-off to reduce Unit’s damages, and thus awarded Unit a net recovery of $70,768.02.

This interpretation is not only consistent with the instructions but is the only one that makes sense of the verdict. *See United States v. Hines, 347 F.2d at 580* (“Where a jury returns a verdict which is ambiguous, the court must interpret it in a way that gives effect to the jury’s intent, if possible.”). The district court’s contrary interpretation—subtracting the set-off from the damages—would have required the jury to award Enron a set-off *in addition to* reducing Unit’s damages, which is not what the instructions permitted. The jury was not told to award damages and then separately award a set-off that could be applied against those damages. It was told to consider the set-off *if* there was a breach, and to award damages accordingly.

The district court’s interpretation renders the set-off instruction meaningless. If the jury could not apply the set-off to reduce damages, then the set-off instruction was superfluous. *See United States v. Hines, 347 F.2d at 580*. The only reasonable construction of the verdict is that the jury applied the set-off to reduce Unit’s damages, and thus awarded Unit $70,768.02 as a net recovery.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN REFUSING TO ASK THE JURY TO CLARIFY THE VERDICT, IN VIOLATION OF FEDERAL RULE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE 49(a).

Rule 49(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provides that when a jury returns a general verdict that is ambiguous, the court “may” ask the jury to clarify its findings. *See Fed. R. Civ. P. 49(a)*. The rule is designed to avoid the need for a new trial when the jury’s intent can be ascertained through a simple inquiry. *See United States v. Hines, 347 F.2d at 580*.

Unit requested clarification before the jury was discharged. The district court refused, stating that it would not “inquire into the jury’s thought process.” This refusal was error. *See United States v. Hines, 347 F.2d at 580* (“The court has a duty to seek clarification when a verdict is ambiguous, so long as the inquiry does not invade the jury’s deliberative process.”). The jury’s verdict was ambiguous: it awarded Unit damages and Enron a set-off, but did not state whether the set-off was to be applied against the damages. The court had the authority—and indeed the duty—to ask the jury to clarify whether the $86,494.30 set-off was intended to reduce the $70,768.02 award.

The court’s refusal to ask the jury to clarify the verdict deprived Unit of a fair opportunity to have its recovery determined according to the jury’s intent. *See United States v. Hines, 347 F.2d at 580*. The court’s decision to interpret the verdict without clarification was not only error but prejudicial, as it resulted in the complete denial of Unit’s claim.
