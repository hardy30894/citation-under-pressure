**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCSLA’S CHOICE-OF-LAW PROVISION REQUIRES A “GAP” IN FEDERAL COVERAGE BEFORE STATE LAW MAY BE ADOPTED AS SURROGATE FEDERAL LAW.

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted, the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State … are declared to be the law of the United States” for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. This Court has long recognized that the OCSLA’s incorporation of state law is not automatic; rather, it operates only where federal law leaves a “significant void or gap.” *Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969); *see also Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 359 (1969) (noting that state law applies “to the extent that it is applicable and not inconsistent with federal law”).

The Ninth Circuit erred by dispensing with the “gap” requirement. The court below held that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, without first determining whether federal law is silent or incomplete on the subject. *Parker Drilling Mgmt. Servs., Ltd. v. Newton*, 927 F.3d 1008, 1015 (9th Cir. 2019). This misreads the statute and this Court’s precedents. As the Fifth Circuit has explained, the OCSLA’s incorporation of state law is “limited to situations where federal law is silent or incomplete.” *Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Eng’g, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990) (emphasis added). The Supreme Court has likewise recognized that state law fills gaps in federal coverage only where necessary. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 366 (applying state law because federal law provided no remedy).

Here, the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is not silent on wage and hour protections. It establishes minimum wage and overtime standards, 29 U.S.C. §§ 206, 207, and includes a savings clause allowing more protective state laws, § 218. The FLSA thus provides a comprehensive framework for wage and hour claims. Because there is no gap in federal coverage, California’s wage and hour laws do not apply to Newton’s claims under the OCSLA. *See, e.g., Williams v. Brinderson Constructors Inc.*, 2015 WL 4747892, at *4 (C.D. Cal. 2015) (holding that California wage and hour laws do not apply on the OCS where the FLSA provides a complete remedy).

II. CALIFORNIA’S WAGE AND HOUR LAWS ARE INCONSISTENT WITH THE FLSA’S COMPREHENSIVE SCHEME.

Even if the OCSLA’s “gap” requirement were satisfied, California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA. The OCSLA permits state law to apply only if it is “not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). Inconsistency arises where state law “stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.” *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941).

The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218, expressly preserves state laws that are “more protective” of workers. However, this clause does not authorize state laws that impose additional obligations beyond the FLSA’s framework. *See, e.g., Dole v. Vincent, 35 F.3d 885, 890 (9th Cir. 1994)* (holding that state laws are preempted if they conflict with or frustrate the FLSA’s objectives). California’s meal period requirements, as applied to offshore workers, conflict with the FLSA’s structure. The FLSA does not mandate meal periods, and its overtime provisions already account for long workdays. Imposing California’s meal period rules on offshore workers would disrupt the FLSA’s balance and frustrate its objectives. *See, e.g., Chao v. Tradesmen Int’l, Inc.*, 310 F.3d 904, 910 (6th Cir. 2002) (holding that state laws are preempted if they impose obligations not contemplated by the FLSA).

III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S RULING CONTRADICTS THE OCSLA’S STRUCTURE AND PURPOSE.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation would allow state law to apply broadly on the OCS, undermining the uniformity Congress sought. The OCSLA was enacted to provide a predictable legal framework for offshore activities. *See Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 26 (1988). The Fifth Circuit has correctly recognized that the OCSLA’s incorporation of state law is limited to filling gaps in federal coverage. *See Union Texas Petroleum*, 895 F.2d at 1047. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary approach would inject uncertainty and inconsistency into the regulation of offshore activities, contrary to Congress’s intent.

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA requires a “gap” in federal coverage before state law may be adopted as surrogate federal law. Here, the FLSA provides a comprehensive scheme for wage and hour protections, leaving no gap for California’s laws to fill. Moreover, California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA’s objectives. The Ninth Circuit’s decision should be reversed.
