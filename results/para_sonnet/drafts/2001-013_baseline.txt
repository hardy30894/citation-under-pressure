# ARGUMENT

## OSHA HAS JURISDICTION UNDER SECTION 4(b)(1) OF THE ACT TO CITE UNINSPECTED VESSELS BECAUSE THE COAST GUARD HAS NOT EXERCISED STATUTORY AUTHORITY TO PRESCRIBE OR ENFORCE COMPREHENSIVE WORKING-CONDITION STANDARDS FOR SUCH VESSELS

Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act withdraws OSHA's jurisdiction only where "other Federal agencies... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This Court has repeatedly held that § 4(b)(1) preemption is not triggered by the mere existence of another agency's general statutory authority, but requires that the other agency have actually exercised that authority by promulgating specific standards addressing the working conditions at issue. In *Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. Usery*, 539 F.2d 386 (5th Cir. 1976)—a case whose reasoning this Court should adopt—the touchstone was whether the other agency had issued regulations that in fact "cover" the working condition. The Fifth Circuit's blanket exemption for all "seamen" aboard all "vessels," regardless of whether the Coast Guard has issued any comprehensive regulatory scheme, cannot be squared with this framework.

### A. The Statutory Text Requires Actual Exercise of Regulatory Authority, Not Mere Potential Authority

Section 4(b)(1) speaks in the present tense of agencies that "exercise" authority to prescribe standards—not agencies that merely possess latent statutory power to do so. The Coast Guard's authority over uninspected vessels under 46 U.S.C. § 4102 is narrow and specific: it authorizes regulation of discrete safety equipment—fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation systems, and signaling devices. These are important but limited categories. They do not constitute the kind of comprehensive occupational safety and health regulatory scheme that Congress intended to trigger displacement of OSHA jurisdiction.

The Fifth Circuit's own catalog of Coast Guard regulations for uninspected vessels—citing 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 *et seq.* and 26.01 *et seq.*—confirms this narrowness. These provisions address lifesaving equipment, fire extinguishers, ventilation, and similar equipment-based standards. They say nothing about the workplace hazards actually at issue here: blowout prevention, well-control procedures, and the causes of the explosion that killed four workers and injured two others aboard Rig 52. The Coast Guard's own investigative report in this case, which "did not accuse Mallard of anything" but simply noted that Rig 52 was not an "inspected vessel," itself demonstrates that no comprehensive Coast Guard regulatory scheme governed the hazardous drilling operations at issue.

### B. The Distinction Between Inspected and Uninspected Vessels Is Congressionally Mandated and Dispositive

Congress itself drew a sharp line between inspected and uninspected vessels, subjecting the former to comprehensive Coast Guard oversight under 46 U.S.C. §§ 3301 *et seq.* and leaving the latter subject only to the narrow equipment-based requirements of § 4102. The Fifth Circuit's decision in *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), erased this distinction by announcing a categorical rule that "OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation," *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826, without regard to whether the vessel is inspected or uninspected. That approach reads the word "exercise" out of § 4(b)(1) altogether, transforming a fact-specific inquiry into a status-based categorical exemption keyed solely to the employee's job title as a "seaman."

But § 4(b)(1) does not ask whether the worker is a seaman; it asks whether another federal agency has exercised authority to regulate the working condition. The Fifth Circuit's reliance on the "industry-wide exemption" language from *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826, conflates two distinct questions—whether the Coast Guard has *jurisdiction* to regulate seamen generally, and whether it has *exercised* that jurisdiction with respect to the specific hazard at issue. The former is a red herring; the latter is what § 4(b)(1) requires.

### C. Congress's Purpose of Ensuring Comprehensive Workplace Safety Regulation Confirms OSHA's Jurisdiction Here

The Fifth Circuit worried that overlapping regulation would produce "confusion, governmental proliferation, and stultification of enterprise," *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 827, and the "anomaly" of "steaming in and out of OSHA coverage," *id.* at 829. But the anomaly the Fifth Circuit should have feared is the opposite one it created: leaving workers on uninspected vessels—who face some of the most dangerous working conditions in the maritime and drilling industries—without *any* comprehensive occupational safety regulation at all. Congress enacted the OSH Act to ensure that no American worker would fall into a regulatory vacuum. Interpreting § 4(b)(1) to exempt uninspected vessels from OSHA coverage whenever the Coast Guard possesses *some* residual statutory authority—even where that authority has not been exercised to address the relevant hazard—would create precisely the gap in worker protection that Congress sought to close.

The explosion aboard Rig 52 illustrates the point starkly. Four workers died and two were seriously injured in a blowout-related explosion. The Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels touch on fire extinguishers and life preservers; they do not address blowout prevention or well-control procedures. Under the Fifth Circuit's rule, these workers—and others like them on the thousands of uninspected vessels operating in the Nation's territorial waters—are left with no applicable comprehensive safety standard whatsoever. That result contradicts the Act's remedial purpose.

### D. This Court's Precedent Supports a Standard-Specific, Not Vessel-Wide, Preemption Analysis

This Court's decisions construing analogous preemption provisions confirm that displacement of one regulatory scheme by another requires close attention to whether the specific hazard has actually been addressed by the assertedly preemptive scheme, rather than a categorical, industry-wide exemption. *Cf. Geier v. American Honda Motor Co.*, 529 U.S. 861 (2000) (examining whether a specific federal safety standard actually conflicted with the state requirement at issue, rather than announcing categorical displacement based on the existence of a broader regulatory scheme). The same standard-specific inquiry should govern here: because the Coast Guard has not exercised authority to prescribe standards addressing blowout and well-control hazards on uninspected vessels, OSHA retains jurisdiction to cite Mallard for violations relating to those hazards.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
