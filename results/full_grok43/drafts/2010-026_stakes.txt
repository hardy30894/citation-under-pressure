**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit’s “Predominantly Internal” Standard Exceeds the Statutory Text and This Court’s Precedent Governing Exemption 2.**

Exemption 2 shields only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The phrase “personnel rules and practices” refers to “those rules and practices that relate to an agency’s internal personnel matters—its pay, leave, pensions, hiring, and firing.” Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 368 (1976). The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” test that reaches any operational document whose disclosure might risk circumvention of agency regulation therefore contravenes both the statutory language and Rose.

Rose expressly rejected the notion that Exemption 2 could be read to cover “any ‘internal’ information the agency deems useful in the conduct of its affairs.” Id. at 369. The Court limited the exemption to “routine matters” of “merely internal significance” and to materials whose disclosure would risk circumvention only when they constitute “internal personnel rules and practices.” Id. at 369-70. The ESQD arc maps and related documents at issue here are technical planning tools used to site ammunition storage facilities; they do not address hiring, firing, pay, leave, or any other personnel matter. See id. at 368.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), to expand the exemption is misplaced. Crooker itself acknowledged that its “predominantly internal” gloss was necessary only because the words “related” and “solely” appeared to conflict. Id. at 1056-57. That judicial rewriting cannot override the plain statutory requirement that the material concern “personnel rules and practices.” See Rose, 425 U.S. at 361 (exemptions “are to be interpreted narrowly”).

**II. The Documents Are Not “Law Enforcement Material” Whose Disclosure Would Risk Circumvention of Agency Regulation.**

Even under the narrower approach previously applied in this Circuit, Exemption 2 reaches only “law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980). The ESQD documents were compiled for facility design and long-term safety planning, not for any adjudicative or enforcement purpose. They therefore fall outside the category recognized in Hardy.

Because the documents are neither personnel rules nor practices nor law-enforcement materials, they are not exempt under Exemption 2. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
