## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to The Habitat Company on David Burnett's claims under the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). The court's conclusion that Burnett failed to provide his employer with notice of his medical condition is not supported by the record.

### I. Burnett Provided Sufficient Notice Under the FMLA

The FMLA requires employees to provide their employers with notice of a qualifying reason for leave "as soon as practicable." 29 C.F.R. § 825.303(a). The notice need not be explicit, and the employee need only provide "sufficient information for the employer to reasonably determine whether the FMLA may apply to the leave request." Id. § 825.303(b). In Byrne v. Avon Products, Inc., 328 F.3d 379 (7th Cir. 2003), this Court held that an employee's statement that she was experiencing "stress" and "depression" was sufficient to put her employer on notice of a potential FMLA-qualifying condition. Id. at 381-82.

Here, Burnett informed his supervisor, Sergio Polo, that he was experiencing a "weak bladder" and was going to see a doctor to determine the cause. He later presented Polo with a doctor's order for blood testing and explained that he had been diagnosed with high PSA and cholesterol levels. Burnett also compared his condition to that of his brother-in-law, who had prostate cancer. These statements, taken together, provided sufficient information for Habitat to reasonably determine that Burnett's condition may be FMLA-qualifying. See id.; see also Stevenson v. Hyre Elec. Co., 505 F.3d 720, 725 (7th Cir. 2007) (holding that an employee's statement that he was experiencing "back problems" was sufficient to put his employer on notice of a potential FMLA-qualifying condition).

### II. Burnett Provided Sufficient Notice Under the ADA

The ADA requires employees to provide their employers with notice of a disability or need for accommodation. See 42 U.S.C. § 12112(b)(5)(A). The notice need not be formal or explicit, and the employee need only provide "enough information... as to permit the employer to understand that [the request] is based on [the employee's] medical condition." EEOC v. Sears, Roebuck & Co., 417 F.3d 789, 803 (7th Cir. 2005) (quoting Bultemeyer v. Fort Wayne Cmty. Sch., 100 F.3d 1281, 1285 (7th Cir. 1996)).

Burnett's statements to Polo regarding his "weak bladder" and subsequent diagnoses, including high PSA levels, provided sufficient notice to Habitat that he may be disabled or in need of accommodation. See id. at 803-04 (holding that an employee's request for accommodation based on his "medical condition" was sufficient to put his employer on notice of a potential disability). Furthermore, Burnett's provision of a document describing the prostate ultrasound and biopsy procedures to his supervisor, Mitch Hehr, on January 7, 2004, further supports the conclusion that Habitat was on notice of Burnett's medical condition.

### III. Habitat's Adverse Actions Against Burnett Were Motivated by His Requests for Leave and Accommodation

The record shows that Habitat took a series of adverse actions against Burnett, including issuing reprimands and suspending him without pay, shortly after he requested leave and accommodation. In light of the suspicious timing of these actions and the disputed facts regarding Burnett's understanding of Polo's conduct on January 14, 2004, a reasonable jury could infer that Habitat's actions were motivated by Burnett's requests for leave and accommodation. See, e.g., King v. Preferred Technical Grp., 166 F.3d 887, 893 (7th Cir. 1999) (holding that suspicious timing can be evidence of retaliatory motive).

The district court's grant of summary judgment to The Habitat Company was therefore improper, and this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.
