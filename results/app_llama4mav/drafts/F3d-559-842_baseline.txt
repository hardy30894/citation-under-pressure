## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Rand Corporation because genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether Rand failed to comply with the Truth in Lending Act (TILA) and the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act (HOEPA), thereby extending the Mouas' rescission period to three years.

### I. Rand Failed to Make Material Disclosures Required by TILA and HOEPA

TILA requires lenders to make certain material disclosures to borrowers, including the annual percentage rate, finance charge, and total payments. 15 U.S.C. § 1638(a). HOEPA, which amends TILA, imposes additional disclosure requirements for high-cost mortgages, including a disclosure that must be provided to the borrower at least three business days before the consummation of the transaction. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b). The Mouas contend that Rand failed to provide the required three-day advance HOEPA disclosure. Rand argues that the Mouas waived this disclosure period due to a bona fide personal financial emergency. However, the validity of this waiver is disputed, as the Mouas claim they did not understand the statement they were asked to copy.

The Ninth Circuit has held that a borrower's waiver of the three-day advance disclosure period is only valid if it is made in writing and is based on a bona fide personal financial emergency. Semar v. Platte Valley Fed. Sav. & Loan Ass'n, 791 F.2d 699, 701-02 (9th Cir. 1986). Here, the Mouas' waiver is suspect because they claim they did not understand the statement they were asked to copy, raising a genuine issue of material fact regarding the validity of the waiver. See id. at 702 (holding that a waiver is not valid if it is not "knowing and intelligent"). The district court erred in granting summary judgment on this issue, as the Mouas have raised a triable issue regarding whether Rand complied with TILA and HOEPA's disclosure requirements.

### II. Rand Failed to Provide Clear and Conspicuous Notice of TILA's Three-Day Post-Transaction Right to Cancel

TILA requires lenders to provide borrowers with clear and conspicuous notice of their right to rescind a transaction within three days. 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a). The Mouas contend that Rand failed to provide such notice, as they signed the Confirmation section of the Notice on April 22, 2005, before the three-day rescission period had expired. Rand argues that the Mouas were directed to sign the Confirmation section by Excel Title, without authorization or direction from Rand. However, this does not absolve Rand of its responsibility to ensure that the Mouas received proper notice.

The Seventh Circuit has held that a lender's failure to provide clear and conspicuous notice of the right to rescind extends the rescission period to three years. Handy v. Anchor Mortgage Corp., 464 F.3d 760, 764 (7th Cir. 2006). Here, the Mouas have raised a genuine issue of material fact regarding whether Rand provided clear and conspicuous notice of their right to rescind. The district court erred in granting summary judgment on this issue.

### III. Rand Included a Prepayment Penalty Expressly Prohibited by HOEPA

HOEPA prohibits lenders from including prepayment penalties in high-cost mortgages. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(c). The Mouas contend that Rand included such a penalty in their loan agreement. Rand does not dispute this fact. Instead, it argues that the prepayment penalty was not a material disclosure required by TILA. However, the inclusion of a prohibited prepayment penalty is a material violation of HOEPA, which extends the rescission period to three years.

The Eleventh Circuit has held that a lender's inclusion of a prohibited prepayment penalty in a high-cost mortgage is a material violation of HOEPA, which extends the rescission period to three years. Williams v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc., 504 F. Supp. 2d 176, 187-88 (S.D. Ala. 2007), aff'd, 269 F. App'x 879 (11th Cir. 2008). Here, Rand's inclusion of a prepayment penalty in the Mouas' loan agreement is a material violation of HOEPA, which extends the rescission period to three years.

## Conclusion

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Rand Corporation because genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether Rand complied with TILA and HOEPA. The Mouas have raised triable issues regarding Rand's failure to make material disclosures, provide clear and conspicuous notice of the right to rescind, and inclusion of a prohibited prepayment penalty. Therefore, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.
