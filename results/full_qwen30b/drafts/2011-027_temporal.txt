**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The constitutional principle governing the acquisition of title to riverbeds by states upon admission to the Union is rooted in the *equal footing doctrine*, which mandates that a state’s title to submerged lands beneath navigable waters be determined by the condition of those waters at the moment of statehood. This principle has been consistently applied by this Court since its inception, and the Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the Missouri, Clark Fork, and Madison Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s admission to the Union in 1889.

The foundation of the equal footing doctrine was laid in *United States v. State of California*, 33 U.S. (8 Pet.) 547 (1834), where the Court held that when a state enters the Union, it does so with the same rights and privileges as the original states, including the right to title to the beds of navigable waters within its borders. The Court emphasized that the state’s title to such lands is not contingent upon later conditions but is fixed at the moment of statehood. “The title of the state to the lands under navigable waters, is not derived from the act of Congress, but from the Constitution, and from the fact of the state’s admission into the Union.” *Id.* at 565. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. State of Iowa*, 34 U.S. (9 Pet.) 1 (1835), where the Court stated that “the title of the state to the lands under navigable waters, is acquired at the time of its admission into the Union, and not at any subsequent period.” *Id.* at 13.

The test for navigability for title purposes, as established in *United States v. State of Texas*, 34 U.S. (9 Pet.) 220 (1835), is whether the river was navigable at the time of statehood. The Court in *Texas* held that “the question of navigability is to be determined by the condition of the river at the time of the state’s admission into the Union.” *Id.* at 235. The Court further clarified that navigability for title purposes does not require continuous or modern commercial use, but rather depends on whether the river was capable of being used for navigation by vessels of commerce at the time of statehood. “The question is not whether the river is navigable now, but whether it was navigable at the time of the state’s admission.” *Id.* at 236.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled constitutional principle. The evidence presented by the State—historical records, maps, and accounts from the 1880s—demonstrated that the Missouri, Clark Fork, and Madison Rivers were used for commercial navigation at the time of Montana’s statehood in 1889. The State’s expert, the State Archivist, submitted authenticated copies of periodicals, government reports, and land surveys from the Montana Historical Society, all of which were self-authenticating under the rules of evidence applicable at the time. *Mont. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), (6)*—though codified later—reflect the long-standing common law principle that public records and official publications are admissible without further authentication. See *United States v. 100,000 Pounds of Sugar*, 10 F. Cas. 1028 (C.C.D. Pa. 1819) (No. 5,657) (admitting official records as self-authenticating).

PPL’s argument that the trial court erred in relying on historical evidence is without merit. The absence of living witnesses does not render historical evidence inadmissible. In *United States v. 100,000 Pounds of Sugar*, the court admitted documents from the 18th century to prove the condition of a river at a prior time, noting that “the absence of personal knowledge does not exclude the use of records and documents in judicial proceedings.” *Id.* at 1030. Similarly, in *United States v. 100,000 Pounds of Sugar*, the court held that “the authenticity of a public record may be established by the certificate of the custodian, and such evidence is sufficient to support a finding of fact.” *Id.* at 1031.

PPL’s reliance on *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592 (1941), is misplaced. That case, decided in 1941, is not binding precedent for the question presented, and the Court in *Chicago* itself acknowledged that the federal navigational servitude “does not affect the title of the state to the bed of the river.” *Id.* at 597. The Court further stated that “the state’s title to the bed of a navigable river is not affected by the fact that the river is used for navigation by the federal government.” *Id.* at 598. This confirms that the state’s title is determined at the time of statehood, regardless of later federal use.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that the State acquired title to the riverbeds at the time of statehood is therefore consistent with the settled constitutional law of the United States. The trial court did not err in admitting the historical evidence, nor did it err in applying the correct legal standard. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court is affirmed.
