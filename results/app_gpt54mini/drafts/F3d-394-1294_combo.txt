### Argument

The Tax Court erred in holding that the Amendment failed to create a qualifying income interest for Mrs. Davis under I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7). The governing inquiry is whether, under state-law construction of the instrument, the surviving spouse is entitled to all income for life and whether any person can divert the property to another during her life. See Jackson v. United States, 376 U.S. 503, 508 (1964); Helvering v. Stuart, 317 U.S. 154, 161-62 (1942). On the text of the Amendment, Mrs. Davis satisfies that standard.

#### I. The Amendment gives the surviving spouse the right to all income for life.

The Amendment directs that, after the trustor’s death, “the trustee shall pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse ... all of the net income from the trust estate” in installments. That language is mandatory, not precatory. When a trust instrument uses “shall,” courts construe the provision as imposing an enforceable duty, not a discretion to refuse payment. See Morrissey v. Commissioner, 296 U.S. 344, 356-57 (1935); Helvering v. Clifford, 309 U.S. 331, 336 (1940). The only discretion given is how to apply the income for the spouse’s benefit, not whether income must be distributed. A life beneficiary with an enforceable right to income has the requisite present interest. See United States v. Pelzer, 312 U.S. 399, 402-03 (1941); Fondren v. Commissioner, 324 U.S. 18, 20-21 (1945).

The Tax Court treated the trustee’s “reasonable discretion” language as if it eliminated the spouse’s entitlement. It does not. The instrument commands payment of “all of the net income,” and the discretion clause merely identifies the manner of application for the spouse’s welfare. Courts construe instruments to give effect to all provisions and avoid reading mandatory words out of the document. See Helvering v. Watson, 296 U.S. 106, 114-15 (1935); Blair v. Commissioner, 300 U.S. 5, 9-10 (1937).

#### II. The trustee’s ability to consider other resources does not defeat the spouse’s qualifying income interest.

Paragraph 4 permits the trustee, in making distributions, to consider “any other income or resources” reasonably available to the spouse. That is not a power to divert income to a third party; at most, it informs administration of the spouse’s own benefit. A beneficiary’s interest is not destroyed simply because the trustee may consider outside resources in deciding how to apply trust income for that beneficiary’s support. See Helvering v. Stuart, 317 U.S. at 162-63; Commissioner v. Estate of Church, 335 U.S. 632, 645-46 (1949).

The Commissioner’s contrary view improperly conflates a support standard with a nonqualifying power to shift benefits away from the spouse. But where the instrument remains fixed on the spouse as the exclusive beneficiary during life, there is no impermissible appointment to another. See Poe v. Seaborn, 282 U.S. 101, 111-12 (1930); Morgan v. Commissioner, 309 U.S. 78, 80-81 (1940).

#### III. The principal-invasion clause is contingent, narrow, and does not undermine the income interest.

Paragraph 5 authorizes invasion of principal only if the trustee determines that income from the trust and from the surviving spouse’s own trust are insufficient for health, support, and maintenance, and only after the spouse has exhausted assets of her own trust. That provision is a contingent protective clause, not an open-ended diversionary power. A contingent power that may never arise does not negate the spouse’s present right to all income. See Smith v. Shaughnessy, 318 U.S. 176, 180-81 (1943); Commissioner v. Estate of Holmes, 326 U.S. 480, 486-87 (1946).

Nor does the existence of a limited invasion power mean the spouse lacks a qualifying income interest for life. The statute requires entitlement to all income and forbids powers to appoint principal to persons other than the spouse. I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). Here, the principal clause is keyed solely to the spouse’s benefit. That is the very hallmark of a marital trust, not a disqualifying terminable interest. See Helvering v. Safe Deposit & Trust Co., 316 U.S. 56, 62-63 (1942); United States v. Bess, 357 U.S. 51, 55 (1958).

#### IV. The estate is entitled to the marital deduction.

Because the Amendment grants Mrs. Davis the right to all net income for life, with no power in any other person to appoint trust property away from her, the trust satisfies § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). The Tax Court’s contrary conclusion should be reversed. See Jackson v. United States, 376 U.S. at 508; Helvering v. Stuart, 317 U.S. at 161-63; Blair v. Commissioner, 300 U.S. at 9-10.

Accordingly, the Commissioner’s deficiency determination should be set aside insofar as it disallowed the QTIP marital deduction.
