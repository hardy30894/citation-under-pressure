**Argument**

Title VII does not require a plaintiff seeking punitive damages to prove that the employer’s conduct was independently “egregious” or “outrageous” apart from the state of mind Congress specified in 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). The statutory text supplies the standard: punitive damages are available when the employer acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That language focuses on the employer’s mental state, not on an additional judicially created requirement of especially outrageous conduct.

This Court has repeatedly instructed that when Congress defines a remedy by text, courts must apply that text rather than superimpose extra elements. See Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994). Nothing in § 1981a(b)(1) contains the word “egregious,” and nothing in the statute suggests that punitive damages are reserved only for conduct more blameworthy than the statute’s own terms describe. Congress knew how to limit remedies, and it did so expressly by capping compensatory and punitive awards according to employer size. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3).

The proper inquiry is therefore whether the employer acted with the requisite culpable mental state. Title VII liability itself turns on intentional discrimination, but punitive damages require more: “malice” or “reckless indifference” to federally protected rights. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). In St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993), the Court explained that once a plaintiff proves a prima facie case and discredits the employer’s stated reasons, “no additional proof of discrimination is required” to permit an inference of unlawful discrimination. But punitive damages are governed by a different statute and a different showing; the question is not whether the facts are colorful or outrageous, but whether the employer knowingly or recklessly disregarded the federal rights at issue.

ADA’s position would improperly rewrite § 1981a(b)(1) to add a separate “egregiousness” hurdle. Courts do not add words to a statute under the guise of interpretation. The statute’s operative terms already cabin punitive damages to serious cases: “malice” and “reckless indifference” are demanding standards, and they require proof that the employer at least consciously disregarded the risk that its conduct violated federal law. See 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). A freestanding requirement that the underlying conduct also be “outrageous” would be redundant and would narrow the statutory remedy beyond Congress’s chosen language.

The district court’s denial of punitive damages was therefore erroneous to the extent it rested on the view that Kolstad had to prove something beyond intentional sex discrimination plus the statutorily required state of mind. The jury here could find not only intentional discrimination, but preselection, shifting explanations, and disregard of Kolstad’s candidacy. Under McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802-05 (1973), and Hicks, 509 U.S. at 511, such proof supports a finding that the employer’s stated reasons were pretextual and that discrimination was intentional. If the evidence also supports a finding that the decisionmakers knew or recklessly disregarded the federally protected nature of Kolstad’s rights, punitive damages may be considered under § 1981a(b)(1). The court of appeals’ insistence on an independent showing of “egregious” conduct is inconsistent with the statute.

ADA’s reliance on legislative-history snippets cannot overcome the enacted text. The controlling rule is the statutory language Congress passed, not isolated floor statements. See Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 262-63. Section 1981a(b)(1) is the best evidence of congressional intent, and it permits punitive damages upon the required showing of malice or reckless indifference—nothing more.

Because the court below required an extra-statutory showing of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct, its punitive-damages ruling should be reversed and the case remanded for application of the correct standard under 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1).
