**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, recognizing that questions of criminal responsibility implicate policy judgments legislatures are entitled to make, and cautioning against judicial correction of what the Court described as the "serious defects of present-day American criminal justice" only through constitutional adjudication rather than through the ordinary legislative process. *Id.* at 800–01. If a State may constitutionally place the entire burden of proving insanity on the defendant, it follows a fortiori that a State may also determine the channel through which mental-disease evidence enters the case.

This principle was reinforced in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), where the plurality declined to constitutionalize any single doctrine of criminal responsibility, emphasizing that the desirability of a State's approach to a defense "should be irrelevant in a court charged with the duty of interpretation rather than legislation." *Id.* at 536. Arizona's rule confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry is precisely the sort of substantive line-drawing that *Leland* and *Powell* leave to the States.

This channeling approach is also consistent with history. The insanity defense as historically understood, from *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a mechanism for negating the prosecution's proof of intent on a fact-by-fact basis. Arizona's rule simply preserves that traditional separation.

Moreover, this Court has already sustained the exclusion of similar categories of psychiatric evidence offered to negate intent. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the Court upheld a trial court's refusal to instruct the jury that evidence of the defendant's mental deficiency could reduce murder to manslaughter by negating premeditation. The Court noted the unsettled "status of the defense of partial responsibility in the District" and declined to require its adoption as a matter of federal constitutional law. *Id.* at 475–76. If the exclusion of mental-deficiency evidence offered to negate premeditation was constitutional in *Fisher*, Arizona's more modest rule—which still permits the evidence to be considered on insanity—is a fortiori permissible.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Leland* and *Powell*, Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules; it requires only that such rules not offend "some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). And States remain free to structure their own legislative and judicial processes so long as fundamental fairness is preserved. *See Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908) (recognizing that the States retain broad authority over their own legislative and judicial acts without wholesale subordination to federal constitutional oversight, *id.* at 110–11, and that individuals within a State are "entitled to the exercise and enjoyment" of their state-created legal rights and remedies as local circumstances allow, *id.* at 111).

This Court applied these principles directly to a state evidentiary rule limiting the use of certain evidence in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), where it upheld Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence despite the defendant's claim that the rule improperly affected the jury's assessment of guilt, holding that state rules of evidence are entitled to deference absent a showing that the rule renders the trial fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 563–64. Arizona's limitation on the use of mental-disease evidence is analogous: it does not exclude the evidence altogether, but merely directs the jury to the historically established channel—the insanity defense—for its consideration.

The touchstone under *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), is whether "the absence of that fairness fatally infected the" proceeding. *Id.* at 236. Here, the trial court permitted Clark to introduce the full range of expert psychiatric testimony and to have it evaluated by the factfinder on the issue of insanity—the very evidence he now claims should also negate intent. Nothing in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), which addressed the burden of proof on insanity under federal law, suggests that the Constitution requires evidence relevant to one defense to be simultaneously admissible for an entirely separate purpose.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove. That is precisely the kind of rule the above authorities sanction. Arizona's channeling rule does not offend any principle "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people," *Snyder*, 291 U.S. at 105, and this Court should affirm.
