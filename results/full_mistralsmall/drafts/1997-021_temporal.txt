**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling employees is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll employees only if it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987), is an impermissible construction of the Act. The Board’s authority to regulate polling is not explicit in the statute, and its restrictive standard frustrates the Act’s core policy of encouraging collective bargaining. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 42-43 (1937) (recognizing that the Act was designed to promote industrial peace through collective bargaining). The Board’s approach is arbitrary because it permits employers to withdraw recognition or seek an RM election under the same standard, rendering polling superfluous. *NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Tel. Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 342 (1938) (noting that the Act protects the right to bargain collectively but does not compel it where majority support is lacking).

The Board’s insistence that polling is only permissible when an employer already doubts the union’s majority status ignores the very purpose of polling: to *ascertain* whether such doubt is justified. *NLRB v. Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. 469, 477-78 (1941) (recognizing that the Act contemplates mechanisms for testing employee support). The Board’s rule thus turns the Act’s purpose on its head by discouraging employers from using polling to avoid unfair labor practices. *NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 309 U.S. 206, 226 (1940) (holding that the Board’s remedial authority must be exercised in a manner consistent with the Act’s policies).

**II. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence.**

The Board erred in discounting the statements of employees who indicated a lack of union support. The Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) improperly excluded the views of employees hired after January 25, 1991, despite their relevance to the company’s reasonable doubt. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) (holding that the Board must consider all probative evidence). The ALJ also disregarded statements made during job interviews, such as those of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh, despite their indicative value. *NLRB v. Bradford Dyeing Ass’n*, 310 U.S. 318, 325-26 (1940) (recognizing that employee statements, even if made in informal contexts, may be probative).

The Board’s reliance on the presumption of continuing majority support was misplaced. The presumption is rebuttable, and the Board failed to account for the weakened factual underpinnings of the presumption in a newly formed bargaining unit. *NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 303 U.S. 22, 27 (1938) (holding that the presumption is not absolute). The Board’s conclusion that only seven employees repudiated the union was arbitrary, as it ignored the collective weight of employee statements and the poll results. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951) (requiring the Board to consider the whole record and not isolate specific evidence).

**Conclusion**

The Board’s polling standard is irrational and inconsistent with the Act, and its factual findings are unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should reverse the Board’s order and hold that Allentown Mack acted lawfully in polling its employees and refusing to recognize the union.
