**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a decision by the Railroad Retirement Board denying a request to reopen a prior benefits claim constitutes a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)—is one of first impression before this Court. The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to review the Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application rests on a rigid, outdated interpretation of the Railroad Retirement Act that conflicts with the statutory text, the Supreme Court’s precedent on finality, and the fundamental principles of administrative law. This Court should reverse.

The Fifth Circuit held that it lacked jurisdiction to review the Board’s decision not to reopen Salinas’s 2006 claim because of *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), which itself relied on a strained reading of *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977). But *Califano*—which held that a decision not to reopen a claim is not a “final decision” under the Social Security Act—was not a decision on the merits of a claim, nor did it involve a statutory scheme with a clear, express grant of review for such decisions. The Railroad Retirement Act, by contrast, contains a specific and expansive grant of jurisdiction to the courts of appeals: “The courts of appeals shall have jurisdiction to review final decisions of the Board.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). This language is not limited to decisions on the merits of a claim; it applies to “final decisions” of the Board, regardless of their nature.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a decision is “final” for purposes of judicial review if it “conclude[s] the matter at hand” and “mark[s] the end of the administrative process.” *Burlington Northern R.R. Co. v. United States*, 405 U.S. 105, 108 (1972). The Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application satisfies this test. The decision definitively resolved the issue of whether the Board would reconsider a prior, final determination of non-eligibility. It was not a procedural step or an interlocutory order; it was a conclusive determination that no further administrative action would be taken. As the Board itself acknowledged, the decision “closed the door” on Salinas’s request to reopen. See 20 C.F.R. § 261.2 (establishing a four-year limit on reopening based on new and material evidence or administrative error). Once that limit expired, the Board’s refusal to reopen was not merely a procedural denial—it was a final, binding decision.

Moreover, the Board’s decision directly affects Salinas’s rights. By refusing to reopen the 2006 claim, the Board effectively precluded Salinas from seeking benefits that may have begun earlier than the date the Board ultimately granted his 2013 application. The Board’s decision thus has a direct and substantial impact on the amount and timing of benefits he receives. As this Court has held, “a decision that has a direct and substantial impact on a party’s rights is final for purposes of judicial review.” *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 149 (1967). The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule—under which a decision that extinguishes a claimant’s right to benefits is not reviewable—creates a jurisdictional gap that allows the Board to permanently bar claimants from relief without any meaningful judicial oversight.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Roberts* is misplaced. *Roberts* did not interpret the text of § 355(f) but instead relied on a circuit split and the reasoning of other circuits that had adopted a narrow view of finality. But the D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), correctly rejected that narrow view. *Stovic* held that a decision not to reopen a prior claim is reviewable because it “concludes the administrative process” and “determines the rights of the parties.” Id. at 504. The D.C. Circuit emphasized that the Railroad Retirement Act’s grant of jurisdiction is “broad and unqualified,” and that the Board’s refusal to reopen a claim “is not a mere procedural matter but a substantive determination with real consequences.” Id. at 505. This reasoning is consistent with the text and purpose of § 355(f), and it should be adopted by this Court.

Finally, the Fifth Circuit’s refusal to reconsider *Roberts* under the doctrine of *orderliness* is not a bar to this Court’s review. The rule of orderliness prevents panels from overruling circuit precedent, but it does not bind this Court. When a circuit court’s precedent conflicts with the text of a federal statute and Supreme Court precedent, this Court has a duty to correct the error. *See, e.g., United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537, 543 (1982) (“[W]e have the power and the duty to overrule our own precedent when it is inconsistent with the Constitution or a federal statute.”). The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of § 355(f) is inconsistent with the statute’s plain language and the Supreme Court’s finality doctrine. This Court should grant certiorari and hold that a decision by the Railroad Retirement Board denying a request to reopen a prior benefits claim is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f).
