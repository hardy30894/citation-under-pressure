**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit held that due process requires a "higher level of mental functioning" before a defendant may waive counsel or plead guilty than the level required to stand trial. That holding cannot be reconciled with this Court's competency and waiver decisions, which draw a clear line between the capacity to understand and participate in proceedings, on the one hand, and the voluntariness of a decision to relinquish rights, on the other. The Fourteenth Amendment requires the former in every context; it has never required the heightened "reasoned choice" standard the court below imposed.

**I. Due Process Requires Only the Competency Standard of *Dusky v. United States*, Which Applies Uniformly Throughout Criminal Proceedings.**

This Court defined the constitutional floor of competency in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), holding that a defendant is competent if he has "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. That formulation was not limited to the capacity to stand trial; it announced the constitutional measure of competency itself. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), confirmed as much, holding that due process forbids the conviction of a defendant who lacks the capacity described in *Dusky*, and framing the inquiry as whether the defendant possesses the ability to understand and participate in the proceedings against him. *Id.* at 385.

Nothing about that inquiry changes when a defendant elects to plead guilty or represent himself rather than proceed to trial with counsel. The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard rests on the assumption that the *decision* to waive rights demands more rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But competency doctrine has never measured the wisdom of a defendant's choices; it measures only his capacity to understand and participate. As this Court explained in *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), competency protects a defendant from being subjected to proceedings he cannot comprehend—it does not authorize courts to second-guess the soundness of decisions a competent defendant chooses to make. *Id.* at 108. A rule that requires greater capacity to waive rights than to exercise them at trial has no grounding in this Court's precedent and undermines the very autonomy that the rights to self-representation and to plead guilty are meant to secure.

**II. This Court's Waiver Decisions Confirm That the Relevant Inquiry Is Voluntariness and Knowledge, Not a Separate, Elevated Capacity Threshold.**

The decisions governing waiver of counsel and guilty pleas ask whether the waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent—a question distinct from, and independent of, the *Dusky* capacity inquiry. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court described waiver of counsel as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, and instructed courts to "indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver," *id.* That standard examines the defendant's knowledge and intent, not some heightened mental capacity. *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required that waiver of counsel be made "with eyes open," *id.* at 724, so that the defendant understands the risks of proceeding alone—again a question of knowledge, not of superior rationality.

*Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), confirms that the touchstone is whether the record shows an informed choice: the absence of counsel at arraignment does not invalidate a plea where the record shows it was "entered understandingly." *Id.* at 174–75. *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), recognized that "the Constitution does not force a lawyer upon a defendant," *id.* at 279, because a defendant may competently choose to conduct his own defense; the Court treated that choice as a matter of intelligent waiver, not as proof that self-representation demands a higher order of mental functioning than standing trial. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), similarly required that the record affirmatively show a guilty plea was voluntary and intelligent, because the plea "involves the waiver of several constitutional rights," *id.* at 243—but *Boykin* nowhere suggested that a defendant must clear a threshold of rationality higher than the one that permits him to stand trial.

These decisions demonstrate that this Court has always treated the capacity question and the voluntariness question as separate inquiries. The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard erases that distinction, importing into the capacity inquiry a demand for rationality that belongs, if anywhere, to the voluntariness inquiry already addressed by *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, and *Carter*.

**III. *Westbrook v. Arizona* Does Not Support a Heightened Competency Standard.**

The Ninth Circuit's rule cannot be traced to *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam). *Westbrook* remanded because the record did not show that the trial court had determined "whether petitioner had the capacity to waive his constitutional right to the assistance of counsel," *id.* at 150—but the Court articulated no standard distinct from the one governing competency generally. Nothing in *Westbrook* demands a quantum of rationality beyond the "reasonable degree of rational understanding" required by *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402. *Westbrook* stands only for the proposition that a court must determine, on an adequate record, whether a valid waiver occurred—a voluntariness question already answered by *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, and *Carter*, not a novel mental-capacity threshold.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard governing a defendant's ability to stand trial, articulated in *Dusky* and *Pate*, likewise governs his ability to plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional inquiries required by *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, *Boykin*, and *Westbrook* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
