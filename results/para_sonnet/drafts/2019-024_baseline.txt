# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF § 1113(2) REQUIRES ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE, NOT CONSTRUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE OR MERE ACCESS TO INFORMATION

Section 1113(2) bars suit "three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This Court has repeatedly instructed that statutory interpretation begins with text, and where Congress uses a specific term, courts must give that term its ordinary meaning. See *BedRoc Ltd. v. United States*, 541 U.S. 176, 183 (2004) ("The preeminent canon of statutory interpretation requires us to 'presume that [the] legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there.'").

"Actual knowledge" has a settled meaning: it requires that a plaintiff in fact be aware of something, as distinguished from constructive knowledge, which asks what a reasonable person should have known. This distinction is not merely semantic—it is the precise distinction Congress itself drew and then abandoned. The original 1974 version of ERISA contained *both* an actual-knowledge provision and a constructive-knowledge provision, the latter triggered by the filing of a report "from which [the plaintiff] could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge of such breach." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(a)(2)(B) (1976). Congress repealed the constructive-knowledge provision in 1987. Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330, 1330-46 (1987).

That repeal is decisive. Under the settled canon that "Congress acts intentionally when it uses particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another," *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983), Congress's deliberate elimination of the constructive-knowledge trigger while retaining "actual knowledge" confirms that mere availability of information—information a plaintiff could have discovered through reasonable diligence—cannot satisfy § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit's rule, which permits summary judgment based on Intel's *disclosure* of information regardless of whether Sulyma actually digested it, effectively resurrects the repealed constructive-knowledge standard under a different name. That result contravenes the "cardinal rule that a statute is to be read as a whole, since the meaning of statutory language, plain or not, depends on context." *King v. St. Vincent's Hosp.*, 502 U.S. 215, 221 (1991).

## II. RESPONDENT'S RULE CONFLATING DISCLOSURE WITH KNOWLEDGE CANNOT BE RECONCILED WITH ERISA'S TEXT OR STRUCTURE

Respondent, echoing the Sixth and Ninth Circuits' more permissive approaches, urges a rule under which disclosure of a document containing relevant information is functionally equivalent to actual knowledge. But this Court has cautioned against construing statutes in ways that render statutory language "insignificant, if not wholly superfluous." *TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 31 (2001). If disclosure alone satisfied § 1113(2), the word "actual" would do no work at all—it would collapse into a rule of constructive notice, precisely the regime Congress eliminated in 1987.

This Court's decision in *TRW Inc. v. Andrews* is instructive by contrast. There, the Court considered the discovery rule under the Fair Credit Reporting Act and emphasized that Congress's use of a "discovery of the violation" trigger differed meaningfully from other statutes using constructive-knowledge language. 534 U.S. at 27. The Court's care to distinguish between what a plaintiff *actually* discovered and what she reasonably *could have* discovered supports the same distinction here: § 1113(2)'s "actual knowledge" cannot be satisfied by imputed or constructive awareness arising from documents a plan participant received but never read or does not recall reading.

Moreover, ERISA's broader statutory structure confirms that Congress calibrated different limitations triggers with precision. Section 1113(1) already provides a six-year outer limit running from "the date of the last action which constituted a part of the breach or violation," 29 U.S.C. § 1113(1)—a period that does not depend on the plaintiff's knowledge at all. The three-year provision in § 1113(2) is not a mechanism for accelerating that period based on constructive receipt of paperwork; it is a plaintiff-protective shortening of the limitations period only where the plaintiff was *actually* aware of the fiduciary breach. Respondent's rule inverts this design, penalizing plan participants for fiduciaries' own disclosures regardless of whether those disclosures achieved their purpose of communicating information.

## III. THE RECORD BELOW DEMONSTRATES THAT MERE ACCESS TO PLAN DOCUMENTS, WITHOUT MORE, CANNOT ESTABLISH ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE AS A MATTER OF LAW

The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized, applying this textual analysis, that Sulyma's claims survived summary judgment because he testified he was "unaware that the monies that he had invested through the Intel retirement plans had been invested in hedge funds or private equity" and did "not recall seeing any documents" alerting him to that fact. Pet. App. at [cite]. Petitioner respectfully submits this conclusion follows ineluctably from the text of § 1113(2): a fiduciary cannot manufacture "actual knowledge" simply by placing information on a website or mailing a disclosure that the participant never opens or cannot recall reviewing.

This does not leave fiduciaries without protection. Circumstantial evidence—including the nature of disclosures, the plaintiff's role, and any admissions—remains available to prove actual knowledge, just as it was in *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), where the trustees were parties to the very transaction they later challenged. What the text does not permit is a *presumption* of actual knowledge from disclosure alone, divorced from any inquiry into the plaintiff's subjective awareness.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Ninth Circuit and hold that § 1113(2)'s three-year limitations period requires actual, subjective awareness of the facts constituting the breach—not merely the opportunity to acquire such awareness through undigested disclosures.
