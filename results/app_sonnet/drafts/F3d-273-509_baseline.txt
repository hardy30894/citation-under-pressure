# ARGUMENT

## I. THE RELEASE DOES NOT BAR BANCORP'S CLAIMS BECAUSE ITS PLAIN LANGUAGE IS LIMITED TO CLAIMS ARISING FROM THE DECISION TO CLOSE THE BANK

Under well-settled principles of contract interpretation, a release "is to be interpreted according to standard contract principles," and courts must give effect to the plain meaning of the language the parties chose. *Bank of Am. Nat'l Trust & Sav. Ass'n v. Gillaizeau*, 766 F.2d 709, 715 (2d Cir. 1985). Where the language of a release is unambiguous, "the intent of the parties must be ascertained from the four corners of the instrument." *W.W.W. Assocs. v. Giancontieri*, 77 N.Y.2d 157, 162, 565 N.Y.S.2d 440, 566 N.E.2d 639 (1990).

The release Bancorp executed on June 6, 1988 released the FDIC "from any and all claims which Bancorp . . . has or may have arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985." By its plain terms, this release is confined to claims challenging the *decision to close* the Bank—precisely the type of claim Bancorp had already litigated unsuccessfully in *Golden Pac. Bancorp v. Clarke*, 837 F.2d 509 (D.C. Cir. 1988), and *Golden Pac. Bancorp v. United States*, 15 F.3d 1066 (Fed. Cir. 1994).

Bancorp's claims here are categorically different. They challenge the FDIC's post-closure administration of the receivership: (1) the FDIC's decision to pay itself post-insolvency interest beginning in 1991; (2) the FDIC's breach of fiduciary duty in selling Bank assets below true value; (3) the FDIC's waste of estate assets by charging its own legal and office expenses to the receivership; and (4) the FDIC's failure to provide an adequate accounting. None of these claims concerns the *decision to close* the Bank—they concern how the FDIC conducted itself as receiver *afterward*.

New York courts have consistently held that releases must be construed narrowly according to their specific terms, particularly where—as here—the release recites a specific, limited purpose. *See Cahill v. Regan*, 5 N.Y.2d 292, 299, 184 N.Y.S.2d 348, 157 N.E.2d 505 (1959) ("a release may not be read to cover matters which the parties did not intend to cover"). The release's specific reference to "the decision . . . to close the Bank" signals the parties' intent to resolve only that discrete controversy, not to grant the FDIC blanket immunity for all subsequent receivership conduct. Had the parties intended such sweeping release language, they could easily have used broader terms—as the FDIC itself has done in other releases—covering claims "arising from the receivership" generally, rather than specifically limiting the release to the closure decision.

The district court's contrary reading impermissibly expands the release beyond its negotiated scope. This Court should reverse.

## II. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS DID NOT BEGIN TO RUN UNTIL THE RECEIVERSHIP TERMINATED ON NOVEMBER 1, 1995

New York's six-year statute of limitations for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste, N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7), did not begin to run until the FDIC terminated the receivership on November 1, 1995. Bancorp filed suit on October 31, 1995—one day before termination—rendering its claims timely.

A cause of action accrues, for statute of limitations purposes, when the plaintiff possesses a legally cognizable claim that is complete and enforceable. *See Cubito v. Kreisberg*, 69 A.D.2d 738, 741, 419 N.Y.S.2d 578 (2d Dep't 1979) (accrual occurs when "all of the facts necessary to sustain the cause of action have occurred"). Where a fiduciary relationship exists, however, courts recognize that the ordinary accrual rule may be tolled until the fiduciary relationship terminates, particularly where the fiduciary's own conduct—continuing collection of fees, ongoing administration of assets—constitutes a continuing wrong. *See Golden v. Steam Heat, Inc.*, 216 A.D.2d 440, 442, 628 N.Y.S.2d 375 (2d Dep't 1995) (continuing fiduciary duty tolls limitations period until relationship ends).

Here, the FDIC acted as receiver—a fiduciary role—throughout the period from 1985 until November 1, 1995. See *O'Melveny & Myers v. FDIC*, 512 U.S. 79, 86 (1994) (recognizing FDIC as receiver stands in shoes of the failed institution and assumes fiduciary obligations). The very claims Bancorp asserts—unjust enrichment from post-insolvency interest payments, breach of fiduciary duty in asset disposition, and waste through improper expense charges—are claims that arise directly from the FDIC's conduct *as fiduciary receiver*. Each of these wrongs was part of a continuous course of conduct that did not become complete, and therefore did not accrue, until the receivership itself ended.

This rule finds support in the general principle that a beneficiary's claims against a trustee for mismanagement of a trust estate accrue no later than the termination of the trust relationship, since only then can the beneficiary determine the full scope of injury and demand an accounting. *Cf. Westchester Religious Inst. v. Kamerman*, 262 A.D.2d 131, 132, 691 N.Y.S.2d 502 (1st Dep't 1999) (limitations period for breach of fiduciary duty claims tied to termination of fiduciary relationship where ongoing duties exist). The FDIC's continuing administration of the receivership estate—including its self-payment of interest as late as 1991 and its ongoing charging of expenses—constituted an ongoing fiduciary relationship that did not terminate until November 1, 1995.

Because Bancorp filed its complaint on October 31, 1995, one day before the receivership terminated, its claims are timely under any accrual theory tied to the termination of the FDIC's fiduciary role. The district court's grant of summary judgment on limitations grounds should be reversed.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the district court and remand for further proceedings on the merits of Bancorp's claims.
