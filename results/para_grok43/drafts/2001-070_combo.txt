The Eighth Amendment forbids only those punishments that are both cruel and unusual in the constitutional sense. Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1879). It does not reach every mode of execution or every class of offender. In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890). The Amendment's text and history demonstrate that its prohibition is directed at "torture" and "unnecessary cruelty," not at capital punishment itself when imposed for murder. Wilkerson, 99 U.S. at 136; O'Neil v. Vermont, 144 U.S. 323, 339-40 (1892).

This Court has repeatedly held that the death penalty for murder is not cruel and unusual. In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 447; Wilkerson, 99 U.S. at 134-35. The same result follows when the offender possesses limited mental capacity, provided the record shows he understood the nature of his act and its wrongfulness. Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 368 (1910). Mental retardation does not place the offender beyond the reach of otherwise constitutional sanctions. Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 463-64 (1947).

The Eighth Amendment's meaning is measured by reference to the common-law understanding at the time of adoption and the punishments then regarded as acceptable. Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 100-01 (1958) (plurality). At common law, persons of limited intellect were punishable for capital crimes when they possessed sufficient reason to distinguish right from wrong. That standard, carried forward in American jurisprudence, permits execution where the evidence establishes such capacity. Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962).

A punishment is not rendered cruel and unusual merely because it falls upon an offender whose mental faculties are impaired. Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 531-32 (1968). The Amendment does not require the States to exempt every defendant whose intelligence falls below a particular level; it requires only that the punishment not be grossly disproportionate to the offense or inflicted in a manner involving unnecessary pain. Weems, 217 U.S. at 367-68; Resweber, 329 U.S. at 463.

The record here shows that petitioner was convicted of capital murder after a full trial in which the jury found he possessed the requisite mental state. Under the authorities above, that determination places the sentence within the range of punishments the Eighth Amendment permits. No pre-1970 decision of this Court holds otherwise. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
