The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are intended to provide a fresh start for honest debtors, but they do not shield those who have committed fraud. Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Code excepts from discharge debts obtained by false pretenses, false representations, or actual fraud. The question presented here is whether the debts incurred by Edward S. Cohen through his fraudulent overcharging of rent are dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code.

The language of § 523(a)(2)(A) is clear: a discharge under the Bankruptcy Code does not discharge an individual debtor from any debt for money or property obtained by false pretenses or false representations. The courts have long recognized that a debtor's fraudulent conduct can render a debt nondischargeable. In Brown v. O'Keefe, 300 U.S. 598 (1937), the Supreme Court held that a bankrupt's liability for conversion was not dischargeable, as it was not a debt that could be discharged under the Bankruptcy Act.

Similarly, in Cline v. Kaplan, 323 U.S. 97 (1944), the Court held that a debt arising from a fiduciary's defalcation was not dischargeable under § 17(a)(4) of the Bankruptcy Act, the precursor to § 523(a)(4) of the Code. While these cases do not directly address § 523(a)(2)(A), they demonstrate the principle that debts incurred through fraudulent or deceitful conduct are not entitled to discharge.

Here, Cohen's conduct in overcharging his tenants and failing to comply with the rent control ordinance constitutes a false representation, as he implicitly represented that the rents he charged were lawful. The fact that Cohen was reckless in his disregard for the truth, failing to inform himself of the rent control ordinance's provisions and ignoring the warnings of the Rent Leveling and Stabilization Board, further supports the conclusion that his debt is nondischargeable.

Furthermore, the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are also nondischargeable. The Act's provisions are intended to punish and deter fraudulent conduct, and the treble damages award is a direct result of Cohen's fraudulent actions. Allowing Cohen to discharge this debt would undermine the purpose of the Act and the Bankruptcy Code's exception for debts incurred through fraud.

In conclusion, the debts incurred by Cohen through his fraudulent overcharging of rent are not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. The courts below correctly determined that Cohen's conduct constituted a false representation, and that the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are nondischargeable. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
