# ARGUMENT

## I. Woodard Has No Constitutionally Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Ohio's Clemency Proceedings.

### A. Clemency Has Always Been Understood as an Act of Executive Grace, Not a Matter of Right.

Since the founding, this Court has recognized clemency as fundamentally different from adjudicative process. In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150 (1833), Chief Justice Marshall described a pardon as "an act of grace," proceeding from the executive, "which exempts the individual on whom it is bestowed from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed." *Id.* at 160. Three decades later, this Court reaffirmed that a pardon is "the private, though official act of the executive magistrate." *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866). And in *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480 (1927), the Court explained that clemency "is not a private act of grace from an individual happening to possess power," but rather "a part of the Constitutional scheme" committed to executive discretion. *Id.* at 486. Nowhere in this lineage has clemency been treated as an extension of the trial or appellate process to which due process protections attach.

This distinction is decisive. Just as parole was long understood to be "a matter of favor, not of right," such that the state could withhold it "upon terms as it pleases," *Ughbanks v. Armstrong*, 208 U.S. 481, 487 (1908), clemency is a discretionary act of executive mercy that creates no entitlement enforceable through the Due Process Clause. A defendant who has been convicted and sentenced through full and fair adjudicative process—as Woodard was—has received all the process the Constitution requires. Clemency lies beyond and outside the criminal justice system, not within it.

### B. Due Process Protects Only Interests Already Created by Law; Clemency Creates None.

This Court has consistently held that the process due in any given proceeding depends upon "the precise nature of the government function involved as well as of the private interest that has been affected by governmental action." *Cafeteria & Rest. Workers Union v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961). Where no cognizable interest exists, no process is constitutionally compelled.

Clemency does not diminish any liberty interest that the state has already promised to protect—it is instead a mechanism for the *conferral* of mercy beyond what the law requires. Because the inmate has no underlying entitlement to a commuted sentence or pardon, the state's decision to consider granting one, through whatever procedure it chooses, cannot itself create a liberty interest subject to judicial oversight. The Sixth Circuit's holding that "minimal" due process nonetheless attaches merely because clemency is temporally connected to the criminal proceeding conflates the existence of a process with the existence of a protected interest. That reasoning has no mooring in this Court's precedents.

### C. Even Assuming Some Process Were Due, Its Content Must Be Assessed According to the Nature of the Proceeding, Which Here Is Wholly Discretionary and Investigatory.

This Court has long recognized that "due process" is not a fixed, technical concept, but one whose requirements vary with the character of the governmental action at issue. *Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm. v. McGrath*, 341 U.S. 123, 162–63 (1951) (Frankfurter, J., concurring). In *Hannah v. Larche*, 363 U.S. 420 (1960), this Court held that investigatory and non-adjudicatory proceedings need not adopt trial-type procedures, explaining that "the requirements of due process frequently vary with the type of proceeding involved." *Id.* at 442. Clemency review—an executive investigation into whether to extend mercy, unmoored from any adjudicative function—falls squarely within this category. No court has ever required a state to structure clemency proceedings as if they were criminal trials or appeals, and none should begin here.

## II. The Voluntary Option to Participate in a Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.

### A. The Privilege Against Self-Incrimination Protects Only Against Compelled Testimony.

The Fifth Amendment privilege safeguards individuals from being compelled to give evidence against themselves; it does not forbid voluntary disclosure. *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 8 (1964) (privilege protects against being "compelled to answer official questions" where the answers might incriminate). Ohio's regulation imposes no such compulsion. Woodard may request an interview or decline one; if he requests one, he may answer some questions, all questions, or none. Nothing in the regulation obligates him to speak.

### B. The Privilege Must Be Invoked; It Is Not Self-Executing.

This Court has repeatedly held that the privilege against self-incrimination is not automatically triggered by the mere opportunity to speak—it must be claimed. *United States v. Monia*, 317 U.S. 424, 427 (1943) ("immunity does not run to all who testify, but only to those who claim its protection"). Because Woodard retains complete control over whether to answer any question posed during a voluntary interview, no impermissible compulsion exists, and the privilege remains fully available to him should he choose to invoke it. *Cf. Rogers v. United States*, 340 U.S. 367, 371 (1951) (waiver of the privilege is evaluated according to the circumstances of each case).

### C. Any Asserted Risk of Self-Incrimination Is Speculative and Premature.

The privilege applies only where there is a real and substantial hazard of incrimination, not a remote or speculative one. *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951). Woodard identifies no pending or contemplated prosecution to which any clemency statement could relate; indeed, he has disclaimed any effect on his existing conviction and sentence. Absent a concrete showing of hazard, his Fifth Amendment claim rests on hypothetical harm insufficient to support constitutional invalidation of Ohio's procedure.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
