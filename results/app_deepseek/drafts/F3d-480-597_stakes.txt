## ARGUMENT

### I. The Government Failed to Prove the Required Mens Rea That the Weapon Was a Machine Gun

The district court erred in denying Nieves-Castaño’s motion for judgment of acquittal on the machine-gun count because the government adduced no evidence that she knew the weapon she possessed had the characteristics that made it a machine gun. Under *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 619 (1994), to convict a defendant of possession of a machine gun under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), the government must prove that the defendant “knew of the features of his gun that brought it within the scope of the Act.” This mens rea requirement is not satisfied by proof that the defendant knew she possessed a firearm or even a rifle; the government must show knowledge of the specific automatic-fire capability that defines a machine gun under the statute.

The record here contains no such evidence. The government’s sole evidence of Nieves-Castaño’s knowledge came from an agent’s testimony that she said “she knew it was an AK-47.” But knowledge that a weapon is an AK-47—a rifle model that exists in both semiautomatic and fully automatic variants—does not establish knowledge that the particular weapon possessed the automatic-fire characteristics that make it a machine gun. *See United States v. Bello*, 194 F.3d 18, 26 (1st Cir. 1999) (holding that *Staples* requires proof “that the defendant knew the weapon he possessed had the characteristics that made it a ‘machine gun’”). In *Bello*, this court reversed a machine-gun conviction where the government presented no evidence that the defendant knew of the gun’s automatic capability, even though the defendant knew he possessed a firearm. *Id.* at 26-27. The same deficiency exists here.

Nieves-Castaño’s statement that she once opened the bag, looked in, and observed that there was a rifle does not fill the gap. Viewing a rifle’s exterior reveals nothing about its internal firing mechanism. Indeed, many AK-47-style rifles are externally identical but function only as semiautomatic weapons. *Cf. Staples*, 511 U.S. at 615-16 (recognizing that firearms are often outwardly indistinguishable from their fully automatic counterparts). The government presented no evidence that Nieves-Castaño ever tested or observed the weapon’s rate of fire, heard it discharge, or received any explanation about its mechanical features. Mere possession of a firearm, without more, cannot sustain a machine-gun conviction under *Staples*.

The sufficiency of the evidence must be assessed under the standard of *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979), asking whether “any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.” This standard is not met where, as here, the record is devoid of any proof on an essential element. Because the government failed to introduce evidence that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon had automatic-fire capability, her conviction under § 922(o) must be reversed and the district court should have granted her Rule 29 motion. *See United States v. Medina-Martinez*, 396 F.3d 1, 5 (1st Cir. 2005) (reversing conviction where “the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the government, is insufficient to support a rational finding of guilt on each essential element”). The conviction on this count cannot stand.
