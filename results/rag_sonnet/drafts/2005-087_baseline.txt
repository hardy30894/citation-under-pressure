# ARGUMENT

## I. STATES HAVE BROAD AUTHORITY TO CHANNEL MENTAL-STATE EVIDENCE INTO A STRUCTURED INSANITY DEFENSE

This Court has long recognized that "a State may choose" how to characterize and structure the insanity defense within its criminal justice system. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952). In *Leland*, this Court upheld Oregon's requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting the argument that due process compelled a different allocation of proof. The Court there acknowledged that "the jury might have found appellant to have been mentally incapable of the premeditation and deliberation required to support a first degree murder verdict or of the intent necessary to find him guilty of either first or second degree murder, and yet not have found him to have been legally insane." *Id.* Critically, Oregon's system permitted the jury to consider mental-state evidence for both purposes simultaneously, with the trial court instructing that "evidence adduced during this trial to prove defendant's insanity shall be considered and weighed by you, with all other evidence, whether or not you find defendant insane, in regard to the ability of the defendant to premeditate, form a purpose, to deliberate, act wilfully, and act maliciously." *Id.*

Petitioner asks this Court to constitutionalize a particular evidentiary structure—one that treats mens rea evidence and insanity evidence as always fungible. But *Leland* itself recognized that these are analytically distinct inquiries, even while permitting a State to allow overlapping consideration of the same evidence. Nothing in *Leland* holds that the Constitution *requires* such overlap. To the contrary, *Leland*'s deference to state procedural choices in this area demonstrates that Arizona's channeling rule—confining Clark's expert testimony to the insanity inquiry, where he bore the burden of proof—falls comfortably within permissible state authority.

## II. ARIZONA'S RULE MERELY ALLOCATES THE BURDEN OF PROOF IN A MANNER THIS COURT HAS SANCTIONED

The distinction between insanity and mens rea has deep roots in this Court's jurisprudence. Justice Rehnquist's concurring opinion, quoted with approval in subsequent discussion, observed that "the existence or nonexistence of legal insanity bears no necessary relationship to the existence or nonexistence of the required mental elements of the crime." *Rivera v. Delaware*, 429 U.S. 877 (1976) (Rehnquist, J., concurring in denial of certiorari). This is precisely the theoretical foundation for Arizona's rule: while evidence of mental illness *may* be relevant to both insanity and intent, a State may rationally decide that such evidence should be channeled into the insanity defense—where the defendant bears the burden—rather than allowing it to undermine the prosecution's burden on intent without structure or limitation.

This Court's decision in *Jones v. United States*, 463 U.S. 354 (1983), reinforces the constitutionality of treating insanity as a distinct legal construct with its own procedural consequences. This Court there held that "[a] defendant could be required to prove his insanity by a higher standard than a preponderance of the evidence," citing *Leland*, and recognized that the insanity plea carries unique consequences flowing from the defendant's own choice to raise it. *Jones*, 463 U.S. at 366 n.19 (quoting *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 799). Just as Congress and the States may impose heightened burdens on defendants who invoke insanity, Arizona may likewise structure *how* the evidence supporting that plea is used, without offending due process.

## III. THE STATE'S INTEREST IN RELIABLE FACT-FINDING JUSTIFIES CHANNELING EXPERT PSYCHIATRIC EVIDENCE

The reliability concerns animating Arizona's rule are not novel. This Court in *Lynch v. Overholser*, 369 U.S. 705 (1962), recognized the unique evidentiary posture created when a defendant "avoid[s] the ordinary criminal penalty merely by submitting enough evidence of an abnormal mental condition to raise a reasonable doubt of his responsibility at the time of committing the offense." The Court's analysis presupposed that psychiatric evidence bearing on insanity operates differently, and may be treated differently, than ordinary evidence bearing on the elements of the offense.

Moreover, this Court has consistently required that challenges to a State's evidentiary structure be evaluated with deference to the State's chosen procedural framework, so long as the prosecution's ultimate burden—proof of every element beyond a reasonable doubt—remains intact. *See Moore v. Duckworth*, 443 U.S. 713 (1979). Arizona's rule does not relieve the prosecution of that burden; it simply directs the jury as to *which* body of evidence bears on *which* question, and requires the defendant to prove the distinct, State-created insanity defense according to State-defined procedures. This is materially different from the burden-shifting on the elements themselves.

## IV. CLARK RECEIVED A FULL AND FAIR OPPORTUNITY TO PRESENT HIS EVIDENCE

Clark was not denied the opportunity to present his expert psychiatric evidence—he was permitted to introduce it in full, for the purpose Arizona law prescribed: to support his insanity defense. The trial court "ruled that Arizona law confined the use of the expert evidence to his insanity claim" but did not exclude the evidence itself. This is a structural allocation of relevance, not a due process violation. The situation is thus unlike cases involving wholesale exclusion of a defendant's evidence or denial of expert assistance altogether. *Cf. Granviel v. Texas*, 495 U.S. 963 (1990) (Marshall, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (addressing complete denial of confidential expert assistance).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals.
