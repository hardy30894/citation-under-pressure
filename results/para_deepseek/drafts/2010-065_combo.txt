**ARGUMENT**

The Tenth Amendment is a structural limitation on federal power, reserving to the States those powers not delegated to the United States. It does not confer individual rights upon private parties. A criminal defendant prosecuted under a federal statute therefore lacks standing to challenge that statute on the ground that it exceeds the federal government’s enumerated powers and violates the Tenth Amendment. This Court has long held that only a State—or a state officer acting in a representative capacity—may invoke the Tenth Amendment to invalidate federal action. Because Carol Anne Bond is a private individual, not a State, her Tenth Amendment challenge must be dismissed for lack of standing.

**I. The Tenth Amendment Protects State Sovereignty, Not Individual Rights**

The Tenth Amendment “states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered.” *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). It is a “declaration of the relationship between the national government and the states,” not a source of individual liberties. *Stearns v. Wood*, 236 U.S. 75, 78 (1915). Accordingly, “a private person cannot set up a claim that the Tenth Amendment has been violated by an act of Congress, because the amendment is a limitation upon the powers of Congress in favor of the states.” *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939). In *Tennessee Electric*, the Court squarely held that private utility companies lacked standing to assert that the federal government’s sale of electricity infringed the Tenth Amendment. *Id.* at 144. The Court explained that “absent the states or their officers, [the private parties] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Id.* That holding is dispositive here.

**II. Bond Cannot Assert the Rights of the States**

Bond, a private individual, is no differently situated than the utility companies in *Tennessee Electric*. She was convicted under a federal statute, 18 U.S.C. § 229, which implements the Chemical Weapons Convention. She argues that the statute exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers and encroaches upon state sovereignty. But the Tenth Amendment “is not a source of any right of action on the part of a private party.” *Fairchild v. Hughes*, 258 U.S. 126, 129–30 (1922). In *Fairchild*, the Court held that a private citizen lacked standing to challenge the ratification of a constitutional amendment, because the plaintiff “had no interest in the matter other than that of a citizen” and could not “represent the state.” *Id.* at 129. Similarly, Bond cannot represent Pennsylvania or any other State.

The rule is well established: “One who would strike down a state statute as unconstitutional must show that he is within the class of persons upon whom the statute operates.” *Standard Stock Food Co. v. Wright*, 225 U.S. 540, 550 (1912). The same principle applies to federal statutes. A party must “show that he is within the class of persons who are affected by the statute” and that the alleged constitutional violation causes him “a personal and proprietary interest.” *Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 297 U.S. 288, 319 (1936). Bond does not claim that the statute violates any personal right; she claims only that it disrupts the federal balance. That is a grievance that belongs to the States, not to her.

**III. Bond Has Suffered No Personal Injury from the Alleged Federalism Violation**

Standing requires “a direct injury—a personal and substantial interest in the outcome of the litigation.” *Ex parte Levitt*, 302 U.S. 633, 636 (1937). Bond’s only injury is her conviction, which she suffers because she violated a valid federal law. She does not allege that the State of Pennsylvania has objected to the federal prosecution or that her own liberty has been infringed by any state action. Her interest is “merely a generalized grievance,” not a particularized injury. *Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 488 (1923). In *Frothingham*, the Court held that a federal taxpayer lacked standing to challenge the Maternity Act because her interest was “shared with millions of others.” *Id.* at 487. Bond’s interest in federalism is similarly shared by every citizen; it is not a personal stake.

Moreover, a party cannot “set up the invalidity of a statute in order to obtain immunity from its operation unless he shows that the unconstitutional feature injures him.” *Stearns v. Wood*, 236 U.S. 75, 78 (1915). Bond does not claim that the statute’s alleged federalism defect causes her any injury distinct from the prosecution itself. The prosecution is lawful under the treaty power, as this Court recognized in *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432–33 (1920). But even if the statute were constitutionally suspect, Bond would lack standing to raise that claim. *United States v. Sprague*, 282 U.S. 716, 731 (1931) (“The validity of the amendment is not open to question by the appellants.”).

**IV. The States Have Not Complained, and Bond Cannot Do So for Them**

If a State believes that a federal statute infringes its Tenth Amendment rights, the State may sue. See *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 479–80 (1923) (holding that a State may sue to protect its own sovereign interests, but not as *parens patriae* for its citizens). No State has challenged § 229. Bond may not “usurp the function of the state” by asserting a claim that the State itself has chosen not to litigate. *Fairchild*, 258 U.S. at 130. To allow a private defendant to invoke the Tenth Amendment would enable every federal criminal defendant to attack the statute on grounds that rightfully belong to the States, undermining the orderly administration of federal criminal law.

**Conclusion**

Because the Tenth Amendment is a structural protection for state sovereignty, not a source of individual rights, Bond lacks standing to argue that § 229 exceeds the federal government’s enumerated powers. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Third Circuit.
