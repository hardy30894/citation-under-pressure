The district court lacked authority to keep this case for trial.

Section 1407(a) provides that actions transferred for multidistrict “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings” “shall be remanded by the panel” to the transferor district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). That command is mandatory. In Thompson v. United States, the Court recognized that statutory commands imposed by Congress must be followed as written. 142 U.S. 471, 474 (1892). And where Congress prescribes the forum and the timing of transfer, courts may not rewrite that command in the name of convenience. See Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282, 289 (1929); Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 250-51 (1926).

The district court’s action also contradicts the settled understanding that venue-transfer statutes must be applied according to their text. In Ex parte Collett, the Court construed 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) as a limited change-of-venue provision that “does not change the law applicable to a case,” but simply permits transfer “for the convenience of parties and witnesses.” 337 U.S. 55, 58, 72 (1949). Nor did Hoffman v. Blaski authorize a court to manufacture venue where Congress had not provided it; the Court held that the transferee forum must be one where the action could have been brought originally, not one selected after the fact. 363 U.S. 335, 344 (1960). The Arizona court therefore could not use § 1404(a) to accomplish what § 1407(a) forbids: retaining for trial a case that Congress required to be remanded. See also Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612, 636-37 (1964).

This Court has repeatedly enforced the principle that venue statutes are not optional. In United States v. National City Lines, Inc., the Court rejected an expansive reading of transfer authority that would have displaced the statutory venue structure. 334 U.S. 573, 581-82 (1948). Likewise, Norwood v. Kirkpatrick explained that § 1404(a) was meant to permit transfer only within the limits Congress imposed, not to create free-floating authority to choose any forum a judge prefers. 349 U.S. 29, 32 (1955). And in Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585, the Court again treated § 1404(a) as a narrow codification of transfer convenience, not a license to ignore statutory venue commands. 364 U.S. 19, 26-27 (1960).

The district court’s approach is especially untenable because it nullifies the distinct function of § 1407. Congress enacted multidistrict transfer to centralize common pretrial matters, not to confer trial jurisdiction on the transferee court. See In re Plumbing Fixture Cases, 298 F. Supp. 484, 495 (J.P.M.L. 1968) (describing § 1407 as a mechanism for pretrial proceedings). Earlier transfer cases likewise recognized that a court may not enlarge its own power by stretching procedural statutes beyond their terms. In Ex parte Falk, the Court insisted that statutory limitations on venue and process must be respected as written. 10 F. 434, 436 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1881). And in Ker v. Illinois, the Court’s reluctance to create extra-textual procedural bars underscores that courts must follow the procedures Congress has actually enacted. 119 U.S. 436, 444 (1886).

The government will likely rely on cases allowing transfer where statutes are ambiguous, but ambiguity cannot be conjured to defeat an express remand command. In Caminetti v. United States, the Court emphasized that the judiciary’s role is to apply the statutory scheme Congress adopted, not to adjust it for perceived efficiency. 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). That principle controls here. Section 1407(a) says the case “shall be remanded.” A transferee court’s order retaining the case for trial is the opposite of remand. Courts are not free to treat Congress’s mandatory directive as advisory. See Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 250-51 (1926); Thompson v. United States, 142 U.S. 471, 474 (1892).

Because the district court had no authority to assign the case to itself for trial, the order must be vacated and the case remanded to the Northern District of Illinois.
