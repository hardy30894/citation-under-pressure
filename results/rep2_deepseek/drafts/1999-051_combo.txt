**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals erred in holding that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. Under well-settled habeas jurisprudence predating 1970, a federal court may not consider a claim that has been procedurally defaulted in state court unless the petitioner demonstrates both cause and prejudice. Critically, the cause itself must be a claim that was properly exhausted in state court. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot supply cause because it is itself barred from federal review.

The exhaustion requirement is foundational. This Court has long held that a state prisoner must first exhaust all available state remedies before seeking federal habeas relief. *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116–17 (1944) (federal courts will not interfere with state custody until state remedies are exhausted). That requirement applies to every claim, including claims of ineffective assistance of counsel. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 203–04 (1950) (exhaustion is a “prerequisite” to federal habeas). A petitioner cannot circumvent state procedural rules by raising an unexhausted claim as cause for another default. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 486–87 (1953) (federal habeas review is limited to claims “properly presented” to state courts).

In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438–39 (1963), this Court held that a deliberate bypass of state procedures bars federal review. Although *Fay*’s standard has been refined, the principle endures: a petitioner must not have intentionally relinquished a claim. Here, Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was procedurally defaulted because he failed to timely file his application to reopen his direct appeal. That default cannot be excused by the very claim that is defaulted. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963) (a petitioner must have a “fair opportunity” to present claims in state court).

The right to effective assistance of counsel does not override state procedural rules. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 342–43 (1963) (recognizing the right to counsel but not as a license to ignore procedural requirements). A petitioner must raise all claims, including ineffective assistance, in state court. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 724–25 (1961) (exhaustion requires presentation of the federal claim to the state courts). Carpenter failed to do so.

Allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to serve as cause would undermine the finality of state judgments. *Parker v. Ellis*, 362 U.S. 574, 575–76 (1960) (per curiam) (finality concerns are paramount in habeas). The court of appeals’ decision would permit a petitioner to circumvent state procedural bars by simply repackaging a defaulted claim as “cause” for another default. That result is inconsistent with the exhaustion doctrine and the respect owed to state court judgments.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
