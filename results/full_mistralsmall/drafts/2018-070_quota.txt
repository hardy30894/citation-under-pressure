**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCSLA’S CHOICE-OF-LAW PROVISION PERMITS STATE LAW TO APPLY ONLY WHEN FEDERAL LAW LEAVES A “SIGNIFICANT VOID OR GAP.”

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), expressly incorporates the civil and criminal laws of adjacent states “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” The Ninth Circuit erred by reading this provision to allow state wage-and-hour law to supplement the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) whenever the state law is merely “applicable” and not “inconsistent.” That interpretation ignores the settled rule that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law on the OCS only where federal law contains a “significant void or gap.” *Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969); *see also Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (state law applies “to the extent that it is applicable and not inconsistent with federal law”).

This Court has repeatedly recognized that federal statutes are presumed to occupy a legislative field fully before state law may intrude. *See Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (“When Congress has ‘occupied the field,’ state law is preempted.”); *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (state law may not supplement federal law unless Congress has “left a gap” for state regulation). The FLSA is no exception. It establishes a comprehensive scheme governing minimum wages, overtime, and recordkeeping, and it contains an express savings clause preserving state laws that are “more protective” of workers. 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). That savings clause does not create a “gap”; it merely allows states to set higher standards. *See Cipriano v. City of Houma*, 395 U.S. 701, 706 (1969) (savings clause permits state laws that are “more favorable to employees”).

The Fifth Circuit has consistently applied the “significant void or gap” test under the OCSLA. *See, e.g., Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Eng’g, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990) (“State law is adopted as federal law only where federal law is silent or contains a significant void or gap.”); *Breton Energy, L.L.C. v. Mariner Energy Res., Inc.*, 764 F.3d 394, 398 (5th Cir. 2014) (state law applies only if “federal law is silent or contains a significant void or gap”). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—allowing state wage-and-hour claims whenever state law is “applicable” and not “inconsistent”—would eviscerate the presumption against concurrent state regulation in a field fully occupied by federal law. *See Hillsborough Cty. v. Automated Med. Labs., Inc.*, 471 U.S. 707, 713 (1985) (“State laws can be preempted . . . when they ‘actually conflict’ with federal law or ‘stand as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.’”).

II. CALIFORNIA’S WAGE-AND-HOUR LAWS ARE PRE-EMPTED BECAUSE THEY CONFLICT WITH THE FLSA’S COMPREHENSIVE SCHEME.

Even if the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision were read to allow state law where federal law is merely “applicable,” California’s meal-period and overtime rules are preempted because they conflict with the FLSA. The FLSA’s savings clause preserves state laws that are “more protective,” but it does not permit state laws that impose additional obligations inconsistent with the FLSA’s structure. *See, e.g., Dole v. Vincent, 35 F.3d 885, 889 (9th Cir. 1994)* (state law preempted where it “stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment of the full purposes and objectives of Congress”).

Here, California’s requirement that employees receive a 30-minute meal period for every five hours worked conflicts with the FLSA’s flexible approach to meal breaks. The FLSA does not mandate specific timing or duration for meal periods; it merely requires that employees be paid for all hours worked and that employers comply with the minimum wage and overtime provisions. *See 29 C.F.R. § 785.19* (“Bona fide meal periods are not worktime.”). California’s rigid meal-period rule would impose obligations that the FLSA does not require and that are inconsistent with the FLSA’s goal of providing flexible work arrangements. *See Cipriano*, 395 U.S. at 706 (state law preempted where it “frustrates the full effectiveness of federal law”).

Moreover, California’s overtime rules for daily work exceed the FLSA’s weekly threshold and would impose duplicative or conflicting obligations on employers operating on the OCS. *See 29 U.S.C. § 207(a)(2)* (FLSA requires overtime for hours worked over 40 in a workweek); *Cal. Lab. Code § 510* (California requires overtime for hours worked over 8 in a day or 40 in a workweek). The Ninth Circuit’s rule would permit California to impose its daily overtime scheme on federal enclaves where the FLSA’s weekly standard applies. This conflicts with the OCSLA’s directive that state law only applies where it is not inconsistent with federal law. *See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A)*.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT THE FLSA LEAVES NO GAP FOR STATE WAGE-AND-HOUR CLAIMS.

The district court properly held that the FLSA’s comprehensive scheme leaves no “significant void or gap” for state wage-and-hour laws to fill. *See Rice*, 331 U.S. at 230 (state law preempted where federal law is comprehensive). The FLSA provides a detailed framework for wage-and-hour disputes, including remedies for unpaid wages and overtime. *See 29 U.S.C. §§ 206–216*. The savings clause in § 218(a) does not create a gap; it merely allows states to impose stricter standards. *See Cipriano*, 395 U.S. at 706. Because the FLSA already addresses wage-and-hour disputes, California’s laws are not needed to fill any void.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule would permit every adjacent state to impose its own wage-and-hour regime on the OCS, creating a patchwork of conflicting laws that would frustrate Congress’s intent to establish a uniform federal standard. *See Hillsborough Cty.*, 471 U.S. at 713 (state laws preempted where they conflict with federal objectives). This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision and reinstate the district court’s judgment.
