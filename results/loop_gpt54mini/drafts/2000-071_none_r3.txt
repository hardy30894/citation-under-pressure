Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club violated the Free Speech Clause because the school opened its after-hours facilities to community use, but denied access to the Club based on the religious viewpoint from which it sought to teach morals and character. Once the government creates a forum for private expression, it may not favor one set of ideas over another. Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939). It may not suppress speech because officials dislike its message. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368-69 (1931); Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940); West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943). The school’s exclusion here did exactly that.

The Club did not seek special treatment; it sought the same access afforded to other community groups. Milford permitted groups to use its facilities for the welfare of the community, including youth development and character-building activities, yet excluded the Club because its lessons on those subjects were religious. That is viewpoint discrimination. Barnette, 319 U.S. at 642; De Jonge v. Oregon, 299 U.S. 353, 364-65 (1937). The Constitution does not permit government to confine discussion of a subject to secular perspectives while barring religious ones. Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 303-04; Murdock v. Pennsylvania, 319 U.S. 105, 110-11 (1943). Nor may it deny a religious group access merely because its speech takes the form of prayer, Bible reading, or religious exhortation. Follett v. McCormick, 321 U.S. 573, 577-78 (1944); Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272-73 (1951).

Milford’s effort to relabel the Club’s speech as “religious worship” does not alter the analysis. The Court has repeatedly recognized that religious dissemination and religious advocacy are protected speech. Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 303-04; Murdock, 319 U.S. at 110-11; Follett, 321 U.S. at 577-78. The government may not exclude speech from a forum simply because the speech is religious in content or form. Hague, 307 U.S. at 515-16; Niemotko, 340 U.S. at 272-73. The Free Speech Clause protects the Club’s right to present its moral message from a Christian viewpoint just as it protects secular organizations’ right to present competing views. Barnette, 319 U.S. at 642; De Jonge, 299 U.S. at 364-65.

The Establishment Clause does not justify Milford’s exclusion. That Clause prohibits governmental sponsorship of religion, but it does not authorize hostility toward private religious speech. Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15-18 (1947), held that government must remain neutral “between religion and nonreligion.” Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313-15 (1952), reaffirmed that neutrality does not require “callous indifference to religious groups.” Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 425-32 (1962), and School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 222-23 (1963), invalidated state-sponsored prayer and Bible reading in public schools, but those cases do not permit the government to exclude private religious speakers from an otherwise open forum. The constitutional line is between state endorsement and private expression, not between secular viewpoints and religious ones. Everson, 330 U.S. at 17-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 314.

Milford’s concern that allowing the Club to meet might appear to endorse religion cannot carry the day. The Establishment Clause cases on which Milford relies involved the State itself composing or leading devotional exercises, or channeling official authority to religious instruction. McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 212-13 (1948); Engel, 370 U.S. at 425-26; Schempp, 374 U.S. at 222-23. Equal access for private speakers is different. It does not establish religion; it respects the government’s obligation of neutrality. Everson, 330 U.S. at 15-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 314-15. If Milford wished to dispel any confusion, it could do so through neutral administration, not exclusion based on viewpoint. Barnette, 319 U.S. at 642; Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 303-04.

For these reasons, Milford violated the Good News Club’s free speech rights, and its Establishment Clause defense fails. The judgment should be reversed.
