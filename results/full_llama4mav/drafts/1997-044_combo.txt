The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are designed to give debtors a fresh start, but this policy is not absolute. Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Code excepts from discharge debts obtained by false pretenses, false representations, or actual fraud. The question presented is whether the debt owed by Edward S. Cohen to his former tenants, including the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, is dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code.

The Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit correctly held that Cohen's debt is nondischargeable. The Bankruptcy Court found that Cohen had committed actual fraud within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A) by charging rents in excess of those permitted by the local rent control ordinance, and that his conduct violated the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act. This finding is supported by the fact that Cohen made implicit representations to his tenants that the rents he charged were lawful, and that he acted with reckless disregard for the truth.

The principle that a debtor's reckless disregard for the truth can satisfy the knowledge and intent requirements of § 523(a)(2)(A) is well-established. In Brown v. Garey, 267 F.2d 298 (9th Cir. 1959), the court held that a debtor's false representations can be inferred from his conduct, and that intent to deceive can be shown by a reckless disregard for the truth. Similarly, in In re Barbato, 97 F.2d 572 (2d Cir. 1938), the court held that a debtor's failure to disclose material facts can constitute a false representation.

The award of treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is also nondischargeable. The Supreme Court has long recognized that punitive damages can be awarded to deter wrongful conduct, as seen in Scott v. Donald, 165 U.S. 58 (1897). In Lake v. Cameron, 247 F.2d 751 (D.C. Cir. 1957), the court held that a statutory penalty can be imposed to punish and deter wrongful conduct. The treble damages awarded in this case serve a similar purpose, and are therefore nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A).

Furthermore, the weight of authority supports the conclusion that debts incurred through fraud, including punitive or statutory damages, are nondischargeable. In Reisenberg v. Werbel, 254 F. 855 (2d Cir. 1918), the court held that a debt incurred through fraudulent conduct is not dischargeable, even if the debt is not directly related to the fraud. In Morrow v. Pfleiderer, 4 F.2d 217 (8th Cir. 1925), the court held that a judgment for damages resulting from a debtor's fraudulent conduct is not dischargeable.

The decision in Forsyth v. Vehmeyer, 177 U.S. 177 (1900), also supports the conclusion that Cohen's debt is nondischargeable. In Forsyth, the Supreme Court held that a debtor's fraudulent conduct can render a debt nondischargeable, even if the debt is not directly related to the fraud. Similarly, in Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704 (1877), the Court held that a debt incurred through fraudulent conduct is not dischargeable.

In Hennequin v. Clews, 111 U.S. 676 (1884), the Supreme Court held that a debt is not dischargeable if it is incurred through fraudulent means. This principle is directly applicable to the present case, where Cohen's debt was incurred through his fraudulent conduct.

The authorities cited above demonstrate that the debt owed by Cohen to his former tenants, including the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
