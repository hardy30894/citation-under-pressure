**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in denying remand because neither 28 U.S.C. § 1331 nor 16 U.S.C. § 825p confers subject matter jurisdiction over the Pressls’ purely state-law declaratory judgment action.

**I. The well-pleaded complaint rule forecloses federal question jurisdiction.**

The Pressls’ complaint seeks a declaration of their rights under a 1960 flowage easement—a private contract governed by Virginia law. It alleges no violation of the Federal Power Act, no order of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, and no right created by federal statute. Under *American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916), “a suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action.” Here, the law creating the cause of action is Virginia contract law, not federal law.

In *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908), the Supreme Court held that federal jurisdiction cannot be predicated on anticipated defenses. The district court based jurisdiction on APCO’s assertion that its FERC license justifies its restrictions. That assertion is a defense, not part of the Pressls’ claim. As the Court explained in *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank*, 299 U.S. 109, 115 (1936), “Not every question of federal law emerging in a suit is proof that a federal law is the basis of the suit.” A federal question must be an essential element of the plaintiff’s cause of action, not a potential answer to the defendant’s position. *Great Northern Ry. Co. v. Merchants Elevator Co.*, 259 U.S. 285, 287 (1922).

Because the Pressls’ claim arises exclusively under state law, the Declaratory Judgment Act cannot confer jurisdiction. That Act “is not an independent source of federal jurisdiction.” *Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.*, 339 U.S. 667, 671 (1950). The district court should have examined the coercive action the Pressls might have brought—a suit to quiet title or for breach of the easement—which would clearly sound in state law.

**II. Section 825p does not render the dispute federal.**

The district court relied on 16 U.S.C. § 825p, which grants federal courts jurisdiction over suits “brought for the purpose of enforcing” the Federal Power Act. The Pressls do not seek to enforce the FPA or any FERC order; they seek to vindicate private, state-law property rights against a licensee. Federal regulatory schemes do not eclipse state courts’ jurisdiction over such disputes. *Pan Am Petroleum Corp. v. Superior Court*, 366 U.S. 656, 663 (1961) (state courts retain jurisdiction over contracts involving FERC-regulated entities). Likewise, *Thompson v. Consolidated Gas Utilities Corp.*, 300 U.S. 55, 70 (1937), holds that a claim growing out of a federally regulated business does not automatically “arise under” federal law.

*FPC v. Union Electric Co.*, 381 U.S. 90, 109 (1965), is directly on point. There, the Court held that the Federal Power Act “does not purport to clothe the Commission with jurisdiction over the acquisition of the lands necessary for such projects.” The same logic applies here. The Pressls’ property rights are defined by the easement, not by APCO’s license. The mere fact that the land lies within the project boundary is irrelevant to the source of the Pressls’ cause of action. The district court’s conclusion that FERC’s licensing authority converts every state-law property dispute within the project boundary into a federal case is unsupportable.

**III. Removal was improper, and the district court’s merits ruling is void.**

Removal jurisdiction is strictly construed, and any doubt must be resolved against removal. *Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets*, 313 U.S. 100, 108–09 (1941). APCO failed to carry its burden of demonstrating that the Pressls’ complaint states a federal claim. The district court therefore lacked subject matter jurisdiction. Its interpretation of the easement and its dismissal on the merits were ultra vires. A judgment entered without jurisdiction is null and must be vacated. The proper course is to vacate and remand with instructions to remand the case to the Circuit Court for Franklin County, Virginia.
