The Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from an insanity defense. The Arizona Supreme Court correctly held that Arizona law permits the use of such evidence only in connection with an insanity defense, on which the defendant bears the burden of proof.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that states have considerable latitude in defining the elements of crimes and the defenses thereto. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant, stating that "the burden of proof of 'sanity' or 'insanity' has generally been held to rest on the defendant" and that this allocation of the burden "does not offend the constitutional requirement of due process." Id. at 798-799.

Similarly, in Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the Court noted that the insanity defense is a matter of state law, and that states are free to define the contours of the defense as they see fit. The Court stated that "the plea of not guilty by reason of insanity is, in its nature, a plea of confession and avoidance" and that the defendant bears the burden of proving the defense. Id. at 485.

Arizona's rule, which confines the use of expert evidence about a defendant's mental state to the insanity defense, is consistent with these principles. By requiring the defendant to bear the burden of proving insanity, Arizona's law does not relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove the elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The prosecution remains obligated to prove the defendant's guilt, including the element of criminal intent, beyond a reasonable doubt.

The trial court's ruling, which precluded Clark from using expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, was therefore consistent with Arizona law and did not violate Clark's due process rights. The Arizona Supreme Court's affirmance of Clark's conviction was proper, and this Court should uphold it.
