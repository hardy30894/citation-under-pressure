The district court's award of a fee enhancement to the respondent's attorneys was proper under the Civil Rights Attorney's Fees Awards Act of 1976, 42 U.S.C. § 1988(b). The Supreme Court has consistently held that a district court has discretion to enhance a fee award in "rare" and "exceptional" cases, supported by "specific evidence" on the record. Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984); Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air ("Delaware Valley I"), 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986).

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied this precedent in affirming the district court's fee enhancement. Kenny A. v. Perdue, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008). The district court's detailed findings and observations, based on its "substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court's observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs' counsel throughout this long and difficult case," justified the enhancement. Kenny A. v. Perdue, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286 (N.D.Ga. 2006).

The petitioners argue that the district court's reliance on its own experience and observations constitutes a due process violation. However, the district court's findings were not "testimony" but rather explanations for the enhancement, as required by Blum and Delaware Valley I. See Blum, 465 U.S. at 899; Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 565. The district court's unique position to evaluate the attorneys' performance and results obtained is entitled to deference. See Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 437 (1983) ("the district court has discretion in determining the amount of a fee award"); see also Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery, 836 F.2d 1292, 1304 (11th Cir. 1988) (district court's "superior understanding of the litigation" is a key factor in determining fee awards).

Other circuits have also upheld fee enhancements based on exceptional performance and results. See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co., 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co., 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999); Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc., 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); Hyatt v. Apfel, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc., 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n. 8 (10th Cir. 1997); Shipes v. Trinity Indus., 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993).

The petitioners' argument that the fee enhancement was improper because it was based on factors already accounted for in the lodestar calculation is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has acknowledged that, in "rare" and "exceptional" cases, an enhancement may be justified even if some of the factors are already included in the lodestar. Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 565; see also City of Burlington v. Dague, 505 U.S. 557, 562 (1992) (noting that "in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases" an enhancement may be justified).

The district court's award of a fee enhancement was a proper exercise of its discretion, supported by specific evidence and detailed findings. The Eleventh Circuit's affirmance was consistent with Supreme Court precedent and the decisions of other circuits. The judgment should be affirmed.
