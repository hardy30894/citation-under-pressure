# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Section 1254a(f)(4) Satisfies Section 1255(a)'s Admission Requirement

Congress provided that once TPS is granted, "the alien shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant." 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). That statutory fiction operates "for adjustment of status purposes under § 1255," and it does so by treating the TPS recipient exactly as any other nonimmigrant would be treated. A nonimmigrant's lawful status is, by definition, predicated on admission: one cannot occupy nonimmigrant status without having gone through the inspection process that produces it. As the Ninth Circuit explained in Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954 (9th Cir. 2017), "[b]y the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted." Id. at 960 (internal quotation marks omitted). Section 1254a(f)(4) does not merely excuse a gap in status; it directs that the TPS recipient be treated, fictively, as if he holds the very nonimmigrant status that Section 1255(a) requires as a threshold matter.

Section 1255(a) permits adjustment for "an alien who was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States." 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). Section 1101(a)(13)(A) defines admission as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). Congress's choice to place TPS recipients into "lawful status as a nonimmigrant" necessarily incorporates the admission that lawful nonimmigrant status presupposes. The Board of Immigration Appeals has itself recognized the close relationship between status and admission, observing that "[a]n adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission." In re Espinosa-Guillot, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). If a later change in status can retroactively supply an admission, there is no textual reason why Congress's express command that a TPS recipient be considered "as being in ... lawful status as a nonimmigrant" cannot do the same.

## II. The Structure and Purpose of the TPS Program Confirm that Petitioners Are Eligible to Adjust Status

Congress enacted the TPS program to provide a path by which nationals of countries suffering armed conflict or natural disaster could remain, and ultimately stabilize their lives, in the United States. Reading Section 1254a(f)(4) to accomplish nothing more than a temporary reprieve from removal—while denying its plain command that the recipient be treated as lawfully admitted—would strip the statute of practical effect for the very population Congress most wished to help: those who, like Petitioners, fled a natural disaster and had no opportunity to be inspected before their flight became necessary.

The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that the title and structure of Section 1255 reflect Congress's intent that TPS recipients be able to "make use" of that adjustment process. Ramirez, 852 F.3d at 961. Forcing TPS recipients to depart the country and seek lawful readmission before applying for adjustment—the consequence of the government's reading—would undermine the very protective purpose of TPS, since many TPS recipients come from countries that remain unsafe for the duration of their protected status. Requiring departure as a precondition to the very relief Congress made available inverts the statute's remedial design.

Nor does this reading render other provisions superfluous. Section 1254a(h)'s supermajority requirement for "special legislation" adjusting the status of TPS recipients addresses direct legislative grants of permanent status untethered to any individual application or eligibility showing under Section 1255—a wholly different mechanism than the individualized, discretionary adjustment process at issue here. Recognizing that Section 1254a(f)(4) satisfies Section 1255(a)'s admission element does not collapse the two provisions; it simply gives full effect to Congress's express statutory directive.

## III. Contrary Authority Misreads the Interplay Between "Status" and "Admission"

The courts that have rejected this reading, including the Third Circuit below, have relied on a rigid distinction between "status" and "admission" that Congress itself did not observe when drafting Section 1254a(f)(4). That provision does not merely say a TPS recipient may be treated as maintaining a lawful status for some limited purpose; it says the alien "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant," 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4)—language that tracks precisely the kind of nonimmigrant status that itself depends on admission. The Sixth Circuit likewise concluded that TPS recipients are eligible to seek adjustment of status regardless of the manner of their entry. Flores v. U.S. Citizenship & Immigration Servs., 718 F.3d 548 (6th Cir. 2013). Petitioners respectfully submit that the Sixth and Ninth Circuits' approach, not the Third Circuit's, gives full and faithful effect to the text Congress enacted.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should hold that a grant of Temporary Protected Status under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an admission for purposes of adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255, and should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals.
