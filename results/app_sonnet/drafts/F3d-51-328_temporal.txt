**ARGUMENT**

**I. Shapiro Failed to Satisfy the Traditional Equitable Standard for a Preliminary Injunction.**

A preliminary injunction is "one of the most drastic tools in the arsenal of judicial remedies," and this Court has long required a "clear showing" before that remedy issues. *Doeskin Products, Inc. v. United Merchants & Mfrs., Inc.*, 233 F.2d 913, 914 (2d Cir. 1956). The movant must demonstrate either probable success on the merits together with the possibility of irreparable injury, or serious questions going to the merits with the balance of hardships tipping decidedly in her favor. *Societe Comptoir de l'Industrie Cotonniere Etablissements Boussac v. Alexander's Dep't Stores, Inc.*, 299 F.2d 33, 35–36 (2d Cir. 1962). Irreparable injury cannot rest on speculation or on harm that is compensable or avoidable through ordinary channels. *Hamilton Watch Co. v. Benrus Watch Co.*, 206 F.2d 738, 742 (2d Cir. 1953) (injunctive relief requires a showing of injury that cannot be remedied "by an award of money damages").

Shapiro's claimed injury—delay attributable to street parking—was neither uniquely caused by Cadman Towers' waiting-list policy nor incapable of amelioration through means short of a mandatory reallocation of a scarce cooperative asset. Cadman Towers had already offered to accommodate her once she reached the normal course of the list, a course available to every other resident. Under *Doeskin* and *Societe Comptoir*, the district court was obliged to test that record rigorously before displacing the settled expectations of dozens of other apartment owners; it did not.

**II. The District Court Disregarded the Settled Equitable Requirement That a Court Weigh the Interests of Third Parties and the Public Interest Before Granting Extraordinary Relief.**

Equity has never treated the grant of injunctive relief as "a matter of right, even though irreparable injury may otherwise result to the plaintiff." *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 440 (1944). Where relief will "adversely affect" the settled expectations of others, "the court may . . . withhold relief until a final determination of the rights of the parties, though the postponement may be burdensome to the plaintiff." *Id.* The essence of equity jurisdiction "has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case," with "[f]lexibility rather than rigidity" as its hallmark. *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321, 329–30 (1944). That flexible power cuts against Shapiro, not for her.

The record shows that dozens of Cadman Towers residents have waited, in some cases for years, on the first-come/first-served list for one of the cooperative's scarce sixty-six ground-floor spaces at 101 Clark Street. The district court's order does not merely benefit Shapiro; it necessarily displaces whichever resident stood next in the queue, without any inquiry into that resident's own circumstances, need, or reliance on the established system. *Hecht* and *Yakus* teach that a court sitting in equity must consider precisely this kind of third-party effect before ordering relief that reallocates a scarce resource among competing claimants. The district court's order rests on no such consideration. It treated the case as though only two parties—Shapiro and the cooperative—had an interest in the outcome, when in truth every apartment owner on the waiting list has a stake in the integrity of the allocation system Cadman Towers has followed for years.

**III. The Injunction Exceeded the Equitable Discretion to Mould Relief No Broader Than the Necessities of the Case Required.**

Even where some interim relief might be warranted, equity requires that the remedy be no broader than necessary to prevent the harm shown. *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321, 329–30 (1944) (equity "mould[s] each decree to the necessities of the particular case"). The district court did not consider intermediate measures—such as expedited placement on the existing waiting list, or accommodation through car-service subsidies pending an opening—that would have addressed Shapiro's stated difficulty without commandeering a specific ground-floor space and displacing the resident next entitled to it. Instead, the court imposed the single most intrusive remedy available: an outright, permanent reassignment of a scarce cooperative resource, ordered before trial, on a preliminary record, and without any finding as to who would be dispossessed of the spot or on what timetable that person had been waiting.

This is precisely the sort of relief that traditional equitable principles counsel against granting on a preliminary basis. See *Virginian Ry. Co. v. System Federation No. 40*, 300 U.S. 515, 552 (1937) (a court of equity must fashion its decree with regard to the practical consequences for those affected by it). Because the district court neither weighed the competing claims of other waiting-list members nor considered less drastic alternatives, its order cannot stand as a proper exercise of the "flexib[le]" equitable discretion described in *Hecht*, 321 U.S. at 329–30.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the preliminary injunction should be vacated.
