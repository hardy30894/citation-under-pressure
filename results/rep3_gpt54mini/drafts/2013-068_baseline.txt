## Argument

### I. The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’s buffer-zone law as a valid time, place, and manner regulation.

The Massachusetts statute regulates only speech in a traditional public forum, outside reproductive-health facilities, and it does so in a content-neutral way. Under established First Amendment doctrine, the question is whether the law is narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest and leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983); Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). The First Circuit correctly applied that standard.

Massachusetts has compelling interests in public safety, patient access, and clinic order. The Court has repeatedly recognized the government’s authority to protect access to health-care facilities and prevent obstruction, intimidation, and harassment. Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 715, 729–30 (2000); Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc., 512 U.S. 753, 768 (1994); Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western N.Y., 519 U.S. 357, 376–77 (1997). The First Circuit’s record-based conclusion that the Act protects those interests without banning speech is fully consistent with those decisions. McCullen v. Coakley, 571 F.3d 167, 176–82 (1st Cir. 2009); McCullen v. Coakley, 753 F.3d 4, 12–16 (1st Cir. 2014).

Petitioners argue the statute is not narrowly tailored because it burdens their preferred method of close, personal conversation. But narrow tailoring does not require the least restrictive means. Ward, 491 U.S. at 798–99. A law may burden more speech than necessary so long as it does not “burden substantially more speech than is necessary to further the government’s legitimate interests.” Id. at 799. Hill upheld a Colorado law that, like Massachusetts’s law, limited face-to-face counseling near clinic entrances and preserved other avenues for speech. Hill, 530 U.S. at 716–30. The First Circuit reasonably relied on that precedent and on the statute’s limited 35-foot fixed buffer, which still leaves petitioners free to speak, hold signs, distribute literature, pray, and counsel outside the marked zones. McCullen, 753 F.3d at 18–22.

Petitioners’ as-applied challenge likewise fails. This Court has upheld carefully tailored access restrictions even when they make speakers less effective, because the First Amendment protects the ability to speak, not the speaker’s preferred degree of proximity. Ward, 491 U.S. at 798; Hill, 530 U.S. at 729–30. The First Circuit found that meaningful alternatives remained at each clinic site. McCullen, 753 F.3d at 18–22. That conclusion is factual and entitled to deference absent legal error. The court did not err.

### II. Hill v. Colorado controls this case and should not be limited or overruled.

Petitioners ask the Court either to limit or overrule Hill. The Court should decline both requests. Hill is squarely on point. It upheld a content-neutral statute regulating speech within 100 feet of a health-care facility entrance, reasoning that the law regulated the place and manner of communication, not its content, and left open ample alternatives. Hill, 530 U.S. at 719–30. Massachusetts’s law is narrower than the statute sustained in Hill: it applies only within 35 feet of designated clinic entrances, exits, and driveways. McCullen, 753 F.3d at 12–16. A lower court could not disregard controlling precedent. Rodriguez de Quijas v. Shearson/Am. Express, Inc., 490 U.S. 477, 484–85 (1989).

Nor is there any basis to overrule Hill. Stare decisis carries special force in constitutional cases where overruling would upset settled reliance interests and invite doctrinal instability. Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S. 808, 827–28 (1991); Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pa. v. Casey, 505 U.S. 833, 854–55 (1992). Petitioners identify no intervening constitutional development that has undermined Hill’s core holding. To the contrary, later First Amendment decisions continue to recognize the constitutionality of content-neutral regulation of speech in public forums when justified by significant governmental interests. Ward, 491 U.S. at 791; Snyder v. Phelps, 562 U.S. 443, 456–58 (2011). Hill remains good law.

Petitioners’ attack on Hill would also destabilize long-settled precedent governing protest activity around abortion clinics. See Madsen, 512 U.S. at 768–70; Schenck, 519 U.S. at 376–81. Those cases reflect the Court’s consistent view that states may protect access to clinics and the safety of patients without silencing advocacy. The First Circuit faithfully applied that line of authority. Its judgment should be affirmed.
