The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment (Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969)), precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal (North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969)). Although the Supreme Court has not directly addressed whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, its precedents establish that the Clause is not limited to trials on guilt or innocence.

In Bullington v. Missouri, the Supreme Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to capital sentencing proceedings because they involve characteristics that make them resemble a trial on guilt or innocence (Bullington, supra, as discussed in the lower court opinion). The same logic applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, like the one in this case, where the state has elected to provide a trial of sentencing allegations.

The trial of prior conviction allegations under California's Three Strikes law (§ 1025) involves many of the same procedural protections as a trial on guilt or innocence. The prosecution must prove the prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt (People v. Tenner, as cited in the lower court opinion; see also In re Yurko, as cited in the lower court opinion), and the accused enjoys the privilege against self-incrimination (In re Yurko, as cited in the lower court opinion). The rules of evidence apply in these trials (People v. Reed, as cited in the lower court opinion; People v. Myers, as cited in the lower court opinion), and the defendant has a right to be confronted with witnesses against him and to cross-examine those witnesses (People v. Reed, as cited in the lower court opinion; Specht v. Patterson, as cited in the lower court opinion).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause is not limited to trials on guilt or innocence. In Green v. United States, the Court held that a defendant could not be retried for first-degree murder after being acquitted of that charge and convicted of second-degree murder, even though the conviction was reversed on appeal (355 U.S. 184 (1957)). Similarly, in Sapir v. United States, the Court held that a judgment of acquittal for lack of evidence bars retrial (348 U.S. 373 (1955)).

The historical context of the Double Jeopardy Clause also supports its application to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Clause was designed to protect individuals from the harassment and oppression that can result from repeated prosecutions for the same offense (United States v. Ewell, 383 U.S. 116 (1966); Murphy v. Massachusetts, 177 U.S. 155 (1900)). This concern is equally relevant in the context of sentencing proceedings, where a defendant may face significant consequences, including enhanced punishment, based on prior convictions.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has established that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies when a sentencing proceeding involves a trial-like process (Bullington, as discussed in the lower court opinion). The trial of prior conviction allegations under California law meets this standard, as it involves a formal hearing with procedural protections similar to those in a trial on guilt or innocence.

In McDonald v. Massachusetts, the Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of a habitual offender statute, but the Court's reasoning implies that the Double Jeopardy Clause would apply if the statute had involved a trial-like process to determine the defendant's status as a habitual offender (180 U.S. 311 (1901)). Similarly, in this case, the trial of prior conviction allegations involves a trial-like process, and the Double Jeopardy Clause should apply to bar retrial after an acquittal.

The state's interest in retrying prior conviction allegations does not outweigh the defendant's right to be free from double jeopardy. The Supreme Court has recognized that the state's interest in punishing defendants is not unlimited and that the Double Jeopardy Clause is an important check on the state's power (Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184 (1957); United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662 (1896)).

In conclusion, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The trial of prior conviction allegations under California law involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial on guilt or innocence, and the Clause applies to bar retrial after an acquittal. The state's interest in retrying prior conviction allegations does not outweigh the defendant's right to be free from double jeopardy. Therefore, the judgment of the Court of Appeal should be affirmed.
