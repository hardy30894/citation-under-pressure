**ARGUMENT**

Ohio's clemency process violates neither the Due Process Clause nor the Fifth Amendment. Clemency has never been treated as an extension of the trial or appellate process to which due process protections attach, and Ohio's interview procedure is entirely voluntary, imposing no penalty for silence. Both of Woodard's constitutional theories therefore fail.

**I. Woodard Has No Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings Protected by the Due Process Clause.**

From its earliest clemency decisions, this Court has described the pardon power as an act of sovereign grace standing outside the machinery of adjudication—not as a further stage of trial or appeal to which due process attaches. In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833), Chief Justice Marshall explained that a pardon is "an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws, which exempts the individual on whom it is bestowed from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed." That definition forecloses any characterization of clemency as a redetermination of guilt; clemency instead mitigates a judgment that the adjudicatory process has already lawfully produced.

This Court reaffirmed clemency's singular, non-judicial character in *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866), holding that the power "is unlimited, with the exception stated," and may be exercised "at any time after [the offense's] commission, either before legal proceedings are taken, or during their pendency, or after conviction and judgment." Clemency thus operates on a different plane than trial or appeal, unconstrained by the sequential procedural guarantees that due process ordinarily supplies. *Ex parte Grossman*, 267 U.S. 87, 120–21 (1925), confirms the point: the pardoning power was deliberately vested in the Executive, separate from and unreviewable by the courts, because clemency serves interests distinct from those vindicated at trial.

Justice Holmes captured this understanding in *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927): a modern pardon "is not a private act of grace from an individual happening to possess power," but "a part of the Constitutional scheme" reflecting "the determination of the ultimate authority that the public welfare will be better served" by mercy than by continued enforcement of the sentence. Nothing in this description treats clemency as an extension of the adjudicatory process that produced the sentence in the first place.

Due process protections arise only when governmental action threatens an interest to which the individual is already legally entitled. *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961) (the process due depends on "the precise nature of the governmental function involved" and "the private interest that has been affected"). A capital prisoner's hope for clemency is not such an entitlement; as *Wilson*, *Garland*, *Grossman*, and *Biddle* establish, it depends entirely on executive grace, exercised outside the ordinary sequence of adjudication. The court below therefore erred in extending due process scrutiny—even scrutiny it called "minimal" or "barely perceptible"—to a proceeding this Court's precedents have consistently placed beyond the reach of judicial review.

**II. Because Participation in the Clemency Interview Is Voluntary, It Does Not Compel Self-Incrimination in Violation of the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment forbids only *compelled* self-incrimination. In *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 8 (1964), this Court held that the privilege "is fulfilled only when the person is guaranteed the right 'to remain silent unless he chooses to speak in the unfettered exercise of his own will, and to suffer no penalty . . . for such silence.'" Ohio's regulation satisfies that standard precisely: an inmate may request a personal interview or decline one, and if he participates, he remains free to answer as much or as little as he chooses, with no penalty attached to silence.

Where disclosure is voluntary, no Fifth Amendment violation occurs. *Rogers v. United States*, 340 U.S. 367, 371 (1951) (the privilege protects against compulsion, not voluntary testimony); *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951) (the privilege shields only compelled answers that would themselves support a conviction or "furnish a link in the chain of evidence" needed for prosecution). The privilege belongs to the individual, who may invoke or waive it question by question; it does not automatically bar all participation in a proceeding. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 594–97 (1896). Indeed, in *United States v. Sullivan*, 274 U.S. 259, 263–64 (1927), Justice Holmes explained that a person asked for information ordinarily must decline to answer particular questions rather than refuse to appear altogether—precisely the choice Ohio's regulation preserves for every clemency applicant.

Nor can Woodard convert speculation about a hypothetical future prosecution into a present constitutional violation. This Court has repeatedly declined to extend the privilege to remote, contingent risks untethered from any realistic threat of prosecution. *Ullmann v. United States*, 350 U.S. 422, 430–31 (1956). Woodard's own complaint disclaims any effect on his existing conviction or sentence, and Ohio's regulation imposes no penalty for silence or incomplete answers at the interview. Any risk of self-incrimination he identifies is thus wholly theoretical—precisely the kind of abstract injury the privilege does not reach.

Because the clemency interview is optional and carries no adverse consequence for declining it or limiting one's answers, Ohio's procedure does not compel Woodard to be a witness against himself. The judgment below should be reversed.
