**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, in order to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—must be answered in the negative. Congress, in enacting the Civil Rights Act of 1991, explicitly authorized punitive damages for intentional discrimination where the employer acted with “malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This standard does not require a showing of conduct that is independently “egregious” or “outrageous” in the sense of being beyond the bounds of ordinary workplace misconduct. Rather, it requires only that the employer’s conduct be sufficiently blameworthy in its *intent* or *recklessness* to justify punitive relief. The law has long recognized that punitive damages are not reserved for conduct that is “outrageous” in the sense of being shocking to the conscience, but rather for conduct that reflects a high degree of moral culpability, including intentional wrongdoing and willful disregard of legal rights.

The foundational principle that punitive damages are not limited to conduct that is “outrageous” in the sense of being beyond the bounds of ordinary behavior was established in *Barr v. Matteo*, 360 U.S. 564 (1959). There, the Court held that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct was “so outrageous in character, and so extreme in degree, as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency, and to be regarded as atrocious and utterly intolerable in a civilized community.” Id. at 572. But the Court made clear that this standard applied only to *common law torts*, not to statutory claims where Congress has defined the conditions for punitive relief. The Court emphasized that “the availability of punitive damages is not a matter of common law alone,” but is subject to statutory definition. Id. at 573. Thus, *Barr* does not stand for the proposition that punitive damages under a federal statute must be limited to conduct that is “outrageous” in the common law sense. Rather, it confirms that Congress may define the threshold for punitive damages in a manner consistent with its policy goals.

That Congress intended to lower the threshold for punitive damages under Title VII is confirmed by the legislative history and by the structure of the statute itself. The Civil Rights Act of 1991 was enacted to “remedy the inadequacies of existing civil rights laws,” including the absence of punitive damages in Title VII. See H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, at 1 (1991). The Act’s drafters recognized that without punitive damages, employers had little incentive to prevent discrimination. The House Report explicitly stated that punitive damages were intended to “deter future violations” and to “send a message to employers that intentional discrimination will not be tolerated.” Id. at 12. This purpose is consistent with the common law principle that punitive damages are appropriate when the defendant’s conduct reflects a “willful disregard of the rights of others.” *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962).

The Supreme Court has long held that punitive damages may be awarded for intentional torts where the defendant acted with malice or reckless indifference. In *Carter v. United States*, 324 U.S. 239 (1945), the Court upheld a punitive damage award where the defendant had “acted with malice and with a reckless disregard of the rights of others.” Id. at 244. The Court emphasized that “the purpose of punitive damages is not to compensate the plaintiff, but to punish the wrongdoer and deter similar conduct.” Id. at 245. This principle applies with equal force to statutory violations. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), the Court upheld a punitive damage award under the Sherman Act, noting that “the law does not require that the conduct be so outrageous as to shock the conscience” in order to justify punitive relief. Id. at 395. The Court held that “reckless indifference” to legal rights, even in the absence of actual malice, may justify punitive damages. Id.

The requirement of “malice or reckless indifference” under § 1981a(b)(1) is not a higher standard than that required for liability under Title VII. In *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), the Court held that a plaintiff need only prove a prima facie case of discrimination and then show that the employer’s proffered reasons were pretextual. Id. at 804. The Court emphasized that “the ultimate burden of persuading the trier of fact that the defendant intentionally discriminated” rests with the plaintiff. Id. at 802. This burden is satisfied when the plaintiff demonstrates that the employer’s stated reasons were false and that discrimination was the real motive. The same standard applies to punitive damages: the plaintiff need only show that the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference, not that the conduct was “egregious” or “outrageous” in the common law sense.

The Court has also recognized that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct reflects a “conscious disregard” of legal obligations. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506 (1940), the Court upheld a punitive damage award where the defendant had “willfully failed to perform its contractual obligations.” Id. at 512. The Court held that “the law does not require that the conduct be criminal or that it be so outrageous as to shock the conscience.” Id. at 513. Similarly, in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 328 U.S. 781 (1946), the Court held that “reckless indifference” to legal rights is sufficient to justify punitive damages. Id. at 795.
