**Argument**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly recognized that navigability for title turns on the condition of the river at the moment the State entered the Union. That rule follows directly from the equal-footing doctrine and this Court’s repeated insistence that title to riverbeds vests, if at all, at statehood. See *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230-31 (1845); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 57 (1894). This Court has long held that “the title to the beds of navigable waters . . . passed to the State upon its admission to the Union.” *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). Accordingly, the relevant inquiry is whether the stretch of river was navigable “at the time of statehood,” not whether it became navigable later or whether later conditions show the waterway’s present character. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1935); *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551-52 (1981).

I. **The navigability-for-title test is fixed at statehood.**

This Court in *United States v. Utah* framed the inquiry in terms of the river’s condition at the relevant sovereign transition: the question is whether the waters were “used, or were susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce” at the time title could vest. 283 U.S. at 76. The Court reaffirmed that state title depends on navigability “at the time of statehood” in *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14-15. And in *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 55-56 (1926), the Court explained that the decisive issue is whether the waters were navigable when the State came into the Union.

That rule is not a mere evidentiary shortcut; it defines the property right. Because the equal-footing doctrine operates at admission, a state acquires title only to those submerged lands beneath waters that were navigable at that time. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. at 551-52; *Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Mississippi*, 484 U.S. 469, 475-76 (1988). A contrary rule would untether title from the constitutional event that creates it. This Court has never suggested that later improvements, dams, dredging, or changing hydrology can retroactively create state title. To the contrary, the inquiry is whether the river was “navigable in fact” at statehood in its “ordinary condition.” *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76.

II. **The Montana Supreme Court’s approach properly looked to statehood-era evidence.**

The Montana Supreme Court held that the State acquired title to the riverbeds because the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable when Montana entered the Union in 1889. That is exactly the correct constitutional framework. As this Court explained in *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 565 U.S. 576, 592-93 (2012), “navigability for title is determined at the time of statehood” and requires a “segment-specific” inquiry into the relevant stretch of river. The Court also emphasized that later evidence may be useful only insofar as it sheds light on the river’s condition at statehood. *Id.* at 593-94.

So the trial court did not err by focusing on historical evidence; it erred only if it failed to confine that evidence to the proper constitutional moment. But the question presented here asks whether the test itself requires a statehood determination. Under *PPL Montana*, the answer is plainly yes. 565 U.S. at 592-94.

III. **PPL’s contrary position would collapse title into present-day utility.**

PPL’s theory would allow title to depend on contemporary conditions or post-statehood changes in use. That is inconsistent with the settled distinction between navigability for title and navigability for federal regulatory purposes. This Court has repeatedly explained that the two doctrines serve different ends and use different timing rules. See *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 591-92; *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 408-09 (1940). Regulatory navigability may consider reasonable improvements and modern commercial realities, but title navigability asks a different question: whether the river at statehood was capable of use as a highway of commerce. *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 592-93; *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76.

The constitutional structure also confirms the point. Equal footing ensures that new States enter the Union with the same rights as the original States, including ownership of lands beneath navigable waters. *Pollard’s Lessee*, 44 U.S. at 230-31. That equalization occurs at the moment of admission, not decades later. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. at 551-52.

IV. **The judgment should be affirmed.**

Because the constitutional test asks whether the relevant river segment was navigable at statehood, the Montana Supreme Court applied the correct rule. Its judgment should therefore be affirmed. As this Court stated in *PPL Montana*, the “navigability inquiry” must be tied to “the time of statehood,” 565 U.S. at 592, and nothing in the Constitution permits title to arise from later conditions.
