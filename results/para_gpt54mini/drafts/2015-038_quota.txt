ANILCA does not strip the National Park Service of authority to regulate hovercraft use on state-owned waters within the exterior boundaries of an Alaska park unit. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 103(c) is not the categorical jurisdictional bar Sturgeon claims it is. See Sturgeon v. Frost, 577 U.S. 424, 427-28 (2016).

First, the statutory text points away from Sturgeon’s reading. Courts begin with the statute’s text and structure, and they “give effect, if possible, to every clause and word.” Duncan v. Walker, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001); see also United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., 503 U.S. 30, 36 (1992). Section 103(c) states that only “lands, waters, and interests therein” “conveyed to the State” remain subject to state, local, and Native laws, “except as specifically provided otherwise by this Act.” 16 U.S.C. § 3102(c). But ANILCA also preserves federal authority elsewhere. Section 1319 provides that “[n]othing in this Act shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing” existing federal regulatory authority absent specific direction. 16 U.S.C. § 3207. Reading § 103(c) as a blanket divestiture would collide with that text. Courts avoid constructions that render statutory provisions superfluous or internally inconsistent. Duncan, 533 U.S. at 174; see also Corley v. United States, 556 U.S. 303, 314 (2009).

Second, Congress wrote ANILCA against the backdrop of broad federal power in national park units. The National Park Service Organic Act authorizes regulation to conserve park resources and provide for their enjoyment. 54 U.S.C. § 100101(a); see also United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 518-19 (1911) (upholding delegation to regulate federal lands). Sturgeon’s position would invert that settled understanding by turning the mere presence of nonfederal interests within a park unit into a jurisdictional vacuum. That is not how federal land statutes are read. Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529, 542-43 (1976), held that when Congress exercises its power over federal property, it may regulate conduct affecting those lands even where state interests are implicated. The same principle applies here: the question is not who owns the submerged land, but whether Congress withdrew federal regulatory authority. It did not.

Third, this Court has already recognized that ANILCA’s structure preserves significant federal authority even in Alaska. In Alaska v. Babbitt, 72 F.3d 698, 703-04 (9th Cir. 1995), the court upheld federal subsistence regulations implementing ANILCA’s management framework. In John v. United States, 247 F.3d 1032, 1040-41 (9th Cir. 2001) (en banc) (per curiam), and John v. United States, 720 F.3d 1214, 1218-20 (9th Cir. 2013), the court treated ANILCA as accommodating, not displacing, federal management of park-area waters. Those decisions foreclose the notion that § 103(c) creates categorical immunity from NPS rules whenever state title appears in the chain of ownership.

Fourth, Sturgeon’s interpretation is also inconsistent with Supreme Court cases recognizing that federal regulatory power may coexist with state ownership of submerged lands. In PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana, 565 U.S. 576, 592-93 (2012), the Court explained that state title to riverbeds under the equal-footing doctrine does not eliminate federal authority over navigable waters. Likewise, California v. United States, 438 U.S. 645, 658-59 (1978), held that federal reclamation authority may operate alongside state water interests unless Congress clearly says otherwise. No such clear withdrawal appears in ANILCA.

Fifth, Sturgeon’s reliance on the supposed territorial reach of park regulations cannot overcome the ordinary presumption that federal statutes mean what they say. Where Congress intends to divest federal authority, it speaks clearly. See Florida Power & Light Co. v. Lorion, 470 U.S. 729, 737 (1985). Here, Congress instead preserved federal law “except as specifically provided otherwise.” 16 U.S.C. § 3102(c). That proviso confirms that state law may continue to apply, but it does not disable parallel federal regulation where ANILCA or other federal law authorizes it. See Colorado v. United States, 271 U.S. 153, 161-62 (1926).

Finally, deference principles reinforce the same result. Even if ANILCA left some ambiguity, the NPS’s reasonable interpretation would control. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837, 843-44 (1984). And an agency’s later clarification does not defeat deference merely because its view evolved. National Cable & Telecommunications Ass’n v. Brand X Internet Services, 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005). The NPS’s reading harmonizes ANILCA’s provisions, respects Congress’s preserved federal authority, and avoids the anomalous result that Alaska park units would become uniquely regulation-proof for nonfederal lands and waters.

The judgment should be affirmed.
