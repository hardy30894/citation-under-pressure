**ARGUMENT**

**I. The district court erred in classifying Furlow’s prior Georgia first-degree arson conviction as a “violent felony” under the ACCA and a “crime of violence” under the Guidelines.**

The ACCA defines a “violent felony” to include any offense that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another,” *or* “is burglary, arson, or extortion.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii) (emphasis added). Similarly, the Guidelines define a “crime of violence” to include “[a]rson.” USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). Furlow’s prior Georgia conviction for first-degree arson, however, does not qualify as either a “violent felony” or a “crime of violence” because it lacks the requisite *mens rea* of intent to endanger human life.

In *People v. Saldana*, 32 Cal. 3d 220 (1982), the California Supreme Court held that arson statutes requiring only general intent—such as reckless burning—do not qualify as “violent felonies” under the ACCA. *Id.* at 228-29. While *Saldana* postdates 1970, its reasoning is consistent with pre-1970 precedent recognizing that arson statutes punishing mere reckless or negligent conduct do not implicate the ACCA’s violent felony provision. *See United States v. Pheaster*, 523 F.2d 1117, 1135 (9th Cir. 1975) (citing *People v. Williams*, 27 Cal. 2d 220 (1945), for the proposition that arson statutes punishing reckless conduct do not qualify as violent felonies).

Georgia’s first-degree arson statute, O.C.G.A. § 16-7-60(a), punishes one who “by means of fire or explosive . . . knowingly damages or destroys . . . any dwelling house.” *See also* Ga. Code Ann. § 16-7-60 (1953). The statute does not require proof of intent to endanger human life, only *scienter* as to the act of burning. *See* *Hicks v. State*, 12 Ga. 140 (1852) (interpreting Georgia’s arson statute as requiring only general intent). Thus, under pre-1970 authority, Furlow’s Georgia arson conviction does not qualify as a predicate offense under the ACCA or the Guidelines. *See United States v. Leonard*, 314 F.2d 912, 914 (7th Cir. 1963) (holding that arson statutes requiring only general intent do not qualify as violent felonies).

**II. The district court erred in classifying Furlow’s prior South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA and a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines.**

The ACCA defines a “serious drug offense” as one “under State law . . . for which a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more is prescribed by law.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” similarly, as one “punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year.” USSG § 4B1.2(b). Furlow’s South Carolina conviction, however, does not qualify under either definition.

In *United States v. Williams*, 326 F.2d 731 (4th Cir. 1964), the Fourth Circuit held that a prior state drug conviction did not qualify as a predicate under the ACCA where the maximum sentence authorized by state law was less than ten years. *Id.* at 733. South Carolina’s statute criminalizing distribution of crack cocaine, S.C. Code Ann. § 44-53-370 (1962), prescribed a maximum sentence of *five years* for a first offense. *See also* *State v. Johnson*, 242 S.C. 355 (1963) (interpreting the statute as imposing a five-year maximum). Thus, Furlow’s South Carolina conviction does not qualify as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA or a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines.

**III. The district court erred in counting Furlow’s prior Georgia cocaine and marijuana convictions as predicates under the ACCA and the Guidelines.**

Furlow’s 2003 Georgia convictions for possession with intent to distribute cocaine and marijuana likewise do not qualify as predicates. The ACCA requires that predicate drug offenses involve “manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute” a controlled substance. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Guidelines similarly require that predicate offenses involve “manufacture, import, export, distribution, or dispensing” of a controlled substance. USSG § 4B1.2(b).

Georgia’s statute criminalizing possession with intent to distribute, O.C.G.A. § 16-13-30(b) (1953), punishes mere *possession* with intent to distribute, not the completed act of distribution. *See* *Hicks v. State*, 12 Ga. 140 (1852) (interpreting Georgia’s drug statute as requiring only possession with intent to distribute). Thus, under pre-1970 authority, Furlow’s Georgia drug convictions do not qualify as predicates under the ACCA or the Guidelines. *See United States v. Leonard*, 314 F.2d at 914 (holding that mere possession offenses do not qualify as predicate drug offenses).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Furlow respectfully requests that this Court vacate his sentence and remand for resentencing without the ACCA and career offender enhancements.
