# ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act does not strip the National Park Service of authority to enforce a generally applicable, activity-based regulation—the hovercraft ban—merely because the regulation happens to apply to waters within a conservation system unit that flow over state-owned submerged lands. That conclusion follows from the statute's text, from the settled understanding of federal authority over federally reserved lands and navigable waters, and from the presumption against implied curtailment of federal regulatory power.

## I. The Plain Text of § 103(c) Controls, and It Does Not Divest NPS of Regulatory Authority Over Activities on Navigable Waters Within System Units.

"It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain … the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 103(c) limits the *applicability* of System-wide regulations to non-public lands within conservation units; it does not, by its terms, extinguish NPS's independent authority to regulate conduct—such as the operation of hovercraft—on navigable waters that Congress placed within the boundaries of a National Preserve for purposes of resource protection. Sturgeon's reading would require this Court to treat § 103(c) as an implicit repeal of NPS's pre-existing regulatory jurisdiction over navigation-related conduct within Yukon-Charley. But "repeals by implication are not favored," and a court will not presume that Congress silently withdrew a coordinate federal agency's regulatory authority absent a clear statement to that effect. *Posadas v. National City Bank*, 296 U.S. 497, 503 (1936). Nothing in § 103(c)'s text works such a withdrawal here.

## II. Congress's Property Clause Power and the Federal Navigational Servitude Support NPS Authority Over Conduct on Navigable Waters Within Park Boundaries, Regardless of Who Holds Title to the Submerged Lands.

Even if Alaska owns the submerged lands and waters of the Nation River, that title is not inconsistent with continued federal regulatory authority over navigation-related conduct there. The United States has long possessed a "dominant servitude" over the nation's navigable waters that exists independent of, and is not defeated by, state title to the streambed. *United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co.*, 229 U.S. 53, 62 (1913) (holding that title to submerged lands under navigable waters is "subject to the paramount authority of Congress to control their navigation"). This servitude authorizes federal regulation of activity on such waters even where a State holds proprietary title. *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 426 (1940). The principle traces to *United States v. Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co.*, 174 U.S. 690, 703 (1899), which recognized that a State's control over waters within its borders does not defeat the federal government's paramount interest in regulating navigation on streams subject to that interest.

That authority is reinforced by Congress's power under the Property Clause to protect federal reservations from activity, wherever situated, that threatens the purposes for which the reservation was created. In *Camfield v. United States*, 167 U.S. 518, 525–26 (1897), the Court sustained federal regulation reaching even privately owned land adjoining public land because Congress may act to prevent interference with the use and enjoyment of the public domain. And in *Light v. United States*, 220 U.S. 523, 537 (1911), the Court confirmed that the United States, in administering reserved lands for the purposes Congress assigned them, may "prescribe rules and regulations" governing the use of those lands and the conduct that occurs on them, notwithstanding state law to the contrary. *See also Utah Power & Light Co. v. United States*, 243 U.S. 389, 404 (1917) (state law cannot "deprive the United States of its power of disposal or of control" over its reservations). Yukon-Charley was established by Congress to protect the region's Nation River corridor; NPS's hovercraft regulation implements that purpose directly, and neither *Camfield* nor *Light* countenances the notion that a state property interest in submerged land defeats such protective regulation.

## III. Sturgeon's Asserted Right to Navigate Is Not Absolute and Does Not Exempt Him from Reasonable, Generally Applicable Federal Regulation.

Sturgeon's invocation of a personal right "to use the navigable waters of the United States, however they may penetrate the territory of the several States," *Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. 36, 79 (1872), does not aid him. That right of navigation has always coexisted with, and been subordinate to, Congress's paramount authority over navigable waters in the exercise of its commerce power. *Gilman v. Philadelphia*, 70 U.S. 713, 724–25 (1866) (Congress's power over navigable waters is "vested in a single government" and cannot be impaired by conflicting private or local claims of right). A generally applicable, non-discriminatory equipment regulation—prohibiting a particular mode of watercraft to protect wildlife and preserve backcountry character—falls squarely within the government's authority to regulate navigation-related conduct on waters lying within a reservation created for conservation purposes. Sturgeon's right to navigate does not include a right to navigate by hovercraft free of federal regulation simply because Alaska may hold title to the streambed beneath him.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, and because § 103(c)'s plain text works no implied divestiture of NPS's independent regulatory authority over navigation-related conduct within System units, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
