ARGUMENT

I.   THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO WORKERS INJURED AS A RESULT OF OPERATIONS CONDUCTED ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain language of the statute requires a direct connection between the injury and operations on the outer Continental Shelf. The Fifth Circuit has correctly interpreted this provision to require that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it. Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc); see also Becker v. Tidewater, Inc., 586 F.3d 358, 366-67 (5th Cir. 2009); Pickett v. Petroleum Helicopters, Inc., 266 F.3d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 2001).

The Supreme Court's dictum in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), supports this interpretation. Although the Court did not directly address the issue, it noted that "Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA's coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed." Id. at 219. The Court further stated that § 1333(b) "superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement." Id. at 219 n.2. This language suggests that the OCSLA's coverage is tied to the location of the injury.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case, adopting a "substantial nexus" test, is inconsistent with the plain language of the statute and the Supreme Court's dictum in Tallentire. The "substantial nexus" test would extend coverage to injuries that occur on land, as long as there is a connection between the injury and operations on the outer Continental Shelf. This interpretation is too broad and ignores the statutory requirement that the injury occur "as the result of" operations on the outer Continental Shelf. See Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray (Herb's Welding I), 470 U.S. 414, 425-26 (1985) (noting that the OCSLA's coverage is limited by its geographic scope).

Other circuits have also recognized the importance of the location of the injury in determining OCSLA coverage. See, e.g., Sisson v. Davis & Sons, Inc., 131 F.3d 555, 558 (5th Cir. 1998) (holding that an injury on a vessel in navigable waters was not covered under the OCSLA because it was not related to operations on the outer Continental Shelf); cf. Smith v. Penrod Drilling Corp., 960 F.2d 456, 459-60 (5th Cir. 1992) (holding that an injury on a jack-up rig in navigable waters was covered under the OCSLA because it was related to operations on the outer Continental Shelf).

II.  THE BRB CORRECTLY DENIED BENEFITS UNDER THE LHWCA BECAUSE LA CONCHITA IS NOT A MARITIME SITES

The LHWCA requires that an injury occur on a maritime situs, which includes "any adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel." 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Ninth Circuit has adopted a "functional relationship" test to determine whether a facility is a maritime situs. Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978). Under this test, the facility must be "used as an integral part of longshoring operations." Id.

La Conchita does not meet this test. Although it is located near the ocean, it is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks and has no direct access to any pier, dock, or other loading facility. See Motoviloff v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 692 F.2d 87, 89 (9th Cir. 1982) (holding that a facility was not a maritime situs because it was not adjacent to navigable waters). The primary purpose of La Conchita is to process crude oil slurry, a non-maritime activity. Herb's Welding I, 470 U.S. at 422-24.

The Ninth Circuit has consistently applied the "functional relationship" test to determine whether a facility is a maritime situs. See, e.g., Williams v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 825 F.2d 246, 248-49 (9th Cir. 1987) (holding that a facility was not a maritime situs because it was not used for longshoring operations); Hurston v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 989 F.2d 1547, 1550-51 (9th Cir. 1993) (holding that a facility was a maritime situs because it was used for repairing vessels).

The petitioner's reliance on P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford, 444 U.S. 69 (1979), is misplaced. Pfeiffer held that certain warehousemen were "employees" under the LHWCA because they were engaged in maritime employment, but it did not address the situs requirement. Id. at 83. In this case, the situs requirement is dispositive, and La Conchita does not meet the test.

CONCLUSION

The OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to workers injured as a result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The BRB correctly denied benefits under the LHWCA because La Conchita is not a maritime situs. The Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the BRB's denial of benefits under the OCSLA should be reinstated.
