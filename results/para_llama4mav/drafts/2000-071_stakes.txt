The exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at Milford Central School did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy, which prohibits use of its facilities for religious purposes, is a constitutional restriction on speech in a limited public forum. 

In a limited public forum, the government may impose reasonable restrictions on speech that are viewpoint-neutral and related to the purpose of the forum. See Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). The policy at issue here excludes "religious purposes" from the scope of permissible uses, and the school applied this policy to exclude the Good News Club because its activities were deemed to be "the equivalent of religious worship" or "religious instruction and prayer." 

The Court of Appeals correctly held that the school's exclusion of the Club was not viewpoint discrimination. The Club's activities, which included singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying, were not merely a religious viewpoint on a secular subject, but rather were "quintessentially religious" in nature. See Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch., 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000). The school's policy did not exclude a particular viewpoint on a subject, but rather excluded a particular subject - religious instruction and worship - from the forum. 

The Supreme Court has recognized that the government may exclude certain subjects from a limited public forum without violating the First Amendment. See Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37, 49 (1983) (holding that a school district could limit access to its internal mail system to certain groups without creating a public forum). The school's exclusion of the Good News Club was based on the subject matter of its activities, not its viewpoint, and was therefore constitutional.

Furthermore, permitting the Club's activities would have raised Establishment Clause concerns. The Establishment Clause prohibits government endorsement or promotion of religion. See County of Allegheny v. ACLU, 492 U.S. 573, 592 (1989). By allowing a religious organization to use its facilities for religious instruction and worship, the school would have been seen as endorsing or promoting the organization's religious message. The school's exclusion of the Club was therefore justified by its concern for avoiding an Establishment Clause violation.

In sum, Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club's First Amendment free speech rights by excluding it from meeting after hours at the school. The school's community use policy is a constitutional restriction on speech in a limited public forum, and the exclusion of the Club was based on the subject matter of its activities, not its viewpoint. The school's concern for avoiding an Establishment Clause violation also justified its exclusion of the Club.
