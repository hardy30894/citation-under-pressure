**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration properly cited Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc. for violations that caused death and injury aboard Rig 52. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act preempted OSHA’s jurisdiction. Under the plain text of the Act and settled pre-1970 precedent, Rig 52 was a “workplace” in a State, and the Coast Guard had not “exercise[d]” statutory authority over the specific conditions that OSHA cited.

**I. Rig 52 Is a Workplace Under the Act**

The OSH Act applies to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). This Court has long instructed that exemptions from remedial statutes must be narrowly construed. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940); *Piedmont & Northern Ry. Co. v. ICC*, 286 U.S. 196, 205–06 (1932). Rig 52 was located in Louisiana’s territorial waters, and the work performed there—drilling for oil from a stationary platform—constituted employment in a workplace in a State. Nothing in the Act excludes a floating platform used for mineral extraction.

Mallard’s argument that Rig 52 was a “vessel in navigation” fails. A vessel is “every description of watercraft or other artificial contrivance used, or capable of being used, as a means of transportation on water.” *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 559 (1870). Rig 52 was not a means of transportation. It was moored to the seabed and connected to the well by drill pipe; its sole function was to extract oil. The ability to be towed to a new location does not make it a transportation vehicle. *Desper v. Starved Rock Ferry Co.*, 342 U.S. 187, 191 (1952) (ferry that actively navigated was a vessel because it carried passengers). This Court has consistently distinguished work platforms from vessels. *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565, 571–72 (1944) (floating derrick not a vessel); *Gahagan Construction Corp. v. Armao*, 350 U.S. 879 (1955) (per curiam) (dredge not a vessel); *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251, 260 (1940) (worker whose duties were tied to shore-based facility was not a seaman). In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 360 (1969), this Court recognized that the risks of offshore oil production are risks of production, not navigation. That rationale applies with equal force to a moored drilling barge.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction**

Section 4(b)(1) excludes only “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1) (emphasis added). The exemption is tied to *actual, existing regulation* of specific conditions, not to a generalized grant of maritime authority. *Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R.R. Co.*, 272 U.S. 605, 612–13 (1926) (preemption flows from an agency’s actual exercise of power over a subject matter, producing comprehensive rules that leave no room for additional regulation). Where the other agency has not prescribed or enforced standards covering the conditions at issue, section 4(b)(1) has no application.

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is minimal. Congress granted it rulemaking power over only fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and emergency locating equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. That is a far cry from comprehensive regulation of working conditions. The Coast Guard investigated the explosion at Rig 52 but issued no citation, and it has never promulgated a standard governing well drilling, blowout prevention, or the other hazards involved here. Because the Coast Guard has not exercised statutory authority over these working conditions, the section 4(b)(1) exclusion does not apply.

The Fifth Circuit held that the Coast Guard’s general safety-of-life-at-sea mission preempts OSHA. That conclusion finds no support in this Court’s preemption jurisprudence. Preemption requires a “clear and manifest purpose” of Congress to displace other regulation. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941). The OSH Act contains no such purpose; rather, section 4(b)(1) contemplates that OSHA yields only where another agency has actually occupied a particular subject. A rule that an agency’s bare statutory jurisdiction suffices would render the word “exercise” surplusage and would allow an agency that has never regulated a hazard to block all oversight.

**III. The Seaman Label Does Not Compel a Different Result**

The label “seaman” does not control. The OSH Act contains no exemption for seamen as a class; it exempts working conditions actually regulated by another federal agency. Even if Rig 52’s employees could be considered seamen under some maritime definition, the inquiry remains whether the Coast Guard regulates the conditions OSHA cited. It does not. Moreover, these oilfield workers are not the traditional maritime workers that seamen’s remedies were designed to protect. *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co.*, 309 U.S. at 260–61; *Norton*, 321 U.S. at 571–72. The judgment below should be reversed.
