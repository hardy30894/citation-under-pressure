**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 113(f)(3)(B) Applies Only to Settlements That Resolve CERCLA Liability**

Congress built CERCLA's remedial scheme in two tiers. Section 107(a) is the Act's general liability provision, making any potentially responsible party "liable for . . . all costs of removal or remedial action," 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4)(A), while section 113(f) provides a narrower "contribution" remedy for parties that have already paid another's share. See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 161–62 (2004). This Court has recognized that these are "clearly distinct" causes of action, each with its own triggering conditions and its own limitations period. United States v. Atlantic Research Corp., 551 U.S. 128, 138 (2007). Section 113(f)(3)(B) is triggered only when a party "has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement." 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B).

That text does not sweep in every settlement that happens to require environmental cleanup. "Response action" and "costs of such action" are terms drawn from, and defined by, CERCLA itself. A settlement resolving liability under an entirely different statute—here, the Clean Water Act—does not resolve liability "for . . . a response action" within the meaning of section 113(f)(3)(B) merely because the remedial work required by that settlement overlaps with what CERCLA might also demand. As this Court has cautioned, CERCLA is "not a model of legislative craftsmanship," Key Tronic Corp. v. United States, 511 U.S. 809, 814 (1994), and that caution counsels against reading section 113(f)(3)(B) to reach beyond its plain terms into settlements Congress never contemplated. The provision's function is to reward parties that settle *CERCLA* liability with EPA or a State by insulating them from further CERCLA contribution suits, § 9613(f)(2); it cannot logically extend that reward—and its attendant three-year clock—to a party that has settled liability under a wholly separate regulatory regime.

**II. The 2004 Consent Decree Resolved Guam's Liability Under the Clean Water Act, Not CERCLA**

The Court of Appeals nonetheless held that the 2004 Consent Decree triggered section 113(f)(3)(B) because its substantive terms—closure of the dump and construction of a cover system—resembled a CERCLA remedial action. Pet. App. 22–23. But EPA sued Guam exclusively under the Clean Water Act, alleging that Guam was "discharging pollutants . . . into waters of the United States without obtaining a permit." Complaint ¶ 26. Guam settled that Clean Water Act suit, and only that suit. The Decree nowhere invokes CERCLA, nowhere cites section 107 or section 113, and nowhere purports to resolve Guam's liability for a CERCLA response action. It provides only that it is "based on the pleadings . . . and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." Consent Decree 3.

The court of appeals brushed aside that disclaimer, reasoning that "parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability." Pet. App. 22 (quoting Asarco, 866 F.3d at 1123). Whatever force that reasoning carries where a CERCLA-authorized settlement is at issue, it cannot substitute for the threshold requirement that the settlement resolve *CERCLA* liability in the first place. A party cannot "resolve" liability for a statute under which it was never sued. Other courts have recognized as much, finding that a consent decree does not trigger section 113(f)(3)(B) where, as here, "the plaintiff had not conceded the question of its liability." Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015). The Sixth Circuit's approach, not the D.C. Circuit's, honors Congress's careful, two-track design—a design this Court has repeatedly enforced by refusing to let one CERCLA provision's requirements be satisfied by proceedings brought under an entirely different one. See Cooper Industries, 543 U.S. at 166–67 (holding that section 113(f)(1) contribution is unavailable absent a qualifying "civil action" under sections 106 or 107, and declining to stretch the provision's plain terms to reach unlitigated liabilities).

**III. Because No CERCLA Settlement Has Resolved Guam's Liability, Guam's Section 107 Claim Is Timely**

Because the 2004 Consent Decree never resolved Guam's CERCLA liability, Guam never acquired a section 113(f)(3)(B) contribution right, and the three-year clock of section 9613(g)(3) never began to run. Guam therefore remains free to pursue the remedy CERCLA actually affords a party in its position: a section 107 cost-recovery action, subject to the Act's six-year limitations period for remedial actions. § 9613(g)(2)(B). That result accords with Congress's purpose in enacting CERCLA "in response to the serious environmental and health risks posed by industrial pollution," United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998), and with CERCLA's animating principle that responsible parties, not innocent territories left to administer a landfill their sovereign built and filled with military waste, should "foot the bill" for contamination they did not create. General Electric Co. v. Jackson, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
