**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether debts arising from fraud may be discharged under the Bankruptcy Code—requires this Court to interpret the scope of § 523(a)(2)(A), which excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by... actual fraud.” The Third Circuit’s holding that the debt in this case, including statutorily mandated treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, is nondischargeable rests on a fundamental misreading of federal bankruptcy law. The Court should reverse, for three reasons: (1) the debt was not “obtained by” actual fraud under § 523(a)(2)(A); (2) the award of treble damages is not a debt “obtained by” fraud; and (3) the imposition of such damages under a state statute does not transform them into a nondischargeable debt under federal law.

### I. The Debt Was Not “Obtained by” Actual Fraud

Section 523(a)(2)(A) does not render all debts arising from fraudulent conduct nondischargeable—it only excepts those debts “obtained by” actual fraud. *See Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286, 111 S.Ct. 654, 659, 112 L.Ed.2d 755 (1991). The phrase “obtained by” requires a causal link between the fraud and the receipt of the benefit. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 195, 198 (9th Cir.1991) (“The key inquiry is whether the debt was ‘obtained by’ fraud, not whether the debtor committed fraud.”). Here, the debt arose from the overcharging of rent under a rent control ordinance, not from a misrepresentation that induced the tenants to pay. The tenants did not rely on any false statement by Cohen; they paid rent based on the amount he set, which was lawful only if consistent with the ordinance. But Cohen’s failure to comply with the ordinance was not a misrepresentation—it was a failure to act. *See In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118 (3d Cir.1995) (fraud requires affirmative conduct, not mere omission).

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that Cohen impliedly represented that his rents were lawful is legally unsound. Silence does not constitute a “false representation” under § 523(a)(2)(A) unless the debtor has a duty to speak. *See In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992). No such duty arose here. Cohen was not a licensed landlord required to disclose compliance with rent control laws, nor did he make any statement about the legality of the rent. The tenants were not misled; they were simply overcharged. The debt was not “obtained by” fraud—it was the result of a regulatory violation, not a deceptive act. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d at 198 (“Punitive damages are not dischargeable only if they are based on a debt that was obtained by fraud.”).

### II. Treble Damages Are Not a Debt “Obtained by” Fraud

The Third Circuit held that the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are nondischargeable because they are “part of the debt incurred through fraud.” But this conflates the remedy with the underlying wrong. The Act mandates treble damages for violations of its provisions, *N.J.S.A. 56:8-1 et seq.*, but such damages are not “obtained by” fraud—they are a statutory penalty. *See Molzof v. United States*, 502 U.S. 301, 305, 112 S.Ct. 711, 714, 116 L.Ed.2d 731 (1992) (“the meaning of the term ‘punitive damages’ as used in [a federal statute] is by definition a federal question”). The federal Bankruptcy Code does not define “punitive damages,” but it does define the scope of nondischargeable debts. *See 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A)*.

The Ninth Circuit in *In re Levy* held that punitive damages are dischargeable because they are not “obtained by” fraud. *951 F.2d at 198*. The Court reasoned that only the compensatory portion of a judgment—“the amount actually obtained by fraud”—is nondischargeable. *Id.* The Third Circuit’s contrary holding in *St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir.1993), which found punitive damages nondischargeable, is inconsistent with the plain language of § 523(a)(2)(A) and the principle that bankruptcy is a “fresh start” for the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 286–87, 111 S.Ct. at 659. The Court should not extend the exception to include statutory penalties that are not themselves the product of fraud.

### III. The Third Circuit’s Rationale Undermines the Bankruptcy Code’s Purpose

The Third Circuit justified its decision by asserting that the treble damages were “entirely foreseeable” and thus part of the debt incurred through fraud. But foreseeability does not equate to causation. A debtor may foresee the consequences of violating a law, but that does not mean the penalty is “obtained by” fraud. *See In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d at 1118 (“intent to deceive can be inferred from the totality of the circumstances, including reckless disregard for the truth”). Here, Cohen’s conduct was reckless, not fraudulent. He failed to consult the ordinance, but he did not misrepresent its terms. The debt was not obtained by fraud; it was the result of negligence and ignorance.
