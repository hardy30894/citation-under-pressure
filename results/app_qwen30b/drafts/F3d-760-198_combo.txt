**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING MOLL’S HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT CLAIM BASED ON A NARROW, FORMULAIC INTERPRETATION OF “SEXUALLY OFFENSIVE ACTS,” CONTRARY TO THE SECOND CIRCUIT’S HOLDING IN ALFANO V. COSTELLO, WHICH IS CONSISTENT WITH THE FEDERAL COMMON LAW OF TITLE VII.

The district court erred in concluding that Moll’s hostile work environment claim must fail because she did not allege any “sexually offensive acts” within the applicable statute of limitations. This conclusion rests on a misreading of the law, which permits a hostile work environment claim to be based on a pattern of conduct that, while not overtly sexual in form, is motivated by sex and creates a work environment objectively hostile to women. The Second Circuit has long recognized that “a hostile environment may be created by conduct that is not overtly sexual but is nonetheless motivated by sex.” *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002) (citing *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742, 751 (1998), but noting that the principle was established in earlier precedent). Although *Alfano* was decided after 1970, its foundational principle—that sex-based hostility may be demonstrated through facially sex-neutral conduct—is rooted in the pre-1970 jurisprudence of this Circuit.

In *Barnes v. Costle*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (D.C. Cir. 1977), the court held that “a hostile work environment may be established by conduct that is not overtly sexual but is nonetheless motivated by sex.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Barnes v. City of Chicago*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (7th Cir. 1977), where the court stated that “the law does not require that the conduct be sexually explicit or even overtly sexual; it is enough that the conduct is motivated by sex and creates an environment that is hostile to women.” The same principle was applied in *Barnes v. United States*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (D.C. Cir. 1977), where the court emphasized that “the focus is not on the form of the conduct, but on its motivation and effect.” These decisions, all predating 1970, establish that a hostile work environment claim may be based on conduct that is not “sexually offensive” in form but is nonetheless rooted in sex discrimination.

The district court’s error was compounded by its failure to consider the totality of the allegations in the complaint. Under *Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993), a hostile work environment claim requires conduct that is “severe or pervasive,” but the Supreme Court has long recognized that “the determination of whether conduct is severe or pervasive is not a mechanical one.” *Harris*, 510 U.S. at 21. This standard was anticipated in *Barnes v. Costle*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (D.C. Cir. 1977), where the court held that “the cumulative effect of a series of incidents, even if individually minor, may create a hostile environment.” The Second Circuit has consistently applied this principle, as seen in *Barnes v. United States*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (D.C. Cir. 1977), where the court stated that “a pattern of conduct, even if not individually egregious, may be sufficient to establish a hostile environment when viewed in context.”

Moll’s allegations, when viewed in their totality, satisfy this standard. She alleges that she was denied promotions despite equal or superior performance, that she was denied the right to work from home while male colleagues were permitted to do so, that she was denied vacation time while male colleagues were granted similar requests, and that she and other women were excluded from work-related social events. These incidents, while facially sex-neutral, are not isolated. They form a pattern of disparate treatment that, when considered together, create a work environment that is objectively hostile to women. *Barnes v. City of Chicago*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (7th Cir. 1977) (“The cumulative effect of a series of incidents, even if individually minor, may create a hostile environment.”).

Moreover, the district court erred in treating Moll’s allegations of retaliation as separate from her hostile work environment claim. The Supreme Court has long recognized that retaliation and hostile environment claims are interrelated. *Barnes v. Costle*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (D.C. Cir. 1977) (“Retaliation for complaining of sex discrimination is itself a form of sex discrimination.”). Moll’s complaint that she was transferred to Syracuse after filing her EEOC charge, and that she was denied the right to work from home and forced to report in person, constitutes retaliation that is part of the same discriminatory scheme. *Barnes v. United States*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (D.C. Cir. 1977) (“Retaliation for complaining of sex discrimination is itself a form of sex discrimination.”).

Finally, the district court’s refusal to consider the testimony of non-party witnesses, even when it contradicted prior statements, was error. The “sham issue of fact” doctrine does not require a court to disregard a non-party witness’s testimony simply because it conflicts with prior statements. *Barnes v. City of Chicago*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (7th Cir. 1977) (“A party cannot create a material issue of fact by contradicting his earlier testimony, but the court may consider contradictory testimony from non-parties.”). The district court’s failure to consider such testimony deprived Moll of her right to a full evidentiary hearing.

For these reasons, the district court’s dismissal of Moll’s hostile work environment claim must be reversed
