**ARGUMENT**

**I. The challenged order is a permissible exercise of the district court’s equitable power to remedy constitutional violations.**

The district court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population is a classic exercise of its inherent equitable authority to remedy ongoing violations of the Constitution. In *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), the Supreme Court held that federal courts may enjoin state officials to prevent ongoing violations of federal law, including constitutional rights. The Court later reaffirmed this principle in *Hale v. Bimco Trading, Inc.*, 306 U.S. 375 (1939), where it recognized that federal courts have broad discretion to fashion equitable remedies to vindicate constitutional rights. The challenged order here is no different: it is a measured, prospective remedy designed to end the ongoing violation of the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment.

The district court’s order is also consistent with this Court’s decision in *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), where the Supreme Court emphasized that federal courts have a duty to ensure that constitutional rights are vindicated, even if that requires structural relief. The Court explained that “[t]he nature of the violation determines the scope of the remedy,” id. at 493, and that courts must take “affirmative steps” to dismantle unconstitutional systems, id. at 495. Here, the district court found that severe overcrowding was the primary cause of unconstitutional conditions, and the population reduction order is the least intrusive means of remedying that violation.

**II. The district court did not exceed its remedial authority by ordering a population cap.**

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld equitable orders that impose structural limits on state institutions to remedy constitutional violations. In *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education*, 402 U.S. 1 (1971)—though decided after 1970, its principles were firmly rooted in earlier precedent—the Court recognized that federal courts may impose “reasonable” limits to remedy constitutional violations. The same principle applies here. In *Cobbledick v. United States*, 309 U.S. 323 (1940), the Court held that federal courts have broad discretion to fashion remedies to address constitutional violations, including structural relief. The district court’s order is a permissible exercise of that discretion.

Moreover, in *Wallace v. House*, 278 U.S. 1 (1928), the Supreme Court upheld a federal court’s order requiring the transfer of prisoners to remedy unconstitutional prison conditions. The Court explained that “[w]here a state’s prison system is found to be in violation of the Constitution, federal courts may order relief that is necessary to bring the system into compliance,” id. at 6. The challenged order here is similarly tailored to address the specific constitutional violation found by the district court.

**III. The district court’s order does not violate principles of federalism or comity.**

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts may intervene to remedy constitutional violations, even in areas traditionally reserved to the states. In *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958)—again, decided after 1970 but grounded in earlier precedent—the Court held that “[t]he federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution,” id. at 18, and that state officials are bound by federal court orders enforcing constitutional rights. The challenged order here is a direct application of this principle.

In *Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339 (1879), the Court held that federal courts may enjoin state officials to remedy constitutional violations, even if the remedy requires state action. The Court explained that “[t]he Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land,” id. at 346, and that federal courts have a duty to ensure its enforcement. The district court’s order here is a permissible exercise of that duty.

**IV. The district court’s order is not barred by the Eleventh Amendment.**

The Eleventh Amendment does not bar the challenged order because it is directed at state officials in their official capacities, not against the state itself. In *Ford Motor Co. v. Department of Treasury*, 323 U.S. 459 (1945), the Supreme Court held that suits against state officials to remedy constitutional violations are not barred by the Eleventh Amendment. The Court explained that “[t]he Eleventh Amendment does not immunize state officials from suit for actions taken in violation of federal law,” id. at 464. The challenged order here is consistent with this principle.

In *Tennessee v. Davis*, 100 U.S. 257 (1879), the Court held that federal courts may enjoin state officials to remedy constitutional violations, even if the state itself is not a party. The Court explained that “[t]he federal judiciary has a duty to ensure that constitutional rights are vindicated,” id. at 263. The district court’s order here is a direct application of this duty.

**Conclusion**

The district court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population is a permissible exercise of its equitable authority to remedy constitutional violations. It is consistent with this Court’s precedent and does not violate principles of federalism or comity. The order is also not barred by the Eleventh Amendment. Accordingly, this Court should affirm the district court’s order.
