The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. As the Supreme Court held in Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678 (2001), interpreting the statute to permit indefinite detention would raise serious constitutional concerns. 533 U.S. at 699. To avoid these concerns, the Court construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation. Id. 

The Court's holding is grounded in the principle that indefinite detention of aliens who have been admitted to the United States but subsequently ordered removed raises significant due process concerns. Id. at 690-91. The statute's broad application to aliens ordered removed for various reasons, including minor infractions like tourist visa violations, further supports the conclusion that indefinite detention is not authorized. Id. at 691.

In Zadvydas, the Court established a framework for determining when post-removal-period detention becomes unreasonable. After a presumptively reasonable six-month period, the government must provide evidence to rebut an alien's showing that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. Id. at 701. As the period of prior post-removal confinement grows, what counts as the "reasonably foreseeable future" conversely shrinks. Id.

Here, Zadvydas has been in INS custody for an extended period beyond the 90-day removal period, and efforts to deport him have failed. The District Court found that Zadvydas "will never be deported because there is no place to send him." Zadvydas v. Caplinger, 986 F. Supp. 1011, 1027 (E.D. La. 1997). Under the Supreme Court's framework, Zadvydas has provided good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, and the government has not rebutted this showing.

The Fifth Circuit's prior holding that the government may detain a resident alien based on danger to the community or risk of flight while good faith efforts to effectuate the alien's deportation continue is no longer controlling. Zadvydas v. Underdown, 185 F.3d 279, 297 (5th Cir. 1999). The Supreme Court rejected this standard, holding that it demands more than the statute can bear. Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 702.

In light of the Supreme Court's decision, the District Court's order granting habeas relief should be affirmed, with the modification that it does not preclude the INS from seeking to return Zadvydas to custody or modify the conditions of his release if there is a substantial likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. The statute, as construed by the Supreme Court, does not permit indefinite detention, and Zadvydas's continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.
