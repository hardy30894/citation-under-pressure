**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This principle ensures that a defendant is not subjected to litigation in a jurisdiction where it has not purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities there. When the claims do not stem from the defendant’s forum-related conduct, the exercise of jurisdiction violates due process. The Montana Supreme Court’s decision in this case—affirming jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company based solely on the sale of parts and advertising in Montana—contravenes this foundational rule and must be reversed.

**I. The Claims Do Not Arise Out of or Relate to Ford’s Contacts with Montana**

The Montana Supreme Court held that Ford’s sale of parts and advertising in Montana constituted sufficient “availing” to justify specific jurisdiction. But the claims here—design defect, failure to warn, and negligence—arise from a tire tread separation that occurred in Montana after the vehicle had been sold through a chain of transactions that began in Washington and passed through Oregon. *See* *Ford Motor Co. v. Bandemer*, 140 S. Ct. 1700, 1704 (2020) (per curiam) (summarizing facts). Ford did not sell the vehicle in Montana, nor did it design or manufacture the tire in Montana. The vehicle was assembled in Kentucky and first sold to a Washington dealer. The Montana resident who later purchased the vehicle did so from a private party, not Ford. Thus, Ford’s only contacts with Montana were the sale of parts and advertising—activities that had no causal connection to the accident or the plaintiff’s injuries.

This is precisely the kind of “attenuated” or “unrelated” contact that *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980), held insufficient to justify jurisdiction. There, the Court rejected jurisdiction over a New York-based auto dealer who sold a defective car in Oklahoma, even though the car had been advertised in Oklahoma newspapers. The Court emphasized that “the foreseeability that is critical to due process is not the mere likelihood that a product will find its way into the forum State, but foreseeability that the product’s presence in the state will give rise to a claim.” *Id.* at 297. Here, Ford could not reasonably foresee that a vehicle sold in Washington and later transferred to Montana would be driven in Montana in a way that would trigger a design defect claim. The causal chain is too attenuated.

Similarly, in *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985), the Court held that jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant was proper only because the defendant had entered into a long-term franchise agreement with a Florida-based corporation, which included ongoing obligations and communications. The defendant’s contacts were not merely passive or incidental but were “purposeful” and “related to the cause of action.” *Id.* at 475. By contrast, Ford’s sale of parts and advertising in Montana were not part of a continuing relationship with Montana residents, nor were they tied to the vehicle’s use or the injury. They were peripheral and unrelated.

**II. The Montana Supreme Court’s “Availment” Theory Is Contrary to Precedent**

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed itself” of the privilege of doing business in Montana by selling parts and advertising is legally unsound. *See* *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 475 Mont. 1, 277 P.3d 1001, 1007 (2012). The “availing” doctrine requires that the defendant’s forum-related conduct be “purposefully directed” toward the forum state and that the claims arise from that conduct. *See* *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 112 (1987). The Court in *Asahi* rejected jurisdiction over a Japanese manufacturer of a motorcycle part, even though the part was sold in California, because the manufacturer had no direct contact with California and the injury occurred there. The Court emphasized that “the defendant’s conduct must create a substantial connection with the forum State.” *Id.* at 113.

Here, Ford’s sale of parts to dealers in Montana does not create a substantial connection to the injury. The parts were not used in the vehicle at issue, and the tire tread failure was not caused by a defect in any part sold in Montana. The Montana Supreme Court’s reasoning effectively equates any sale of parts in a state with a “purposeful availment” of the forum, regardless of whether the product is used there or whether the injury is related to the sale. This would allow any state to assert jurisdiction over any manufacturer based on the mere sale of parts—even if the product is never used in that state. Such a rule would violate the constitutional limits on jurisdiction.

**III. The Due Process Clause Requires a Causal Nexus**

The Due Process Clause demands a “causal nexus” between the defendant’s forum contacts and the plaintiff’s claims. *See* *Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 789 (1984). In *Calder*, the Court upheld jurisdiction over a California-based journalist who published a defamatory article in California that was intended to harm a plaintiff in Florida. The Court found that the defendant’s conduct was “targeted” at the forum state. *Id.* at 790. Here, Ford’s advertising and part sales were not targeted at Montana residents or the vehicle’s use in Montana. The vehicle was not even sold in Montana. The causal link is absent.
