The petitioner, Carol Anne Bond, lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on grounds that the statute exceeds the federal government's enumerated powers and violates the Tenth Amendment. The Tenth Amendment challenge is foreclosed by Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), where the Supreme Court held that private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims because the Amendment is a structural provision that protects the states, not individuals. Id. at 143-144. The Court's holding in Tennessee Electric Power Co. is not dicta, as it was an alternative ground for the decision, and thus binds this Court. See Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently held that the Treaty Power is a broad grant of authority that allows Congress to enact legislation necessary to implement treaties. Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), established that the Treaty Power is not limited by the enumerated powers of Congress, and that legislation enacted pursuant to a treaty is constitutional if it is "necessary and proper" to execute the treaty. Id. at 432. The Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act, which includes 18 U.S.C. § 229, is a valid exercise of the Treaty Power, as it implements the United States' obligations under the Chemical Weapons Convention. See United States v. Lue, 134 F.3d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1998).

The Necessary and Proper Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18, grants Congress the authority to enact legislation that is necessary to execute its enumerated powers, including the Treaty Power. See McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. 316 (1819). In this case, 18 U.S.C. § 229 is a necessary and proper means to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention, and thus is a valid exercise of Congress's authority under the Necessary and Proper Clause. See United States v. Ferreira, 275 F.3d 1020, 1027-28 (11th Cir. 2001).

Furthermore, Bond's challenge to the statute on vagueness grounds also fails. The statute is not vague as applied to her conduct, as it clearly prohibits the possession and use of toxic chemicals for non-peaceful purposes. See United States v. Powell, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975). The statute provides fair notice to individuals that their conduct is prohibited, and its terms are not so broad as to be unconstitutionally vague. See United States v. Lanier, 520 U.S. 259, 266 (1997).

Additionally, the search warrants for Bond's home and car were valid, as they were supported by probable cause. See United States v. Hodge, 246 F.3d 301, 305 (3d Cir. 2001). The affidavit supporting the warrants provided sufficient evidence to establish a nexus between Bond's conduct and the locations to be searched. See United States v. Whitner, 219 F.3d 289, 297 (3d Cir. 2000).

In conclusion, Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds, and her other challenges to the statute and her conviction are without merit. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
