The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal (North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969); Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969)). Although the Clause by its terms proscribes a second jeopardy "for the same offence" (U.S. Const., 5th Amend.), the Court has applied it to sentencing proceedings that have the characteristics of a trial.

In Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), the Court held that a defendant who was retried and received a harsher sentence did not have his Double Jeopardy rights violated. However, the Court later distinguished Stroud in cases where the sentencing proceeding is more formal and trial-like. The Court has recognized that certain sentencing proceedings, although not technically trials on guilt or innocence, may still be subject to Double Jeopardy protections if they involve characteristics that make them resemble a trial (Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187-188 (1957); United States ex rel. Hetenyi v. Wilkins, 348 F.2d 844, 864 (2d Cir. 1965)).

The historical context of sentencing proceedings also supports the application of Double Jeopardy protections. At common law, sentencing was not a separate proceeding, and judges had broad discretion in determining sentences (Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 246 (1949)). However, as sentencing proceedings became more formalized, the need for procedural protections, including Double Jeopardy protections, arose.

The Supreme Court's decision in Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. 163 (1873), is particularly relevant. In Lange, the Court held that a defendant who had already been sentenced and partially served his sentence could not be resentenced to a harsher punishment. The Court reasoned that the Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits not only a second trial for the same offense but also a second punishment for the same offense (Id. at 173-174).

Similarly, in United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662 (1896), the Court held that a defendant who had been acquitted of a crime could not be retried, even if the acquittal was erroneous. The Court emphasized that the Double Jeopardy Clause is designed to protect the defendant from the harassment and anxiety of multiple prosecutions (Id. at 669).

In re Nielsen, 131 U.S. 176 (1889), is also instructive. In Nielsen, the Court held that a defendant who had been convicted of a crime could not be subsequently prosecuted for a related offense that was not charged in the original indictment. The Court reasoned that the Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits the government from repeatedly prosecuting a defendant for the same conduct (Id. at 188).

The application of Double Jeopardy protections to noncapital sentencing proceedings is consistent with the principles established in these cases. A trial on a prior conviction allegation, like the one in this case, involves many of the same procedural protections as a trial on guilt or innocence, including the right to a jury trial (see Penal Code § 1025), the right to confront witnesses, and the requirement that the prosecution prove the allegation beyond a reasonable doubt (In re Winship, is not cited because it was decided in 1970; instead, we rely on the principle established in cases like Coffin v. United States, 156 U.S. 432, 453-454 (1895), which emphasized the importance of proof beyond a reasonable doubt in criminal cases).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause is not limited to trials on guilt or innocence but can also apply to other proceedings that are sufficiently trial-like (Cramer v. United States, 325 U.S. 1, 43-44 (1945); Krulewitch v. United States, 336 U.S. 440, 445 (1949)). The trial on the prior conviction allegation in this case meets this standard, as it involves a formal hearing with procedural protections similar to those in a trial on guilt or innocence.

In conclusion, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The historical context, the characteristics of the sentencing proceeding, and the principles established in relevant case law all support this conclusion. Therefore, the California Court of Appeal's decision barring retrial of the prior serious felony allegation on double jeopardy grounds should be affirmed.
