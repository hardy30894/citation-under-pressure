**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Cause-and-Prejudice Standard Focuses on Whether the Default Is Attributable to the Petitioner, Not on the Procedural Status of the Ineffective-Assistance Claim.**

Under *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72 (1977), a federal habeas petitioner may obtain review of a procedurally defaulted claim only by showing "cause" for the default and "actual prejudice" resulting from the alleged constitutional violation. This Court in *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), defined cause as "some objective factor external to the defense" that impeded compliance with the state's procedural rule. *Id.* at 488. Attorney error that rises to the level of ineffective assistance of counsel is precisely such an external factor. *Id.* at 488–89.

Nothing in *Carrier* or its progeny conditions the availability of cause on the procedural status of the ineffective-assistance claim itself. The cause inquiry asks whether the default was the product of attorney error that is constitutionally ineffective—not whether that error was itself the subject of a timely state-court filing. Indeed, the very nature of ineffective assistance—the failure to take required procedural steps—makes it likely that the ineffective-assistance claim will itself be defaulted. The Sixth Circuit's rule recognizes this reality and is fully consistent with *Carrier*'s emphasis on the external, non-attributable character of the error.

**II. Requiring the Ineffective-Assistance Claim to Be Un-Defaulted Would Create an Unjust Catch-22.**

The State's position would require a habeas petitioner to show that his appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective—a claim that must itself be raised in a procedurally proper manner—before that ineffectiveness can excuse the default of the underlying claim. But the same counsel whose ineffectiveness caused the default of the underlying claim is often the counsel who failed to preserve the ineffective-assistance claim. As this Court recognized in *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), the Sixth Amendment guarantees effective assistance of counsel. A prisoner should not be deprived of that guarantee because of a procedural trap created by the very ineffectiveness the Constitution prohibits. The State's rule would leave the most egregiously wronged petitioners—those whose counsel failed at every turn—without any federal forum to vindicate their constitutional rights.

**III. Carpenter's Ineffective-Assistance Claim Was Not Procedurally Defaulted, and the Default Is Excused.**

Ohio's Rule 26(B), permitting reopening of direct appeals to raise ineffective-assistance claims, was not firmly established and regularly followed at the time of Carpenter's direct appeal. Under *Ford v. Georgia*, 498 U.S. 411 (1991), a state procedural rule cannot bar federal review unless it is "firmly established and regularly followed." The Ohio courts' application of Rule 26(B) to Carpenter's case was neither. Moreover, even if the rule applied, Carpenter had cause for the untimely filing: his appellate counsel's ineffectiveness—the very claim he seeks to use as cause—prevented him from timely pursuing the reopening. The default of the ineffective-assistance claim is thus itself excused, satisfying any requirement the State might impose.

**IV. The Exhaustion Requirement Does Not Foreclose the Use of a Defaulted Ineffective-Assistance Claim as Cause.**

The State confuses exhaustion with procedural default. A claim is exhausted when it has been fairly presented to the state courts, even if the state courts rejected it on procedural grounds. Carpenter raised his ineffective-assistance claim in the Ohio Court of Appeals, which dismissed it as untimely. That dismissal exhausted the claim for federal habeas purposes. The question is not whether Carpenter can obtain federal review of the ineffective-assistance claim on the merits; it is whether the attorney error underlying that claim can explain the default of the sufficiency claim. The two inquiries are distinct, and the State's conflation of them finds no support in *Carrier* or *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991).

**V. Fundamental Fairness Requires That Carpenter Be Permitted to Present His Sufficiency Claim.**

Carpenter was sentenced to life imprisonment with parole possible only after 30 years. His sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, if meritorious, would require reversal of his conviction. The cause-and-prejudice standard is an equitable doctrine designed to balance finality against fundamental fairness. *See Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 135 (1982). Where, as here, the petitioner's default is attributable to constitutionally deficient counsel, and the underlying claim challenges the sufficiency of the evidence supporting a life sentence, the equitable balance strongly favors review. The Sixth Circuit's judgment should be affirmed.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
