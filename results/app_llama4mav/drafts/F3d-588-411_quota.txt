## Argument

The district court's supplemental jury instruction on the meaning of "shielding" was erroneous and prejudicial, and Ye's conviction under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) should be reversed. The statute prohibits "concealing, harboring, or shielding from detection" illegal aliens. The district court's definition of "shielding" was overly broad and vague, and failed to provide the jury with a clear understanding of the term.

The court's instruction defined "shielding" as "protecting or keeping safe from detection or danger." Ye objected to this definition, arguing that it was too broad and did not accurately reflect the statutory meaning of the term. The Seventh Circuit has not previously defined "shielding" in the context of § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii), but other circuits have addressed the issue. For example, the Ninth Circuit has held that "shielding" requires "conduct that tends to substantially facilitate an alien's remaining in the United States illegally." United States v. You, 382 F.3d 958, 966 (9th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Lopez, 521 F.2d 437, 441 (2d Cir. 1975)). Similarly, the Fifth Circuit has defined "shielding" as "acts that tend to facilitate an alien's illegal presence in the United States." United States v. Rubio-Gonzalez, 674 F.2d 1067, 1072 (5th Cir. 1982).

In contrast, the district court's definition of "shielding" in this case was overly broad and could have led the jury to convict Ye based on conduct that did not substantially facilitate the aliens' remaining in the United States illegally. For instance, providing housing or transportation to illegal aliens, without more, does not necessarily constitute "shielding" under the statute. See United States v. DeJesus-Batres, 410 F.3d 154, 160 (5th Cir. 2005) (holding that providing food and shelter to illegal aliens was not sufficient to establish "harboring" or "shielding").

The Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of providing clear and accurate jury instructions, particularly in cases involving complex or ambiguous statutory terms. See Bollenbach v. United States, 326 U.S. 607, 612 (1946) ("The correctness of each [jury instruction] must be determined by the reading of all."). In this case, the district court's instruction failed to provide the necessary clarity, and Ye was prejudiced as a result.

Furthermore, the evidence presented at trial was not so overwhelming that the erroneous instruction was harmless. See Neder v. United States, 527 U.S. 1, 17 (1999) (holding that an erroneous jury instruction is subject to harmless error analysis). Ye's testimony and other evidence presented at trial raised questions about his intent and the nature of his conduct, and the jury's verdict may have been influenced by the overly broad definition of "shielding." See United States v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506, 522-23 (1995) (reversing conviction due to erroneous jury instruction).

In addition, the district court's failure to provide a clear definition of "shielding" undermined Ye's right to a fair trial. See United States v. D'Amato, 39 F.3d 1249, 1256 (2d Cir. 1994) (holding that a jury instruction that fails to accurately convey the elements of the offense is reversible error). The Seventh Circuit should reverse Ye's conviction and remand for a new trial with proper jury instructions.

The sufficiency of the evidence is also a close question. To convict Ye under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii), the government was required to prove that he intended to prevent the government from detecting his illegal alien employees. See United States v. Ozcelik, 527 F.3d 88, 97-98 (3d Cir. 2008) (holding that the government must prove that the defendant knowingly and intentionally harbored or shielded illegal aliens). While the evidence presented at trial suggested that Ye knew his employees were illegal aliens and took steps to conceal their presence, it is not clear that he intended to prevent the government from detecting them. See United States v. Silveus, 542 F.3d 993, 1001 (3d Cir. 2008) (reversing conviction due to insufficient evidence of intent).

In conclusion, the district court's supplemental jury instruction on the meaning of "shielding" was erroneous, and Ye's conviction should be reversed. The Seventh Circuit should remand the case for a new trial with proper jury instructions, and consider whether the evidence was sufficient to prove Ye's intent to prevent the government from detecting his illegal alien employees.
