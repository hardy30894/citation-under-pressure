**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Revision of GGC's Policies Eliminated the Live Controversy That Article III Requires.**

Article III confines federal courts to the resolution of genuine, ongoing disputes, not to the adjudication of policies that no longer govern the parties' relations. This Court has repeatedly measured the existence of a live controversy by whether the challenged restriction continues to threaten the plaintiff's conduct. In *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503 (1969), the Court sustained a First Amendment challenge only because the school's regulation actually "forbidding discussion" and "expression by any student" remained in force and applied to the students before it. *Id.* at 511. Here, by contrast, GGC's "Freedom of Expression Policy" and the challenged provision of the Student Code of Conduct have been repealed; students, including Bradford, may now "speak anywhere on campus without having to obtain a permit except in certain limited circumstances." There is no regulation left to forecast disruption of, and no live threat left to enjoin.

This Court's practice of resolving constitutional challenges to campus and public-forum restrictions has always proceeded on the premise that the offending rule remains operative. In *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105 (1943), the Court invalidated a licensing ordinance that continued to burden the "distribution of religious literature," *id.* at 108–09, precisely because the ordinance was still being enforced against the petitioners' "personal visitations." *Id.* And in *Jones v. Opelika*, 316 U.S. 584 (1942), the Court's review turned on the continuing operation of the municipal ordinances under which the petitioners had been convicted and remained subject to prosecution. Where, as here, the offending policy is gone, the predicate for that mode of review disappears as well.

Nor does petitioners' underlying grievance survive merely because it once implicated important interests. In *Zwicker v. Boll*, 391 U.S. 353 (1968), this Court summarily affirmed the dismissal of a challenge to a state disorderly-conduct statute where the posture of the case did not present the kind of live, continuing threat that would warrant equitable intervention, notwithstanding the students' claim that the statute chilled their "peaceful and constitutionally protected protest activities." A policy that has been rescinded, and that GGC has "unambiguously terminated" with "no reasonable basis to expect that it will return," presents even less occasion for continued adjudication than the statute in *Zwicker*.

**II. A Bare Request for Nominal Damages, Untethered to Any Actual Injury, Cannot Revive an Otherwise Moot Dispute.**

This Court's damages jurisprudence has consistently distinguished between damages that compensate a demonstrated injury and requests that merely gesture at the "abstract value" of a violated right. In *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964), and *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130 (1967), the Court examined damages awards only where a jury had found, on a developed record, that the plaintiff suffered a genuine, litigated injury—there, reputational harm assessed by a jury that returned "$60,000 in general damages," *Curtis Publishing*, 388 U.S. at 133—not a nominal sum requested as a placeholder for a right rather than an injury. Petitioners' complaint contains no comparable allegation of completed, compensable harm; it seeks only a token award divorced from any showing of loss.

The justiciability of a claim, moreover, depends on the presence of parties with a concrete stake in the outcome, not simply on the assertion of a constitutional interest in the abstract. In *Board of Education of Central School District No. 1 v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236 (1968), this Court confirmed that even the threshold question of standing to litigate a constitutional claim depends on a genuine, adverse stake in the resolution of the policy at issue—there, whether the school boards' "standing to attack the validity of a state statute," *id.* at 241, existed at all. A nominal-damages request that is not moored to any real, adjudicable injury fails that same test of adverseness. Likewise, in *Monroe v. Board of Commissioners*, 391 U.S. 450 (1968), the relief this Court approved was calibrated to a demonstrated constitutional violation and its concrete effects on identified students, not to the vindication of a right in the abstract once the discriminatory practice had been corrected.

Finally, *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), and *Millsaps College v. City of Jackson*, 275 U.S. 129 (1927), illustrate that this Court reviews the operation of a challenged policy as it actually affects the parties' concrete legal relations—released-time arrangements actively "put behind" a program, *Zorach*, 343 U.S. at 308, or a tax assessment presently levied, *Millsaps College*, 275 U.S. at 131—not policies that have already been abandoned. Petitioners identify no comparable ongoing legal relation here. Once GGC's speech-zone policy and disciplinary code were rescinded, and once no compensable injury was pled, nothing remained but a request to adjudicate a superseded rule for its own sake. Article III does not permit that exercise. The judgment below should be affirmed.
