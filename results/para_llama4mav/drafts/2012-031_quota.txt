The District Court erred in certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. Under Rule 23(b)(3) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, a class action may be maintained only if "the court finds that the questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members, and that a class action is superior to other available methods for fairly and efficiently adjudicating the controversy." The predominance requirement is not met here because the plaintiffs' damages model fails to measure damages attributable to their theory of liability.

In Hydrogen Peroxide, this Court emphasized that a district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" of the evidence and arguments in making the class certification decision, which "may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits." 552 F.3d at 317. The Court must "assess all of the relevant evidence admitted at the class certification stage" to determine whether the plaintiffs have met their burden of proof. Id. at 323. Here, the District Court failed to properly scrutinize the plaintiffs' damages model, which is a critical aspect of the predominance inquiry.

The plaintiffs' damages expert, Dr. McClave, presented a model that purported to measure damages on a class-wide basis. However, this model is flawed because it fails to isolate damages attributable to the alleged anticompetitive conduct. As the Supreme Court has held, a plaintiff must prove that their damages are attributable to the defendant's antitrust violation. See Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562-63 (1931). The plaintiffs' damages model does not meet this requirement, and therefore, the District Court erred in certifying the class.

Furthermore, the District Court's decision is inconsistent with the principles enunciated in Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin, 417 U.S. 156 (1974), which held that a district court may not conduct a preliminary inquiry into the merits of a case for purposes other than determining whether the requirements of Rule 23 are met. Id. at 177-78. While the Court has since clarified that some overlap between the class certification inquiry and the merits is permissible, see Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, -U.S.-, 131 S.Ct. 2541, 2551-52 (2011), the District Court's inquiry here went too far.

The Third Circuit's decision in In re New Motor Vehicles Canadian Export Antitrust Litig., 522 F.3d 6 (1st Cir. 2008), is also instructive. There, the court held that a district court must carefully scrutinize the plaintiffs' damages model to ensure that it is capable of measuring damages on a class-wide basis. Id. at 28. The court emphasized that a damages model that fails to account for individualized damages is insufficient to support class certification. Id.

Other circuits have reached similar conclusions. See, e.g., Blades v. Monsanto Co., 400 F.3d 562, 569-70 (8th Cir. 2005) (holding that a district court must carefully evaluate the plaintiffs' damages model to ensure that it is capable of measuring damages on a class-wide basis); In re Visa Check/MasterMoney Antitrust Litig., 280 F.3d 124, 139-40 (2d Cir. 2001) (same).

In addition, the Supreme Court's decision in Anderson v. Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564 (1985), is relevant here. The Court held that a district court's factual findings are reviewed for clear error, and that a reviewing court may not reverse a finding of fact simply because it is convinced that it would have decided the case differently. Id. at 573-74. However, the Court also emphasized that a district court's decision may be reversed if it is based on an erroneous view of the law or a clearly erroneous assessment of the evidence. Id.

Here, the District Court's decision to certify the class was based on a clearly erroneous assessment of the evidence, as it failed to properly scrutinize the plaintiffs' damages model. The Court of Appeals erred in affirming the District Court's decision, and this Court should reverse.

The decision below is also inconsistent with the principles enunciated in Brown Shoe Co. v. United States, 370 U.S. 294 (1962), which held that a relevant geographic market is defined as "the area in which the seller operates, and to which the purchaser can practicably turn for supplies." Id. at 336-37. Comcast's argument that the relevant geographic market is each individual household is not supported by the case law, and the District Court's acceptance of the Philadelphia DMA as the relevant geographic market is not clearly erroneous.

In Krasnov v. Dinan, 465 F.2d 1298 (3d Cir. 1972), this Court held that a district court's factual findings are reviewed under the clearly erroneous standard, and that a reviewing court may not reverse a finding of fact unless it is "completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support displaying some hue of credibility, or bears no rational relationship to the supportive evidentiary data." Id. at 1302. The District Court's finding that the plaintiffs had demonstrated a common methodology to quantify damages on a class-wide basis is not supported by the evidence, and therefore, the Court of Appeals should have reversed.

In conclusion, the District Court erred in certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The Court of Appeals should have reversed the District Court's decision, and this Court should reverse the judgment below.
