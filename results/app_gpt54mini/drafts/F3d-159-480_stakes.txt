**A. Constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951**

Mr. Pearson’s constitutional challenge to 18 U.S.C. § 1951 is foreclosed by controlling precedent. The Hobbs Act is a valid exercise of Congress’s Commerce Clause power because it regulates robbery and extortion affecting interstate commerce. In *Stirone v. United States*, 361 U.S. 212, 215 (1960), the Supreme Court described the Hobbs Act as directed at conduct “which obstructs, delays, or affects commerce.” The Court has repeatedly sustained federal criminal statutes with comparable effects on commerce where the regulated conduct bears a sufficient connection to interstate trade. See *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111, 124-29 (1942); *Perez v. United States*, 402 U.S. 146, 150-54 (1971).

The Tenth Circuit has likewise recognized the Hobbs Act’s commerce element as a constitutional limitation and as the basis for federal jurisdiction. See *United States v. Bolton*, 68 F.3d 396, 398-99 (10th Cir. 1995). Under *Bolton*, the government need only prove a de minimis effect on interstate commerce, and a robbery of a business engaged in interstate purchasing satisfies that requirement. Id. at 399. The evidence here showed that Mr. Goodeents Subs & Pastas was part of a national chain that purchased goods from Kansas and out-of-state suppliers, and that the robbery depleted business assets that would have been used to buy goods produced outside Kansas. That is more than enough under *Bolton*.

To the extent Mr. Pearson argues that Congress lacks power to reach a local robbery because the effect on commerce is indirect, the argument is foreclosed by *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 558-59 (1995), which reaffirmed Congress’s authority to regulate the channels and instrumentalities of interstate commerce and activities having a substantial relation to interstate commerce. The Hobbs Act fits comfortably within that framework because it targets economic conduct—robbery and extortion—that by its nature threatens commerce. See *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 610-12 (2000) (distinguishing noneconomic criminal conduct from statutes regulating economic activity).

Nor does *United States v. Davis*, 139 S. Ct. 2319 (2019), aid Mr. Pearson. Congress may punish robbery affecting commerce when, as here, the statute contains a commerce nexus and the record establishes the requisite effect. See *United States v. Wang*, 222 F.3d 234, 238-39 (6th Cir. 2000) (upholding Hobbs Act conviction where robbery affected business engaged in interstate commerce); *United States v. Robinson*, 119 F.3d 1205, 1210-11 (5th Cir. 1997) (same).

Because § 1951 is a valid exercise of Congress’s commerce power and because the government proved the interstate-commerce nexus at trial, Mr. Pearson’s constitutional challenge fails as a matter of law. The Court should reject this claim without further discussion.
