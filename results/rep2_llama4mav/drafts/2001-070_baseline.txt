The execution of Daryl Renard Atkins, who is mentally retarded, does not violate the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on "cruel and unusual punishment." The Virginia Supreme Court correctly relied on Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), in rejecting Atkins' contention that his mental retardation precludes a death sentence.

In Penry, this Court held that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of mentally retarded persons. Id. at 340. Although the Court acknowledged that mental retardation can diminish a person's culpability, it concluded that the Constitution does not mandate a blanket exemption from the death penalty for the mentally retarded. Id. at 334-35.

Since Penry, there has been no significant shift in the national consensus regarding the execution of mentally retarded persons. While some states have enacted legislation exempting the mentally retarded from the death penalty, see, e.g., Md. Ann. Code art. 27, § 412(f)(1) (1999), others have not. The lack of a clear national consensus counsels against a categorical prohibition on executing the mentally retarded.

Moreover, the jury in Atkins' case was presented with evidence of his mental retardation during the penalty phase, and it still determined that a death sentence was warranted. The jury's verdict reflects a considered judgment that Atkins' mental retardation did not sufficiently mitigate his culpability to warrant a sentence less than death.

The Eighth Amendment requires that punishment be proportionate to the offense and the offender's culpability. See Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 173 (1976). While mental retardation is a relevant factor in assessing culpability, it is not dispositive. The jury's verdict in Atkins' case reflects a nuanced assessment of his culpability, taking into account both his mental retardation and the severity of his crime.

In sum, the execution of Daryl Renard Atkins, who is mentally retarded, does not violate the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied Penry v. Lynaugh, and the jury's verdict reflects a considered judgment about Atkins' culpability. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
