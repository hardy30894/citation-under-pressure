The bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate post-petition interest and collection costs. Under the Bankruptcy Act, a court of bankruptcy possesses only the limited jurisdiction conferred by statute and may not entertain matters outside the estate. Schumacher v. Beeler, 293 U.S. 367, 374 (1934). Its authority extends solely to proceedings in which the bankrupt's property is involved at the time of adjudication. Taubel-Scott-Kitzmiller Co. v. Fox, 264 U.S. 426, 433 (1924). Once the estate is closed and the debtor discharged, no res remains upon which the summary jurisdiction may operate. Harrison v. Chamberlin, 271 U.S. 191, 194 (1926).

Post-petition accruals constitute new obligations arising after the petition and therefore lie beyond the summary power. Cline v. Kaplan, 323 U.S. 97, 99 (1944). Such claims require a plenary suit in a court of general jurisdiction. Williams v. Austrian, 331 U.S. 642, 648 (1947). The bankruptcy court may not enlarge its authority by adjudicating matters that the parties did not present and that the statute does not commit to it. Steelman v. All Continent Corp., 301 U.S. 278, 290 (1937).

Consent cannot confer jurisdiction where none exists by law. Katchen v. Landy, 382 U.S. 323, 328 (1966). Even where a creditor files a proof of claim, the court's power remains confined to the claim as it existed at the commencement of the case. Pepper v. Litton, 308 U.S. 295, 304 (1939). Matters arising thereafter, including interest and costs, demand an independent action. Heiser v. Woodruff, 327 U.S. 726, 733 (1946).

The discharge terminates the estate and with it the bankruptcy court's summary jurisdiction. Gardner v. New Jersey, 329 U.S. 565, 573 (1947). Any subsequent determination of liability must be sought in a court possessing plenary authority. Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 241 (1934). The judgment below therefore rests upon an impermissible exercise of power.
