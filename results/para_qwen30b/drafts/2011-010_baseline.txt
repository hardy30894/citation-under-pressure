**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore Act coverage only to injuries occurring *on* the outer Continental Shelf—requires this Court to interpret the statutory language of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) in light of Congress’s intent and the settled principles of maritime law. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which rejects the long-standing *situs-of-injury* requirement and instead adopts a vague “substantial nexus” test, misreads the statute, undermines settled precedent, and creates a dangerous expansion of federal jurisdiction beyond the outer Continental Shelf. This Court should reverse.

### I. The OCSLA’s Workers’ Compensation Provision Applies Only to Injuries Occurring on the Outer Continental Shelf

The plain language of § 1333(b) makes clear that Longshore Act benefits are available only when an injury “occur[s] as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The statute does not say “as the result of operations *related to*” or “as the result of operations *connected with*” the outer Continental Shelf. It requires that the injury *occur* as a result of operations *on* the Shelf. This is not a mere technicality—it is the foundational principle of statutory construction.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the starting point for interpreting a statute is the language of the statute itself.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 223, 231 (1989). Here, the language is unambiguous: the injury must occur *as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf*. The phrase “on the outer Continental Shelf” modifies “operations,” not the injury. The injury may occur elsewhere, but only if it is the direct consequence of operations *on* the Shelf. This is not a “but-for” test, as the Ninth Circuit suggests, but a *situs* requirement tied to the location of the *operation*, not the *injury*.

The Court’s decision in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), confirms this interpretation. In *Tallentire*, the Court considered whether a choice-of-law provision in OCSLA applied to a helicopter crash occurring *above* the outer Continental Shelf. The Court noted that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Id.* at 219. The Court then added a footnote stating: “Only one provision of OCSLA superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement; § 1333(b) makes compensation . . . payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act.” *Id.* at 219 n.2. Though dictum, this footnote is not “unconsidered” or “unpersuasive.” It reflects the Court’s understanding that § 1333(b) is subject to the same *situs* requirement that governs the rest of OCSLA—namely, that the operation must be *on* the outer Continental Shelf.

The Ninth Circuit dismisses this footnote as “textbook dictum” and “unconsidered,” but that is a misreading of *Tallentire*. The Court was not addressing the *situs-of-injury* issue, but it was affirming that OCSLA’s coverage is *primarily* determined by *locale*. The footnote underscores that § 1333(b) is not an exception to this rule—it is a provision that *incorporates* the situs requirement. The Court’s observation that § 1333(b) “superimposes a status requirement” on the situs requirement confirms that the *situs* of the operation is the threshold condition. The injury may occur elsewhere, but only if the operation was *on* the Shelf.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s “Substantial Nexus” Test Is Unworkable and Contrary to Precedent

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “substantial nexus” test—under which an injury on land may qualify if there is a “substantial nexus” between the injury and offshore operations—creates a standard that is both vague and unmoored from statutory text. This test invites endless litigation over what constitutes a “substantial” connection, with no clear line between maritime and non-maritime activities.

The Fifth Circuit, in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989), correctly rejected such a test. In *Mills*, the court held that an injury must occur *on* the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it to qualify under § 1333(b). The court reasoned that “Congress intended to limit the reach of OCSLA to the physical area of the outer Continental Shelf.” *Id.* at 362. This interpretation is consistent with the statutory language and with the principle that federal jurisdiction over the outer Continental Shelf is *geographically limited*. See *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985) (holding that a platform in state waters is not subject to OCSLA).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Valladolid* undermines this principle. It allows workers’ compensation claims to be brought for injuries occurring on land—such as at an onshore processing facility—so long as the injury is “connected” to offshore operations. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978). The Ninth Circuit itself has held that a facility three miles from a pier, with no direct access to water, is not a maritime situs. *Motoviloff v.
