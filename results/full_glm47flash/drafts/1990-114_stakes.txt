**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA EXCLUDES STATE JUDGES FROM COVERAGE**

The ADEA defines "employee" to exclude "an appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The Eighth Circuit correctly determined that state judges perform policymaking functions and are therefore excluded from the Act's coverage. See *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). The court noted that "policymaking" is a part of judging, as judges fill interstices of authority. This interpretation is consistent with the statute's structure, which exempts elected officials and their advisors. See *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971) (requiring clear statement for federal intrusion into state affairs). Congress did not intend to cover state judges in this area.

**II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SURVIVES EQUAL PROTECTION REVIEW**

Even assuming the ADEA applies, the mandatory retirement provision survives rational basis review. The state has a legitimate interest in ensuring judicial competence and reducing delays in the administration of justice. See *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980) (mandatory retirement eliminates the "unpleasantness of selectively removing aged... judges"). See also *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979) (mandatory retirement reduces delays caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges). Furthermore, the provision promotes a representative judiciary. See *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988) (an age limit "tends to assure that important decisions... will be made by judges who command respect... and makes it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary"). These interests are rationally related to the age limit.
