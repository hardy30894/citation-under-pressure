**ARGUMENT**

I. **OSHA HAS JURISDICTION OVER WORKPLACES ON UNINSPECTED VESSELS UNDER THE OSH ACT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) applies to "employment performed in a workplace." 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The Fifth Circuit has recognized that the term "workplace" is defined broadly to include "any ... place where work is performed by an employee." *Martin v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n*, 499 U.S. 144, 155 (1991) (quoting *Whirlpool Corp. v. Marshall*, 445 U.S. 1, 19 n.16 (1980)). The OSH Act’s coverage is not limited to traditional land-based workplaces; it extends to "any ... place where work is performed by an employee," including vessels operating on navigable waters. *Id.* (emphasis added). The fact that Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52 was an uninspected vessel does not remove it from OSHA’s jurisdiction. The Supreme Court has made clear that the OSH Act’s coverage is "broad and comprehensive," *Whirlpool*, 445 U.S. at 12, and courts must "give effect to the unambiguous meaning of the statute," *Martin*, 499 U.S. at 151.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), does not support Mallard’s argument that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over uninspected vessels. *Clary* involved a seaman injured on a drilling barge, but the court’s holding was not based on the vessel’s inspection status. Rather, the court concluded that OSHA did not apply to "working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation" because the Coast Guard had exclusive authority over such matters. *Id.* at 1122. The court did not suggest that OSHA’s jurisdiction turned on whether a vessel was inspected or uninspected. *See also Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825, 826 (5th Cir. 1983) (reaffirming that OSHA does not apply to "working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation," without distinguishing between inspected and uninspected vessels).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has cautioned against reading exceptions into the OSH Act where Congress has not expressly provided them. *Martin*, 499 U.S. at 151. The OSH Act contains no exception for uninspected vessels, and this Court should not engraft one onto the statute. *See also Chao v. Gunite Corp.*, 542 U.S. 901 (2004) (per curiam) (holding that the OSH Act’s coverage is not limited to traditional workplaces and rejecting an attempt to carve out an exception for a particular industry).

II. **SECTION 4(b)(1) OF THE OSH ACT DOES NOT PREEMPT OSHA’S JURISDICTION OVER UNINSPECTED VESSELS**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions with respect to which other Federal agencies ... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard has authority to regulate certain safety aspects of uninspected vessels, but this does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction over workplace safety standards. *See generally United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 108 (2000) (explaining that federal law preempts state law only where Congress has "clearly manifested" its intent to do so).

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), does not support Mallard’s preemption argument. In *Donovan*, the court held that the Coast Guard’s comprehensive regulation of seamen’s working conditions created an "industry-wide exemption" from OSHA. *Id.* at 826. However, the court’s holding was based on the fact that the employees in question were "seamen" subject to the jurisdiction of the Coast Guard under 46 U.S.C. § 10301. *Id.* The court did not suggest that the preemption analysis would be the same for workers who are not seamen, such as the drilling rig employees in this case. *See also Chao v. Russell P. LeFrois Constr. Co.*, 291 F.3d 219, 225 (2d Cir. 2002) (holding that OSHA’s jurisdiction is not preempted where the Coast Guard’s regulations do not address the specific workplace hazard at issue).

The Supreme Court has recognized that OSHA and the Coast Guard can regulate different aspects of maritime safety without conflict. *See generally Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775, 806 (1998) (noting that "federal law may preempt state law in a variety of ways," but courts should avoid "needless conflict" between federal regulations). Here, the Coast Guard regulates certain safety aspects of uninspected vessels, such as lifesaving equipment and fire extinguishers, but it does not regulate workplace safety standards for drilling rig employees. *See* 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. OSHA’s jurisdiction over workplace safety does not conflict with the Coast Guard’s authority; rather, it complements it by ensuring that employees on uninspected vessels are protected from hazards not addressed by the Coast Guard. *See Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 537 U.S. 984 (2002) (denying certiorari and leaving undisturbed the Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Donovan*).

III. **THE COAST GUARD’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY OVER UNINSPECTED VESSELS DOES NOT PREEMPT OSHA’S JURISDICTION**

Mallard argues that the Coast Guard’s authority to regulate uninspected vessels under 46 U.S.C. § 4102 preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction. However, the Supreme Court has made clear that federal preemption requires a showing that the federal agency’s regulations "cover the field" of regulation. *Pac. Gas & Elec. Co. v. State Energy Res. Conservation & Dev. Comm’n*, 461 U.S. 190, 204 (1983). The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels are limited to specific safety equipment and do not address workplace safety standards for drilling rig employees. *See* 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. Therefore, the Coast Guard has not "covered the field" of workplace safety on uninspected vessels, and OSHA’s jurisdiction is not preempted.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Clary*, 609 F.2d at 1122, does not support Mallard’s preemption argument. In *Clary*, the court held that the Coast Guard’s authority over seamen’s working conditions preempted OSHA’s jurisdiction, but the court did not suggest that the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels would have the same preemptive effect. *See also Chao v. Russell P. LeFrois Constr. Co.*, 291 F.3d at 225 (holding that OSHA’s jurisdiction is not preempted where the Coast Guard’s regulations do not address the specific workplace hazard at issue).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, OSHA has jurisdiction under the OSH Act to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52. The Fifth Circuit’s decisions in *Clary* and *Donovan* do not support Mallard’s arguments, and the Supreme Court has made clear that the OSH Act’s coverage is broad and comprehensive. The Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over uninspected vessels does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction over workplace safety standards. Accordingly, the Court should reverse the Fifth Circuit’s decision and uphold OSHA’s citation.
