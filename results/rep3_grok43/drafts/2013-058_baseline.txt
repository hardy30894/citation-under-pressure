**ARGUMENT**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts only “retirement funds” held in accounts tax-exempt under specified Internal Revenue Code provisions. The text requires that the funds be set aside for the debtor’s own retirement. An inherited IRA fails this requirement because its mandatory distribution schedule compels withdrawal before the beneficiary’s retirement and bars further contributions or rollovers. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); § 401(a)(9)(B).

Statutory exemptions are construed according to their ordinary meaning. See United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc., 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989) (“The language of the statute is clear.”). The phrase “retirement funds” is not defined, yet its plain import is funds reserved for the owner’s post-employment years. See also Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) (per curiam) (“no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs”).

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that once the original owner dies, the assets cease to be anyone’s retirement funds. Clark v. Rameker (In re Clark), 714 F.3d 559, 562 (7th Cir. 2013). They become a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle available for current consumption. The contrary view—that funds retain their character merely because they once belonged to a decedent—finds no support in the statute’s text or structure. See, e.g., In re Barker, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985) (exemptions turn on the debtor’s use of the asset at filing).

Other exemptions in § 522 expressly reference “the debtor’s” interest; the retirement-fund provisions do not. This omission permits post-petition additions by employers but does not convert non-retirement assets into exempt ones. Homestead and tool-of-the-trade exemptions likewise depend on the debtor’s actual use, not a predecessor’s. See, e.g., 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A).

Policy arguments for broader protection cannot override the statutory language. As the Court observed in Rodriguez, “Deciding what competing values will or will not be sacrificed … is the very essence of legislative choice.” 480 U.S. at 526. Congress chose to protect only funds held for the debtor’s retirement; inherited IRAs do not qualify.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
