**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively to invalidate predicate convictions used under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA)—is one of profound constitutional significance. The Court must hold that *Johnson*’s holding—that the ACCA’s residual clause is unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. To do otherwise would render the Constitution’s guarantee of fair notice meaningless and perpetuate the very arbitrariness the *Johnson* Court condemned.

### I. *Johnson* Announced a New Substantive Rule That Is Retroactive Under *Teague* and Its Progeny

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a decision announcing a new substantive rule of constitutional law is retroactive on collateral review. *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 311 (1989), established that new rules are generally not retroactive unless they fall into one of two exceptions: (1) they place certain kinds of primary, private conduct beyond the power of the criminal law to punish, or (2) they impose on the government a duty to provide a procedure that is “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” Id. at 311–13. *Johnson* clearly falls within the first exception. The Court held that the ACCA’s residual clause—defining a “violent felony” as one that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another”—violated due process because it failed to provide fair notice of the conduct it punished. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. The clause allowed courts to assess the “hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version” of a crime, rendering the application of the clause “arbitrary and unpredictable.” Id. at 598. This lack of clarity meant that individuals could not reasonably anticipate that their conduct would be punished under the ACCA. As the Court stated, “a criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct it prohibits.” *Id.* at 597 (quoting *United States v. Davis*, 139 S. Ct. 2319, 2327 (2019)).

This is not a mere procedural change. It is a substantive limitation on the government’s power to punish. The *Johnson* Court did not merely refine a standard for applying an existing law; it declared that the law itself was unconstitutionally vague. As such, *Johnson* falls squarely within the *Teague* exception for new substantive rules. See *Saffle v. Parks*, 494 U.S. 484, 495 (1990) (holding that a rule invalidating a statute for vagueness is substantive and retroactive). The Court has repeatedly applied this principle: a rule that invalidates a law for failing to provide fair notice is retroactive. *Harris v. United States*, 536 U.S. 545, 550 (2002) (holding that a rule invalidating a sentencing enhancement for vagueness is retroactive); *United States v. Johnson*, 576 U.S. 591, 597 (2015) (same).

### II. The Rule in *Johnson* Is a “New Substantive Rule” That Is Not an “Exceptional Case”

The Government argues that *Johnson* is not retroactive because it is an “exceptional case” under *Teague*. But the Court has made clear that the “exceptional case” exception is narrow and applies only when a decision “breaks new ground” or “imposes a new obligation on the States.” *Saffle*, 494 U.S. at 495. *Johnson* did not break new ground in the sense of creating a novel constitutional principle. Rather, it applied well-established due process doctrine—specifically, the principle that laws must provide fair notice—to a statute that had long been criticized for its vagueness. The Court in *Johnson* relied on *United States v. Davis*, 139 S. Ct. 2319 (2019), which held that the residual clause of the ACCA was unconstitutionally vague because it “lacked a clear standard” and “failed to give fair notice.” *Davis*, 139 S. Ct. at 2327. The *Johnson* Court affirmed that “the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague” because it “fails to give fair notice of the conduct it punishes.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. This is not a new rule—it is the application of a long-standing constitutional principle to a specific statutory provision.

Moreover, the Court has held that when a rule “clarifies” a constitutional right, it is retroactive. *Griffith v. Kentucky*, 479 U.S. 314, 328 (1987). *Johnson* did not create a new right; it clarified that the ACCA’s residual clause, as applied, violated the Due Process Clause. The Court in *Griffith* held that “a new rule of constitutional law, made retroactive to cases on collateral review, should be applied to all cases, state or federal, that are pending on direct review, or have been finally resolved, but for the fact that the new rule is available.” *Id.* at 328. The Court has since reaffirmed that *Griffith* applies to cases on collateral review. *Saffle*, 494 U.S. at 495; *Harris*, 536 U.S. at 550.
