# ARGUMENT

## I. THE ACT IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION THAT SURVIVES INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY

The First Amendment does not guarantee speakers access to every forum on their preferred terms. This Court has long held that reasonable time-place-manner restrictions on speech in public fora are constitutional so long as they "are justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech, . . . are narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and . . . leave open ample alternative channels for communication of the information." *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (quoting *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984)). The Massachusetts Act satisfies each requirement.

### A. The Act Is Content-Neutral and Viewpoint-Neutral

The Act draws no distinctions based on the content of speech. It prohibits "knowingly enter[ing] or remain[ing]" within thirty-five feet of a reproductive health care facility's entrance, exit, or driveway—regardless of what, if anything, a person says while there. Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½(b). This Court's decision in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), controls. There, the Court upheld a Colorado statute restricting approach within eight feet of persons near healthcare facility entrances, holding that "[i]t is not a 'content-based' or 'viewpoint-based' restriction ... simply because it applies with equal force to those espousing opposing views." *Id.* at 725. The Court explained that the statute was "not directed to the *content* of the speech but rather to the *places* where some speech may occur." *Id.* at 719 (emphasis in original). The Massachusetts Act, like the Colorado statute, applies to sidewalk counselors and clinic escorts alike, to pro-life and pro-choice advocates alike, and to religious and secular speech alike.

Petitioners' argument that the Act is content-based because it applies only outside abortion clinics finds no support in this Court's precedent. A restriction is not content-based merely because it responds to a problem—clinic-entrance congestion and safety—that happens to arise in a particular context. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 723 ("The unwilling listener's interest in avoiding unwanted communication has been repeatedly identified in our cases."). The Act's exemptions for clinic employees and persons entering or leaving the facility do not render it content-based; they are, as this Court recognized in analogous circumstances, "justified by the enhanced need for crowd control" at facility entrances. *Id.* at 731. Exemptions defined by function and location, not by the viewpoint expressed, do not transform a neutral law into a content-based one. *See Members of City Council v. Taxpayers for Vincent*, 466 U.S. 789, 804 (1984) (a regulation is content-neutral where "[t]he text of the ordinance is neutral").

### B. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored

A time-place-manner regulation need not be the least restrictive means available; it need only avoid burdening "substantially more speech than is necessary to further the government's legitimate interests." *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799. The Commonwealth's interests—ensuring "public safety on streets and sidewalks" and "patient access to healthcare, including abortion services," *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715—are undeniably significant. This Court in *Hill* upheld a scheme addressing the same problem: "vulnerable" patients confronted by "unwanted" and sometimes obstructive encounters "outside the entrances to medical facilities." *Id.* at 729–30.

The First Circuit correctly found, after a full trial on the as-applied challenge, that petitioners retain "adequate communicative channels," including "oral speech of varying degrees of volume and amplification, distribution of literature, displays of signage and symbols, wearing of evocative garments and costumes, and prayer alone and in groups." Pet. App. at [cite]. Petitioners' own testimony establishes their continued effectiveness: petitioner McCullen estimated persuading approximately eighty women against abortion between 2007 and 2011—after the thirty-five-foot zone took effect. A restriction that leaves speakers this capable of success cannot be deemed insufficiently tailored.

### C. The Act Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels

The buffer zone is modest—far smaller than the zone at issue in *Hill*, which combined a fixed eight-foot no-approach zone with a 100-foot regulated area. 530 U.S. at 707. Petitioners may still speak, pray, hold signs, and distribute literature from just beyond thirty-five feet, well within normal eyesight and hearing range on public sidewalks. This Court has never required that speakers be given their optimal location; it requires only that "ample alternative channels" remain. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. They plainly do here.

## II. *HILL v. COLORADO* WAS CORRECTLY DECIDED AND SHOULD NOT BE OVERRULED

Petitioners' invitation to overrule *Hill* ignores the demanding standard for departing from precedent. "[A]ny departure from the doctrine of stare decisis demands special justification." *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 212 (1984). No such justification exists.

*Hill* rests on sound and enduring First Amendment principles: that governments may protect unwilling listeners and ensure ingress/egress at sensitive locations without engaging in content discrimination, so long as the regulation targets *where* speech occurs rather than *what* is said. 530 U.S. at 719. This principle has been neither undermined nor abandoned by intervening decisions. *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), and *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), addressed distinguishable problems—corporate political speech restrictions, tort liability for protest speech, and speaker-based restrictions on pharmaceutical data—not location-based, viewpoint-neutral buffer zones outside medical facilities. As the First Circuit observed below, these cases articulate only "conventional First Amendment principles" applied to "factual scenarios far different than the scenario at issue here."

Overruling *Hill* would destabilize decades of settled law governing buffer zones nationwide, contrary to the "very concept of the rule of law." *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827 (1991). No changed circumstances, new legal developments, or demonstrated unworkability justify such a departure. *Hill* should stand.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
