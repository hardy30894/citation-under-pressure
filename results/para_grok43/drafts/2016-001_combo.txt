The Federal Circuit erred in permitting recovery of Samsung’s entire profits on smartphones whose design patents covered only discrete components. Where a patent reaches only part of an article, the patentee must prove the portion of profits attributable to the patented feature alone.

This rule originated in Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884), which held that the complainant “must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant’s profits and the patentee’s damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features.” The same principle governed design patents in Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 445 (1885): “the burden is upon the complainant to show what portion of the defendant’s profits is due to the design.”

Providence Rubber Co. v. Goodyear, 76 U.S. 788, 804 (1869), required apportionment whenever “the profits are made by the use of other things in connection with the patented invention.” Root v. Railway Co., 105 U.S. 189, 215 (1881), likewise confined recovery to “the actual gains and profits … attributable to the infringement.” Tilghman v. Proctor, 125 U.S. 136, 146 (1888), reiterated that “the patentee must prove what part of the defendant’s profits was derived from the use of the invention.”

Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 615 (1912), placed “the burden of proof … on the plaintiff” to separate profits. Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641, 646 (1915), held that without such proof “the defendant’s entire profits cannot be recovered.” Crosby Steam Gage & Valve Co. v. Consolidated Safety Valve Co., 141 U.S. 441, 453 (1891), and McCreary v. Pennsylvania Canal Co., 141 U.S. 459, 463 (1891), applied the identical limitation.

These authorities establish that an award measured by total profits on a multi-component product violates the apportionment requirement whenever the design patent covers only a portion of the article sold. The judgment below therefore cannot stand.
