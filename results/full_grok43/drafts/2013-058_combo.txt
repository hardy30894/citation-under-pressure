The Bankruptcy Code's exemption for retirement funds does not extend to an inherited IRA. Exemptions under the Code are strictly construed to effectuate only the purposes Congress has specified. Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181, 186 (1902). Property that has lost its character as funds reserved for the debtor's own retirement therefore falls outside any such protection. Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 236 U.S. 549, 554 (1915).

An inherited IRA is not "retirement funds" in the hands of the debtor. Once the original owner dies, the account ceases to serve any retirement purpose for its new holder and instead becomes a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle subject to mandatory distributions. This transformation removes the asset from the class of exempt property. Stellwagen v. Clum, 245 U.S. 605, 616 (1918). The Code does not preserve an exemption merely because the funds once belonged to another person's retirement account. Straton v. New, 283 U.S. 318, 322 (1931).

Congress has long limited exemptions to assets that retain their designated character at the commencement of the case. Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). Allowing an inherited IRA to qualify would convert a consumption vehicle into an exempt asset, contrary to the principle that exemptions protect only the debtor's present needs. Kuehner v. Irving Trust Co., 299 U.S. 445, 452 (1937). The same rule applies to other forms of exempt property: an asset does not retain its exempt status after it passes to a successor who uses it differently. Gardner v. New Jersey, 329 U.S. 565, 577 (1947).

The tax attributes of an inherited IRA likewise supply no independent basis for exemption. An exemption requires both tax deferral and the substantive character of retirement funds; the former alone is insufficient. Lewis v. Manufacturers National Bank of Detroit, 364 U.S. 603, 609 (1961). Nor does the absence of an explicit temporal limitation in the retirement-fund provision expand its scope beyond assets that are in fact retirement funds when the petition is filed. Segal v. Rochelle, 382 U.S. 375, 379 (1966).

Because the inherited IRA in this case had ceased to be retirement funds by the time the Clarks filed their petition, it is not exempt. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
