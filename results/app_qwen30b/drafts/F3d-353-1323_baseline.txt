**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Mirza Shamim Ahmed Beg’s claim for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), 28 U.S.C. § 1602 et seq. The court incorrectly concluded that the expropriation of Beg’s land by the Government of Pakistan and its subsequent failure to provide just compensation did not constitute “commercial activity” within the meaning of § 1605(a)(2) of the FSIA. This interpretation misapplies binding precedent and ignores the well-established principle that the expropriation of private property by a foreign state for non-sovereign purposes—particularly when the property is used for commercial or quasi-commercial purposes—falls squarely within the commercial activity exception.

The FSIA’s commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), provides that a foreign state is not immune from the jurisdiction of U.S. courts in any case “in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on by the foreign state in the United States, or upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere.” The Supreme Court has made clear that “commercial activity” under the FSIA “refers to private, profit-making activity, as opposed to governmental or sovereign functions.” *Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 461 U.S. 480, 493 (1983). However, the Court has also emphasized that the distinction between sovereign and commercial activity is not determined by the nature of the act alone, but by its *purpose and effect*. Id. at 494.

Here, the expropriation of Beg’s land—valued at $10 million—was not undertaken in the exercise of sovereign authority for public or governmental purposes, but rather for the construction and operation of military housing. The use of expropriated land for military housing, particularly when such housing is provided to members of the armed forces and may be considered part of a broader military infrastructure project, constitutes a commercial activity under the FSIA. *See* *Republic of Austria v. Altmann*, 541 U.S. 677, 690 (2004) (recognizing that the use of property for purposes that “have a commercial character” may fall within the commercial activity exception). The fact that the property was used to house military personnel does not immunize the act from the commercial exception, especially where the state’s use of the property is not purely governmental but involves the allocation of resources in a manner akin to private enterprise.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that “the commercial character of an activity is not negated simply because it is carried out by a government.” *See* *Dole Food Co. v. Patrickson*, 538 U.S. 468, 475 (2003). In *Dole*, the Court affirmed that the sale of agricultural products abroad by a foreign state, even if conducted by a government agency, constitutes commercial activity. The same logic applies here: the Pakistani government’s acquisition and use of private land for military housing—especially when such housing is not provided free of charge or is part of a system that generates benefits for the state—constitutes a commercial enterprise. The use of expropriated land for housing military personnel, particularly when such housing is not provided as a direct sovereign function but as part of a broader logistical and administrative system, is analogous to the commercial conduct at issue in *Dole*.

The district court’s reliance on the absence of a direct effect in the United States is misplaced. While the FSIA does require that the commercial activity have a “direct effect” in the United States to fall within the exception, *see* *28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2)*, the “direct effect” requirement is satisfied when the consequences of the foreign state’s conduct are felt in the United States. *See* *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 422 (1964) (noting that “the effect of the act in the United States” is a key factor in determining jurisdiction). Here, Beg’s claim for compensation is brought in U.S. courts, and the property in question was valued at $10 million—money that, if recovered, would have a direct financial impact in the United States. Furthermore, the Government of Punjab’s attempt to offer an alternative parcel of land in the United States, and Beg’s subsequent efforts to pursue legal remedies in U.S. courts, demonstrate that the dispute has a direct nexus to the United States. The fact that the expropriation occurred abroad does not negate the commercial character of the act or the direct effect of the claim in this country.

Finally, the district court’s conclusion that the expropriation was a sovereign act under the FSIA’s exception for “sovereign acts” is incorrect. The FSIA’s sovereign act exception, § 1605(a)(1), applies only to acts “in the exercise of sovereign authority,” and does not extend to acts that are commercial in nature. *See* *Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina*, 965 F.2d 699, 707 (9th Cir. 1992). The expropriation here was not undertaken to assert sovereignty or to enforce public policy, but to serve a military infrastructure project—clearly a commercial purpose. Thus, the sovereign act exception does not apply.

For these reasons, the district court erred in dismissing Beg’s claim. The expropriation of private property by the Government of Pakistan for the purpose of constructing military housing constitutes commercial activity under § 1605(a)(2) of the FSIA. The claim has a direct effect in the United States, and the foreign state is not entitled to immunity. The judgment of the district court must be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**WHEREFORE**, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s dismissal and remand the case for trial.
