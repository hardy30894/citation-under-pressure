**ARGUMENT**

The bankruptcy court exceeded its jurisdiction when, after the Chapter 13 case had been closed and discharge entered, it adjudicated the amount of post-petition interest and then denied collection costs. Federal courts “have no jurisdiction to render judgments for costs except as given by statute.” *The Baltimore*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 377, 385 (1869). And once a bankruptcy estate has been administered and closed, the court’s power is limited to matters necessary to enforce or effectuate its prior orders. *Barton v. Barbour*, 104 U.S. 126, 133 (1881). Here, there was no live bankruptcy administration to which the disputed interest and costs pertained; the proceeding concerned a closed case, a discharged debtor, and a post-closure dispute over liability. That is beyond the proper scope of bankruptcy adjudication. *Ex parte Christy*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 292, 311-12 (1845).

I. **The bankruptcy court lacked authority to determine post-petition interest after the case was closed.**

A bankruptcy court’s power is derivative and limited; it exists to administer the estate, not to issue advisory rulings on collateral post-closure disputes. *Ex parte Christy*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) at 311-12. The Supreme Court has long held that bankruptcy jurisdiction is tied to the “proceedings in bankruptcy” and the distribution of the estate. *Mueller v. Nugent*, 184 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1902). Once the debtor has been discharged and the case closed, the court’s jurisdiction does not extend to resolving a new, post-closure controversy that was neither necessary to administration nor part of the original claims allowance process. *In re Watts and Sachs*, 190 U.S. 1, 27-28 (1903).

That principle controls here. ECMC did not ask the bankruptcy court to reopen or re-administer the estate. Nor did Kirkland seek a determination of post-petition interest in her complaint; she sought only a declaration that the principal had been paid through the plan, “except to the extent of any interest that may have” accrued. Because the estate was closed, any later adjudication of interest was not a continuation of claims administration but a separate determination of a debtor-creditor dispute. Bankruptcy courts do not have general equity jurisdiction to decide whatever is convenient once the statutory case has ended. *Northern Pacific Railroad Co. v. Boyd*, 228 U.S. 482, 509 (1913).

II. **The court also lacked authority to deny collection costs absent a statutory basis.**

Costs are not recoverable unless authorized by statute or rule. *The Baltimore*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) at 385. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that taxation of costs is a matter of positive law, not judicial discretion untethered from statutory authorization. *Arcambel v. Wiseman*, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 306, 306 (1796). Thus, if ECMC was entitled to collection costs under the governing loan instruments or applicable federal law, the bankruptcy court could not simply refuse them because ECMC had not, in the court’s view, made a sufficient showing within the closed bankruptcy proceeding. *The Baltimore*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) at 385.

The court’s refusal was especially improper because the issue of collection costs had not been squarely put before it as part of the dischargeability dispute. A federal court may decide only the controversy actually presented. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346, 361-62 (1911). By adjudicating costs sua sponte in a closed case, the bankruptcy court went beyond the live dispute and issued an unsupported merits ruling. *Baker v. Grice*, 169 U.S. 284, 292 (1898).

III. **The district court erred in affirming jurisdiction because the bankruptcy court’s ruling was not necessary to decide dischargeability.**

The only question properly before the bankruptcy court was whether Kirkland’s loan principal had been discharged notwithstanding the Chapter 13 misallocation. A bankruptcy court may decide dischargeability questions because they are central to administration of the estate and the debtor’s fresh start. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244-45 (1934). But that authority does not extend automatically to every downstream issue involving the same debt. Interest and collection costs are separate incidents of liability, and once the court had determined that the principal remained due, it had no further bankruptcy function to perform. *In re Watts and Sachs*, 190 U.S. at 27-28.

Nor could the bankruptcy court bootstrap jurisdiction by characterizing the matter as “core.” Jurisdiction depends on the statutory and constitutional limits of the court’s authority, not on labels. *Ex parte Christy*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) at 311-12. A closed case does not remain a continuing source of authority for all later disputes between debtor and creditor. *Barton v. Barbour*, 104 U.S. at 133.

For these reasons, the orders below should be reversed insofar as they sustained the bankruptcy court’s authority to fix post-petition interest and deny collection costs.
