**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Court Exceeded Its Jurisdiction by Adjudicating a Dispute Over Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs That No Party Placed Before It.**

A bankruptcy court's authority, even in a "core" proceeding, is not without limit. Its summary jurisdiction extends only to matters "in the actual or constructive possession of the bankruptcy court," and to issues the parties have actually tendered for decision through the claims process. *Katchen v. Landy*, 382 U.S. 323, 327 (1966). In *Katchen*, the Supreme Court sustained the bankruptcy court's power to decide a voidable-preference issue only because the creditor, by filing a proof of claim, "subjected himself to all the consequences" of invoking the court's equitable jurisdiction over that specific claim. *Id.* at 336. No comparable submission occurred here. Kirkland's Complaint asked the bankruptcy court to determine that the *principal* of claim no. 9 had been paid and discharged, "except to the extent of any interest that may have" accrued during the case. J.A. 5. ECMC's answer was limited to defending the nondischargeability of that principal absent a showing of undue hardship. Neither party asked the court to fix the amount of post-petition interest or to rule on collection costs. The bankruptcy court nonetheless purported to liquidate both.

That exceeds the office of adjudication. A court's jurisdiction to decide a controversy presupposes that the controversy has been submitted for decision, with the parties afforded "notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to present their objections." *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950). ECMC had no notice that the sufficiency of its own documentation on interest, or the existence of collection costs, would be tested and finally resolved in this proceeding; the court's observation that the parties merely "agree that some amount of interest has accrued," J.A. 87, is not a submission of the amount for adjudication, still less a submission of collection costs, which the court itself found "no statutory or factual basis" to support. J.A. 87. A judgment rendered on an issue never joined is not the exercise of jurisdiction but its usurpation.

**II. Because the Chapter 13 Case Had Been Closed and the Estate Fully Administered, the Bankruptcy Court's Authority Did Not Extend to Fixing New Obligations Between Kirkland and ECMC.**

Bankruptcy jurisdiction is tethered to the administration of the estate; it does not persist as a freestanding forum for resolving every dispute that later arises between a former debtor and a former creditor. *Continental Illinois National Bank & Trust Co. v. Chicago, Rock Island & Pacific Railway Co.*, 294 U.S. 648, 675 (1935) (bankruptcy jurisdiction attaches to the property and claims comprising the estate, not to matters extraneous to it). By February 2006, the Chapter 13 Trustee had filed his final report, the discharge had entered, and the case was closed. The estate no longer existed as a res over which the court retained administrative control. What remained was a dispute about the post-discharge legal relationship between Kirkland and ECMC concerning a debt Congress has made presumptively nondischargeable—precisely the kind of ordinary creditor-debtor dispute that belongs to a plenary forum, not to the vestigial jurisdiction of a closed bankruptcy case.

This Court's predecessors have long insisted that a bankruptcy court's power be exercised for the limited purpose the statute contemplates: assuring the "honest but unfortunate debtor" a "new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). That purpose is served by declaring what is discharged; it is not served by the bankruptcy court reaching out, after the estate is closed, to quantify a nondischargeable creditor's post-petition interest and collection costs—an exercise having nothing to do with administering the estate and everything to do with adjudicating a garden-variety debt-collection dispute.

Nor can the bankruptcy court's action be justified as an equitable adjunct to its discharge order. The equitable powers recognized in *Pepper v. Litton*, 308 U.S. 295, 304 (1939), exist "to secure or protect" the integrity of the bankruptcy process itself; they do not authorize a court to resolve, sua sponte, a monetary dispute the parties never asked it to resolve. And to whatever extent the bankruptcy court's 2001 orders approving the plan and confirming the claims already settled the parties' rights, those orders are entitled to preclusive effect only as to what was "actually litigated and determined," *Stoll v. Gottlieb*, 305 U.S. 165, 172 (1938); *see also Chicot County Drainage District v. Baxter State Bank*, 308 U.S. 371, 375 (1940), not as a springboard for the court, years later, to compute new figures on interest and costs that were never part of that adjudication.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the district court's judgment affirming the bankruptcy court's determination of post-petition interest and collection costs should be reversed.
