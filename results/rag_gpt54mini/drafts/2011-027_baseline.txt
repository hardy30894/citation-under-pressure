### Argument

The constitutional test for navigability for title purposes does require a court to determine whether the relevant stretch of a river was navigable at the time of statehood. The equal footing doctrine turns on whether the State received title to the bed of a waterway when it entered the Union, and that inquiry is fixed at the moment of admission. *Utah Division of State Lands v. United States*, 482 U.S. 193 (1987), explains that under the doctrine States take title to lands underlying navigable waters “upon entry into the Union.” Likewise, *Idaho v. United States*, 533 U.S. 262 (2001), states that it is “improper” to look to events after admission to discern whether Congress intended to divest the entering State of submerged lands, because the relevant moment is when the State enters the Union. See also *Oregon ex rel. State Land Board v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co.*, 429 U.S. 363 (1977) (the “critical inquiry” concerns the State’s sovereign right under the equal-footing doctrine).

That rule follows directly from the structure of title doctrine. In *Utah Division of State Lands*, this Court described the equal footing doctrine as the principle that States “hold title to the land under navigable waters within their boundaries upon entry into the Union.” 482 U.S. at 196. Title does not arise from later use, later improvements, or later evidence of present-day navigability; it depends on the condition of the waterway when state sovereignty attached. The Court’s submerged-lands cases repeatedly tie title to the statehood moment because the question is whether the waterway was navigable when the federal government surrendered the bed to the new State. See *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931); *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377 (1941).

The Montana Supreme Court departed from that settled framework by treating subsequent evidence of river conditions and later river use as sufficient to establish title without a proper statehood-focused inquiry. That approach is inconsistent with *United States v. Utah*, which recognized that navigability for title purposes is determined by examining whether the stream was navigable at the relevant historical point, not by its later condition. 283 U.S. 64 (1931). It is also inconsistent with *United States v. West Virginia*, 295 U.S. 463 (1935), which treated the navigability inquiry as an historical one centered on the condition of the riverbed at the time title would have vested. And it conflicts with *Idaho*, which cautioned against using post-statehood events to recast the sovereign-title question. 533 U.S. at 275-76.

Respondent’s position also accords with the equal footing doctrine’s purpose. The doctrine ensures that new States enter the Union with the same sovereignty as the original States, including title to the beds of navigable waters. *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845); *Utah Division of State Lands*, 482 U.S. at 195-96. That sovereignty is measured as of admission, because federal title to submerged lands is transferred, if at all, at that time. *Idaho*, 533 U.S. at 273-76. If later conditions could govern title, then the doctrine would cease to be an admission-based rule and become a moving target dependent on later engineering, channel changes, or changing commerce. This Court has rejected that kind of fluid title rule. See *Corvallis Sand & Gravel*, 429 U.S. at 368-69.

Petitioner’s contrary argument, insofar as it suggests that later navigability evidence can substitute for statehood navigability, misunderstands the difference between navigability for title and other forms of navigability analysis. *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377 (1941), addressed the scope of federal commerce power and licensing authority, not title in a State under the equal footing doctrine. The Court there considered whether a river was navigable for federal regulatory purposes, which is a distinct inquiry from whether a State acquired title to a riverbed at statehood. See also *Federal Power Commission v. Union Electric Co.*, 381 U.S. 90 (1965). Those cases do not authorize a court to ignore the statehood moment in a title dispute.

Because the relevant question is whether each stretch of river was navigable at the time Montana entered the Union, the trial court had to make that determination before concluding that Montana acquired title. The judgment below should therefore be reversed, and the case remanded for application of the correct statehood-based navigability standard.
