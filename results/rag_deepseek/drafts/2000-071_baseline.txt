**Argument**

Milford Central School’s exclusion of the Good News Club from its after-hours facilities did not violate the First Amendment. The school created a limited public forum—open only for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community”—and expressly excluded uses “for religious purposes.” Because the Club’s proposed activities constituted religious instruction and worship, rather than a mere viewpoint on a secular subject, Milford’s decision was permissible subject-matter discrimination. Alternatively, even if the discrimination were viewpoint-based, Milford’s compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation would justify the exclusion.

**I. The School Created a Limited Public Forum and the Exclusion of Religious Worship Is Viewpoint-Neutral Subject Discrimination**

A public entity that opens its facilities for limited purposes may restrict speech to those purposes without engaging in unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384, 392–93 (1993). There, the Court upheld a school district’s exclusion of a religious film series because the district had not opened its facilities for “religious purposes.” *Id.* at 393–94. Similarly, in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 268 (1981), the Court distinguished between a university forum open to all student groups and a secondary school’s limited forum. Here, Milford’s policy explicitly permits only secular uses—such as civic meetings and recreational events—and prohibits “religious purposes.” The Good News Club’s meetings, which include prayer, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and an invitation to accept Jesus Christ as a personal savior, fall squarely within the prohibited category. The Club’s activities are not merely a “religious viewpoint” on morals; they are “religious instruction” itself, as the Second Circuit correctly held. *Good News Club v. Milford Central School*, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000). Because the school has not opened its forum for religious worship or instruction, it may exclude such content without discriminating among viewpoints. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393–94.

**II. The Club’s Activities Are Religious Worship, Not a Viewpoint on a Secular Subject**

The Club’s proposed meetings involve far more than discussion of moral values from a religious perspective. The record shows that the Club’s curriculum includes prayer, Bible stories, memorization of scripture, and a “challenge and invitation” segment that exhorts children to “believe on the Lord Jesus” and “be saved.” *See* J.A. 12–15. These activities are “quintessentially religious,” *202 F.3d at 510*, and constitute “religious worship” or “religious instruction,” not the expression of a viewpoint on a secular matter. In *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 831 (1995), the Court held that viewpoint discrimination occurs when the government excludes speech based on “the specific motivating ideology or the opinion or perspective of the speaker.” But the Court also recognized that a forum may be limited to certain subjects, and that “religious worship” may be excluded as a subject. *Id.* at 830–31. Here, the Club’s activities are not a perspective on morals; they are a religious exercise. The school permits other groups—such as the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H—to discuss moral development from a secular perspective. The Club seeks to do something fundamentally different: to conduct a religious service for children, complete with prayer, proselytizing, and an invitation to accept Christ. That is a change of subject, not a change of viewpoint. *See Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394 (noting that a school may exclude “religious worship” even if it permits other expressive activities).

**III. Even If the Exclusion Were Viewpoint-Based, Milford’s Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation Justifies the Denial**  

The Establishment Clause prohibits public schools from endorsing or promoting religion. *Board of Ed. of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 249–50 (1990). When a school opens its facilities to private groups, it must ensure that the “primary effect” of the policy is not to advance religion. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271–72. Allowing the Good News Club to meet on school grounds—with its overtly proselytizing activities aimed at young children who may perceive the school’s imprimatur—would risk conveying a message of state endorsement of religion. *See Bender v. Williamsport Area School District*, 475 U.S. 534, 553 (1986) (Powell, J., concurring) (noting that “the risk of official endorsement of religion” is high in elementary and secondary schools). Milford’s decision to exclude the Club was a reasonable exercise of its authority to avoid that constitutional violation. *Cf. Columbia Union College v. Clarke*, 527 U.S. 1013, 1019 (1999) (Thomas, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (noting that the “pervasively sectarian” test is no longer viable, but the school’s interest in avoiding establishment remains compelling). Because the Club’s activities are “quintessentially religious,” the school’s exclusion was narrowly tailored to serve a compelling interest.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
