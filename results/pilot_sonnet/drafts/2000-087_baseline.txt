# ARGUMENT

## I. THE POST-REMOVAL-PERIOD STATUTE MUST BE CONSTRUED TO CONTAIN AN IMPLICIT LIMITATION ON THE DURATION OF DETENTION

### A. Indefinite Civil Detention Raises Serious Constitutional Concerns That Require This Court to Construe the Statute Narrowly

The canon of constitutional avoidance controls the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). Where "a serious doubt" exists as to a statute's constitutionality, courts must "first ascertain whether a construction of the statute is fairly possible by which the question may be avoided." *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). Indefinite civil detention of a removable alien presents precisely such a serious constitutional question.

The Due Process Clause protects "freedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint" as lying "at the heart of the liberty" the Clause protects. This protection applies to all "persons" within the United States, and it "applies to aliens" whether their presence is "lawful, unlawful, temporary, or permanent." Once an alien has been "admitted to the United States," the "Due Process Clause applies to all 'persons' within the United States, including aliens, whether their presence here is lawful, unlawful, temporary, or permanent."

Freedom from indefinite detention is not absolute, but the Government must identify a sufficiently strong special justification to override an individual's liberty interest. In cases where civil confinement has been sustained, "the confinement's duration is instead linked to the termination of a special justification" such as mental illness combined with dangerousness. See *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346 (1997). No such limiting principle exists here: § 1231(a)(6) does not apply narrowly to "a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals," but broadly to "aliens ordered removed for many and various reasons, including tourist visa violations." The Government's reading would permit detention that is potentially permanent, for the civil purpose of holding a removable alien until deportation becomes possible—a purpose that "bears no relation to the individual's dangerousness" once the possibility of removal has evaporated.

Neither of the Government's proffered justifications—preventing flight or protecting the community—suffices to render such detention presumptively reasonable when it is not limited to a fixed term and is instead linked to an event, the alien's removal, that may never occur. Preventive detention based on dangerousness alone, unaccompanied by strong procedural protections, has been sustained only in "certain special and 'narrow' non-punitive circumstances." Where, as here, detention is potentially permanent, "the sole procedural protections available to the alien are found in administrative proceedings," a review process this Court has recognized as inadequate to guard against the risk of erroneous and indefinite confinement.

### B. Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei Does Not Control Because Petitioner Was Lawfully Admitted to the United States

The Fifth Circuit erred in relying upon *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), to sustain Zadvydas's detention. *Mezei* involved an excludable alien stopped at the border who was "‘treated,’ for constitutional purposes, ‘as if stopped at the border.'" That alien had never effected entry into the United States and therefore was not entitled to the same due process protections as an alien who has been lawfully admitted.

Zadvydas, by contrast, was lawfully admitted as a resident alien in 1956 and lived in this country for decades before his removal order issued. This Court has long recognized that "once an alien enters the country," the legal circumstance changes, for the Government's power to detain "is subject to constitutional limitations" inapplicable to those still seeking entry. The distinction between excludable and deportable aliens is not a mere formalism; it reflects the settled principle that admission works a fundamental change in the alien's constitutional status. The Fifth Circuit's reliance on *Mezei*, and on its own prior decision in *Gisbert v. U.S. Attorney General*, 988 F.2d 1437 (5th Cir. 1993)—which similarly involved excludable aliens paroled into the country—improperly imported a rule applicable to those seeking initial entry into the wholly different context of a lawfully admitted resident alien.

### C. The Statute Should Be Construed to Limit Detention to a Period Reasonably Necessary to Effect Removal

To avoid the constitutional infirmities that would arise from open-ended detention, § 1231(a)(6) should be read to authorize detention only for "a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien's removal from the United States." The statutory text refers to detention "beyond the removal period," a phrase that presupposes an endpoint, not an unlimited authorization. Construing the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation—subject to federal court review—avoids the serious constitutional question that the Government's limitless interpretation would present, consistent with this Court's obligation under *Crowell*, 285 U.S. at 62, to adopt a saving construction where "fairly possible."

Under this construction, once a habeas petitioner demonstrates that removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute. A six-month period following the removal period may serve as a presumptively reasonable interval, after which the alien who "provides good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future" shifts the burden to the Government to rebut that showing. Critically, "as the period of prior post-removal confinement grows, what counts as the 'reasonably foreseeable future' conversely would have to shrink."

The Fifth Circuit's contrary standard—requiring the alien to "show the absence of any prospect of removal, no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable"—inverts this framework and "demands more than [any fair] reading of the statute can bear." Zadvydas cannot be required to prove the negative of all possibility; he need only show, as the record here establishes after years of detention and repeated diplomatic failures with Germany, Lithuania, and the Dominican Republic, that no significant likelihood of removal exists in the foreseeable future.

### D. This Construction Does Not Leave the Government Without Recourse

Adopting a reasonable-time limitation does not force a choice between "imprisonment and the alien 'living at large.'" Release under this construction is "conditioned on any of the various forms of supervised release that are appropriate in the circumstances," and the alien "may no doubt be returned to custody upon a violation of those conditions." The Government retains full authority to subject Zadvydas to supervision, to monitor his compliance, and to reincarcerate him should circumstances change or should he pose a demonstrated danger. This case does not implicate the very different concerns applicable to suspected terrorists or matters of national security, "where special arguments might be made for forms of preventive detention." It concerns only whether an admitted resident alien, whose deportation has proven impossible for years despite good-faith efforts, may be detained without any foreseeable end.

For these reasons, § 1231(a)(6) must be construed to preclude Zadvydas's continued, indefinite detention.
