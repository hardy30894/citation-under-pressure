The question presented is whether damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the infringed component when the infringed design patent applies only to a component of a product. The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits, rather than limiting damages to the fraction of profits directly attributable to the infringed patents themselves.

The Patent Act of 1870, as amended in 1887, governs design patent infringement damages. According to this statute, an infringer is liable for "the total profit made by the defendant... but the court shall, in its discretion, render judgment for such part of the total profit as it shall adjudge to be attributable to the use of the infringing design." However, the Supreme Court has established that when a design patent is only a component of a larger product, the infringer is only liable for the profits attributable to that component. 

In Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), the Court held that in cases involving design patents for carpets, the infringer is not liable for the entire profit from the manufacture and sale of the carpet, but rather only for the value imparted to the carpet by the design. Similarly, in Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885), the Court held that the infringer is only liable for the actual damages sustained by the patentee, which is the value imparted to the carpet by the design.

The Court has also addressed the issue of apportioning profits in cases involving patent infringement. In Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Co. v. Wagner Electric & Manufacturing Co., 225 U.S. 604 (1912), the Court held that when a patent is only a part of a larger machine, the infringer is liable for the profits attributable to that part. The Court stated that the patentee must "give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features." (Id. at 615, citing Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120 (1884)).

Furthermore, in McCreary v. Pennsylvania Canal Co., 141 U.S. 459 (1891), the Court held that when a patent is for an improvement on a prior invention, the infringer is only liable for the damages occasioned by the infringement of the improvement. The Court stated that the patentee is not entitled to recover damages as if the defendant had infringed the entire machine. (Id. at 463-464, citing Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. 480 (1854)).

In Seymour v. McCormick, the Court held that damages for infringing a patented improvement on a machine are not the same as damages for infringing the entire machine. The Court stated that the patentee is only entitled to recover damages for the infringement of the improvement, not for the entire machine. (57 U.S. at 489).

The principles established in these cases demonstrate that damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the infringed component. The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits. 

Additionally, the Court has held that when a copyright is infringed, the infringer is liable for the profits attributable to the infringement, and that apportionment is possible. In Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp., 309 U.S. 390 (1940), the Court held that an apportionment of profits is permissible in copyright infringement cases, and that the owner of the copyright is entitled to recover only that part of the profits found to be attributable to the use of the copyrighted material. (Id. at 406).

Similarly, in F. W. Woolworth Co. v. Contemporary Arts, Inc., 344 U.S. 228 (1952), the Court held that the Copyright Act allows for the recovery of actual damages and profits, and that the court may award statutory damages in its discretion. (Id. at 231-232).

The principles established in these copyright cases are analogous to the issue at hand. Just as the infringer is only liable for the profits attributable to the copyrighted material, an infringer of a design patent should only be liable for the profits attributable to the infringed component.

In Lehnbeuter v. Holthaus, 105 U.S. 94 (1882), the Court held that a design patent is valid if it is new and useful, and that the infringer is liable for damages. (Id. at 96-97). However, the Court did not address the issue of apportioning damages.

In Douglas v. Cunningham, 294 U.S. 207 (1935), the Court held that the Copyright Act allows for the recovery of statutory damages, and that the court has discretion in awarding such damages. (Id. at 209-210).

In Sperry Gyroscope Co. v. Arma Engineering Co., 271 U.S. 232 (1926), the Court held that a patent infringement suit is maintainable even when the infringement is done under a contract with the United States. (Id. at 235-236).

These authorities collectively establish that damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the infringed component. The Federal Circuit's decision to affirm the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits was erroneous. This Court should reverse the Federal Circuit's decision and remand for a determination of damages based on the profits attributable to the infringed components.
