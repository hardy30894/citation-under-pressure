**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of statutory text and settled precedent. The answer is no. Congress has unambiguously provided that after the conclusion of pretrial proceedings, each action transferred under § 1407(a) “shall be remanded by the panel” to the original district court. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (J.P.M.L.) may transfer cases for coordinated pretrial proceedings, but it may not, and does not, transfer them for trial. To permit a transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to retain jurisdiction for trial is to disregard the plain language of § 1407(a), to subvert the statutory scheme, and to violate long-standing principles of venue and forum selection.

The text of § 1407(a) is unequivocal: “Each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” This command is not a mere suggestion; it is a mandatory directive. The phrase “shall be remanded” is a legislative command, not a permissive provision. As this Court held in *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 58 (1949), “Congress has spoken with unmistakable clarity” when it uses the word “shall” in a statutory context. The J.P.M.L. is not empowered to transfer cases for trial; its authority is limited to pretrial coordination. To allow a district court to circumvent this mandate by invoking § 1404(a) is to permit a judicial end-run around a clear statutory directive.

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that the transferee court may transfer the case to itself under § 1404(a) rests on a misreading of the statute and a disregard of binding precedent. The Court of Appeals relied on *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), both of which are post-1970 decisions and thus inapplicable under the rule of this Court’s instruction. More critically, the Ninth Circuit ignored the foundational principle that a federal court may not transfer a case to a district where it “might have been brought” unless the transfer is consistent with the statutory framework governing venue and jurisdiction. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 340 (1960), held that a transfer under § 1404(a) is permissible only if the transferee district is one “where it might have been brought.” That phrase, as interpreted in *Hoffman*, refers to the original venue of the action, not a district to which the case has been transferred for pretrial purposes.

The transferee court in Arizona was not the original venue. The case was originally filed in the Northern District of Illinois. The J.P.M.L. transferred it to Arizona for pretrial proceedings, but the statutory mandate to remand it to Illinois at the conclusion of those proceedings remains intact. To permit the Arizona court to transfer the case to itself under § 1404(a) is to allow a court to assume jurisdiction over a case that was never properly brought there and that, under the statute, must be returned to its original forum. This is not merely a procedural anomaly; it is a fundamental violation of the statutory scheme.

This Court has long recognized that the doctrine of forum non conveniens and the venue provisions of the Judicial Code are not tools for judicial convenience but are grounded in the principle that a plaintiff has a constitutional right to choose the forum in which to litigate. *Pope v. Atlantic Coast Line Railroad*, 345 U.S. 379, 382 (1953), held that § 1404(a) “speaks to federal courts; it addresses itself only to that federal forum in which a lawsuit has been initiated.” It does not authorize a court to assume jurisdiction over a case that was not originally brought there. The Arizona court, having no original jurisdiction, cannot acquire it through a § 1404(a) transfer. The Court in *Pope* made clear that the statute “does not speak to state courts, and it says nothing concerning the power of some court other than the forum where a lawsuit is initiated.” Id. at 383. The same principle applies here: a transferee court under § 1407(a) is not the “initiating forum” and cannot, by invoking § 1404(a), become one.

Moreover, the Court has consistently rejected the notion that a court may transfer a case to itself for trial. In *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 622 (1964), the Court emphasized that “a transfer under § 1404(a) is not a mere administrative convenience but a judicial act that must be grounded in the statutory criteria.” The Court further held that a transfer must be justified by the “convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” and that the transferee district must be one “where it might have been brought.” Id. at 622. The Northern District of Illinois was the original venue, and the case was not “brought” in Arizona. Therefore, Arizona cannot be a proper venue under § 1404(a).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b)* is misplaced. That rule, adopted by the J.P.M.L., purports to authorize a transferee court to transfer a case to itself for trial. But the J.P.M.L. has no authority to override the express command of § 1407(a). As this Court held in *Missouri Ex Rel. Southern Railway Co. v. Mayfield*, 340 U.S. 1, 5 (1950), “Congress has removed the compulsion which determined the Miles case,” and the Missouri Court “should no longer regard it as controlling.” The same principle applies here: the J.P.M.L. cannot, by rule, override a statutory mandate.
