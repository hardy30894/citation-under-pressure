## ARGUMENT

The government’s position is incompatible with the text and structure of the Internal Revenue Code and with the settled pre-1970 authorities governing tax assessment and collection. To trigger the ten-year collection period in 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a), the IRS must make a valid assessment against the taxpayer from whom it later seeks to collect. An assessment against the partnership alone does not transform each individual partner into an assessed taxpayer. Nor does state partnership liability dispense with the need for a proper federal assessment or a timely judicial action to collect from the partners personally.

### I. A valid assessment must identify the taxpayer against whom collection is sought.

The Code defines an “assessment” as recording “the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The Supreme Court has long treated assessment as the administrative act that fixes the government’s claim against the particular person assessed. See Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247, 259-60 (1935) (“Assessment is an administrative act . . . .”). That principle is reflected in the Court’s repeated insistence that federal tax collection proceeds from a specific legal obligation of a specific taxpayer. See Phillips v. Commissioner, 283 U.S. 589, 595-96 (1931); Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282, 289-90 (1929).

Nothing in the Code authorizes the IRS to extend § 6502(a)’s ten-year period by assessing one taxpayer and then collecting from another who was never assessed. The Supreme Court has described assessment as the step that “fixes the amount of the tax” and enables collection against the assessed person. Spring Canyon Coal Co. v. Commissioner, 43 F.2d 78, 79 (10th Cir. 1930); United States v. Updike, 281 U.S. 489, 495-96 (1930). If the government could assess the partnership and thereby obtain ten years to collect from each partner individually, the statutory distinction between assessment and collection would collapse.

The government’s contrary theory also conflicts with the Court’s treatment of notice and identity in tax administration. See Miller v. Standard Nut Margarine Co., 284 U.S. 498, 508-09 (1932) (tax procedure must comply with the governing statute); Pacific Coast Steel Co. v. McLaughlin, 288 U.S. 426, 432-33 (1933). A partner is not the partnership. The assessment here named only the partnership; it did not identify Respondents as assessed taxpayers.

### II. Pre-1970 precedent recognizes that joint liability does not eliminate the need for a lawful federal collection step.

The government relies on the fact that partners may be liable under state law for partnership debts. But state-law liability does not by itself create a federal right to collect without complying with federal collection procedures. In United States v. Updike, 281 U.S. 489 (1930), the Court held that suit to collect a tax debt must be brought within the statutory collection period; the government cannot evade that limit by recasting the same liability. Id. at 495-97. Likewise, in Gulf Refining Co. v. United States, 269 U.S. 125, 135-36 (1925), the Court enforced the statutory limits on tax collection despite the government’s underlying claim.

The same principle appears in cases holding that tax liens and collection remedies depend on the statutory prerequisites being satisfied as to the person pursued. In Glass City Bank v. United States, 326 U.S. 265, 267-69 (1945), the Court described federal tax lien enforcement as tied to the taxpayer’s own liability. And in United States v. City of New York, 315 U.S. 510, 516-17 (1942), the Court emphasized that federal collection rights arise only within the framework Congress prescribed. State law may define substantive liability, but federal law controls when and how the government may collect.

### III. The cases on which the government is likely to rely do not support dispensing with individual assessment here.

The pre-1970 partnership cases do not hold that assessment of the partnership alone authorizes collection from individual partners after the limitations period for those partners has expired. In United States v. Janis, 428 U.S. 433, 441-42 (1976), the Court later explained that assessment is central to collection; but even before 1970, the Court’s decisions in Bull, Phillips, Updike, Botany Worsted Mills, Gulf Refining, and Pacific Coast Steel reflected the same structure: the government must act within the statutory period against the taxpayer it seeks to reach.

Nor does the government gain support from cases recognizing partnership liability in other contexts. See, e.g., Commissioner v. Harmon, 323 U.S. 44, 47-48 (1944) (tax consequences turn on the legal entity and the statutory scheme); Burk-Waggoner Oil Ass’n v. Hopkins, 269 U.S. 110, 114-16 (1925) (tax consequences depend on the legal status Congress has chosen to tax). Those cases reinforce, rather than undermine, the rule that a partnership and its partners are distinct for federal tax administration.

### IV. The government’s rule would nullify § 6501’s three-year assessment period as to partners.

Section 6501(a) gives the IRS three years to assess the taxpayer. If assessment of the partnership alone automatically extended collection against each partner, the government could bypass § 6501(a) entirely. That is precisely the kind of circumvention the Court rejected in Updike, 281 U.S. at 495-97, and in Gulf Refining, 269 U.S. at 135-36. Congress could have written § 6502(a) to provide ten years from assessment of “the debt” or “the liability,” but it did not. It extended collection only after assessment of “the tax.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). That language presupposes an assessment against the taxpayer from whom collection is sought.

In short, the government’s assessment against the partnership did not extend the limitations period for Respondents individually. Because no timely assessment or judicial collection action was taken against them, the IRS’s claims are unenforceable. The judgment should be affirmed.
