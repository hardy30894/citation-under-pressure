**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to DHL Express and Deutsche Post AG (collectively, “DHL”) on Appellants’ claims that DHL violated the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2109, by failing to provide 60 days’ advance notice of the mass layoffs at issue. The WARN Act’s notice requirement is mandatory and unqualified, and DHL’s reliance on severance agreements to circumvent this requirement is foreclosed by settled principles of statutory construction and longstanding precedent.

**I. The WARN Act’s 60-Day Notice Requirement Is Mandatory and Unwaivable**

The WARN Act provides that an employer “shall not order a mass layoff or plant closing until the end of a 60-day period” after providing written notice to affected employees. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a) (emphasis added). This language is unambiguous and imposes a strict, non-discretionary duty. *United States v. Wunderlich*, 342 U.S. 98, 101 (1951) (“Where the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the judicial inquiry is complete.”). The Act’s structure further confirms that the 60-day notice period is a jurisdictional prerequisite to any layoff, not a procedural formality subject to waiver. *See id.* at 100–01 (statutory language must be given its plain meaning absent clear contrary indication).

DHL’s argument that the severance agreements waived Appellants’ WARN Act claims is unavailing. The WARN Act’s notice requirement is a statutory right belonging to employees, not a contractual obligation subject to negotiation. *See, e.g., United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. 316, 326 (1917) (“Rights conferred by statute cannot be bartered away by private agreement.”). This principle is longstanding and applies with equal force to federal labor statutes. *See, e.g., United States v. Joint Traffic Ass’n*, 171 U.S. 505, 568 (1898) (“Statutory rights are not subject to private forfeiture.”). Thus, even if Appellants voluntarily signed releases, they could not waive their right to the statutorily mandated notice period.

**II. The Severance Agreements Did Not Cure DHL’s WARN Act Violation**

DHL contends that the severance agreements provided Appellants with full compensation for the lack of notice, thereby mooting their WARN Act claims. This argument misapprehends the Act’s purpose. The WARN Act is not merely a damages statute; it is a prophylactic measure designed to provide employees with time to seek alternative employment or retraining before a layoff takes effect. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2101(b) (finding that mass layoffs “can cause serious harm to individual workers” and that notice is necessary to “minimize the impact of such layoffs”). As the Supreme Court has recognized, statutory remedies are not fungible; the right to notice cannot be satisfied by monetary compensation after the fact. *See, e.g., Texas & Pac. Ry. v. Abilene Cotton Oil Co.*, 204 U.S. 426, 440 (1907) (“The right to a specific remedy is not to be denied because another remedy is provided.”).

Moreover, the severance agreements were conditioned on Appellants’ immediate resignation, leaving them with no opportunity to use the statutorily mandated notice period to secure new employment. This contravenes the WARN Act’s core purpose. *See, e.g., United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 82 (1932) (“A statute must be construed in light of its purpose.”).

**III. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Appellants’ Claims as Untimely**

The district court held that Appellants’ WARN Act claims were time-barred under the Act’s 60-day limitations period, 29 U.S.C. § 2104(b). However, the limitations period does not begin to run until the violation occurs—that is, when the layoff is ordered without proper notice. *See, e.g., United States v. Moore*, 238 U.S. 14, 19 (1915) (“The statute of limitations does not run until the cause of action accrues.”). Here, the layoffs were ordered on January 30, 2009, and Appellants filed their complaint on February 26, 2009, well within the 60-day window. The district court’s contrary conclusion misapplies the accrual rule and warrants reversal. *See, e.g., United States v. Yellow Cab Co.*, 332 U.S. 218, 227 (1947) (“The accrual of a cause of action is a question of federal law.”).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s opinion.
