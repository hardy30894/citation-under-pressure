The American Dental Association (ADA) respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act.

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 provides that punitive damages may be awarded in cases of intentional discrimination. However, the statute does not define the standard for awarding punitive damages. In the absence of a statutory definition, the Court should look to the common law principles governing punitive damages.

At common law, punitive damages were awarded in cases where the defendant's conduct was "outrageous" or "egregious." See, e.g., Philadelphia, W. & B. R. Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202, 214 (1858) (punitive damages awarded where defendant's conduct was "wanton, malicious, or reckless"). The Court has long recognized that punitive damages are intended to punish and deter wrongful conduct, rather than simply to compensate the plaintiff for their losses. See, e.g., Scott v. Donald, 165 U.S. 107, 86 (1897) (punitive damages "are given to the plaintiff to punish the defendant for his outrageous conduct").

In the context of employment discrimination, the Court has held that an employer's conduct must be more than simply intentional to warrant punitive damages. See, e.g., Lake Shore & M. S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893) (punitive damages require "gross fraud, malice, oppression, or willful wrong"). The ADA submits that this principle is consistent with the language and purpose of Title VII, which aims to eliminate employment discrimination and promote equal employment opportunities.

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the ADA's conduct need not be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages. By failing to require a showing of egregious conduct, the Court of Appeals effectively lowered the standard for awarding punitive damages under Title VII. This is inconsistent with the common law principles governing punitive damages and the purpose of Title VII.

In light of the common law principles governing punitive damages and the purpose of Title VII, the ADA respectfully submits that the Court should hold that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
