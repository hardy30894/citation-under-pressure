# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Section 1333(b) Ties Coverage to the Situs of the Qualifying Operations, Not the Situs of the Injury.

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act compensation to a covered employee for "disability or death . . . resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The geographic modifier "conducted on the outer Continental Shelf" attaches grammatically to "operations," not to "injury." The statute asks only whether the *operations* that caused the injury took place on the Shelf; it says nothing about where the *employee's body* happened to be when the injury manifested. Congress could easily have written "any injury occurring on the outer Continental Shelf," as it did elsewhere in the Act when it wished to impose a situs-of-injury limitation. See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (applying adjacent-state law to "the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon"). It did not do so in § 1333(b).

The Third Circuit correctly read the statute this way in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), holding that a claimant need only satisfy "a 'but for' test in establishing that the injury occurred 'as the result of' operations on the outer continental shelf." Id. at 809–11. Under that causal, rather than geographic, standard, an employee injured in a car accident en route to a helicopter that would carry him to an offshore platform was eligible for benefits, because the accident would not have occurred but for OCS operations. Id. at 806, 811. The Fifth Circuit's contrary rule in Mills v. Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), superimposes a situs-of-injury requirement nowhere found in the statutory text, denying benefits to a welder injured while constructing a platform destined for the Shelf. Id. at 357, 362. Mills should not be followed, because it rewrites § 1333(b) to require what Congress did not write.

## II. The Tallentire Footnote Is Unconsidered Dicta That Does Not Resolve This Case.

Petitioner will rely on a footnote in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), stating that "§ 1333(b) makes compensation for the death or injury of an 'employee' resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen's and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act," and expressly noting that "because this case does not involve a suit by an injured employee against his employer pursuant to § 1333(b), this provision has no bearing on this case." Id. at 219 n.2. That admission is decisive: the Court itself disclaimed any holding on § 1333(b)'s scope. Tallentire addressed only the choice-of-law provision in § 1333(a)(2)(A), determining whether Louisiana wrongful-death law governed a helicopter-crash suit; it did not present, brief, or analyze the causation-versus-location question at issue here. Id. at 209, 219.

This Court has long recognized that "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used[,] . . . [and] their possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." Humphrey's Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935) (quoting Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399–400 (1821)). The Tallentire footnote is precisely such an unconsidered aside—offered without argument from the parties, without analysis of § 1333(b)'s text, and expressly flagged by the Court as immaterial to its disposition. It supplies no reliable "prophecy" of how this Court would resolve the question actually presented here, and it should not be permitted to override the statute's plain causal language.

## III. A Situs-of-Injury Rule Would Undermine the Remedial Structure Congress Built for Offshore Extractive Workers.

OCSLA's compensation provision was designed to fill the gap left by the Longshore Act's own maritime-situs and maritime-employment requirements, ensuring that workers whose injuries arise from Shelf extraction activities are not left without a remedy simply because the mechanics of oil production—transportation by pipeline, onshore processing, shore-based support—inevitably straddle water and land. This Court has recognized that the "explicit geographic limitation" Congress imposed operates at the level of where the *extractive activity* occurs, not at the level of where every incidental injury must land. See Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414, 422–24 (1985) (describing OCSLA's coverage as tied to Shelf-based extractive operations). A rule confining compensation to injuries physically sustained on a platform or on the waters above the Shelf would exclude workers like Juan Valladolid, whose employment was integrated into a single, continuous extractive enterprise spanning platform and shore facility, from any remedy at all—an outcome inconsistent with the remedial purpose of extending Longshore coverage to Shelf operations in the first place.

## IV. The Substantial-Nexus Test Faithfully Implements the Statute While Preserving a Meaningful Limit.

The Ninth Circuit's "substantial nexus" standard—requiring that the injury be causally connected to Shelf extractive operations—gives effect to the statutory phrase "resulting from" without either eliminating any geographic limitation or fabricating one the text does not contain. It ensures that § 1333(b) reaches only injuries genuinely tied to OCS operations, while avoiding the arbitrary line-drawing inherent in a strict situs-of-injury rule. This Court should affirm.
