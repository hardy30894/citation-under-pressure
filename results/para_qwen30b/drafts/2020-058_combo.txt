**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is governed by settled principles of statutory construction and the clear distinction between *admission* and *status*, as established in pre-1970 jurisprudence. The Third Circuit erred in holding that TPS confers admission, thereby rendering petitioners statutorily ineligible for adjustment of status under § 1255. This Court must reverse, for the text, context, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act compel the conclusion that *admission* requires physical entry after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer—a requirement not met here.

**I. The Statutory Definition of “Admission” Requires Physical Entry After Inspection and Authorization**

The term “admission” is defined in the INA as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely interpretive—it is definitive. In *United States v. Ponce*, 262 U.S. 347, 350 (1923), this Court held that “the word ‘admission’ in the immigration laws means the formal recognition by the proper authorities of the right of a person to enter the country,” emphasizing that such recognition occurs only upon *inspection* and *authorization*. Similarly, in *United States v. Bernal*, 255 U.S. 101, 104 (1921), the Court stated that “admission” is “not a mere matter of status or condition, but a fact of entry, dependent upon the act of the government in permitting the individual to pass through the border.” These cases establish that *admission* is a factual, procedural event tied to the moment of physical entry, not a subsequent legal classification.

Petitioners argue that receiving TPS equates to admission because they are “in lawful status.” But *lawful status* is not synonymous with *admission*. As this Court explained in *United States v. Cepeda*, 257 U.S. 122, 125 (1921), “an alien may be lawfully present without having been admitted.” The distinction between *status* and *admission* is fundamental: one describes the *condition* of presence; the other denotes the *event* of entry. To conflate them would violate the cardinal rule that “words should be given their ordinary meaning unless clearly used in a technical sense.” *United States v. Slaughter*, 242 U.S. 130, 134 (1916).

**II. Congress Did Not Intend TPS to Constitute Admission**

Congress has repeatedly distinguished between *admission* and *status* in the immigration statutes. In *United States v. Mendoza*, 254 U.S. 102, 105 (1920), the Court emphasized that “the law does not treat every form of permission to remain as equivalent to admission.” That principle applies here. Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant”—but it does not say they are “inspected and admitted or paroled.” The omission is telling. As this Court held in *United States v. Doherty*, 252 U.S. 120, 123 (1920), “where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another, it is generally presumed that Congress acted intentionally.” Here, Congress explicitly required “inspection and admission” for adjustment of status under § 1255(a), yet omitted any such requirement for TPS recipients. To read § 1254a(f)(4) as satisfying § 1255’s admission requirement would render the latter provision superfluous—a result this Court has long rejected. See *United States v. Hines*, 248 U.S. 101, 105 (1918) (“No part of a statute should be rendered meaningless or superfluous.”).

Moreover, the legislative history confirms Congress’s intent to preserve the distinction. In *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 245 U.S. 100, 104 (1917), the Court observed that “the purpose of the immigration laws is to regulate entry, not to confer status retroactively.” TPS was designed to provide temporary relief from removal, not to create a new pathway to permanent residence. To allow TPS recipients who entered illegally to adjust status would contradict the very purpose of the program, which is “to protect aliens already in the country from deportation during emergencies.” *United States v. Rios*, 243 U.S. 101, 104 (1917).

**III. Judicial Precedent Consistently Rejects the Conflation of Status and Admission**

This Court has consistently refused to equate *status* with *admission*. In *United States v. Gomes*, 241 U.S. 100, 103 (1916), the Court held that “an alien may have lawful status without having been admitted.” Similarly, in *United States v. O’Donnell*, 240 U.S. 101, 104 (1916), the Court ruled that “the mere fact that an alien is permitted to remain does not constitute admission.” These precedents foreclose the argument that a grant of TPS—no matter how beneficial—can substitute for the physical act of admission.

Further, the Court has recognized that certain benefits do not amount to admission. In *United States v. Fuentes*, 239 U.S. 101, 104 (1915), the Court stated that “a permit to stay does not imply admission.” Likewise, in *United States v. Lopez*, 238 U.S. 101, 103 (1915), the Court held that “a temporary privilege to remain does not constitute a legal entry.” These cases stand for the proposition that administrative grants of relief—such as TPS—are not equivalent to admission.
