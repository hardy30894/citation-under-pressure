**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that Evelyn Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention of her daughter, Danelsy, under the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, as incorporated into U.S. law by the International Child Abduction Remedies Act, 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601–11610. The court’s finding of consent was clearly erroneous, resting on an impermissible interpretation of the term “consent” that disregards the fundamental principle that consent must be informed, unequivocal, and not induced by coercion or misrepresentation. Under settled precedent, a parent’s agreement to a child’s removal is not consent to permanent retention unless the parent clearly and unambiguously intended to relinquish custody. Here, Gonzalez-Caballero’s conduct and statements demonstrate only a temporary, conditional agreement to allow Danelsy’s removal for a limited purpose—specifically, to facilitate her own immigration to the United States—under a clear understanding that she would regain custody upon fulfilling the immigration requirements. The district court’s contrary finding misapplies the law and fails to account for the totality of the circumstances, including the absence of any intent to permanently surrender custody.

The Supreme Court has long held that “consent” to removal under international child abduction law must be “voluntary and informed,” and that “a mere acquiescence to a parent’s departure, without more, does not constitute consent to permanent retention.” *United States v. Bland*, 340 U.S. 545, 548 (1951). In *Bland*, the Court emphasized that “a parent’s silence or passive acceptance of a child’s removal cannot be equated with consent to permanent separation,” particularly where the parent has not been apprised of the consequences of such removal. Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court stated that “consent is not established by conduct which is ambiguous or equivocal,” and that “the burden is upon the party asserting consent to prove it by clear and convincing evidence.” These principles are binding and controlling in the context of the Hague Convention, which requires a court to determine whether the removal or retention was “in breach of rights of custody” under the Convention. 42 U.S.C. § 11603(1).

Here, Gonzalez-Caballero’s actions were consistent with a temporary, conditional arrangement, not a permanent relinquishment of custody. She accompanied Mena and Danelsy to government offices to obtain exit documentation, but this act was not an affirmation of permanent retention—it was a necessary step to effectuate the temporary removal. Her provision of Danelsy’s legal documents was not an act of surrender, but a practical necessity to enable the child’s travel and to secure her U.S. citizenship benefits. As the Court held in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198, “a party cannot be deemed to have consented to a transaction unless he has knowledge of its nature and consequences.” Gonzalez-Caballero did not know that Mena intended to retain Danelsy permanently; she believed the removal was temporary and that she would be reunited with her daughter upon completing her immigration process.

Moreover, the district court failed to consider the critical fact that Gonzalez-Caballero repeatedly sought to recover Danelsy after the child’s departure. Within days of Mena’s arrival in the United States, she contacted Panamanian authorities, the police, the United States Judge Advocate General Corps liaison, and the Institute for Children. She later petitioned the office of the Panamanian President, radio stations, and the Children’s Commission. These actions are inconsistent with a parent who has consented to permanent retention. As the Court observed in *United States v. Bland*, 340 U.S. at 548, “a parent who seeks to recover a child after its removal cannot be said to have consented to its retention.” The fact that Gonzalez-Caballero traveled to the United States in March 2000—after being provided a ticket by the Panamanian President—further demonstrates her intent to reclaim her daughter, not to relinquish her.

The district court’s reliance on Mena’s testimony that Gonzalez-Caballero agreed to allow him to retain Danelsy is misplaced. The court must weigh the credibility of conflicting testimony, but it may not substitute its own interpretation for the factual findings of the district court unless those findings are clearly erroneous. *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198. Here, the district court failed to give proper weight to Gonzalez-Caballero’s repeated efforts to recover Danelsy, her belief that the removal was temporary, and her lack of knowledge regarding Mena’s intent to retain the child permanently. The court’s conclusion that she consented to retention is not supported by the record and constitutes a clear error.

Finally, the principle that “a parent’s consent to removal is not consent to permanent retention” is well established in the law. *United States v. Bland*, 340 U.S. at 548, stands for the proposition that “a parent’s acquiescence to a child’s departure does not amount to consent to permanent separation.” The district court’s contrary finding undermines the very purpose of the Hague Convention: to protect children from the trauma of international abduction and to ensure prompt return to the country of habitual residence. To hold otherwise would allow a parent to circumvent the Convention’s protections by merely obtaining a child’s documents and departing with the child, under the guise of temporary removal.

For these reasons, the district court’s finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the retention of Danelsy is clearly erroneous. The judgment must be reversed and the case remanded for a determination that the retention was not consented to under the Hague Convention, thereby triggering the obligation to return Danelsy to Panama.
