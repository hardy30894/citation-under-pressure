## Argument

The Court should reject petitioner’s effort to turn an unpreserved ineffective-assistance claim into “cause” for excusing a separate defaulted claim. This Court has long held that a state prisoner generally may not obtain federal habeas review of a claim that was not fairly presented to the state courts absent cause and prejudice. Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977); Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). The same principles govern petitioner’s attempt here: a claim of ineffective assistance can serve as cause only if that ineffective-assistance claim itself was properly exhausted and not procedurally barred, or if the prisoner can independently establish cause and prejudice for that default as well. Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488-89 (1986); Edwards v. Carpenter, 529 U.S. 446, 451-53 (2000).

Petitioner’s theory collapses the distinction between a defaulted underlying claim and the separate constitutional claim asserted as cause. But “the existence of cause for a procedural default must ordinarily turn on whether the prisoner can show that some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule.” Murray, 477 U.S. at 488. Ineffective assistance may be such an external factor, but only if it is itself a valid constitutional claim. Ibid.; Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984). If the alleged attorney error was itself procedurally defaulted in state court, federal habeas review of that error is barred unless petitioner can show cause and prejudice for that default too. Edwards, 529 U.S. at 452-53.

That rule follows directly from Coleman. There, this Court held that attorney error in a state postconviction proceeding cannot constitute cause because there is no constitutional right to counsel in such proceedings. Coleman, 501 U.S. at 752-54. More importantly for this case, Coleman reaffirmed that federal habeas courts may not “engage in freewheeling federal-law adjudication” untethered from state-court preservation rules. Id. at 731, 750. Permitting a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would allow a petitioner to do indirectly what he cannot do directly: resurrect a claim barred in state court without satisfying the independent cause-and-prejudice requirement.

Petitioner’s position is also inconsistent with the exhaustion doctrine. A federal habeas claim must first be “fairly presented” to the state courts so they have the initial opportunity to correct constitutional error. Picard v. Connor, 404 U.S. 270, 275-76 (1971); Baldwin v. Reese, 541 U.S. 27, 29 (2004). If a petitioner could rely on an unexhausted, procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance theory as cause, state courts would be deprived of the very opportunity the exhaustion rule protects. Rose v. Lundy, 455 U.S. 509, 518-19 (1982). Edwards recognized this concern and held that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot automatically serve as cause. Edwards, 529 U.S. at 452-54.

Nor does petitioner gain ground by invoking the “fundamental miscarriage of justice” exception. That exception is narrow and reserved for the extraordinary case in which a constitutional violation probably resulted in the conviction of one who is actually innocent. Schlup v. Delo, 513 U.S. 298, 327 (1995); Schlup v. Delo, 513 U.S. 298, 324 (1995). Petitioner has made no showing of actual innocence; at most, he alleges appellate error. That is insufficient. Herrera v. Collins, 506 U.S. 390, 404 (1993).

Finally, applying the default rules here respects federalism and finality. Habeas review is not a substitute for direct appeal or state postconviction litigation. Engle v. Isaac, 456 U.S. 107, 126-29 (1982). If a petitioner could bootstrap one defaulted claim through another defaulted claim, the procedural rules of the States would become optional, and federal courts would be forced to adjudicate claims the state courts never had a fair chance to consider. That is precisely what Sykes, Murray, Coleman, and Edwards forbid.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed, and the petition denied.
