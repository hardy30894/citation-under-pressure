**Argument**

A criminal defendant lacks standing to challenge a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds. Such claims assert structural limitations on national power that states alone may invoke. *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581 (1900). The Tenth Amendment “secures to all persons the equal protection of the laws” only through state prerogatives, not private litigation. *Lang v. New Jersey*, 209 U.S. 467 (1908). Absent a state’s objection, an individual “have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939).

This Court has long required that constitutional objections be presented by parties with a direct interest. *Premier-Pabst Sales Co. v. Grosscup*, 298 U.S. 226 (1936) (dismissing bill “as wanting in equity”). A defendant’s mere participation in the charged conduct supplies no basis for litigating federalism limits. *Haynes v. Washington*, 373 U.S. 503 (1963). Nor does an acquittal or conviction on related counts confer standing to contest the statute’s validity on structural grounds. *Giaccio v. Pennsylvania*, 382 U.S. 399 (1965).

The Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act rests on the treaty power, which Congress may implement without a separate federal-interest element. *Serra v. Mortiga*, 204 U.S. 470 (1907) (court competent to decide whether charged act constitutes crime). Private parties cannot convert that legislative choice into a personal defense. *Jenkins v. McKeithen*, 395 U.S. 411 (1969) (complaint failed to state cause of action under federal statutes). Voluntariness of statements or right-to-counsel questions likewise do not open the Tenth Amendment door. *Davis v. North Carolina*, 384 U.S. 737 (1966); *Heller v. Connecticut*, 389 U.S. 902 (1967); *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948).

Because Bond presents only an undifferentiated grievance shared with the citizenry at large, the judgment below should be affirmed.
