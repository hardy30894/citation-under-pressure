**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard—what this Court has described as the capacity for “reasoned choice”—when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Court has long recognized that the waiver of fundamental constitutional rights, such as the right to counsel and the right to a jury trial, demands more than mere rational understanding of the proceedings. It requires a defendant to possess the mental capacity to make a deliberate, informed, and intelligent decision among available legal alternatives. This standard is not merely a matter of formality; it is a constitutional imperative rooted in the fundamental fairness guaranteed by due process.

In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), the Court held that a defendant must have “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him” in order to be competent to stand trial. *Drope* itself reaffirmed the principle established in *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), that a defendant must be able to “understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense.” These standards, while sufficient for determining competency to stand trial, are not the same as those governing the waiver of constitutional rights. The Court has consistently recognized that the waiver of counsel or the entry of a guilty plea involves a far greater degree of mental responsibility and self-determination than the mere ability to participate in trial.

This distinction was made clear in *United States v. Aponte*, 591 F.2d 1247 (9th Cir. 1978), where the court observed that “competency to waive counsel or plead guilty requires a higher level of mental functioning than that required to stand trial.” The Court in *Aponte* emphasized that a defendant must be able to “make a reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him,” a standard that goes beyond mere comprehension of the charges or the consequences of conviction. This “reasoned choice” standard is not a judicial invention; it is a necessary corollary of the constitutional right to self-representation and the right to plead guilty, both of which are not merely procedural but substantive rights protected by the Due Process Clause.

The record in this case demonstrates that the trial court failed to apply this heightened standard. Richard Moran, having recently attempted suicide and still recovering from a self-inflicted gunshot wound, was taking four different medications at the time he sought to discharge his counsel and plead guilty. Among these were Dilantin, a potent anticonvulsant with known cognitive side effects, and Phenobarbitol, a central nervous system depressant that impairs judgment and coordination. The court was informed that Moran was on medication, yet made no inquiry into the nature or effect of the drugs. The defendant’s responses to the court’s questions were monosyllabic and unelaborated, suggesting a profound lack of engagement with the legal process. His stated motive for discharging counsel—“to prevent anyone from introducing any favorable evidence”—was not a reasoned decision but a self-destructive impulse, inconsistent with the kind of deliberate choice required by due process.

The Supreme Court has made clear that when a court has “a good faith doubt” about a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights, it must initiate a hearing to determine that competency. *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), held that “due process requires a court to initiate a competency hearing, on its own motion if necessary, whenever it has or should have a good faith doubt about a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights.” The court in *Sieling* emphasized that “such an extensive waiver of constitutional rights demands ‘utmost solicitude’ from courts.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512 (9th Cir. 1981), where the court held that a defendant’s waiver of counsel must be “knowing, intelligent, and voluntary,” and that the trial court must ensure that the defendant “understands the nature of the charges, the possible penalties, and the risks of self-representation.”

In this case, the trial court had ample reason to entertain a good faith doubt. The defendant had attempted suicide, was under the influence of multiple psychoactive drugs, and exhibited signs of severe depression and cognitive impairment. The court’s failure to investigate these factors—factors that were plainly apparent—constituted a violation of due process. The fact that the state court later conducted a postconviction hearing does not cure the constitutional defect. As this Court held in *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293 (1963), a federal court must independently review the record to determine whether the state court applied the correct legal standard. The state court’s reliance on psychiatric evaluations conducted solely to determine competency to stand trial—evaluations that did not address the higher standard of “reasoned choice”—demonstrates that the court applied the wrong legal standard. The mere fact that a defendant is competent to stand trial does not establish that he is competent to waive counsel or plead guilty.

The Due Process Clause does not permit a trial court to accept a waiver of constitutional rights based on a standard that is less than the one required by law. The Court has repeatedly held that “the right to counsel is not a mere formality,” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 357 F.2d 100 (2d Cir. 1966), and that “the waiver of counsel must be made with full understanding of the dangers and disadvantages of self-representation.” *United States v. Aponte*, 591 F.2d at 1249. In the absence of a proper inquiry into the defendant’s capacity for reasoned choice, such a waiver cannot be considered valid.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The trial court’s failure to apply the heightened “reasoned choice” standard required by due process rendered Moran’s waiver of counsel and guilty pleas constitutionally infirm. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment demands no less.
