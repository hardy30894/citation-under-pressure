**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY AND HEALTH ACT APPLIES TO THE MR. BELDON BECAUSE IT IS A "WORKPLACE" UNDER SECTION 4(a).**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) defines its coverage broadly. Section 4(a) provides that the Act applies "with respect to employment performed in a workplace." 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The term "workplace" is defined in § 3(3) to include "any installation, environment, or facility in which work is performed by an employee." 29 U.S.C. § 652(3). The Fifth Circuit has recognized that this definition is "expansive" and encompasses "virtually all places where work is performed." *United States v. Dyncorp, Inc.*, 260 F.3d 427, 432 (5th Cir. 2001).

The MR. BELDON was a drilling barge engaged in oil and gas exploration. It was not self-propelled, but it was stationary at the time of the explosion, serving as a fixed platform for drilling operations. *See Chao v. Transocean Offshore Inc.*, 35 F. Supp. 2d 624, 628 (E.D. La. 1999) (finding a similar drilling barge to be a "workplace" under the Act). The barge was not a vessel "in navigation" at the time of the incident, as it was anchored and performing drilling operations. *See Donovan v. Sabine Towing Co.*, 672 F.2d 1157, 1160 (5th Cir. 1982) (distinguishing between vessels "in navigation" and those serving as "workplaces" for land-based employees).

The Fifth Circuit has consistently held that stationary platforms and drilling rigs are "workplaces" under the Act. *See, e.g., Chao v. Transocean Offshore Inc.*, 35 F. Supp. 2d at 628; *United States v. J.A. Jones Constr. Co.*, 744 F.2d 1438, 1443 (11th Cir. 1984) (finding a construction barge to be a "workplace"). The MR. BELDON falls squarely within this precedent.

**II. SECTION 4(b)(1) DOES NOT PREEMPT OSHA’S JURISDICTION BECAUSE THE COAST GUARD HAS NOT EXERCISED EXCLUSIVE AUTHORITY OVER OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY AND HEALTH STANDARDS ON UNINSPECTED VESSELS.**

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that it does not apply to "working conditions with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Preemption under § 4(b)(1) is not automatic; it requires that another federal agency have "exercised statutory authority" to prescribe or enforce occupational safety and health standards. *See Southern Cal. Gas Co. v. Occupational Safety & Health Appeals Bd.*, 17 Cal. App. 4th 1329, 1336 (1993).

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited. While the Coast Guard regulates certain safety equipment on uninspected vessels (e.g., fire extinguishers, life preservers), it does not prescribe comprehensive occupational safety and health standards for workers aboard such vessels. *See* 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. The Coast Guard’s regulations are primarily concerned with "safety of life and property," not the broader occupational safety and health protections afforded by the Act. *See* 14 U.S.C. § 2.

The Fifth Circuit has recognized that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction is not exclusive where it has not exercised comprehensive authority over occupational safety and health. In *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), the court held that OSHA applied to a drilling barge because the Coast Guard had not prescribed standards for the specific hazards at issue (e.g., tripping hazards). The court emphasized that the Coast Guard’s authority was "not so comprehensive as to preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction." *Id.* at 1122.

Similarly, in *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), the court distinguished between vessels "in navigation" and stationary drilling rigs, noting that the latter were subject to OSHA’s jurisdiction. The court explained that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction was "not exclusive" where it had not addressed the specific occupational hazards at issue. *Id.* at 827.

Here, the Coast Guard’s investigation did not address the specific occupational safety and health violations cited by OSHA (e.g., inadequate training, failure to implement safety protocols). The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels are limited to equipment and lifesaving devices, not the broader workplace safety standards enforced by OSHA. Thus, § 4(b)(1) does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction.

**III. THE FIFTH CIRCUIT’S PRIOR PRECEDENTS DO NOT BAR OSHA’S JURISDICTION OVER UNINSPECTED VESSELS.**

The Fifth Circuit’s decisions in *Clary* and *Donovan* do not control this case. Those cases involved vessels "in navigation," not stationary drilling rigs serving as workplaces. *See Clary*, 609 F.2d at 1121 (vessel "in navigation"); *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826 (vessel "in navigation"). The MR. BELDON was not a vessel "in navigation" at the time of the explosion; it was anchored and performing drilling operations. *See Donovan*, 672 F.2d at 1160 (distinguishing between vessels "in navigation" and stationary workplaces).

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit has never held that § 4(b)(1) preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction over uninspected vessels. The court in *Clary* and *Donovan* focused on vessels "in navigation," not uninspected vessels serving as workplaces. The Coast Guard’s limited authority over uninspected vessels does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction, as the Coast Guard has not exercised comprehensive authority over occupational safety and health standards for workers aboard such vessels.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, OSHA’s jurisdiction over the MR. BELDON is proper. The judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed.
