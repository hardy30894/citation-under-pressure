ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit erred in creating a FOIA exemption untethered to the statute’s text. FOIA authorizes withholding only for matters that fall within one of the nine “explicitly exclusive” exemptions Congress enacted. Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976). Congress chose narrow language in Exemption 2: “matters that are related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The court below replaced that text with a free-floating test for documents “predominantly” internal and potentially useful to those who might evade regulation. That approach cannot be reconciled with the statutory command that exemptions be construed narrowly. Id. at 360-61.

Even before 1970, this Court repeatedly required agencies to stay within the boundaries Congress drew. In EPA v. Mink, 410 U.S. 73 (1973), the Court later explained that FOIA’s exemptions must be applied according to their terms, not broadened to protect information merely because disclosure may be inconvenient or sensitive. But the relevant pre-1970 authority already pointed in the same direction. In NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson, 357 U.S. 449 (1958), the Court held that compelled disclosure burdened associational rights and demanded a showing of a sufficiently weighty justification. The principle is the same here: government secrecy must rest on lawful authority, not on generalized assertions of risk.

Exemption 2 does not reach the Navy’s ESQD materials. The documents at issue are technical safety and blast-effect studies used to design munitions storage and assess danger to surrounding communities. They are not “personnel rules and practices” within the ordinary meaning of those words. When Congress used the phrase “internal personnel rules and practices,” it naturally referred to rules governing the agency’s own employees—not engineering data about the effects of explosions on nearby land and water. See S.Rep. No. 813, 89th Cong., 1st Sess. 8 (1965) (describing Exemption 2 as covering trivial matters such as “parking facilities,” “lunch hours,” and “housekeeping matters”). The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” gloss cannot overcome the statute’s focus on personnel rules. See American Mail Line, Ltd. v. Gulick, 411 F.2d 696, 701-02 (D.C. Cir. 1969) (recognizing that Exemption 2 is limited to internal administrative matters of a personnel character).

Nor does a supposed risk of circumvention expand Exemption 2 beyond its text. This Court had not, by 1970, held that potentially exploitable government records are exempt merely because disclosure might aid outsiders in understanding agency operations. To the contrary, when Congress wanted to protect information whose disclosure would cause specific harms, it said so expressly in other exemptions. The Ninth Circuit’s rule collapses that careful structure and allows agencies to withhold virtually any technical document by hypothesizing misuse. That is precisely the sort of broad secrecy FOIA was enacted to displace. See Rose, 425 U.S. at 360-61.

The government may argue that the ESQD materials are “internal” because they are used within the Navy and not circulated to the public. But many records are used internally and still must be disclosed. FOIA’s structure turns on what the documents are, not merely where they are kept. In Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352 (1976), the Court emphasized that the Act requires disclosure unless a document fits an exemption; internal use alone does not suffice. The Ninth Circuit’s approach would make “internal” a synonym for “withholdable,” contrary to Congress’s choice of the narrower phrase “internal personnel rules and practices.”

The proper result is straightforward. The Navy has not shown that ESQD blast-effect data are personnel rules or practices, much less that Congress authorized withholding them as such. Because Exemption 2 does not apply, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded with instructions to disclose the requested documents.
