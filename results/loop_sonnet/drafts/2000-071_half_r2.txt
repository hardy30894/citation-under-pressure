# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Policy Reasonably Confines Its Limited Forum to Secular Community Uses, and Excluding Religious Worship Is a Permissible Definition of That Forum's Boundaries, Not Viewpoint Discrimination.

A school building is not a street or park held, as this Court has described traditional public fora, "immemorially . . . for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. Committee for Indus. Org.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Milford's building is a facility of limited and specific dedication—to the education of children during the day and, after hours, to social, civic, and recreational activities benefiting the community, as the Community Use Policy itself provides. Because the building is not a traditional public forum, the school retains broad authority to define the boundaries of the access it grants. As this Court has explained, "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). A government need not convert its property into a forum for every use citizens might wish to make of it merely because it has opened the property for some limited purposes. *Id.*

This Court has likewise recognized that reasonable, evenhanded regulation of the use of public property is consistent with the First Amendment so long as it is not a subterfuge for suppressing particular ideas. In upholding a licensing requirement for parades on public ways, the Court observed that "[c]ivil liberties, as guaranteed by the Constitution, imply the existence of an organized society maintaining public order without which liberty itself would be lost in the excesses of unrestrained abuses." *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941). Milford's Community Use Policy operates on the same principle: it excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which faith or sect might wish to conduct it. The Good News Club's proposed meeting, which the record shows opens and closes in prayer, includes a "challenge and invitation" inviting children to accept Christ as their personal savior, and culminates in a recited sinner's prayer, is not the teaching of morals from a religious perspective; it is religious instruction and worship of the very kind the Policy reserves against. Excluding it from a limited forum dedicated to civic and recreational activity is a permissible definition of the forum's boundaries, not discrimination against a viewpoint on a subject otherwise open.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Involving School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the Policy's application to the Club implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that permitting the Club's activities in its facility would create Establishment Clause difficulties was reasonable and sufficient justification. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that public schools occupy a uniquely sensitive position in Establishment Clause doctrine because they involve compulsory, impressionable, school-age children. "[I]t is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government," *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962), and the state may not sponsor devotional religious exercises within the school setting, *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 223 (1963). The concern is heightened, not diminished, when the activity takes place in the school building immediately after the close of the school day, using the same classrooms, under the continuing auspices of the school's own property, and directed at the same young children who attend that school. As this Court held in striking down devotional exercises conducted in public school classrooms, the use of "tax-supported public school buildings" for religious instruction offends the Establishment Clause even absent formal compulsion. *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948).

To be sure, this Court has recognized that the Religion Clauses do not require the state to be actively hostile to religion, observing that "[w]e are a religious people whose institutions presuppose a Supreme Being." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313 (1952). But *Zorach* permitted released-time programs precisely because they occurred *off* school property, avoiding any appearance that the school itself sponsored religious exercise. *Id.* at 308–09. Milford's building, by contrast, is the very site of instruction, and the Establishment Clause commands that "[g]overnment in our democracy, state and national, must be neutral in matters of religious theory, doctrine, and practice." *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 103–04 (1968). Given the Club's own description of its activities as prayer, scripture memorization, and religious conversion directed at grade-school children on school premises, Milford could reasonably conclude that hosting the Club would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *Engel*, *Schempp*, and *McCollum* forbid. The exclusion was therefore justified on this independent ground as well.
