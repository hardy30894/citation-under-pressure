**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s "Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt" Standard for Polling Employees on Union Support is Arbitrary and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The National Labor Relations Act does not expressly address employer polling. However, this Court has long recognized that employer conduct must be evaluated under the general prohibitions of §§ 8(a)(1) and 8(a)(5). *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). The Board’s current polling standard—requiring an employer to possess objective evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition before it may conduct a poll—defeats the legitimate purpose of polling and is therefore arbitrary and capricious.

An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). When an employer has a good-faith reasonable doubt about that majority status, the employer may: (1) withdraw recognition; (2) petition for a Board-conducted RM election; or (3) conduct an internal poll. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1989 WL 224426 (1989). The Board requires the same quantum of evidence—objective evidence sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about majority status—for all three options. *Id.*

This “one-size-fits-all” standard is irrational. A poll is a diagnostic tool, not a final act. It serves the legitimate purpose of enabling an employer to ascertain the truth about employee sentiment before taking the drastic step of withdrawing recognition. As the Fifth Circuit recognized, “[w]hen an employer has not engaged in unfair labor practices or otherwise created a coercive atmosphere, it may poll the employees for their union sentiment if there is other substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support (even if that evidence is not sufficient by itself to justify withdrawal).” *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981) (quoting *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)).

The Sixth and Ninth Circuits have similarly rejected the Board’s standard. *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). These courts correctly reasoned that if an employer must already have sufficient evidence to withdraw recognition before it can poll, polling becomes “useless.” *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1299. A rational system permits an employer to gather information—via a poll conducted with adequate procedural safeguards—before committing to a final course of action.

The Board’s insistence that polling requires the same showing as withdrawal of recognition creates an anomaly: an employer must have enough evidence to act definitively before it can seek confirming evidence. This undermines the statutory policy of promoting stable collective bargaining by encouraging employers to verify their doubts rather than act on them unilaterally. The Board’s standard should be vacated as arbitrary.

**II. Even Under the Board’s Own Standard, the Determination That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

In the alternative, even if the Board’s standard is upheld, its factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The Board itself found that seven employees made statements repudiating the union. But the Board erroneously excluded additional evidence that demonstrated widespread loss of support.

First, the Board improperly discounted the statements of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh. Ridgick stated that “as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union.” Marsh stated that he was paying $35 monthly in union dues and “not getting his money’s worth.” Both statements were made during job interviews before they were hired. The Board applied a presumption—based on *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888 (1978)—that such statements are insincere because the employer had indicated the company would be non-union. This presumption is unsupported. An applicant who has already been offered a job has no motive to feign opposition to union representation; if anything, an applicant with prounion sympathies would hide them to secure employment. The Board’s contrary assumption defies common sense.

Second, the Board improperly excluded the statements of Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr, who reported that other employees—the entire night shift and service department employees respectively—did not want the union. The Board dismissed these as “unverified assertions” even though no contradictory evidence was introduced. The Board has no authority to reject competent, uncontradicted evidence merely because it is hearsay or because the reporting employee did not testify. *Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 868 (reversing Board where “unrefuted evidence” showed loss of union support).

Third, the Board relied on an arbitrary cutoff date of January 25, 1991—the date of Allentown Mack’s letter refusing recognition. However, the poll was conducted in February 1991. The views of employees hired after January 25 but before the poll were relevant to whether the poll itself was supported by a reasonable doubt at the time it was conducted. The Board’s rigid date restriction improperly excluded probative evidence.

Finally, the polling results themselves—19 to 13 against the union—are powerful evidence that the doubt was well-founded. This Court has recognized that a poll conducted with scrupulous fairness can be evidence of the employer’s good faith. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 609 (1969). Here, the poll was supervised by a Roman Catholic priest, with no company interference. The results corroborate the objective evidence of loss of support.

Where, as here, an employer possesses substantial, objective evidence of diminished union support—including employee statements, reports of widespread dissatisfaction, and a fair poll confirming the loss—the Board’s conclusion that no reasonable doubt existed is arbitrary and unsupported.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the petition for review should be granted and the Board’s order should be vacated. Alternatively, the case should be remanded for application of a proper legal standard.
