ARGUMENT

I. THE TRIBAL COURT'S FAILURE TO ENSURE SELAM UNDERSTOOD AND COULD EXERCISE HIS RIGHT TO COMPULSORY PROCESS VIOLATED 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6).

Congress guaranteed criminal defendants in tribal court "the right … to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor." 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). That guarantee mirrors the Sixth Amendment, and the Supreme Court has recognized that the right to compulsory process is "a fundamental element of due process of law," Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967), because "the right to offer the testimony of witnesses … is in plain terms the right to present a defense." Id. at 19. The right is not a mere formality; it is "essential to due process," Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400, 409 (1988), and its exercise depends on the defendant actually understanding how to invoke it.

Here, Selam was represented not by a licensed attorney but by a lay Tribal Spokesperson. While the tribal judge and Leno-Baker each told Selam that he had a right to compel witnesses, advising a lay defendant of an abstract legal right is not the same as ensuring that a defendant of ordinary understanding could exercise it. The record shows that Leno-Baker herself was unable to locate two of Selam's five proposed witnesses and that Selam's own efforts to assist her were, at best, disorganized. Nothing in the record establishes that Selam understood that he — rather than his lay representative — needed to personally ask the tribal judge to issue a subpoena, or that his failure to do so would operate as a forfeiture of the right altogether.

Waiver of a fundamental right cannot be presumed from a silent or confused record; it must be the product of a knowing and intentional relinquishment. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (courts must "indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver" of a constitutional right). Because the record does not establish that Selam knowingly relinquished his right to compel the attendance of the witnesses he identified, the tribal court of appeals — and the district court on habeas review — erred in treating his failure to formally invoke that right as a valid waiver rather than as a denial of compulsory process itself.

II. SELAM'S CONFRONTATION CLAUSE CLAIM WAS NOT PROPERLY DEEMED WAIVED, AND IN ANY EVENT SHOULD BE EXCUSED FROM ANY PROCEDURAL DEFAULT.

Section 1302(6) of the ICRA also guarantees a tribal defendant the right "to confront and cross-examine any adverse witnesses." That guarantee tracks the Confrontation Clause, which the Supreme Court has described as forbidding conviction on the basis of "ex parte affidavits" and requiring that testimony against a defendant be given "face to face." Coy v. Iowa, 487 U.S. 1012, 1016 (1988) (quoting Mattox v. United States, 156 U.S. 237, 242-43 (1895)). In the second trial, the five-year-old complainant never testified; instead her accusation reached the tribal judge exclusively through the hearsay recitations of her parents, a physician, and a police investigator. Selam had no opportunity whatsoever to test that evidence through cross-examination.

The district court held that Selam waived this claim by failing to present it to the Warm Springs Tribal Court of Appeals. But exhaustion of tribal remedies is a matter of comity, not an inflexible jurisdictional bar, and federal courts retain discretion to reach the merits of an unexhausted claim where the interests of justice so require. Cf. Granberry v. Greer, 481 U.S. 129, 131 (1987) (a court of appeals may, "in the exercise of its discretion," decide the merits of a habeas claim notwithstanding a failure to exhaust, where "the interests of comity and federalism" are better served by doing so). Requiring exhaustion serves the purpose of allowing tribal courts the first opportunity to correct their own errors and to develop the record on tribal law; that purpose is not served here, because the tribal court of appeals already reviewed the full trial record and had every opportunity to notice the total absence of confrontation as to the second victim.

Even if this Court treats Selam's omission as a procedural default rather than mere non-exhaustion, default may be excused upon a showing of cause and prejudice. Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977); Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986) (cause may be shown where "some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel's efforts" to raise the claim). Selam's cause is plain: he was represented on direct appeal by the same lay advocate who represented him at trial, a person untrained in the law and demonstrably unfamiliar with the confrontation guarantee, since she never raised it despite the wholesale absence of the complaining witness. That failure was not a strategic choice attributable to Selam himself. And the prejudice is equally plain: Selam was convicted and imprisoned on hearsay from a child who was never subjected to cross-examination, in a proceeding that Coy v. Iowa, 487 U.S. at 1016, and Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. at 19, place at the very core of a fair adversarial trial.

For these reasons, the district court's judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to grant the writ unless the Tribe elects to retry Selam on the second charge consistent with his rights under 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6).
