**ARGUMENT**

I. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) does not permit the application of state law unless a gap exists in federal coverage.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), extends federal jurisdiction to the outer Continental Shelf and incorporates state law only “to the extent that [it] is applicable and not inconsistent with [federal law].” The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law only where federal law is silent or incomplete. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 359 (1969) (“[S]tate law is adopted as federal law only where federal law is silent.”); *Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co.*, 375 U.S. 34, 37 (1963) (per curiam) (state law applies “only to the extent that it is applicable and not inconsistent with federal law”).

This Court has long recognized that federal statutes like the Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”) are comprehensive and occupy the field of wage and hour regulation. *Walling v. Helmerich & Payne, Inc.*, 323 U.S. 37, 42 (1944) (“The Fair Labor Standards Act is a comprehensive statute designed to eliminate labor conditions detrimental to the maintenance of the minimum standard of living necessary for health, efficiency, and general well-being of workers.”). The FLSA contains an express savings clause allowing more protective state wage and overtime laws, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), but this clause does not authorize state law to fill perceived gaps where federal law is otherwise complete. *Overnight Motor Transp. Co. v. Missel*, 316 U.S. 572, 581 (1942) (“The Fair Labor Standards Act is a remedial statute and must be liberally construed to effectuate its purposes, but it is not to be extended by judicial legislation beyond the scope of its express provisions.”).

The Fifth Circuit’s “gap-filling” test is consistent with Supreme Court precedent. In *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law applies under the OCSLA only where “necessary to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. This interpretation comports with the Supreme Court’s admonition that state law is not to be applied where federal law is complete. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 359.

II. The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no gap for state wage and hour laws.

The FLSA establishes minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements, and its savings clause expressly preserves state laws that are more protective. 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). However, the savings clause does not authorize state law to supplement federal wage and hour protections where federal law is otherwise complete. *Warren-Bradshaw Drilling Co. v. Hall*, 317 U.S. 88, 91 (1942) (“The Fair Labor Standards Act is a comprehensive statute, and its provisions must be read in harmony with each other.”).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized the FLSA’s comprehensive nature. In *Walling v. Helmerich & Payne, Inc.*, 323 U.S. 37 (1944), the Court held that the FLSA’s overtime provisions apply to employees engaged in production for interstate commerce, even where state law might otherwise provide for different compensation. The Court emphasized that the FLSA’s provisions are mandatory and not subject to supplementation by state law. *Id.* at 42.

Similarly, in *Walling v. A. H. Belo Corp.*, 316 U.S. 624 (1942), the Court rejected an employer’s attempt to avoid the FLSA’s overtime requirements by structuring compensation in a manner inconsistent with the Act. The Court held that the FLSA’s provisions are not subject to alteration by private agreement or state law. *Id.* at 630.

III. California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA and cannot be applied on the OCS.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision incorporates state law only if it is “not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). California’s meal period and overtime laws conflict with the FLSA’s framework, which does not require meal periods and allows for alternative compensation structures. *Walling v. Youngerman-Reynolds Hardwood Co.*, 325 U.S. 419, 424 (1945) (“The Fair Labor Standards Act is a comprehensive statute, and its provisions must be read in harmony with each other.”).

The Supreme Court has held that state laws that frustrate the purposes of the FLSA are preempted. *Walling v. Halliburton Oil Well Cementing Co.*, 331 U.S. 17, 22 (1947) (“State laws that conflict with the purposes of the FLSA are without effect.”). California’s meal period requirements impose obligations not found in the FLSA and thus are inconsistent with federal law.

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA does not permit the application of state law unless federal law is silent or incomplete. The FLSA is a comprehensive statute that leaves no gap for state wage and hour laws. California’s laws are inconsistent with the FLSA and cannot be applied on the OCS. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
