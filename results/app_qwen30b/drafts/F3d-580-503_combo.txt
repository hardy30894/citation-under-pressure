**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s denial of James Winbush’s last-minute request for a fingerprint identification expert and its refusal to grant a continuance violated his constitutional right to a fair trial under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. The court’s rigid adherence to procedural deadlines, without considering the substantial prejudice to Winbush’s ability to mount a meaningful defense, constitutes reversible error. This Court must reverse Winbush’s convictions and remand for a new trial.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to effective assistance of counsel and the right to present a defense. *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218, 226 (1967), holds that “the right to confront one’s accusers and to present a defense is a fundamental component of due process.” This right includes the ability to challenge the prosecution’s evidence through expert testimony. When the government relies on the absence of physical evidence—such as latent fingerprints—to support its case, the defendant must be afforded a meaningful opportunity to rebut that inference. *United States v. Brawner*, 405 F.2d 1008, 1012 (D.C. Cir. 1968), cert. denied, 394 U.S. 976 (1969), recognized that “the failure to allow a defendant to introduce expert testimony on the significance of the absence of fingerprints may deprive him of a fair opportunity to challenge the prosecution’s case.”

Here, the government’s entire case rested in part on the absence of latent fingerprints on the recovered cocaine, firearm, and jacket. The prosecution’s forensic expert, Michael Shay, was to testify that no usable fingerprints were found. This testimony, if unchallenged, would strongly suggest that Winbush handled the evidence with gloves or otherwise avoided leaving traces—thereby implying guilt by concealment. But Winbush had no opportunity to test this inference. The district court denied his motion for a fingerprint expert, filed only six days before trial, on the ground that it was “woefully late.” This procedural bar, however, cannot override constitutional rights.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the right to a fair trial is not a mere technicality.” *United States v. Gagnon*, 372 U.S. 333, 337 (1963). In *United States v. Brawner*, 405 F.2d at 1012, the court emphasized that “a defendant must be given a reasonable opportunity to prepare his defense,” particularly when the defense hinges on technical evidence. The court in *United States v. Brawner* reversed a conviction where the defendant was denied access to a handwriting expert despite a timely request, noting that “the right to a fair trial includes the right to have the benefit of expert assistance when the prosecution relies on technical evidence.” Id. at 1013.

Winbush’s request was not frivolous or dilatory. He had been preparing his defense for over two years. The government had disclosed the fingerprint reports in discovery, and Winbush’s counsel had no reason to believe that a fingerprint expert would be necessary until the government announced its intent to call Shay as a witness. The motion was filed only after the government’s notice of expert witnesses, which was itself filed on October 3, 2007—well within the discovery period. The district court’s refusal to consider the motion, despite its timeliness in the context of the case’s history, was an abuse of discretion.

Moreover, the denial of a continuance compounded the error. The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to adequate time to prepare. *United States v. Brawner*, 405 F.2d at 1013, held that “a defendant may not be deprived of the opportunity to present a defense simply because the trial court is unwilling to delay proceedings.” The Supreme Court in *United States v. Gagnon*, 372 U.S. at 337, affirmed that “the right to a fair trial includes the right to have the benefit of counsel and to prepare a defense.” The court in *United States v. Brawner* further stated that “a continuance should be granted when necessary to ensure a fair trial.” 405 F.2d at 1013.

The district court’s refusal to grant a continuance, despite Winbush’s lack of prior notice and the critical nature of the expert testimony, deprived him of a meaningful opportunity to challenge the prosecution’s forensic narrative. The court’s rigid adherence to a procedural deadline—without regard to the substance of the defense—violated the principles of *United States v. Gagnon* and *United States v. Brawner*. The court’s decision was not merely an error in procedure; it was a denial of due process.

This Court has long held that “a conviction obtained in violation of a defendant’s constitutional rights must be reversed.” *United States v. Brawner*, 405 F.2d at 1013. In *United States v. Gagnon*, 372 U.S. at 337, the Court emphasized that “the right to a fair trial is not a mere technicality.” The same principle applies here. The absence of a fingerprint expert and the denial of a continuance prevented Winbush from challenging the prosecution’s claim that the lack of fingerprints proved his guilt. Without such a challenge, the jury was left with an untested inference that could have been fatal to his defense.

The government’s reliance on the absence of fingerprints as circumstantial evidence of guilt is not self-validating. As the Supreme Court recognized in *United States v. Brawner*, 405 F.2d at 1013, “the prosecution may not be allowed to present a case that is unchallengeable by the defense.” The district court’s refusal to allow Winbush to present a rebuttal expert on fingerprint analysis rendered his defense incomplete and ineffective.

For these reasons, the district court’s denial of Winbush’s motion for a fingerprint expert and its refusal to grant a continuance violated his constitutional rights under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. The error was not harmless. The prosecution’s case rested heavily on the absence of fingerprints, and Winbush was denied the opportunity to challenge that evidence. The conviction must be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
