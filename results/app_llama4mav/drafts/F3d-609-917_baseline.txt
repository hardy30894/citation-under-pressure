ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Failing to Dismiss Ray's State Tort Claims as Preempted by Federal Aviation Legislation

The Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 (ADA) preempts state laws "relating to a price, route, or service of an airline." 49 U.S.C. § 41713(b)(1). The Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to preempt state tort claims that have a "connection with or reference to" airline prices, routes, or services. Morales v. TWA, 504 U.S. 374, 384 (1992). The Court has further clarified that the ADA preempts claims that have a "significant impact" on airline services, even if they are not directly related to prices or routes. American Airlines, Inc. v. Wolens, 513 U.S. 219, 232-33 (1995).

In this case, Ray's claims for false imprisonment and negligence arise from American's decision to keep passengers on the plane during the tarmac delay. The decision to provide or not provide services, including the decision to deplane passengers, is a quintessential airline service. See, e.g., Smith v. Comair, Inc., 134 F.3d 254, 259 (4th Cir. 1998) (holding that a claim related to an airline's boarding procedures was preempted by the ADA). As the Fifth Circuit has held, "boarding procedures are a service within the meaning of § 41713(b)(1)." Hodges v. Delta Airlines, Inc., 44 F.3d 334, 336 (5th Cir. 1995) (en banc). Similarly, the decision to provide or not provide food and other amenities to passengers during a delay is also a service that is preempted by the ADA. See, e.g., Branche v. Airtran Airways, Inc., 342 F.3d 1248, 1255-56 (11th Cir. 2003) (holding that a claim related to an airline's provision of food and beverages was preempted by the ADA).

Ray's claims are therefore preempted by the ADA because they relate to American's services, including its decision to keep passengers on the plane and its provision of food and other amenities during the delay. The district court erred in failing to dismiss these claims as preempted.

II. The District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment in Favor of American was Proper

Even if Ray's claims are not preempted, the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of American was proper. Under Arkansas law, a claim for false imprisonment requires a showing that the defendant intentionally confined the plaintiff without consent or legal justification. See, e.g., Head v. McCotter, 165 F. Supp. 2d 802, 809 (W.D. Ark. 2001) (applying Arkansas law). Ray has failed to present evidence that American intentionally confined her or that she was confined without consent or legal justification. To the contrary, the record shows that American provided Ray with multiple opportunities to deplane, which she declined.

Similarly, Ray's negligence claim requires a showing that American breached a duty of care owed to her. See, e.g., Young v. Paxton, 316 Ark. 655, 658 (1994). Ray has failed to present evidence that American breached any duty of care owed to her. The record shows that American took reasonable steps to care for its passengers during the delay, including providing some food and beverages.

The district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of American was therefore proper, and should be affirmed.

Because the district court erred in failing to dismiss Ray's claims as preempted, this Court should reverse and remand with instructions to dismiss. Alternatively, the Court should affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of American.
