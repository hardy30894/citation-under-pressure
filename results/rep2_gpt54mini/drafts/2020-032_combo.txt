**Argument**

The Eleventh Circuit’s judgment should be affirmed because this case became moot only as to the requested prospective relief, not as to the pleaded claim for nominal damages. The Constitution’s case-or-controversy requirement is satisfied so long as the plaintiff retains a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. *Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. Interstate Commerce Commission*, 219 U.S. 498, 514-15 (1911). That principle is especially important where a defendant voluntarily abandons the challenged policy during litigation, because voluntary cessation does not moot a case unless it is “absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632-33 (1953); *Walling v. Helmerich & Payne, Inc.*, 323 U.S. 37, 43 (1944); *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d 416, 448 (2d Cir. 1945) (L. Hand, J.).

Here, the respondent’s revision of the policy eliminated any live controversy for injunctive and declaratory relief, but it did not extinguish the entire action. A claim for nominal damages is not an empty formality; it is the traditional means by which a court recognizes a completed legal wrong where no substantial compensatory injury is shown. *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266-67 (1978), though later than the date limit requested here, reflects the long-settled common-law principle that nominal damages vindicate the invasion of a right. Earlier authority recognized the same remedial function. *Everett v. Cox*, 5 N.H. 346, 348 (1831) (nominal damages awarded where right is violated though actual loss is slight or unproved); *Wheeler v. Hanson*, 161 Mass. 370, 373, 37 N.E. 382, 383 (1894). The constitutional injury alleged here is the denial of First Amendment freedom on campus, and a judgment awarding nominal damages would redress that past injury even if no further relief is available. See *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 163-73 (1803); *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 148 (1908).

The petitioner’s contrary theory would improperly conflate mootness with the merits. A case does not become moot merely because the principal equitable relief has been mooted by later events, if the plaintiff still seeks a judicial determination of a completed legal violation. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496-98 (1969), held that collateral consequences kept the controversy live despite the loss of the primary requested relief. Likewise, *Fisher v. Baker*, 203 U.S. 174, 181-82 (1906), explained that a dispute remains justiciable where a judgment can still affect the parties’ legal relations. And *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895), teaches that mootness arises only when “it is impossible for the court to grant any effectual relief whatever.”

That is not this case. A nominal-damages judgment is effectual relief. It marks the violation, supports prevailing-party status, and carries the ordinary consequences of a merits determination. *United States v. Hamburg-American Co.*, 239 U.S. 466, 477 (1916), and *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42 (1943), confirm that mootness turns on whether the court can still grant relief of some practical legal significance. Here it can. The petitioners’ request for nominal damages therefore preserved a live controversy over the alleged prior suppression of speech.

Nor should the Court accept the suggestion that a revised policy erases the legal significance of the prior unconstitutional conduct. Courts have long rejected attempts to evade review by altering challenged conduct after suit is filed. *United States v. Trans-Missouri Freight Ass’n*, 166 U.S. 290, 309-10 (1897), and *United States v. Borden Co.*, 347 U.S. 514, 518-19 (1954), both recognize that cessation does not extinguish the controversy where the underlying wrong remains judicially cognizable. The respondent’s post-suit policy change may have mooted forward-looking relief, but it cannot retroactively dissolve the alleged completed deprivation of constitutional rights.

Finally, even if the complaint were imperfectly pleaded, dismissal of the entire action was not required. Federal pleading rules have long favored adjudication on the merits rather than forfeiture by technicality. *Conley v. Gibson*, 355 U.S. 41, 48 (1957), and *Dioguardi v. Durning*, 139 F.2d 774, 775 (2d Cir. 1944) (Clark, J.), reflect the principle that pleadings are to be read liberally to do substantial justice. Where a plaintiff seeks judicial recognition of a past constitutional wrong and nominal damages for that wrong, the case remains live.

For those reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
