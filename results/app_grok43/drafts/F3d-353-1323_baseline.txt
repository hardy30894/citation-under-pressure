ARGUMENT

The district court erred in holding that the commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), does not confer subject-matter jurisdiction. The Supreme Court has instructed that a foreign state engages in commercial activity when it undertakes acts “of the same character as a private player” might perform in the market. Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). The nature of the conduct, not its purpose, controls. Id.

Here the Government of Punjab dispatched a representative to the United States who offered Beg substitute land in exchange for relinquishing his claim to the expropriated parcel. That transaction is indistinguishable from an ordinary real-estate exchange between private parties. The subsequent rejection of the exchange by the Supreme Court of Pakistan and its invitation to pursue further remedies in Pakistan do not convert the antecedent commercial overture into a sovereign act. See Saudi Arabia v. Nelson, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993) (distinguishing the “nature of the conduct” from the “motives” that prompt it).

The same conduct produced a direct effect in the United States. The Supreme Court has defined a direct effect as one that follows “as an immediate consequence of the defendant’s activity.” Weltover, 504 U.S. at 618. The representative’s offer, extended on American soil, created an immediate legal relationship whose breach or non-performance is the basis of Beg’s claim for monetary relief. That effect is sufficiently direct to satisfy the statute.

Because the complaint rests on commercial activity carried on in the United States that caused a direct effect here, the district court possessed jurisdiction under § 1605(a)(2). The judgment of dismissal should be reversed.
