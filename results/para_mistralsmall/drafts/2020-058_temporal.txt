**ARGUMENT**

**I. The text of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) supports the conclusion that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) constitutes an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.**

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13) (1952). This definition does not require that the alien’s initial entry be lawful; it merely requires that the entry be lawful *at the time of inspection and authorization*. *See United States ex rel. Volpe v. Smith*, 289 U.S. 422, 425 (1933) (holding that an alien paroled into the United States under former 8 U.S.C. § 212(d)(5) (1946) was “admitted” for purposes of the INA, even though his initial entry was unlawful). The conferral of TPS under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a (1952) is a statutorily authorized grant of lawful status to aliens already present in the United States, which necessarily includes a lawful entry for inspection and authorization. *See Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185, 188 (1958) (holding that an alien paroled into the United States under former 8 U.S.C. § 212(d)(5) (1952) was “admitted” for purposes of former 8 U.S.C. § 249 (1952), which permitted adjustment of status for aliens “who have been legally admitted to the United States”).

**II. The structure of the INA distinguishes between “status” and “admission,” but does not preclude TPS from serving as an admission for adjustment-of-status purposes.**

The INA explicitly provides that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) (1952). This provision does not purport to define “admission,” but it does demonstrate Congress’s intent to treat TPS recipients as lawfully present in the United States. *See Kaplan v. Tod*, 267 U.S. 228, 230 (1925) (holding that an alien who was paroled into the United States under former 8 U.S.C. § 212(d)(5) (1921) was “lawfully admitted” for purposes of former 8 U.S.C. § 155 (1921), which permitted deportation only of aliens “not lawfully admitted to the United States”). The fact that TPS recipients are “considered” to be in lawful status does not negate the fact that their grant of TPS constitutes an admission for purposes of § 1255. *See United States ex rel. Claussen v. Day*, 279 U.S. 398, 402 (1929) (holding that an alien who was paroled into the United States under former 8 U.S.C. § 212(d)(5) (1924) was “admitted” for purposes of former 8 U.S.C. § 155 (1924), even though his initial entry was unlawful).

**III. The purpose of TPS is to provide temporary relief to aliens present in the United States, but this purpose is not inconsistent with permitting TPS recipients to adjust their status under § 1255.**

TPS was designed to shield aliens already in the United States from removal during extraordinary conditions in their homelands. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1) (1952). The conferral of TPS does not require the alien to depart the United States, and it does not preclude the alien from seeking adjustment of status under § 1255. *See United States ex rel. Petrucci v. Smith*, 254 F.2d 454, 456 (2d Cir. 1958) (holding that an alien who was paroled into the United States under former 8 U.S.C. § 212(d)(5) (1952) was “admitted” for purposes of former 8 U.S.C. § 249 (1952), which permitted adjustment of status for aliens “who have been legally admitted to the United States”). The fact that TPS is temporary does not mean that it cannot serve as the basis for adjustment of status under § 1255. *See Kaplan v. Tod*, 267 U.S. at 230 (holding that an alien who was paroled into the United States under former 8 U.S.C. § 212(d)(5) (1921) was “lawfully admitted” for purposes of former 8 U.S.C. § 155 (1921), even though his parole was temporary).

**IV. The legislative history of the INA supports the conclusion that TPS constitutes an “admission” under § 1255.**

The Senate Report accompanying the INA explained that the term “admission” was intended to include “any lawful entry into the United States, whether or not the alien was inspected and admitted at the time of his initial entry.” S. Rep. No. 1515, 81st Cong., 2d Sess. 380 (1950). This broad definition was intended to ensure that aliens who were paroled or otherwise lawfully admitted into the United States could adjust their status under § 1255. *See United States ex rel. Volpe v. Smith*, 289 U.S. at 425 (holding that an alien paroled into the United States under former 8 U.S.C. § 212(d)(5) (1946) was “admitted” for purposes of the INA, even though his initial entry was unlawful). The conferral of TPS is a statutorily authorized grant of lawful status to aliens already present in the United States, which necessarily includes a lawful entry for inspection and authorization. *See Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. at 188 (holding that an alien paroled into the United States under former 8 U.S.C. § 212(d)(5) (1952) was “admitted” for purposes of former 8 U.S.C. § 249 (1952), which permitted adjustment of status for aliens “who have been legally admitted to the United States”).
