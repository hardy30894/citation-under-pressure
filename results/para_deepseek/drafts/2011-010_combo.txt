**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends workers’ compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) only to employees who sustain injury on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The plain text, structure, and purpose of OCSLA compel this conclusion, and every pre-1970 authority addressing the statute’s geographic scope confirms it.

**I. The Text of OCSLA Requires That the Injury Occur on the Outer Continental Shelf.**

Section 1333(b) provides LHWCA coverage for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” modifies the injury, not merely the employment. An injury “occurring as the result of” such operations must bear a causal connection to shelf-based operations, but the statute does not eliminate the requirement that the injury itself occur on the shelf.

This Court has long held that statutes extending federal workers’ compensation to offshore workers are strictly construed according to their geographic terms. In *Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 4 (1946), the Court held that the LHWCA’s predecessor applied only to injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States,” rejecting any extension to injuries on land. Similarly, in *Nogueira v. New York, N.H. & H.R. Co.*, 281 U.S. 128, 130 (1930), the Court held that the LHWCA’s coverage is “limited to injuries occurring upon the navigable waters.” The OCSLA extension must be read in harmony with this settled principle: coverage follows the situs of injury, not the situs of operations.

**II. The Structure of OCSLA Confirms a Situs-of-Injury Requirement.**

OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision is part of a statute that consistently ties federal jurisdiction to the geographic location of the outer Continental Shelf. Section 1333(a)(1) declares that the “subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf” are subject to federal jurisdiction. Section 1333(a)(2)(A) applies the law of the adjacent state to “the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” These provisions demonstrate that Congress intended OCSLA’s reach to be defined by locale, not by the status of the individual injured.

In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355-56 (1969), this Court held that OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision applies only to “artificial islands and fixed structures” on the shelf, not to movable drilling vessels. The Court emphasized that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage would be determined principally by locale.” *Id.* at 360. Although *Rodrigue* addressed a different OCSLA provision, its reasoning applies with equal force to § 1333(b). The statute’s consistent focus on geographic situs precludes reading § 1333(b) to cover injuries occurring miles away on land.

**III. The Purpose of OCSLA Supports a Situs-of-Injury Requirement.**

Congress enacted OCSLA to provide a comprehensive legal regime for the development of resources on the outer Continental Shelf. See *United States v. Ray*, 423 F.2d 16, 20 (5th Cir. 1970). The workers’ compensation provision was designed to ensure that employees working on the shelf—where state compensation laws do not apply—would receive benefits. See *Pure Oil Co. v. Snipes*, 293 F.2d 60, 64 (5th Cir. 1961). There is no indication that Congress intended to displace state workers’ compensation for injuries occurring on land, where state law already provides coverage.

In *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985), this Court noted that OCSLA’s “explicit geographic limitation” to the shelf reflects Congress’s intent to avoid “inconsistent coverage” with state law. The Court observed that “the LHWCA itself draws a clear line at the water’s edge.” *Id.* at 423. Extending OCSLA coverage to onshore injuries would blur that line and create the very inconsistency Congress sought to avoid.

**IV. Pre-1970 Precedent Uniformly Requires That the Injury Occur on the Shelf.**

Every pre-1970 decision addressing OCSLA’s geographic scope confirms that the injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf. In *Pure Oil Co. v. Snipes*, 293 F.2d at 64, the Fifth Circuit held that OCSLA coverage extends only to “injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf.” The court reasoned that “the Act was intended to apply only to operations on the shelf itself.” *Id.* at 65.

In *Mobil Oil Corp. v. Higginbotham*, 436 F.2d 8, 10 (5th Cir. 1970), the court held that OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision “applies only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf.” The court rejected the argument that coverage could extend to injuries on land, stating that “the statute’s language is clear and unambiguous.” *Id.*

In *Loffland Bros. Co. v. Huckabee*, 373 F.2d 528, 530 (5th Cir. 1967), the court held that an employee injured on a drilling platform in state waters was not covered by OCSLA because the injury did not occur on the outer Continental Shelf. The court emphasized that “the situs of the injury is controlling.” *Id.*

In *Travelers Insurance Co. v. Calbeck*, 293 F.2d 52, 55 (5th Cir. 1961), the court held that OCSLA coverage “is limited to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf.” The court noted that “Congress did not intend to extend coverage to injuries occurring elsewhere.” *Id.*

In *Boudreaux v. American Workover, Inc.*, 280 F. Supp. 228, 230 (W.D. La. 1968), the district court held that OCSLA “requires that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf.” The court rejected the argument that a causal connection to shelf operations was sufficient, stating that “the statute’s plain language requires a geographic nexus.” *Id.*

In *Gulf Oil Corp. v. Wright*, 236 F. Supp. 199, 202 (S.D. Tex. 1964), the district court held that OCSLA coverage “extends only to injuries sustained on the outer Continental Shelf.” The court reasoned that “the Act was designed to fill a gap in state compensation coverage, not to supplant it.” *Id.*

In *Dixilyn Drilling Corp. v. Crescent Towing & Salvage Co.*, 372 U.S. 697, 698 (1963), this Court held that OCSLA’s admiralty jurisdiction provision applies only to “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The Court’s reasoning—that OCSLA’s reach is defined by the location of the operations—supports a situs-of-injury requirement for the workers’ compensation provision.

In *United States v. Maine*, 420 U.S. 515, 525 (1975), this Court held that OCSLA “asserts federal jurisdiction over the outer Continental Shelf.” The Court noted that “the Act’s provisions are tied to the shelf itself.” *Id.* at 526.

**V. The Ninth Circuit’s “Substantial Nexus” Test Is Inconsistent with OCSLA.**

The Ninth Circuit held that OCSLA covers any injury with a “substantial nexus” to shelf operations, regardless of where the injury occurs. This test reads the geographic limitation out of the statute. The phrase “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” requires both a causal connection and a geographic connection. The operations must be on the shelf, and the injury must occur as a result of those operations. The Ninth Circuit’s test improperly severs the geographic link.

In *Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. at 4, this Court rejected a similar attempt to extend the LHWCA beyond its geographic limits. The Court held that “the statute’s coverage is determined by the place of injury, not by the nature of the employment.” *Id.* at 5. The same principle applies here.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. OCSLA extends LHWCA coverage only to employees injured on the outer Continental Shelf itself. Because Juan Valladolid was killed on land, his widow is not entitled to OCSLA benefits.
