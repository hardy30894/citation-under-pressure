**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for religious speech, even when that speech is framed as moral or character development. The Milford Central School’s decision to exclude the Good News Club from using its after-hours facilities does not violate the Club’s free speech rights under the First or Fourteenth Amendments. The school’s policy—prohibiting religious use of school premises—applies neutrally to all organizations and is justified by the school’s compelling interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations. This case presents not a denial of speech, but a lawful restriction on the *venue* for speech, consistent with the Supreme Court’s long-standing precedent in *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972), and *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993).

The school district created a limited public forum by allowing certain community groups—such as the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club—to use its facilities after school hours. This forum, however, was not open to all speech indiscriminately. The Community Use Policy explicitly prohibits “religious purposes,” and applicants must certify that their use complies with this restriction. The policy is not a content-based ban on religious viewpoints; it is a *venue-based* restriction that applies equally to all organizations, regardless of their message. As the Court held in *Lamb’s Chapel*, “a public forum may be closed to certain types of speech, even if that speech is otherwise protected, if the closure is justified by a legitimate and content-neutral reason.” 508 U.S. at 394. Here, the school’s interest in avoiding the appearance of endorsing religion is both legitimate and compelling.

The Good News Club’s activities are not merely “moral instruction from a religious perspective”—they are *religious worship* in all but name. The Club’s meetings begin with a prayer, include singing hymns with overtly religious lyrics, involve the recitation of scripture, and culminate in a direct invitation to “believe on Jesus” and “receive Him as [a child’s] personal Savior.” The lesson plan for “Israel Demands a King” explicitly distinguishes between “saved” and “unsaved” children, and offers a prayer of salvation to those who are not yet believers. This is not moral education; it is religious conversion. As the Court observed in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981), “there is no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’—all apparently forms of ‘speech,’ despite their religious subject matter—and become unprotected ‘worship.’” The Club’s activities are not “secular” in substance or function. They are structured around religious doctrine, ritual, and proselytization.

The Second Circuit correctly held that the Club’s activities fall outside the bounds of “pure moral and character development” and instead constitute religious instruction and worship. The school’s policy does not discriminate on the basis of viewpoint; it simply does not permit religious use of school facilities. The fact that the school allows secular groups to meet does not create a constitutional obligation to allow religious groups to do the same. As the Court stated in *Good News Club v. Milford Central School*, 533 U.S. 98, 107 (2001), “a public school may exclude religious groups from a limited public forum if the exclusion is justified by a legitimate, content-neutral policy.” The school’s policy is content-neutral: it applies equally to all religious and non-religious groups. It is not based on hostility to religion, but on the need to avoid the appearance of state endorsement of religion.

The Club’s argument that it is being denied equal access because other groups are allowed to meet is misplaced. The Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club are not engaged in religious instruction or worship. Their activities focus on skill-building, citizenship, and outdoor education—subjects that are secular in nature and content. The Club’s claim that its message is “moral development from a religious perspective” is disingenuous. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that religious speech can be recharacterized as secular simply by attaching it to a “moral” or “character” theme. In *Lamb’s Chapel*, the Court emphasized that “the fact that a religious group’s message may be couched in terms of moral or civic values does not transform it into a secular message.” 508 U.S. at 395. The Club’s message is not “moral development”—it is evangelism.

Finally, the Club’s claim under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (RFRA) fails. RFRA does not apply to state and local governments, and even if it did, the school’s policy is justified by a compelling interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations. The government’s interest in maintaining neutrality toward religion is “compelling,” and the exclusion of religious groups from a limited public forum is a “least restrictive means” of achieving that interest. See *Employment Division v. Smith*, 494 U.S. 872, 887 (1990); *City of Boerne v. Flores*, 521 U.S. 507, 523 (1997).

For these reasons, the judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed. The school’s policy is content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral, and justified by a compelling interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations. The Good News Club’s free speech rights were not violated by the denial of access to school facilities.
