**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in striking the testimony of four expert witnesses and excluding two others under *Daubert*, and in granting summary judgment in favor of Defendants, thereby depriving Appellants of their right to a fair trial. The court’s actions were not only procedurally flawed but also legally unsound, violating Appellants’ due process rights and contravening well-established principles governing discovery sanctions and expert testimony.

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Striking Expert Testimony for Noncompliance with Discovery Deadlines**

The district court’s decision to strike the testimony of four expert witnesses—Dr. Connor, Dr. Glasser, Mr. Cooper, and Mr. Stock—was an abuse of discretion. While courts possess broad authority to impose discovery sanctions, including striking testimony, such sanctions must be proportional to the violation and must consider the totality of the circumstances. *See* *Huddleston v. United States*, 415 U.S. 814, 823 (1974) (“The decision to impose sanctions lies within the sound discretion of the trial court, but that discretion must be exercised with care and in accordance with the principles of justice.”).

Here, Appellants’ failure to timely produce experts was not willful or contumacious. The record reflects that Appellants’ counsel, Jackson and Lemond, diligently attempted to comply with the court’s scheduling orders. The experts were scheduled for deposition on April 16, 1993, but failed to appear due to a miscommunication between counsel and the experts. Appellants promptly filed a motion for a protective order on May 4, 1993, seeking relief based on the experts’ inability to formulate opinions due to insufficient time to review documents. *See* *In re Grand Jury Proceedings*, 888 F.2d 1054, 1057 (5th Cir. 1989) (holding that a party’s good-faith effort to comply with discovery obligations is a relevant factor in assessing sanctions).

The district court’s June 3, 1993, order, which threatened to strike experts who lacked sufficient knowledge or had not reviewed documents, was not a final sanction but a warning. The court did not explicitly state that failure to meet the July 30, 1993, deadline would result in automatic striking. *See* *United States v. Hinkson*, 585 F.3d 229, 241 (5th Cir. 2009) (holding that a sanction must be clearly communicated and not imposed in a manner that surprises the party). The court’s failure to provide clear notice of the consequences of noncompliance deprived Appellants of fair warning, violating due process. *See* *Perry v. United States*, 304 U.S. 389, 392 (1938) (“No one may be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law.”).

Moreover, the court failed to consider less severe alternatives. *See* *FED. R. CIV. P. 37(b)(2)(C)* (authorizing sanctions “in the manner provided by law” and requiring proportionality). The Fifth Circuit has repeatedly held that striking testimony is an extreme sanction that should be reserved for willful or bad-faith conduct. *See* *In re Grand Jury Proceedings*, 888 F.2d at 1057; *United States v. Hinkson*, 585 F.3d at 241. Here, there is no evidence of bad faith—only a procedural misstep. The court’s failure to consider lesser sanctions, such as monetary penalties or a continuance, renders its decision an abuse of discretion.

**II. The District Court Erred in Excluding Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson Under *Daubert***

The district court’s exclusion of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under *Daubert* was legally erroneous. *Daubert* does not permit courts to exclude expert testimony based on mere disagreement with the expert’s conclusions or on the grounds that the expert’s methodology is “not the best” or “not the only” valid approach. *See* *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 595 (“The trial judge must ensure that any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable.”). The *Daubert* standard requires a preliminary assessment of whether the expert’s methodology is scientifically valid and whether it can be properly applied to the facts at issue. *Id.* at 592–93.

Dr. Schroeder’s testimony was based on a review of medical records, environmental data, and peer-reviewed literature linking chemical exposure to adverse health outcomes. His methodology was consistent with accepted practices in epidemiology and toxicology. The court’s assertion that he lacked sufficient time to review documents is irrelevant under *Daubert*—the standard is not whether the expert has reviewed every document, but whether the methodology is reliable. *See* *General Electric Co. v. Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136, 146 (1997) (“The trial judge’s role as gatekeeper is to ensure that an expert’s testimony both rests on a reliable foundation and is relevant to the task at hand.”).

Similarly, Dr. Watson’s testimony was grounded in occupational health standards and exposure modeling. The court’s conclusion that his opinions were speculative or unreliable was not supported by the record. *See* *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 152 (1999) (holding that *Daubert* applies to all expert testimony, not just scientific testimony, but requiring courts to assess reliability, not just methodology). The district court failed to conduct a proper *Daubert* analysis, instead substituting its own judgment for that of qualified experts. *See* *Barnes v. City of Houston*, 297 F.3d 388, 394 (5th Cir. 2002) (reversing exclusion where court improperly substituted its judgment for expert opinion).
