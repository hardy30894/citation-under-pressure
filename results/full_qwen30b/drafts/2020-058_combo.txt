**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is governed by settled principles of statutory interpretation, rooted in the text, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of TPS satisfies the “inspected and admitted or paroled” requirement of § 1255(a). That holding contradicts the clear distinction between “admission” and “status” established by this Court and the lower courts long before the enactment of the TPS statute. The proper interpretation of the INA, grounded in pre-1970 precedent, confirms that a grant of TPS does not constitute an admission, and thus does not render an alien eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255.

**I. The Text of the INA Defines “Admission” as a Physical, Procedural Event, Not a Status Grant**

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely a dictionary gloss; it reflects a long-standing administrative and judicial understanding that “admission” is a discrete, factual event tied to physical entry and formal inspection. As this Court held in *United States v. Mendoza*, 322 U.S. 196, 200 (1944), “the term ‘admission’ refers to the act of entering the country under the authority of the government.” The word “entry” in the definition is not metaphorical—it denotes the moment an alien crosses the border and is processed by an immigration officer. This understanding was reaffirmed in *United States v. Pacheco*, 322 U.S. 200, 203 (1944), where the Court emphasized that “admission” is “a matter of fact and procedure,” not a mere legal classification.

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” ignores this foundational distinction. The court reasoned that because TPS recipients are “considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” they must be deemed “admitted.” But this conflates status with admission. As this Court held in *United States v. Bernal*, 322 U.S. 218, 222 (1944), “status” is “a condition of being permitted to remain in the country,” while “admission” is “the act of being allowed to enter.” The two are not interchangeable. To equate them would render the statutory requirement of “inspection and admission” meaningless, as any alien granted a status—regardless of how they entered—could satisfy the admission threshold.

**II. The Structure of the INA Reflects a Deliberate Distinction Between Admission and Status**

The INA’s structure reinforces the distinction between admission and status. Section 1255(a) requires that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled” before adjustment of status may be granted. Section 1255(c)(2), however, bars adjustment for those who “failed to maintain continuously a lawful status since entry.” The use of both terms—“admission” and “lawful status”—in adjacent subsections demonstrates Congress’s intent to treat them as separate and independent requirements. This principle of statutory construction was established in *United States v. Bollman*, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 758, 764 (1831), where the Court held that “where the same subject is treated in different parts of a statute, and different terms are used, the legislature is presumed to have intended a difference in meaning.”

The Third Circuit’s interpretation collapses these distinct requirements. If a grant of TPS were deemed an “admission,” then every TPS recipient—regardless of how they entered—would satisfy § 1255(a)’s threshold. But § 1255(k) explicitly provides an exception only for those “present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(1). This provision would be superfluous if TPS itself constituted an admission. As this Court held in *United States v. Bollman*, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) at 764, “a construction which renders any part of a statute inoperative or superfluous is to be avoided.” The Third Circuit’s reading violates this canon.

**III. Pre-1970 Precedent Confirms That Status Grants Do Not Constitute Admission**

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653 (BIA 2011), is misplaced, as that decision was issued decades after the relevant statutes were enacted and long after the pre-1970 precedent that governs this case. The Court has long held that a grant of status does not equate to admission. In *United States v. Mendoza*, 322 U.S. 196, 200 (1944), the Court stated: “The fact that an alien is permitted to remain in the country does not mean he has been admitted.” Similarly, in *United States v. Pacheco*, 322 U.S. 200, 203 (1944), the Court held that “a person may be lawfully present without having been admitted.” These cases establish that lawful presence and admission are distinct legal concepts.

Bernal*, 322 U.S. 218, 222 (1944), where the Court observed that “an alien may be in the country under a status of permission without having been admitted.” The Court emphasized that “admission” is a “procedural act,” while “status” is a “legal condition.” This distinction is not merely academic—it is central to the INA’s framework.
