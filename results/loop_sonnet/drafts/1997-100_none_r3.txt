# ARGUMENT

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This guaranty binds the States with the same force it binds the federal government, because it is, in this Court's words, "fundamental to the American scheme of justice." Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). The Clause protects against two distinct evils relevant here: a second prosecution after acquittal, and multiple punishments for the same offense. North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The California Supreme Court's holding that neither protection extends to a noncapital sentence-enhancement proceeding cannot be reconciled with the principles this Court has developed in analogous contexts. Petitioner respectfully submits that both branches of the Clause bar the State's attempt to retry the prior-conviction allegation.

## I. The Court of Appeal's Insufficiency Ruling Is the Functional Equivalent of an Acquittal and Bars Retrial.

The Court of Appeal held that the State's evidence failed to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Monge personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury in his prior offense. That determination is indistinguishable in substance from a judgment of acquittal, and this Court has long treated such judgments as final regardless of the soundness of the reasoning behind them. In United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), the Court held that a verdict of acquittal "could not be reviewed, on error or otherwise, without putting [the defendant] twice in jeopardy, and thereby violating the Constitution." The Court reaffirmed that rule in Fong Foo v. United States, 369 U.S. 141 (1962), holding that a judgment of acquittal bars further prosecution even where the trial court erred as a matter of law in reaching it. And in Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100 (1904), the Court held that the government may not obtain appellate correction of an acquittal, because doing so would subject the defendant to a second jeopardy on the same charge.

These decisions embody a principle of enduring force: once a tribunal charged with weighing the government's proof has found it insufficient, the government does not get a second opportunity to marshal better evidence. See Sealfon v. United States, 332 U.S. 575, 578-80 (1948) (a determination resolving a factual issue against the government may bar relitigation of that same issue in a later proceeding). Nothing about the character of a sentence-enhancement allegation removes it from this settled rule. The consequence to a defendant of a successful enhancement—years added to a term of imprisonment—is no less severe than a conviction on a separate count, and the State's incentive to relitigate a losing factual issue is no less real merely because that issue arises at sentencing rather than at trial.

## II. The Multiple-Punishment Guarantee Independently Bars Retrial.

The Clause also forbids multiple punishments for the same offense. Pearce, 395 U.S. at 717; Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 168 (1874) (once a defendant has suffered lawful punishment, the Constitution forbids a second, additional punishment for the same offense). That guarantee applies with full force to the enhancement allegation itself, which California requires the State to prove beyond a reasonable doubt through an adversarial proceeding affording the rights of confrontation and cross-examination. See Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967) (due process requires that a defendant subject to a factfinding proceeding materially affecting the length of confinement be afforded the right "to be confronted with witnesses against him [and] to cross-examine" them). Having failed once to carry that burden in a proceeding it designed to resemble a trial, the State should not be permitted to try again for the added punishment it seeks.

## III. Williams v. New York Does Not License Repeated Adjudication of a Contested Sentencing Allegation.

Respondent may invoke Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), for the proposition that sentencing need not mirror trial procedure. But Williams held only that due process does not require a hearing before a judge may rely on information gathered informally, outside an adversarial proceeding, in exercising sentencing discretion. Id. at 245-46. It said nothing about whether a State may relitigate a specific, contested factual allegation that it has already tried once under the reasonable-doubt standard, with full confrontation and cross-examination rights. Where a State elects, as California has, to structure an enhancement proceeding as a trial-like adjudication, it must accept the consequences that attend that choice. See Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919) (recognizing that double jeopardy principles are implicated by successive proceedings affecting the severity of punishment, though finding no violation on the facts there presented).

## IV. The Policies Underlying the Clause Confirm Its Application Here.

This Court has explained that "the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity, as well as enhancing the possibility that even though innocent he may be found guilty." Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957). That concern applies with equal force to a defendant compelled to defend a second time against the same prior-conviction allegation—particularly where the State's first attempt already failed under a searching, trial-like procedure of its own design. Having chosen to try the allegation as it would try guilt itself, California cannot now disclaim the double jeopardy consequences of that choice.

## CONCLUSION

Because the Court of Appeal's insufficiency ruling functions as an acquittal, and because the Double Jeopardy Clause's protections against successive prosecution and multiple punishment for the same offense are not confined to adjudication of the substantive charge, this Court should hold that the Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation and should reverse the judgment of the California Supreme Court.
