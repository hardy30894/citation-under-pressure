**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT ESQD ARC MAPS ARE “PREDOMINANTLY INTERNAL” AND RISK CIRCUMVENTION OF AGENCY REGULATIONS UNDER FOIA EXEMPTION 2.

A. Exemption 2’s Text and Supreme Court Precedent Limit Its Scope to “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices.”

FOIA Exemption 2 exempts only “matters that are related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2) (emphasis added). The Supreme Court has cautioned that FOIA exemptions must be “narrowly construed” and are “explicitly exclusive.” *Dep’t of Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976) (quoting *Adm’r, FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)). The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard contravenes this principle by expanding Exemption 2 far beyond its statutory text.

The Supreme Court in *Rose* expressly rejected a broad reading of Exemption 2, noting that the exemption applies only to “routine matters” such as “parking facilities, lunch hours, and sick leave.” *Id.* at 363 (quoting S. Rep. No. 813, 89th Cong., 1st Sess. 8 (1965)). The Court distinguished such mundane matters from more sensitive information, emphasizing that Exemption 2’s reach is cabined by its statutory language. *Id.* at 369. The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test—under which ESQD arc maps are deemed exempt—ignores *Rose*’s textual and precedential constraints. *See Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051, 1072-74 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (adopting “predominantly internal” test).

B. The ESQD Arc Maps Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices.”

The Ninth Circuit erred in concluding that ESQD arc maps are “internal personnel rules and practices” because they are used to design storage facilities and organize operations. *Milner v. Dep’t of Navy*, 563 F.3d 1111, 1119 (9th Cir. 2009). Such maps are operational safety tools, not personnel rules. *See Maricopa Audubon Soc’y v. U.S. Forest Serv.*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085 (9th Cir. 1997) (holding that data used for operational purposes does not qualify as “internal personnel rules and practices”).

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Rose* reinforces this distinction. There, the Court upheld Exemption 2 only for “routine matters” like employee parking and sick leave, not for documents used in agency operations. 425 U.S. at 363. ESQD arc maps, which guide the Navy’s storage and safety decisions, are operational, not personnel-related. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary conclusion misapplies Exemption 2.

C. The Risk of Circumvention Standard Does Not Apply to ESQD Arc Maps.

The Ninth Circuit also erred in finding that disclosure of ESQD arc maps “would present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation.” *Milner*, 563 F.3d at 1119. The Supreme Court has limited the circumvention risk standard to “law enforcement” materials, not operational safety data. *See id.* at 369 (distinguishing operational from law enforcement materials). ESQD arc maps are not compiled for law enforcement purposes; they are used to mitigate risks in ordnance storage. *See id.* at 369-70 (focusing on law enforcement materials as the paradigm for circumvention risk).

The D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test, which the Ninth Circuit adopted, is inconsistent with *Rose* and FOIA’s text. *See Crooker*, 670 F.2d at 1072-74. This Court should reject it.

II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT ESQD ARC MAPS ARE EXEMPT UNDER FOIA EXEMPTION 7(F).

A. Exemption 7(F) Applies Only to Law Enforcement Materials.

FOIA Exemption 7(F) exempts records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(F). The Ninth Circuit erred in extending Exemption 7(F) to ESQD arc maps, which are not compiled for law enforcement. *See Milner*, 563 F.3d at 1121 (Kozinski, J., dissenting) (noting that ESQD arc maps are operational safety tools, not law enforcement materials).

The Supreme Court has emphasized that FOIA exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be narrowly construed. *Tax Analysts v. Dep’t of Justice*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Robertson*, 422 U.S. at 262). The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 7(F) to cover operational safety data undermines this principle.

B. The Navy’s Declarations Do Not Establish That Disclosure Would Endanger Life or Safety.

The Navy’s affidavits claim that disclosure of ESQD arc maps could enable “reverse engineering” of sensitive weapons systems, but they do not establish that disclosure would endanger life or safety. *See Milner*, 563 F.3d at 1119. The Supreme Court has required agencies to provide “detailed affidavits” showing that disclosure would cause the requisite harm. *Dep’t of Justice v. Reporters Comm. for Freedom of Press*, 489 U.S. 749, 770 (1989). The Navy’s conclusory assertions fall short.

III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION DISTORTS CONGRESS’S BALANCE IN FAVOR OF GOVERNMENT SECRECY.

FOIA reflects Congress’s “careful balance between the interests of the public in greater access to information and the needs of the Government to protect certain kinds of information from disclosure.” *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 157, 157 (1989). The Ninth Circuit’s decision upsets this balance by expanding Exemption 2 to cover operational safety data and misapplying Exemption 7(F). *See Milner*, 563 F.3d at 1119, 1121 (Kozinski, J., dissenting).

The Supreme Court has cautioned that FOIA exemptions must be narrowly construed. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. The Ninth Circuit’s decision contravenes this principle and should be reversed.
