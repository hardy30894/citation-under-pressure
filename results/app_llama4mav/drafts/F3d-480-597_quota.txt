ARGUMENT

I. The Government Failed to Prove the Requisite Mens Rea for the Machine Gun Conviction

The government failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Belen Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 rifle she possessed was a machine gun, as required by Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600, 602 (1994). In Staples, the Supreme Court held that to convict a defendant under 26 U.S.C. § 5861(d) for possessing an unregistered machine gun, the government must prove that the defendant knew the firearm was a machine gun. Id. at 619. The Court reasoned that the statute's silence on the mens rea requirement did not imply that the government need not prove knowledge of the firearm's characteristics. Id. at 605.

The same reasoning applies to 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), which prohibits the possession of a machine gun. See United States v. Rogers, 119 F.3d 1377, 1384 (9th Cir. 1997) (applying Staples to § 922(o)). To convict Nieves-Castaño under § 922(o), the government must prove that she knew the AK-47 was a machine gun.

The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to establish this knowledge. Nieves-Castaño told the agent that she knew the gun was an AK-47, but there is no evidence that she knew it was a machine gun. See United States v. Gergen, 172 F.3d 719, 724 (9th Cir. 1999) (holding that knowledge of a firearm's type is not sufficient to establish knowledge of its characteristics). The government presented no evidence that Nieves-Castaño had ever fired the gun or had knowledge of its firing capabilities. See United States v. Barr, 130 F.3d 711, 712 (5th Cir. 1997) (noting that the defendant's lack of experience with the firearm was relevant to determining whether he knew it was a machine gun).

The government's reliance on Nieves-Castaño's statement that she knew the gun was an AK-47 is misplaced. As the Supreme Court noted in Staples, "the Government should have been required to prove that Staples knew the characteristics of his rifle that made it a 'firearm' under the Act." 511 U.S. at 619. Similarly, the government should have been required to prove that Nieves-Castaño knew the characteristics of the AK-47 that made it a machine gun.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Cassel, 408 F.3d 622 (9th Cir. 2005), is instructive. In Cassel, the court held that the government failed to prove the defendant's knowledge of the firearm's characteristics, despite evidence that the defendant had handled the gun and knew its type. Id. at 638. Similarly, Nieves-Castaño's knowledge that the gun was an AK-47 is insufficient to establish knowledge that it was a machine gun.

The government's failure to prove Nieves-Castaño's knowledge of the AK-47's characteristics is a fatal flaw in its case. See United States v. Harris, 313 F.3d 1228, 1234 (10th Cir. 2002) (reversing a conviction under § 922(o) for insufficient evidence of the defendant's knowledge of the firearm's characteristics). As the Supreme Court noted in In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), "the Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged." The government failed to meet this burden, and Nieves-Castaño's conviction under § 922(o) should be reversed.

Furthermore, the rule of lenity requires that any ambiguity in the statute be resolved in favor of the defendant. See United States v. Santos, 553 U.S. 507, 514 (2008) (applying the rule of lenity to a federal statute). In this case, the ambiguity in § 922(o) regarding the mens rea requirement should be resolved in Nieves-Castaño's favor.

Additionally, the conviction should be reversed because the government failed to provide sufficient evidence to support the conviction. See Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979) (holding that a conviction must be supported by sufficient evidence). The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to establish that Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 was a machine gun, and therefore, the conviction should be reversed.

In conclusion, the government failed to prove the requisite mens rea for the machine gun conviction, and the conviction should be reversed. The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to establish that Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 was a machine gun, and the government's reliance on her statement that she knew the gun was an AK-47 is misplaced. The rule of lenity and the Due Process Clause require that the conviction be reversed due to the government's failure to prove every fact necessary to constitute the crime.
