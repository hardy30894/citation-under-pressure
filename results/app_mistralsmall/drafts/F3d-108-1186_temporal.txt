**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INTERPRETING THE JURY’S AMBIGUOUS VERDICT TO MEAN THAT UNIT RECOVERED NO DAMAGES.**

The district court’s interpretation of the jury’s verdicts was legally erroneous because it failed to give effect to the jury’s apparent intent to award Unit damages after accounting for Enron’s set-off. When a jury returns an ambiguous verdict, the court must interpret it in a way that harmonizes the verdicts and gives effect to the jury’s intent. *McDermott v. Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur*, 247 U.S. 391, 394 (1918) (“[W]here there is a clear intention controlling the general verdict, it will be sustained, although the form of the verdict may be irregular.”); *Chicago, R.I. & P.R. Co. v. Cole*, 251 F. 237, 240 (8th Cir. 1918) (“The court should construe the verdict so as to give effect to the apparent intention of the jury.”).

Here, the jury awarded Unit $70,768.02 on Form 1 (its breach of contract claim) and awarded Enron a set-off of $86,494.30 on Form 3. The district court subtracted the set-off from the damages award, resulting in no recovery for Unit. However, this interpretation ignores the possibility that the jury itself subtracted the set-off from the unpaid invoices to arrive at the $70,768.02 award. *See* *United States v. Birrell*, 242 F. 711, 713 (2d Cir. 1917) (“Where a verdict is susceptible of two constructions, one of which will support the judgment and the other will not, the former will be adopted.”). The court’s refusal to clarify the verdict further compounded this error. *See* *McDermott*, 247 U.S. at 394 (holding that courts should seek to clarify ambiguous verdicts rather than adopt an interpretation that defeats recovery).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN REFUSING TO CLARIFY THE JURY’S AMBIGUOUS VERDICT.**

A district court has broad discretion to clarify an ambiguous verdict but must exercise that discretion to ensure fairness. *See* *McDermott*, 247 U.S. at 394 (noting that courts should seek clarification when a verdict is ambiguous). Here, Unit requested that the court question the jury to clarify whether the $70,768.02 award represented damages after the set-off or an independent award. The court’s refusal to do so was an abuse of discretion because it left the verdict open to conflicting interpretations. *See* *Chicago, R.I. & P.R. Co. v. Cole*, 251 F. at 240 (holding that courts must resolve ambiguities to give effect to the jury’s intent).

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN EXCLUDING EVIDENCE OF ENRON’S DEALINGS WITH ANOTHER CONTRACTOR.**

The district court’s exclusion of evidence regarding Enron’s dealings with another contractor deprived Unit of its right to present relevant evidence supporting its claim for damages. Under the Federal Rules of Evidence, relevant evidence is admissible unless excluded by a specific rule. *See* *Thurston v. Great Northern R. Co.*, 156 F.2d 161, 164 (8th Cir. 1946) (holding that evidence is relevant if it tends to prove or disprove a material fact). Here, the excluded evidence would have shown Enron’s pattern of conduct in handling similar disputes, which was probative of whether Enron’s claimed set-off was reasonable. The court’s exclusion of this evidence was an abuse of discretion. *See* *Chicago, M. & St. P.R. Co. v. Coogan*, 271 U.S. 472, 474 (1926) (holding that exclusion of relevant evidence is reversible error).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Unit respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
