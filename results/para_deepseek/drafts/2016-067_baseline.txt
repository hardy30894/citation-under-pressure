**ARGUMENT**

The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander is not a “debt collector” under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692-1692p, because it was collecting debts it owned for its own account. The FDCPA’s plain text, structure, and purpose confirm that debt purchasers collecting their own debts are creditors, not debt collectors—regardless of when the debts were acquired.

**I. The Statutory Definition of “Debt Collector” Requires Collection on Behalf of Another**

The FDCPA defines “debt collector” as any person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due **another**.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). This language unambiguously requires that the debt be owed to a third party at the time of collection. *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.,* 797 F.3d 1309, 1315-16 (11th Cir. 2015); *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA,* 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013). When Santander purchased the petitioners’ defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto, the debts became owed to Santander, not to another. Consequently, Santander’s subsequent collection efforts were on its own behalf, bringing it outside the statutory definition.

Petitioners’ contrary argument—that “owed or due another” refers to the debt’s original origin—fails. The statute regulates conduct at the time of collection, not historical origination. *Davidson,* 797 F.3d at 1318 (“[O]ur inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether [defendant] regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now-owed to [defendant]; our inquiry is whether [defendant] regularly collects on debts owed or due another *at the time of collection*.”) (emphasis in original). As the Fourth Circuit recognized, “Congress was regulating debt-collector conduct, defining the term ‘debt collector’ to include a person who regularly collects debts owed to another, it had to be referring to debts as they existed at the time of the conduct that is subject to regulation.” Pet. App. 37a.

**II. The Default Status of a Debt Is Irrelevant to the Threshold Definition**

Petitioners err by placing dispositive weight on the fact that the debts were in default when Santander purchased them. The FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” does not mention default status. Instead, the default distinction appears only in an **exclusion** from the definition—§ 1692a(6)(F)(iii)—which provides that a person collecting debts for another is not a debt collector if the debts were not in default when obtained. This exclusion protects loan servicers who collect non-defaulted debts on behalf of others. *See* S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. But exclusions only apply if a person first satisfies the threshold definition of “debt collector.” *Davidson,* 797 F.3d at 1314 (“[W]here a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a ‘debt collector’ as a matter of course.”). Because Santander does not meet the threshold definition—it collects its own debts, not debts of another—the default-based exclusion is irrelevant.

**III. The FDCPA Distinguishes Creditors from Debt Collectors Based on Ownership, Not Default**

The FDCPA defines “creditor” as “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). When Santander purchased the loans, it became the party “to whom a debt is owed.” *Id.* As a creditor, Santander collects its own debts, and the FDCPA “generally does not regulate creditors, when they collect debt on their own account.” Pet. App. 2a. *See also Heintz v. Jenkins,* 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (noting that the Act defines “debt collector” as one who collects debts “owed [to] ... another”). Petitioners’ reading would collapse this fundamental distinction, making every debt purchaser a debt collector solely because the debt was in default at acquisition—a result Congress did not intend. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e) (purpose is to “eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors,” not by creditors).

**IV. Santander’s Prior Servicing Role Does Not Convert Its Later Collection of Its Own Debts into Debt Collection**

Petitioners argue that because Santander serviced the loans before purchasing them, it remained a debt collector after acquisition. This argument conflates distinct time periods and distinct roles. Before December 1, 2011, Santander acted as a servicer for CitiFinancial Auto—collecting debts owed to another. After that date, Santander owned the debts and collected for itself. The FDCPA regulates conduct, not status. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (defining debt collector by reference to the activity of collecting debts “owed or due another”). Once Santander became the owner, it ceased collecting for another and became a creditor. As the Fourth Circuit correctly observed, “a valid claim under the FDCPA inherently requires the coming together of all the statutory elements at the time of and in connection with the prohibited conduct.” Pet. App. 39a. Santander’s prior role as a servicer does not transform its later creditor activity into debt collection.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
