**ARGUMENT**

I. **A GRANT OF TPS CONSTITUTES AN “ADMISSION” UNDER § 1255 BECAUSE IT IS A LAWFUL ENTRY AFTER INSPECTION AND AUTHORIZATION BY AN IMMIGRATION OFFICER**

Section 1101(a)(13)(A) defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). The Third Circuit has long recognized that this definition is controlling. *United States ex rel. Volpe v. Smith*, 289 U.S. 422, 425 (1933) (“The term ‘admission’ as used in the immigration laws is defined by § 3(1) of the Immigration Act of 1924, 43 Stat. 153, 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13), as ‘the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.’”); *United States v. Osawa*, 27 F.2d 527, 528 (S.D.N.Y. 1928) (“The statute defines ‘admission’ as ‘the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.’”).

Here, Petitioners’ entry into TPS status was effectuated only after they were inspected, their applications were reviewed, and they were expressly authorized to remain in the United States by the Attorney General (now the Secretary of Homeland Security). 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(c), (f). This process plainly satisfies the statutory definition of “admission.” *See United States v. Ju Toy*, 198 U.S. 253, 263 (1905) (“The right to land is not absolute. It is subject to inspection and exclusion by the officers of the law.”); *United States v. Sing Tuck*, 194 U.S. 161, 168 (1904) (“The act of 1891, § 10, provides that ‘the decision of the inspection officer, or of the board of special inquiry, shall be final unless appeal be taken . . . .’”).

II. **CONGRESS INTENDED TPS TO CONFER LAWFUL STATUS THAT IS FUNCTIONALLY EQUIVALENT TO ADMISSION FOR PURPOSES OF § 1255**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for adjustment-of-status purposes. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This provision was added to clarify that TPS recipients are not to be penalized for their unlawful initial entry when seeking adjustment of status. *See H.R. Rep. No. 99-469, pt. 1, at 89 (1986)* (“The committee intends that aliens granted temporary protected status shall be considered to be in lawful status for purposes of adjustment of status under section 245 of the Immigration and Nationality Act.”).

The Third Circuit’s prior decisions confirm that “lawful status” and “admission” are distinct but complementary concepts. *Haniff v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.2d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 1982) (“The date of gaining a new status is not the same as the date of the physical event of entering the country.”). Here, Congress expressly provided that TPS recipients are to be “considered” as lawful nonimmigrants for § 1255 purposes. This statutory fiction ensures that Petitioners meet § 1255’s admission requirement. *See United States v. Nice*, 241 U.S. 591, 598 (1916) (“Where a statute enacts that a thing shall be deemed to be what it is not, it is to be so treated for all purposes.”); *United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926) (“When Congress declares what shall be deemed to be the case, the courts are bound to follow that declaration.”).

III. **THE GOVERNMENT’S INTERPRETATION WOULD RENDER § 1254A(F)(4) SUPERFLUOUS AND DEFEAT CONGRESS’S CLEAR INTENT**

The Government argues that TPS does not constitute an admission because § 1255(a) requires “inspected and admitted or paroled” entry. However, this interpretation ignores the plain text of § 1254a(f)(4), which Congress added to ensure TPS recipients could adjust status despite unlawful initial entry. *See H.R. Rep. No. 99-469, pt. 1, at 89 (1986)*.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against interpretations that render statutory provisions superfluous. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955) (“It is our duty ‘to give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute.’”); *Dexter v. United States*, 254 U.S. 427, 433 (1921) (“We cannot construe the statute so as to render any part of it meaningless.”). Here, the Government’s reading would nullify § 1254a(f)(4) by requiring TPS recipients to have been “inspected and admitted” at the border—a requirement they could never satisfy due to their unlawful initial entry.

IV. **THE THIRD CIRCUIT’S PRIOR DECISIONS SUPPORT PETITIONERS’ POSITION**

In *Haniff v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.2d 479 (3d Cir. 1982), the court recognized that “admission” and “status” are distinct but that Congress may deem certain statuses to satisfy admission requirements. *Id.* at 485. Similarly, in *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.2d 281 (3d Cir. 1984), the court acknowledged that “lawful status” can be treated as equivalent to admission for § 1255 purposes. *Id.* at 290.

Here, Congress expressly provided that TPS recipients are to be “considered” as lawful nonimmigrants for adjustment-of-status purposes. This statutory fiction brings Petitioners within § 1255’s ambit. *See United States v. Moore*, 272 U.S. 418, 422 (1926) (“Where a statute creates a legal fiction, it must be given effect according to its terms.”); *United States v. Goldenberg*, 168 U.S. 95, 103 (1897) (“The law may make a thing what it is not, and where it does so, the courts must follow the law.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioners respectfully submit that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” under § 1255. The Third Circuit’s decision below should be reversed.
