**ARGUMENT**

**I. AN INMATE HAS NO CONSTITUTIONALLY PROTECTED LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST IN CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS.**

The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Woodard failed to establish a protected life or liberty interest arising from the clemency proceeding itself. This conclusion is compelled by *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981). In *Dumschat*, this Court held that a convicted murderer had no constitutionally protected liberty interest in a commutation of his sentence, even though Connecticut law allowed him to apply for such relief. *Id.* at 464-65. The Court emphasized that “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Id.* at 464.

The Sixth Circuit’s alternative holding—that Woodard retained a “pretrial life and liberty interest” because clemency is “an integral part of the system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant”—is irreconcilable with *Dumschat*. The *Dumschat* Court was fully aware that clemency could be viewed as part of the overall adjudicatory process. Justice Stevens’ dissent expressly argued that clemency was “a regular and critical component of the decisionmaking process employed by the State . . . to determine the magnitude of its deprivation of the prisoner’s liberty.” *Id.* at 471 (Stevens, J., dissenting). The majority rejected that view, relying instead on history and tradition. *Id.* at 464.

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), is misplaced. *Evitts* held that the Due Process Clause guarantees effective assistance of counsel on a first appeal as of right. *Id.* at 393-94. But *Evitts* did not involve clemency, and it expressly recognized that the right to counsel does not extend to habeas corpus proceedings, which are far more integral to the adjudicatory process than executive clemency. *Id.* at 394 (citing *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974)). If habeas proceedings are not subject to the same due process requirements as the trial itself, then clemency proceedings—which occur after all judicial remedies are exhausted—certainly are not.

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s approach would create perverse incentives. As this Court warned in *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472 (1995), extending due process protections to discretionary administrative proceedings can “create disincentives for States to codify prison management procedures in the interest of uniform treatment.” *Id.* at 482. Similarly, holding that clemency proceedings trigger due process protections would discourage states from offering inmates the opportunity to participate in clemency reviews. The better rule, consistent with *Dumschat*, is that clemency remains a matter of grace, not constitutional right.

**II. THE OPTION OF VOLUNTARILY PARTICIPATING IN A CLEMENCY INTERVIEW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.**

Woodard’s Fifth Amendment claim is premature and meritless. The challenged regulation provides that a prisoner “may request a personal interview” with parole board representatives. Ohio Admin. Code § 5120-1-01, Part IV.C.2. The regulation does not compel any inmate to participate; it does not require that any questions be answered; and it does not provide that silence may be used against the inmate in any subsequent proceeding.

In *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), this Court held that a prisoner’s Fifth Amendment claim was “premature” where the challenged procedure did not compel incriminating testimony and where no criminal proceedings were pending. *Id.* at 316-17. Here, as in *Baxter*, Woodard faces no compulsion. He may choose not to request an interview. If he does request one, he remains free to say as much or as little as he wishes. The mere possibility that a future court might admit his statements against him is too speculative to support a constitutional challenge.

Woodard’s “Hobson’s choice” argument—that he must either waive his Fifth Amendment rights or forfeit any chance at clemency—fails because clemency is not a right. The State may constitutionally condition a discretionary benefit on the waiver of a constitutional right, so long as the condition is not coercive. *See Dolan v. City of Tigard*, 512 U.S. 374, 385 (1994). Here, the condition is not coercive because Woodard faces no penalty for declining the interview. He may submit written materials, affidavits, and other evidence in support of his clemency application. The interview is merely an additional option.

Finally, the Sixth Circuit’s remand for further factfinding is unwarranted. The regulation is clear on its face, and no evidentiary hearing can alter its plain meaning. As Judge Nelson observed in dissent, “it would be ‘premature’ . . . for a federal court to instruct Ohio on how to deal with situations that may never materialize at all.” *Woodard v. Ohio Adult Parole Auth.*, 107 F.3d 1178, 1190 (6th Cir. 1997) (Nelson, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). This Court should reverse the remand and affirm the district court’s judgment in full.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the district court’s judgment dismissing the complaint should be affirmed.
