**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School District’s Facilities Are a Limited Public Forum Open to All Nonreligious Speech, and the Denial of Access to Lamb’s Chapel Violates the First Amendment’s Free Speech Clause.**

The Second Circuit erred in concluding that the School District’s facilities were a “limited public forum” closed to religious speech. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983) (explaining that a limited public forum is “public property which the State has opened for use by certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics”). The District’s policy and practice of permitting secular community groups to use its facilities while excluding religious groups demonstrates that the forum was open to a broad class of speakers and topics, not limited to secular speech. *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985) (holding that a forum is limited only if the government has “reserved [it] for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics”).

The District’s policy explicitly barred only “religious purposes,” not religious speakers. This content-based exclusion is impermissible in a forum open to the public. *Rosenberger v. Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (“[W]henever the government establishes a forum for speech, it must respect the lawful boundaries it has itself set.”). The District cannot exclude speech based on its viewpoint—here, the religious perspective of Lamb’s Chapel’s films—without violating the First Amendment. *Id.* at 828-29.

The Second Circuit’s reliance on *Deeper Life Christian Church v. Board of Educ. of City of New York*, 853 F.2d 676 (2d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. Unlike *Deeper Life*, where the school had never opened its facilities to any outside group, the School District here permitted secular community use, demonstrating that its facilities were a limited public forum. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267 (1981) (holding that a state university’s policy of accommodating student meetings created a forum generally open to student groups).

**II. The District’s Policy Violates the Free Speech Clause by Discriminating Against Religious Speech.**

The District’s policy is not viewpoint-neutral. It permits secular discussions of family values but bars religious ones. Such discrimination is unconstitutional. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 828-29 (“[T]he government violates the First Amendment when it denies access to a speaker solely to suppress the point of view he espouses.”). The District cannot justify this discrimination by invoking the Establishment Clause. *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612 (1971), does not permit viewpoint discrimination. *See also Agostini v. Felton*, 521 U.S. 203, 231 (1997) (rejecting the notion that the Establishment Clause requires discrimination against religious speech).

**III. The District’s Policy Violates the Equal Protection Clause by Treating Religious and Nonreligious Speech Differently.**

The District’s policy facially discriminates against religious speech. *Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah*, 508 U.S. 520, 542 (1993) (holding that facially neutral laws may still violate the Equal Protection Clause if they target religious conduct for disfavored treatment). Here, the policy explicitly bars religious purposes while permitting secular ones, creating a classification based on religion. *See also Texas Monthly, Inc. v. Bullock*, 489 U.S. 1, 17 (1989) (plurality opinion) (invalidating a sales tax exemption for religious publications while denying it to secular ones).

**IV. The District’s Policy Does Not Violate the Establishment Clause.**

The District’s policy does not violate the Establishment Clause. *See Agostini*, 521 U.S. at 223 (holding that the Establishment Clause is not violated by neutral, generally applicable policies). The policy is neutral on its face and does not endorse religion. *See also Zelman v. Simmons-Harris*, 536 U.S. 639, 652 (2002) (upholding a neutral school voucher program that permitted religious school participation). The District’s exclusion of religious speech is not an endorsement of religion but a refusal to subsidize it. *Locke v. Davey*, 540 U.S. 712, 720 (2004) (upholding a state scholarship program that excluded devotional theology majors).

**CONCLUSION**

The Second Circuit’s decision should be reversed. The School District’s facilities are a limited public forum open to all nonreligious speech, and the District’s policy violates the First Amendment by discriminating against religious speech. The judgment below is erroneous and should be reversed.
