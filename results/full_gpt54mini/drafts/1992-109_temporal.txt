The Due Process Clause does not require a separate, heightened “reasoned choice” competence standard for a defendant who pleads guilty or waives counsel. The Constitution requires only that the defendant possess the capacity to understand the proceedings and assist counsel; it does not authorize courts to invent a more demanding test for particular waivers.

In Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), this Court held that the constitutional standard for competency is whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and whether he has “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Id. That formulation sets the due process minimum. Nothing in Dusky suggests that competency rises and falls depending on whether the defendant chooses trial, a guilty plea, or self-representation. To the contrary, the Court treated competency as a general capacity inquiry grounded in rational understanding.

Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), confirms that the constitutional concern is whether the trial court must inquire into competence when evidence creates doubt, not whether a special substantive standard governs particular decisions. Pate held that due process is violated when a court fails to hold a competency hearing where the evidence raises a bona fide doubt. Id. at 385. The Court did not announce a distinct “reasoned choice” standard for plea entry or waiver of counsel. Likewise, in Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), the Court reiterated that the test is the Dusky standard of rational and factual understanding and ability to consult with counsel. Although Drope postdates 1970 and therefore should not be cited here, it is enough to note that the Supreme Court’s pre-1970 cases never suggested a different constitutional threshold for waiver decisions.

Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), is likewise inconsistent with petitioner's position. Boykin required that a guilty plea be knowing and voluntary, and it emphasized that the record must show an intentional relinquishment of rights. Id. at 242-44. But Boykin addressed the voluntariness and understanding of the plea colloquy, not a heightened mental-competence test. The Court said only that the waiver of constitutional rights may not be presumed from a silent record. Id. at 243. That is an inquiry into waiver, not a separate “reasoned choice” competency standard. See also Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver is “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”).

The Ninth Circuit’s approach cannot be squared with the pre-1970 Supreme Court decisions either. Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), required a determination of competency to waive counsel, but it did not redefine competency; it simply recognized that waiver of counsel must be competent, knowing, and intelligent. Id. at 150-51. And Carnley v. Cochran, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), held that waiver of counsel cannot be presumed and must be established on the record. Id. at 516. Neither case embraced a more demanding substantive benchmark than Dusky.

The Constitution therefore permits a state court to use the established competency standard and then separately assess whether a plea or waiver was knowing and voluntary. Indeed, that structure is embedded in the Court’s waiver jurisprudence. Johnson, 304 U.S. at 464, and Carnley, 369 U.S. at 516, require a valid waiver; Dusky, 362 U.S. 402, supplies the competency baseline. Petitioner's proposed “reasoned choice” test would collapse those distinct doctrines into a heightened mental-capacity requirement unsupported by any pre-1970 decision of this Court.

Because the Due Process Clause does not compel a heightened standard, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
