The Ninth Circuit erred in denying Lexecon's petition for a writ of mandamus. The District of Arizona's decision to invoke 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial contravenes the clear statutory mandate of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a).

Section 1407(a) explicitly requires that cases transferred for pretrial proceedings be remanded to their original district "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." The statute's language is unequivocal, and courts have long recognized that it imposes a strict limitation on the authority of transferee courts. As the Supreme Court held in United States v. Swift & Co., 286 U.S. 106, 116 (1932), "The court cannot 'amend' [a statute] by inserting new words or substituting others for those employed." See also United States v. Goldenberg, 168 U.S. 95, 103 (1897) (courts must apply statutes as written).

The plain meaning of § 1407(a) is reinforced by the legislative history, which indicates that Congress intended to limit the authority of transferee courts to pretrial proceedings. As the court noted in Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 61 (1949), "The [statutory] language is clear and unambiguous." See also United States v. Missouri Pac. R. Co., 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929) (courts must give effect to the plain meaning of a statute).

Despite the clear statutory language, some courts have permitted transferee courts to invoke § 1404(a) to assign cases to themselves for trial. However, these decisions are not supported by the statutory text or legislative history. For example, in Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), the Second Circuit relied on a policy-based argument, rather than a careful reading of the statute. This approach is inconsistent with the principles of statutory interpretation established by the Supreme Court in cases such as Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917), which held that "the elementary rule is that effect must be given, if possible, to every word, clause and sentence of a statute."

Other courts have followed Pfizer, but their decisions are similarly unpersuasive. For instance, in In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire, 342 F.Supp. 907 (D.N.H. 1971), the district court acknowledged that § 1407(a) does not explicitly authorize transferee courts to transfer cases to themselves for trial, but nonetheless permitted the practice. This decision is at odds with the Supreme Court's holding in United States v. First Nat. Bank, 263 U.S. 640, 644 (1924), that "the statute must be construed with reference to its manifest purpose."

The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case perpetuates the error of Pfizer and its progeny. By denying Lexecon's petition for a writ of mandamus, the court failed to correct the District of Arizona's clear statutory error. As the Supreme Court held in McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. 316, 421 (1819), "The government of the Union is a government of the people; it emanates from them; its powers are granted by them; and are to be exercised directly on them, and for their benefit." In this case, the Ninth Circuit's decision undermines the rule of law by permitting a lower court to disregard the clear statutory mandate of § 1407(a).

The Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and grant Lexecon's petition for a writ of mandamus. By doing so, the Court will ensure that the statutory scheme established by Congress is respected and that cases are remanded to their original districts for trial, as required by § 1407(a). As the Court held in Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 439 (1935), "The Constitution has never been regarded as an instrument conferring on the courts the power to revise or rewrite legislation." The Court should adhere to this principle and enforce the statutory text as written.
