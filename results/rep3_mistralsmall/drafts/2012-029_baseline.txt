**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Circuit’s Decision in *Air Measurement* Correctly Recognizes That Federal Courts Have Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Legal Malpractice Claims That Require Resolution of Substantial and Necessary Patent Law Issues.**

The Supreme Court has long held that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases “arising under” federal patent law. *See Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988) (construing 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a)). This jurisdiction extends to state-law claims where “a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” *Id.* at 808-09. The Federal Circuit has correctly applied this principle to state legal malpractice claims arising from patent litigation, holding that such claims fall within exclusive federal jurisdiction when they require resolution of a substantial and necessary patent law issue. *See Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed. Cir. 2007).

In *Air Measurement*, the Federal Circuit reasoned that where a state legal malpractice claim requires resolution of a substantial and contested patent law issue—such as whether a patent is invalid under the on-sale bar—the claim necessarily implicates federal patent law in a way that justifies exclusive federal jurisdiction. *Id.* at 1272-73. The court emphasized that allowing state courts to adjudicate such claims could disrupt the uniformity of patent law, a core concern of federal patent jurisdiction. *Id.* at 1273. This reasoning is consistent with the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005), which requires that federal jurisdiction over state-law claims involving federal issues be limited to cases where the federal issue is both substantial and capable of disrupting federal interests.

Here, Minton’s legal malpractice claim turns entirely on whether his attorneys’ failure to raise the experimental use exception proximately caused his loss in the underlying patent infringement litigation. *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (explaining the experimental use exception). The experimental use exception is a well-established doctrine of federal patent law, and its application is essential to resolving Minton’s malpractice claim. *See id.* Because the exception is a substantial and contested issue of federal patent law, the Federal Circuit’s decision in *Air Measurement* compels the conclusion that this case arises under exclusive federal jurisdiction. *See Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1273.

**II. The Texas Supreme Court Erred in Concluding That the Federal Patent Issue in This Case Is Not Substantial or Necessary.**

The Texas Supreme Court incorrectly minimized the role of the experimental use exception in Minton’s malpractice claim. The court acknowledged that the exception is a substantial federal patent law issue but concluded that its resolution would not disrupt the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *See Gunn v. Minton*, 355 S.W.3d 64, 70 (Tex. 2011). This conclusion is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Grable*, which requires courts to consider whether federal jurisdiction over a state-law claim would disrupt federal interests. *See Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314.

The experimental use exception is a core component of federal patent law, designed to prevent patentees from profiting from commercial activity that would otherwise invalidate their patents. *See Trans Logic*, 417 F.3d at 1210. Allowing state courts to adjudicate malpractice claims that hinge on the application of this exception risks inconsistent interpretations of federal patent law, undermining the uniformity that Congress sought to achieve through federal jurisdiction. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 818-19 (recognizing the federal interest in uniform patent law). The Texas Supreme Court’s decision thus conflicts with the Supreme Court’s precedent and the Federal Circuit’s well-reasoned approach in *Air Measurement*.

**III. The Federal Circuit’s Precedent in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept* Is Binding and Correctly Applies Supreme Court Precedent.**

The Texas Supreme Court declined to follow the Federal Circuit’s decisions in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), reasoning that those decisions failed to apply the *Grable* factors. *See Gunn*, 355 S.W.3d at 70. This reasoning is flawed. The Federal Circuit has consistently applied the *Grable* factors to determine whether a state legal malpractice claim arises under federal patent law, and its decisions are entitled to deference. *See Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1272-73; *Immunocept*, 504 F.3d at 1287-88.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized that the Federal Circuit is the “expert” court in patent law matters, and its interpretations of patent jurisdiction are entitled to substantial weight. *See, e.g., Markham v. Westview Instruments, Inc.*, 517 U.S. 370, 390 (1996) (recognizing the Federal Circuit’s expertise in patent law). The Texas Supreme Court’s refusal to follow the Federal Circuit’s well-reasoned decisions undermines the uniformity of patent law and conflicts with the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Grable*.

**Conclusion**

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Air Measurement* correctly recognizes that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state legal malpractice claims that require resolution of substantial and necessary patent law issues. The Texas Supreme Court erred in concluding otherwise. This Court should reverse the Texas Supreme Court’s decision and hold that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton’s malpractice claim.
