**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively—requires this Court to reaffirm the foundational principle that a criminal statute must give fair notice of the conduct it prohibits, and that a defendant may not be punished under a law that was unconstitutionally vague at the time of his offense. The Court has long held that the Due Process Clause of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments prohibits the application of laws that fail to provide adequate notice of what conduct is forbidden. This principle, established in *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 350 (1964), is not a mere procedural nicety but a constitutional imperative: “a criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct that it makes a crime.” The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the constitutional requirement of definiteness is violated by a criminal statute that fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that his contemplated conduct is” proscribed. *Id.* at 351.

In *Bouie*, the Court invalidated a conviction under a state statute that, when written, did not criminalize remaining on premises after being told to leave. However, the state supreme court had interpreted the statute to include such conduct, thereby punishing the petitioner for an act not clearly criminal at the time it was committed. The Court held that such retroactive expansion of criminal liability violated due process because it deprived the defendant of fair warning. The same principle applies here: the Armed Career Criminal Act’s residual clause, which defined a “violent felony” as conduct that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another,” was unconstitutionally vague when applied to Welch’s 1996 Florida robbery conviction. The clause required courts to assess the “hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version” of the crime, a task that defied any meaningful standard of notice. *Johnson v. United States*, 130 S. Ct. 1265, 1271 (2010). That vagueness rendered the clause unconstitutional under the Due Process Clause, as it failed to provide “adequate notice of the conduct it punished.” *Id.* at 1271.

The Court has long recognized that when a statute is held unconstitutional, its invalidity extends to all applications, past and present, unless a contrary rule is explicitly adopted. In *Gilbert v. California*, 384 U.S. 985 (1966), the Court vacated a conviction based on evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment, holding that the exclusionary rule applies to prevent the use of evidence seized in violation of constitutional rights. Though the Court did not address retroactivity directly, the principle that constitutional violations cannot be used to secure a conviction is foundational. Similarly, in *Giaccio v. Pennsylvania*, 382 U.S. 399, 402 (1965), the Court struck down a statute that authorized juries to impose costs with a threat of imprisonment without standards, declaring it “unconstitutionally vague in violation of the Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause.” The Court emphasized that “the absence of any standards sufficient to enable defendants to protect themselves against arbitrary and discriminatory impositions of costs” rendered the law invalid. This principle is directly applicable here: the residual clause, like the statute in *Giaccio*, lacked any ascertainable standard, making it impossible for a defendant to know whether his conduct would trigger a mandatory fifteen-year sentence.

The Court has also held that when a criminal statute is void for vagueness, its invalidity is not limited to future applications. In *Cole v. Arkansas*, 333 U.S. 196, 200 (1948), the Court affirmed that a conviction must be reversed if the statute under which it was obtained fails to provide fair notice. The Court stated that “the proceedings in that court are a part of the process of law under which the petitioners’ convictions must stand or fall,” and that due process requires that the law be clear. The same logic applies to the residual clause: if the clause was unconstitutionally vague at the time of Welch’s 1996 conviction, then the sentence imposed under it cannot stand.

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that constitutional rights are not forfeited by procedural default. In *Lynumn v. Illinois*, 372 U.S. 528, 530 (1963), the Court emphasized that a state court’s procedural rule cannot bar federal constitutional claims if the federal issue was necessary to the judgment. The Court declined to “search behind” a certificate from the Illinois Supreme Court that the federal claim was necessary to the decision. This principle reinforces the necessity of retroactive application: if a constitutional defect is present, it cannot be waived by procedural technicalities.

The Court has also recognized that the right to fair notice is not a mere formality. In *Williams v. Kaiser*, 323 U.S. 471, 473 (1945), the Court held that a defendant’s right to counsel under the Fourteenth Amendment is violated when he is denied counsel and compelled to plead guilty without understanding the consequences. The Court stated that “the constitutional right to the assistance of counsel” is essential to due process. Though the case involved counsel, the underlying principle—that a defendant must be protected from unfair legal processes—applies equally to statutes that fail to provide fair notice.

Finally, in *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963), the Court declared that “in our adversary system of criminal justice, any person haled into court, who is too poor to hire a lawyer, cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided for him.” The Court emphasized that the right to counsel is not a privilege but a necessity of justice. The same principle applies to the right to fair notice: a defendant cannot be sentenced under a law that is so vague that he cannot know whether his conduct is criminal.

The residual clause of the ACCA, as interpreted in *Johnson*, was not merely ambiguous—it was unconstitutionally vague. It failed to give fair notice, and its application to Welch’s 1996 conviction violated due process. The Court has never held that constitutional invalidity is limited to future cases. On the contrary, the principle of *Bouie*—that a defendant cannot be punished for conduct not clearly criminal at the time—requires that the rule in *Johnson* be applied retroactively.
