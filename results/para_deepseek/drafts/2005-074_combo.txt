## ARGUMENT

The reinstatement provision of § 241(a)(5) cannot be applied to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date in 1997. The text reveals no clear congressional intent to reach prior re-entries, and applying it retroactively would attach new, severe legal consequences to conduct completed years earlier.

### I. The Statute Does Not Reach Pre-Effective-Date Re-Entries

When Congress leaves doubt, courts adhere to the bedrock rule that “statutes are not to be construed to have a retrospective operation unless the language is so clear and positive as to leave no room for doubt.” *United States v. The Schooner Peggy*, 5 U.S. 103, 110 (1801). A “retrospective law” is one that “takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws.” *Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. New Haven*, 21 U.S. 464, 509 (1823). The presumption against retroactivity is “deeply rooted in our jurisprudence,” *Greene v. United States*, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964), and requires explicit direction from Congress. *Murray v. Gibson*, 55 U.S. 421, 424 (1852) (statutes “which are to operate prospectively only are not to be construed retroactively unless the intent is clear”).

Section 241(a)(5) speaks of “any alien who has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed.” The provision does not specify whether it covers re-entries that occurred before its enactment. In such circumstances, “statutes are not to be given retroactive effect unless such construction is required by explicit language or necessary implication.” *Union Pacific R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913); *accord United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T.R. Co.*, 270 U.S. 1, 5 (1926) (same). Nothing in the statute’s text explicitly reaches re-entries before 1997. Because the provision operates on the act of illegal re-entry—a discrete event—it must be read to apply only to re-entries occurring after its effective date.

### II. Retroactive Application Is Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statute could be interpreted to apply to pre-enactment re-entries, such application would be impermissibly retroactive because it attaches new legal consequences to past conduct. The alien who re-entered before 1997 could not have known that his re-entry would later bar him from adjusting status. As this Court has recognized, “deportation is a drastic measure,” *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948), and statutes providing for removal must be “given a liberal interpretation in favor of the alien,” *INS v. Errico*, 385 U.S. 214, 222 (1966).

Petitioner entered the United States in 1982, fifteen years before the reinstatement provision existed. At that time, he had a settled expectation that he could later seek adjustment of status based on marriage to a citizen. To now deny him that relief based on a later-enacted statute “would be inconsistent with the ordinary expectations of the parties,” *Greene*, 376 U.S. at 160, and would impose a retroactive legal burden on conduct that was lawful when performed. Such an application “operates as a penalty for a prior act,” *Union Pacific*, 231 U.S. at 200, and is exactly the kind of retrospective disadvantage that the canon forbids.

Because Congress did not clearly intend the reinstatement statute to apply to pre-1997 re-entries, and because applying it retroactively would disturb settled expectations and impose new disabilities for past conduct, the judgment below must be reversed.
