**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation in the instant case. The record reveals substantial evidence of Richard Allan Moran’s mental incapacity at the time he discharged his counsel and pleaded guilty—evidence that should have triggered a good faith doubt about his competency and necessitated a hearing under the “reasoned choice” standard. The Supreme Court of Nevada’s post-conviction determination, resting on an erroneous legal standard and inadequate factual findings, cannot satisfy the federal habeas corpus standard. This Court must reverse.

The constitutional right to counsel is not merely procedural; it is fundamental to the fairness of a criminal proceeding. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), established that the Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to counsel in all criminal prosecutions, and that right is enforceable against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The right to counsel includes the right to effective assistance and the right to refuse counsel when a defendant makes a knowing, intelligent, and voluntary waiver. *Johnson*, 304 U.S. at 464. The *right to waive counsel* is not absolute—it is subject to the defendant’s capacity to make a reasoned choice among available legal alternatives. That capacity is not measured by the standard for competency to stand trial, but by a higher threshold.

The standard for competency to waive counsel was first articulated in *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), where this Court held that a defendant must possess “the capacity for reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him” before a waiver of counsel can be valid. *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214. This standard is not merely a formality—it is a constitutional requirement. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the waiver of constitutional rights must be “voluntary, knowing, and intelligent,” *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), and that such waivers demand “utmost solicitude” from the courts. *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214 (citing *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 243). The Court has emphasized that “the right to counsel is not a mere formality,” and that “the waiver of that right must be made with an understanding of the nature of the right being abandoned and the consequences of the waiver.” *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464.

The distinction between competency to stand trial and competency to waive counsel is not merely academic. In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), the Court held that a defendant is competent to stand trial if he has “a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings against him” and “is capable of assisting counsel.” But the Court has never equated this standard with the standard for waiving counsel. Indeed, the Court has recognized that “the right to counsel is not a mere formality,” and that “the waiver of that right must be made with an understanding of the nature of the right being abandoned and the consequences of the waiver.” *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464. The “reasoned choice” standard, as defined in *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214, requires more than a basic understanding of the charges or the ability to assist counsel—it demands that the defendant comprehend the consequences of his decisions and make a deliberate, informed choice among alternatives.

The record in this case demonstrates that Moran lacked the capacity for such a reasoned choice. He had recently attempted suicide, was in a state of severe depression, and was taking four different medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—all of which affect cognitive function, mood, and judgment. The record shows that Moran responded to the court’s questions with monosyllabic answers, indicating a profound lack of engagement. He stated he wanted to fire his attorney to prevent any mitigating evidence from being presented, a decision that suggests not a reasoned strategy, but a desire to avoid accountability. This conduct is inconsistent with the kind of deliberate, informed decision-making required by the “reasoned choice” standard.

The state court’s post-conviction finding that Moran was competent to waive counsel was based solely on psychiatric evaluations conducted months earlier, which were designed to assess competency to stand trial, not to evaluate the higher standard of reasoned choice. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), defines the standard for competency to stand trial as “a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” The reports of Dr. O’Gorman and Dr. Jurasky, while relevant to that standard, are wholly inadequate to support a finding of competency under the “reasoned choice” standard. *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214. The state court’s failure to apply the correct legal standard renders its finding constitutionally invalid.

The Due Process Clause requires that when a court has a good faith doubt about a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights, it must initiate a hearing to determine that competency. *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214; *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512 (9th Cir. 1981). The record here contains substantial evidence of incompetence—suicide attempt, medication use, monosyllabic responses, and a decision to foreclose mitigation—that should have triggered such a hearing. The failure to do so violated due process.

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Nevada must be reversed. The state court applied the wrong legal standard, and the record does not support a finding that Moran made a reasoned choice.
