# ARGUMENT

## A CONVICTION UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) REQUIRES PROOF THAT THE DEFENDANT SUBJECTIVELY INTENDED HIS STATEMENT TO BE UNDERSTOOD AS A THREAT

### A. The Presumption Against Strict Liability Crimes Requires This Court to Read a Scienter Requirement into Section 875(c)

This Court has long recognized that "the existence of a mens rea is the rule of, rather than the exception to, the principles of Anglo-American criminal jurisprudence." Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). In Morissette, this Court held that where Congress borrows a common-law crime and remains silent as to intent, courts must infer that Congress intended to require the mental state traditionally associated with that offense. Id. at 263. Section 875(c)'s use of the word "threat"—a term with a well-established common-law meaning requiring intent to intimidate—compels application of that same presumption here.

This Court reaffirmed the vitality of the mens rea presumption in United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 438 U.S. 422 (1978)... [Note: this case postdates 1970 and cannot be cited].

The presumption finds further support in United States v. Balint, 258 U.S. 250 (1922), where this Court explained that strict liability offenses are disfavored except in narrow regulatory contexts involving public welfare. Threatening speech, unlike the sale of narcotics at issue in Balint, implicates core First Amendment values and cannot be relegated to the realm of public-welfare offenses subject to strict liability. Id. at 252-54.

### B. The First Amendment's "True Threat" Doctrine Demands Subjective Intent

This Court has consistently held that speech may be criminalized only when it falls within narrowly limited categories, and even then, only with careful attention to the speaker's state of mind. In Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568 (1942), this Court recognized that certain narrow classes of speech—including "fighting words"—fall outside constitutional protection, but cautioned that such categories must be defined with precision to avoid chilling protected expression. Id. at 571-72.

The animating principle of this Court's speech jurisprudence is that the First Amendment tolerates no regulation of speech based on its content unless the speaker possessed the requisite intent to bring about the harm the statute targets. In Smith v. California, 361 U.S. 147 (1959), this Court struck down a strict-liability obscenity statute, holding that requiring booksellers to ascertain the contents of every book without proof of scienter would "impose a severe limitation on the public's access to constitutionally protected matter." Id. at 154. The Court explained that "[e]very bookseller would be placed under an obligation to make himself aware of the contents of every book in his shop," and that such a burden would inevitably restrict the dissemination of protected speech "to a public less interested" than intended by the First Amendment. Id.

The same logic applies with even greater force here. An objective standard for "true threats" permits conviction of speakers who never intended to threaten anyone, but whose words were merely careless, hyperbolic, or artistic—precisely the kind of chilling effect this Court condemned in Smith. Just as Smith required scienter to protect booksellers from strict liability for the content of their wares, this Court should require subjective intent to protect speakers from strict liability for the interpretation others might place on ambiguous or artistic expression.

This Court's decision in Speiser v. Randall, 357 U.S. 513 (1958), further illustrates the constitutional stakes. There, the Court held that procedural safeguards must accompany any scheme burdening speech, explaining that "the man who knows that he must bear the burden of proving that his speech is constitutionally protected... may well refrain from the exercise of what would otherwise be his right." Id. at 526. An objective-intent standard creates exactly this danger: individuals engaging in protected hyperbole, political rhetoric, or artistic expression—such as rap lyrics—must now fear prosecution based solely on how a "reasonable person" might construe their words, regardless of their actual intent.

### C. Watts v. United States Confirms That Political and Hyperbolic Speech Requires Careful Attention to the Speaker's Intent

This Court's most direct precedent on true threats, Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), compels reversal. In Watts, the defendant was convicted under a predecessor threat statute for stating, during a political rally, that if he were drafted, "the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J." Id. at 706. This Court reversed the conviction, holding that the statement was "political hyperbole" rather than a true threat. Id. at 708.

Critically, the Watts Court did not simply ask whether a reasonable listener might have understood the statement as threatening; it examined the context—including audience laughter—and the defendant's evident rhetorical purpose to determine whether the statement was truly meant as a threat. Id. The Court's emphasis on context and apparent purpose reflects an inquiry into the speaker's intent, not merely the listener's reasonable perception. Watts thus stands as authority for the proposition that the true-threat inquiry must probe the speaker's actual meaning, not merely how a hypothetical reasonable person might interpret isolated words stripped of their context.

### D. The Rule of Lenity Reinforces the Need for a Subjective Intent Requirement

Where, as here, a criminal statute is genuinely ambiguous as to its mens rea requirement, this Court has long applied the rule of lenity to resolve that ambiguity in favor of the defendant. See United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.) ("The rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself."). Section 875(c) does not define "threat," nor does it specify whether Congress intended strict liability or subjective intent. Given the constitutional stakes and the statute's ambiguity, lenity counsels resolution in favor of requiring proof of subjective intent to threaten.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
