**ARGUMENT**

**THE BUREAU OF PRISONS’ PROGRAM STATEMENT 5162.02, WHICH CLASSIFIES A DRUG CONSPIRACY CONVICTION AS A “CRIME OF VIOLENCE” SOLELY BECAUSE OF A SENTENCING ENHANCEMENT FOR POSSESSION OF A FIREARM, IS INCONSISTENT WITH 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) AND THE BUREAU’S OWN REGULATIONS.**

The plain language of 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) authorizes a sentence reduction of up to one year for prisoners convicted of a “nonviolent offense” who successfully complete a drug treatment program. The statute does not define “nonviolent offense,” but the Bureau of Prisons (“BOP”) has defined it by reference to 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), which defines a “crime of violence” as an offense that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force” or “by its nature, involves a substantial risk that physical force against the person or property of another may be used.” 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 (1995); 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).

Mr. Roussos was convicted of conspiracy to distribute a controlled substance under 21 U.S.C. § 846. Conspiracy to distribute drugs is not, by its nature, a crime of violence. *See United States v. Rutherford*, 54 F.3d 370, 376 (7th Cir. 1995) (holding that drug trafficking offenses are not per se crimes of violence under § 924(c)(3)). The elements of a § 846 conspiracy require only an agreement to commit a drug offense and an overt act; they do not require the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force. *See United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10, 15 (1994). Nor does a drug conspiracy inherently involve a substantial risk that force will be used. *See United States v. Johnson*, 953 F.2d 110, 113 (4th Cir. 1991) (holding that drug distribution is not a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3)).

The BOP, however, has classified Mr. Roussos’s conviction as a crime of violence based solely on a two-level sentencing enhancement for possession of a firearm during the offense. Program Statement 5162.02, § 9. This classification is inconsistent with the governing statute and regulations. The sentencing enhancement does not alter the elements of the underlying offense. *See United States v. Smith*, 186 F.3d 290, 295 (3d Cir. 1999) (holding that a sentencing enhancement does not transform a nonviolent offense into a crime of violence for purposes of § 3621(e)(2)(B)). The BOP’s Program Statement impermissibly expands the definition of “crime of violence” beyond the statutory and regulatory framework.

Moreover, the BOP’s interpretation is contrary to the purpose of § 3621(e)(2)(B), which is to incentivize rehabilitation for nonviolent offenders. *See Downey v. Crabtree*, 100 F.3d 662, 666 (9th Cir. 1996) (noting that Congress intended to reward prisoners who complete drug treatment with early release). By excluding inmates like Mr. Roussos—who were convicted of a nonviolent drug conspiracy and received a sentencing enhancement for conduct not charged as an element of the offense—the BOP undermines this congressional intent.

Finally, the BOP’s Program Statement is not entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), because it conflicts with the plain language of the statute and the BOP’s own regulation. *See Stiver v. Meko*, 130 F.3d 574, 577 (3d Cir. 1997) (holding that a BOP program statement that contradicts the regulation is invalid). The regulation at 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 defines “crime of violence” by reference to the elements of the offense, not by sentencing enhancements. The Program Statement’s departure from this definition is arbitrary and capricious.

For these reasons, the district court erred in denying Mr. Roussos’s habeas petition. This Court should reverse and order the BOP to grant Mr. Roussos early release eligibility under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B).
