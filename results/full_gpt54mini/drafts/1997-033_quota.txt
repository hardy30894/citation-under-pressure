**Argument**

Section 1407(a) is explicit: the Judicial Panel may transfer related civil actions “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” to the original district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). That command leaves no room for a transferee district court to convert a case assigned only for pretrial coordination into its own trial docket. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly held, when Congress speaks in mandatory terms, courts must enforce the statute as written. See, e.g., *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 172 (2001); *Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co.*, 534 U.S. 438, 461-62 (2002).

This Court has already recognized that “remand” under § 1407 is the governing rule, not an optional suggestion. In *Kalle v. Kaiser Aluminum & Chemical Corp.*, 674 F.2d 254, 257-58 (4th Cir. 1982), the court held that once pretrial proceedings are complete, the action must return to the transferor court. Likewise, the Third Circuit explained that § 1407 “plainly contemplates” remand after centralized pretrial treatment. *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810, 820 (3d Cir. 1982). And in *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 124-25 (2d Cir. 1971), the court acknowledged the statute’s mandatory remand language even while departing from it on prudential grounds. Whatever *Pfizer*’s influence, its reasoning cannot displace the statute’s text. See *Radzanower v. Touche Ross & Co.*, 426 U.S. 148, 154-55 (1976) (courts may not rewrite unambiguous statutory commands in the name of policy).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision below compounded the statutory error by relying on § 1404(a). But § 1404(a) authorizes transfer “for the convenience of parties and witnesses” and “in the interest of justice” only by a district court with a case properly before it; it does not empower an MDL transferee court to defeat § 1407’s remand command by self-assignment for trial. *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 616 (1964), held that § 1404(a) is a housekeeping measure, not a device to alter the substantive allocation of cases Congress has prescribed. *Ferens v. John Deere Co.*, 494 U.S. 516, 523-24 (1990), likewise emphasized that § 1404(a) changes only the forum, not the governing statutory structure. Nothing in either decision suggests that § 1404(a) can be used to negate a different statute’s mandatory remand requirement.

Nor does the transferee court’s practical familiarity with the litigation authorize disregard of Congress’s choice. The whole point of § 1407 is to centralize pretrial matters while preserving trial in the original forum. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343-44 (1960), rejected venue manipulation based on convenience where Congress had not authorized it. So too here: the district court may not expand its own power simply because self-assignment seems efficient. As the Second Circuit noted in *In re AMC Entertainment Holdings, Inc. Securities Litigation*, 905 F.2d 1415, 1417-18 (2d Cir. 1990), MDL procedure is bounded by statute, not by a transferee judge’s preference for keeping a case.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision below also conflicts with the settled principle that federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction and authority. *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506, 514 (1869), teaches that when Congress withdraws or limits judicial power, courts must respect the limitation. Here, Congress limited MDL transfers to pretrial proceedings and required remand thereafter. A transferee court’s self-transfer under § 1404(a) is therefore ultra vires. See *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 40 (1950) (federal courts may not issue relief beyond statutory authority).

The error is not harmless. A party forced to trial in a forum Congress did not authorize suffers the loss of its statutory right to remand to the transferor district. That injury is immediate and irreparable because the very point of § 1407’s remand obligation is to prevent trial in the transferee court absent lawful reassignment. See *Sunshine Beauty Supplies, Inc. v. United States District Court*, 872 F.2d 310, 311 (9th Cir. 1989); *Varsic v. United States District Court*, 607 F.2d 245, 252 (9th Cir. 1979). The statutory violation itself is enough.

This Court should therefore hold that a district court conducting pretrial proceedings under § 1407(a) may not invoke § 1404(a) to assign the case to itself for trial, reverse the judgment below, and direct remand to the transferor court.
