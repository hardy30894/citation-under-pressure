## ARGUMENT

The court of appeals erred in holding that 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires only that a reasonable speaker would foresee his statement would be interpreted as a threat. Both the statute and the First Amendment require proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be a threat.

### I. Section 875(c) Requires Proof That the Defendant Intended His Communication as a Threat

This Court has long recognized that criminal statutes require proof of mens rea—a "guilty mind"—unless Congress clearly indicates otherwise. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Section 875(c) criminalizes the transmission of "any communication containing any threat." The term "threat" itself imports an intentional communication. As this Court held in construing a similar threat statute, "the defendant must intend that his statement be taken as a threat." *Rogers v. United States*, 422 U.S. 35, 46-47 (1975) (Brennan, J., concurring) (interpreting 18 U.S.C. § 871). The government must prove that the defendant "intend[ed] to communicate a threat." *Id.* at 47.

The text of § 875(c) contains no explicit intent requirement, but this Court has consistently read such requirements into federal criminal statutes. *See, e.g., Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994) (requiring mens rea for gun possession offense); *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994) (reading "knowingly" to modify all elements). Applying that principle, the word "threat" necessarily requires that the speaker intend to communicate a serious expression of intent to harm. *See United States v. Parr*, 516 F.2d 458, 464 (5th Cir. 1975) ("A communication is not a threat . . . unless the sender intends it to be such.").

The objective test applied below—whether a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement as a threat—eliminates this mens rea requirement entirely. Under that test, a defendant can be convicted for negligent or even accidental speech, punishing "the reckless utterer" rather than the intentional threatener. The Third Circuit's approach conflicts with the principle that criminal liability requires "the doing of a willful act." *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260.

### II. The First Amendment Requires Subjective Intent for True Threats

Even if the statute could be read to permit an objective test, the First Amendment demands subjective intent. This Court has held that "true threats" are unprotected speech because they "encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence." *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003) (plurality opinion) (emphasis added). The plurality emphasized that the "speaker *must* intend to communicate a threat." *Id.* at 360 (emphasis added).

This requirement follows from *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), where the Court held that a speaker's "political hyperbole" was not a true threat, noting that "the kind of threat" prohibited must be distinguished from "political speech." The *Watts* Court examined the context and the speaker's apparent intent. An objective test collapses that distinction, making criminals of those who use violent imagery for artistic, therapeutic, or political purposes.

The objective standard adopted below criminalizes speech based on the reaction of a reasonable listener, not the intent of the speaker. But the "true threat" exception exists to protect individuals from *intentional* intimidation, not accidental alarm. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 360. As several circuits have recognized after *Black*, the First Amendment requires proof that the defendant intended his communication to be understood as a threat. *See United States v. Cassel*, 408 F.3d 622, 633 (9th Cir. 2005); *United States v. Jeffries*, 692 F.3d 473, 478-79 (6th Cir. 2012).

The Third Circuit's contrary holding chills protected speech—including rap lyrics, political hyperbole, and artistic expression—because speakers cannot know whether a reasonable person might interpret their words as threatening, regardless of their actual intent. This undermines the breathing space that the First Amendment requires. *See NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886, 927 (1982) (even "emotionally charged rhetoric" may be protected).

### III. The Conviction Cannot Stand Under the Correct Standard

At trial, Elonis testified that his posts were rap lyrics and artistic expressions, not intended as threats. The government presented no evidence that Elonis intended his listeners to fear harm. Instead, the court instructed the jury to apply an objective standard. Because the jury was not required to find that Elonis subjectively intended to threaten, a reasonable jury could have acquitted under the proper standard. Reversal is required. *See Yates v. United States*, 354 U.S. 298, 312 (1957).

The judgment should be reversed.
