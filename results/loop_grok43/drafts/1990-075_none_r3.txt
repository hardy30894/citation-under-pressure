ARGUMENT

Congress may delegate authority when it lays down “by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized * * * is directed to conform.” J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The temporary scheduling provisions meet this standard by requiring the Attorney General to consider the history, current pattern, scope, duration, and significance of abuse, together with the risk to public health, before finding an imminent hazard. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (1988).

This guidance accords with delegations sustained in earlier cases. In Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 420 (1944), Congress authorized the Price Administrator to fix maximum prices that would “effectuate the purposes” of preventing inflation. In Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), the Court upheld standards for inspecting tea that directed the Secretary to act “in accordance with the standards” prescribed by statute. Similar latitude appears in United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516 (1911), where regulations governing forest reserves carried criminal penalties when issued within statutory bounds. In In re Kollock, 165 U.S. 526, 533 (1897), the Court sustained regulations carrying criminal sanctions when promulgated pursuant to statutory direction.

The two decisions invalidating delegations are distinguishable. In Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), the statute “did not declare any policy” or establish any “criterion to govern” executive action. In A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 541-42 (1935), Congress supplied “no standards” whatever. Here the enumerated factors and the imminent-hazard limitation supply the necessary criteria.

Congress may also authorize subdelegation. In United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc., 272 U.S. 1, 12 (1926), the Court approved the transfer of functions vested in the President to subordinate officers. Earlier, Wayman v. Southard, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 1, 43 (1825), recognized that Congress may “commit the details” to those charged with execution. Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), likewise confirmed that administrative officers may exercise authority conferred by statute.

The temporary scheduling provisions therefore rest on a valid delegation that the Attorney General lawfully transmitted to the Administrator of the Drug Enforcement Administration.
