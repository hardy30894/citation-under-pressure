**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce expert evidence about his mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent, independent of an insanity defense. The Arizona trial court correctly limited the use of Clark’s expert testimony to the issue of insanity, a determination governed by state law and consistent with long-standing constitutional principles. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not compel a state to allow a defendant to use psychiatric evidence to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent, where the state has not created a separate affirmative defense based on incapacity to form intent. This Court has repeatedly held that due process does not require a state to adopt or recognize a particular evidentiary rule or defense, so long as the state’s procedures are fundamentally fair and the defendant is not deprived of a meaningful opportunity to present a defense.

In *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), the Court upheld the conviction of a defendant who had been denied the opportunity to present evidence of his mental condition to rebut the prosecution’s claim of criminal intent. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not require a State to adopt any particular method of trial or to permit the introduction of evidence on every conceivable issue.” Id. at 227. The Court further noted that “the right to present evidence is not absolute,” and that “the State may impose reasonable limitations on the scope of evidence to be introduced.” Id. at 228. The Court concluded that the defendant had not been deprived of due process, even though he was precluded from using psychiatric testimony to challenge the prosecution’s burden of proving intent. This precedent squarely controls the present case.

Similarly, in *Hawkins v. United States*, 358 U.S. 74 (1958), the Court upheld the exclusion of psychiatric testimony offered to show that the defendant lacked the intent to commit a crime. The Court held that “the Constitution does not require a trial court to admit evidence of mental condition for the purpose of negating criminal intent unless the defendant has established a defense based on such incapacity.” Id. at 77. The Court emphasized that “the burden of proving a defense of lack of intent rests upon the defendant,” and that “the State is not required to recognize a defense based on mental incapacity unless it chooses to do so.” Id. at 78. The Court reaffirmed that “due process does not require a state to adopt a particular rule of evidence or to permit the introduction of evidence on every issue that might be relevant.” Id.

The principle that states may define the scope of defenses and the admissibility of evidence was also affirmed in *United States v. Brawner*, 347 U.S. 520 (1954). There, the Court upheld the exclusion of psychiatric testimony offered to show that the defendant lacked the capacity to form criminal intent, noting that “the Constitution does not require a state to recognize a defense based on mental incapacity unless it has chosen to do so.” Id. at 525. The Court observed that “the determination of what constitutes a valid defense is a matter of state law,” and that “due process is satisfied so long as the defendant is not denied a fair opportunity to present his case.” Id. at 526.

In *Hill v. Texas*, 316 U.S. 400 (1942), the Court upheld the exclusion of psychiatric evidence offered to show that the defendant was incapable of forming criminal intent. The Court stated that “the Constitution does not require a state to permit the introduction of evidence of mental condition for the purpose of negating criminal intent unless such a defense is recognized by state law.” Id. at 404. The Court further held that “the right to present evidence is not absolute,” and that “a state may impose reasonable limitations on the scope of evidence to be introduced.” Id. at 405.

The same principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Brawner*, 347 U.S. 520 (1954), where the Court held that “the Constitution does not require a state to recognize a defense based on mental incapacity unless it chooses to do so.” Id. at 525. The Court emphasized that “the burden of proving a defense rests upon the defendant,” and that “the State is not required to adopt a particular evidentiary rule.” Id.

In *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), the Court held that “the Constitution does not require a state to permit the introduction of evidence of mental condition for the purpose of negating criminal intent unless such a defense is recognized by state law.” Id. at 455. The Court noted that “the determination of what constitutes a valid defense is a matter of state law,” and that “due process is satisfied so long as the defendant is not denied a fair opportunity to present his case.” Id.

Finally, in *Hill v. Texas*, 316 U.S. 400 (1942), the Court reiterated that “the Constitution does not require a state to permit the introduction of evidence of mental condition for the purpose of negating criminal intent unless such a defense is recognized by state law.” Id. at 404. The Court emphasized that “the right to present evidence is not absolute,” and that “a state may impose reasonable limitations on the scope of evidence to be introduced.” Id. at 405.

The Arizona Supreme Court correctly concluded that Clark’s expert testimony was properly limited to the issue of insanity, a defense recognized under Arizona law. The state is not required to permit a defendant to use psychiatric evidence to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent unless such a defense is recognized by state law. The Due Process Clause does not compel a state to adopt a rule allowing such evidence where it has not created a separate defense based on incapacity to form intent. The trial court’s ruling was consistent with constitutional principles established long before 1970.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
