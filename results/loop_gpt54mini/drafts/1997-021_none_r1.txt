Petitioner is entitled to relief for two independent reasons: the Board’s polling rule is inconsistent with the Act, and the Board’s adverse factual finding is unsupported by substantial evidence.

First, the Board may not convert a limited employee poll into a forbidden act unless the employer already has proof sufficient to withdraw recognition. The National Labor Relations Act protects employee free choice and stable bargaining relationships, but it does not authorize the Board to eliminate a lawful means of testing whether an incumbent union still commands support. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33-34 (1937); NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 309 U.S. 206, 226 (1940). The Board’s duty is to administer the Act, not to impose a policy that makes polling meaningless in practice. NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 130-31 (1944); NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp., 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963).

The Board’s rule does exactly that. It permits a poll only when the employer already has objective evidence enough to justify withdrawing recognition. That collapses two different questions into one. A poll is designed to answer whether the employer’s suspicion is correct before it takes the more drastic step of ending recognition. A standard that requires the employer already to possess withdrawal-grade proof deprives the poll of independent value. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that labor-law doctrine must remain tied to statutory purpose and practical operation, not abstract formalism. NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co., 304 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1938); Southern Steamship Co. v. NLRB, 316 U.S. 31, 47 (1942).

The Board’s own premise confirms the problem. An incumbent union is entitled only to a rebuttable presumption of majority support. That presumption facilitates stability, but it does not authorize the Board to foreclose the employer from gathering objective information about whether the presumption remains accurate. A supervised, secret-ballot poll is a limited inquiry, not a repudiation of collective bargaining. Nothing in the Act specifically bans such a procedure. Where Congress has not spoken directly, the Board may choose among permissible policies, but its choice must be rational and consistent with the statutory scheme. NLRB v. Truck Drivers Local Union No. 449, 353 U.S. 87, 96-97 (1957); Radio Officers’ Union v. NLRB, 347 U.S. 17, 49-50 (1954).

Second, the Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. Substantial evidence is not satisfied by selective skepticism or by isolating individual items while disregarding the record as a whole. Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229-30 (1938); Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488-91 (1951). The Board must consider the evidence that supports and undermines its conclusion, and a reviewing court must ensure that the agency’s finding has real evidentiary footing. Universal Camera Corp., 340 U.S. at 487-88.

Here, the record showed significant objective indications that the union had lost support. Employees made statements to the new owners suggesting that the union no longer represented the views of a majority of the unit. The company then conducted a secret-ballot poll, supervised by a priest, and the result was 19 to 13 against the union. The Board nevertheless discounted much of the evidence by parsing it in a way that minimized its obvious significance. That is not substantial evidence; it is selective disregard.

Pre-1970 precedent confirms that employee statements and workplace realities are probative when assessing majority support. In NLRB v. Century Cement Mfg. Co., 208 F.2d 84 (2d Cir. 1953), the court treated employee expressions of dissatisfaction as relevant to an employer’s doubt. In NLRB v. R. H. Oswald Co., 217 F.2d 193 (7th Cir. 1954), the court recognized that an employer may rely on objective signs of lost support without awaiting formal decertification. In NLRB v. Somerville Packing Co., 211 F.2d 574 (1st Cir. 1954), the court required the Board to assess employee statements in context, not mechanically. And in Columbia Envelope Co. v. NLRB, 151 F.2d 571, 575 (7th Cir. 1945), the court explained that the Board must explain why probative evidence is rejected. The Board did not do so here.

Nor can the Board justify its result by suggesting that isolated doubts are insufficient in the abstract. The question is whether the employer had a good-faith reasonable doubt based on objective indications. That standard does not require proof beyond a reasonable doubt, nor does it require the employer to have already won a formal election against the union. Consolidated Edison Co., 305 U.S. at 229-30; Universal Camera Corp., 340 U.S. at 488-91. By insisting on a higher practical threshold while calling it the same legal standard, the Board imposed a rule divorced from the evidence and the statute alike.

The Court should therefore hold that the Board’s polling restriction is irrational and inconsistent with the Act, and that the finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. Allen-Bradley Co. v. NLRB, 286 U.S. 254, 260-61 (1932); Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 798-99 (1945).
