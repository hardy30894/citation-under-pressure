I. THE HOBBS ACT EXCEEDS CONGRESS’S ARTICLE I POWER AS APPLIED HERE

The convictions resting on 18 U.S.C. § 1951 cannot stand because Congress lacked constitutional authority to reach this local robbery and homicide. The Hobbs Act is tied to the Commerce Clause, but that power is not limitless. Congress may regulate interstate commerce, yet “the power to regulate interstate commerce is plenary,” Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111, 120 (1942), only when the regulated activity bears the requisite constitutional relation to commerce. Congress cannot convert every local crime into a federal offense merely by reciting an attenuated effect on commerce. See Hammer v. Dagenhart, 247 U.S. 251, 276-77 (1918), overruled on other grounds by United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100 (1941).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly required a real constitutional connection between the regulated conduct and interstate commerce. In Carter v. Carter Coal Co., 298 U.S. 238, 303-04 (1936), the Court held that production is not commerce and that the Commerce Clause does not permit federal regulation of purely local activity simply because it may have eventual market consequences. Likewise, in Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 543-48 (1935), the Court rejected federal regulation of local sales and slaughterhouse practices where the transactions had only an indirect relation to interstate commerce. Here, the charged robbery of a local sandwich shop, and the tragic accidental shooting that occurred during it, are quintessentially local criminal conduct. That the business purchased supplies from out of state and later suffered reduced revenue does not transform the offense into interstate commerce. See Carter, 298 U.S. at 308-09; Schechter Poultry, 295 U.S. at 545-48.

The government’s theory reduces the Commerce Clause to a general police power, a result the Court has rejected from the beginning. See Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 194-95 (1824). The Court has distinguished between genuine channels or instrumentalities of commerce and local conduct affecting commerce only indirectly. See Houston, E. & W. Tex. Ry. Co. v. United States (Shreveport Rate Cases), 234 U.S. 342, 351-52 (1914). The robbery here was not transportation, not shipment, and not an instrumentality of commerce; it was a local criminal episode at a neighborhood restaurant. To hold that such conduct falls within § 1951 would erase the distinction preserved in United States v. E.C. Knight Co., 156 U.S. 1, 12-13 (1895), where the Court refused to treat manufacturing monopolies as interstate commerce simply because they affected later sales.

Nor can Congress rely on the fact that the restaurant bought goods from Kansas and out-of-state vendors. In A.L.A. Schechter Poultry, 295 U.S. at 550-51, the Court rejected the argument that local activity becomes federal because it is part of a broader stream of commerce. The Hobbs Act as applied here rests on a chain of speculation: the robbery reduced the business’s receipts, and the business might later buy fewer out-of-state goods. That is precisely the sort of remote effect the Court has found constitutionally insufficient. See Carter, 298 U.S. at 307-08; Schechter Poultry, 295 U.S. at 545-48.

The federal nature of the prosecution is especially problematic because the States possess primary authority over robbery, homicide, and accomplice liability. The Tenth Amendment preserves that reserved authority. See United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941); Hammer, 247 U.S. at 275-76. Nothing in the Constitution authorizes Congress to displace ordinary state criminal law whenever a local robbery touches a business engaged in interstate purchasing. Congress may regulate commerce, not every crime that happens to affect a business operating in commerce. See Gibbons, 22 U.S. at 195; E.C. Knight, 156 U.S. at 12-13.

Accordingly, § 1951 is unconstitutional as applied to Pearson’s prosecution. The conviction depending on that statute must be vacated.

II. AT MINIMUM, THE GOVERNMENT’S COMMERCE SHOWING WAS INSUFFICIENT

Even if the Court does not hold the statute invalid on its face or as applied, the government failed to prove the constitutionally necessary commerce nexus beyond a reasonable doubt. A federal criminal statute with a jurisdictional element still requires proof that the charged conduct falls within congressional power. See Carter, 298 U.S. at 303-04; Shreveport Rate Cases, 234 U.S. at 351-52. Mr. Peterson’s testimony that the restaurant bought goods from out of state and would use stolen money to replenish inventory is too attenuated. Under Schechter Poultry, 295 U.S. at 543-48, and E.C. Knight, 156 U.S. at 12-13, indirect economic consequences do not suffice.

Because the government’s proof established only a local robbery of a local restaurant, the Hobbs Act conviction cannot be sustained. The Court should reverse.
