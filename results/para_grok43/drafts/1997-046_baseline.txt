**ARGUMENT**

**I. Clemency proceedings create no protected life or liberty interest.**

The Due Process Clause does not extend to executive clemency. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), the Court held that a state’s clemency procedures confer no constitutionally protected liberty interest. The decision rested on the recognition that “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts.” *Id.* at 464. Because Ohio’s clemency process is wholly discretionary and independent of the judicial adjudication of guilt or sentence, it likewise creates no life or liberty interest protected by the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that clemency becomes subject to due-process scrutiny once a state makes it “an integral part” of its adjudicatory system—misapplies *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985). *Evitts* addressed the effective assistance of counsel on a first appeal as of right, a stage that directly reviews the trial court’s judgment. Clemency, by contrast, occurs after all judicial remedies have been exhausted and rests in the unfettered discretion of the executive. *Dumschat* expressly rejected the proposition that the Due Process Clause attaches to every post-conviction procedure a state may elect to provide. 452 U.S. at 464-65. No federal court has held otherwise.

**II. The voluntary interview option does not compel self-incrimination.**

Ohio’s regulation permits, but does not require, an inmate to request a personal interview. The inmate remains free to decline the interview or to limit any statements made. Because participation is voluntary, the procedure does not impose an unconstitutional condition on the exercise of Fifth Amendment rights.

In *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), the Court declined to adjudicate a self-incrimination claim arising from prison disciplinary proceedings where the inmate had not yet been compelled to speak and where any future use of statements remained speculative. The same principle governs here. Any risk that statements volunteered during a clemency interview might later be introduced in a new prosecution is “premature” for judicial resolution. *Id.* at 324. The regulation itself contains no provision authorizing the parole authority to compel answers or to penalize silence, and the Fifth Amendment is not violated by the mere possibility that an inmate might choose to speak.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
