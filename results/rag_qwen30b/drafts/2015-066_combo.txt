**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel is not a mere procedural formality; it is a foundational safeguard of due process, ensuring that a defendant’s guilt is not determined by a process that lacks the essential protections of a fair trial. The use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction—valid under tribal law and the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA)—as an essential element of a federal felony offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment because it permits the government to prove a critical element of a new crime based on a prior adjudication that failed to afford the defendant the right to counsel. This is not a sentencing enhancement, but a guilt-phase determination. The Constitution demands more than a preponderance of evidence; it demands proof beyond a reasonable doubt. When the government seeks to establish a defendant’s status as a recidivist offender—thereby triggering a felony charge—it must do so through a process that satisfies the full panoply of constitutional protections, including the right to counsel.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the right to counsel is not limited to the trial on the current charge. In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 114 (1967), the Court held that “the right to counsel is not limited to the trial on the current charge,” but extends to any proceeding that may result in a more severe punishment. The Court emphasized that “the charge of being an habitual offender is as effectively refuted by proof that there was no prior conviction or that the prior convictions were not penitentiary offenses as by proof that the accused is not the person charged with the new offense.” Id. at 115. More importantly, the Court stated that “denial or absence of counsel is an issue raisable on collateral attack of state judgments.” Id. (citing *Williams v. Kaiser*, 323 U.S. 471 (1944)). This principle applies with equal force when the government seeks to use a prior conviction—obtained without counsel—as a basis for a new felony charge. The right to counsel is not a mere procedural nicety; it is a constitutional imperative that must be respected at every stage where a defendant’s liberty is at stake.

The Court has also made clear that the validity of a prior conviction is not immune from constitutional scrutiny simply because it occurred in a different jurisdiction. In *Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448, 456 (1962), the Court held that “unless any infirmities in the prior convictions that can be reached on collateral attack can be reached in these proceedings, the wrong done is seriously compounded.” The Court recognized that “the charge of being an habitual offender is also effectively refuted by proof that the prior convictions were not constitutionally valid as, for example, where one went to trial without a lawyer under circumstances where the appointment of someone to represent him was a requirement of due process.” Id. This language is directly applicable here: Bryant’s tribal court convictions were obtained without the right to counsel, and under the circumstances, that right was a requirement of due process. The Court in *Oyler* did not distinguish between state and federal proceedings; it held that the constitutional validity of prior convictions must be examined when they are used to enhance punishment.

The Court has further rejected the notion that a conviction can be deemed valid for all purposes simply because it was not invalidated on direct appeal. In *Chandler v. Warden Fretag*, 348 U.S. 3, 7 (1954), the Court considered a case in which a defendant was tried as an habitual criminal without notice or counsel. The Court held that “the full procedural panoply of the Bill of Rights, so far as notice and an opportunity to defend are concerned, must be afforded the accused.” Id. The Court emphasized that “the charge of being an habitual criminal is as effectively refuted by proof that there was no prior conviction or that the prior convictions were not penitentiary offenses as by proof that the accused is not the person charged with the new offense.” Id. at 8. The Court concluded that the defendant’s conviction was invalid because he was denied counsel at a stage where his liberty was at stake. The same logic applies here: the use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction to establish a felony offense under § 117(a) is no less a deprivation of due process than the use of such a conviction in a habitual offender proceeding.

The Court has also recognized that the right to counsel is not forfeited simply because a defendant is not sentenced to prison. In *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 372 (1979), the Court held that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply to uncounseled state proceedings in which a sentence of imprisonment is not imposed. But that case is inapposite here. The tribal court convictions in this case resulted in imprisonment. The fact that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to uncounseled proceedings in which no prison sentence is imposed does not mean that a conviction obtained without counsel can be used to establish a new felony offense. The Court has never held that a conviction obtained without counsel can be used to prove an essential element of a new crime. In *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 731 (1948), the Court held that “the State’s failure to provide counsel for this petitioner on his plea to the fourth offender charge did not render his conviction and sentence invalid.” But the Court was not addressing the use of that conviction as an element of a new offense. The Court was merely affirming that the conviction itself was valid for sentencing purposes. The Court did not hold that such a conviction could be used to prove guilt in a new prosecution.
