I. The District Court erred in granting summary judgment, as the record presents genuine issues of material fact on whether Erdman accumulated 1,250 hours of service. Summary judgment is proper only where the movant shows the absence of any triable issue. Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 467 (1962). Courts must draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party and may not weigh credibility. Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). Where affidavits and correspondence conflict on the extent of extra hours authorized and recorded as compensable time, disposition by summary judgment is inappropriate. Whitaker v. Coleman, 115 F.2d 305, 306 (5th Cir. 1940).

II. Erdman’s extra hours, regularly performed with supervisory approval and treated as “comp” time, qualify as hours worked under the governing standard. Working time includes all hours an employee is required or permitted to labor for the employer’s benefit. Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 690-91 (1946). Activities performed outside the scheduled shift count when they are an integral part of the employee’s duties and are not undertaken solely for the employee’s convenience. Tennessee Coal, Iron & R. Co. v. Muscoda Local No. 123, 321 U.S. 590, 598 (1944). Time spent on tasks that the employer knows or has reason to believe are being performed must be credited. Walling v. Portland Terminal Co., 330 U.S. 148, 152 (1947). Where supervisors consistently authorized payment or compensatory time for such hours, the employer cannot later disclaim them. Armour & Co. v. Wantock, 323 U.S. 126, 133 (1944). The same principle applies when extra work is performed at the employer’s premises or in the field with tacit approval. Jewell Ridge Coal Corp. v. Local No. 6167, UMW, 325 U.S. 161, 166 (1945).

III. Nationwide’s sudden prohibition of “comp” time after years of acquiescence does not retroactively eliminate the hours already accumulated. An employer that has long permitted and compensated off-schedule work cannot erase that time by unilateral announcement. See NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 45 (1937) (recognizing that established practices create enforceable expectations). Erdman’s contemporaneous email seeking clarification and the absence of any response further demonstrate that the hours remained creditable until the February 2003 directive. Because the record supports a finding that these hours brought Erdman above the statutory threshold, the District Court’s conclusion that she was ineligible for FMLA leave cannot stand.
