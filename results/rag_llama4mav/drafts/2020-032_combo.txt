The petitioners' constitutional challenge to the school policy is rendered moot by the revision of the policy during litigation, despite their claim for nominal damages. The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the precedent set in Flanigan's Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017), which held that a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. See New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254 (1964). In this case, the revision of the "Freedom of Expression Policy" and the removal of the challenged portion of the "Student Code of Conduct" rendered the petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot.

The petitioners' claim for nominal damages is insufficient to save their otherwise moot constitutional challenge. As the Eleventh Circuit noted, the Supreme Court has held that nominal damages are not sufficient to establish standing in the absence of actual injury. See Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts, 388 U.S. 130 (1967). The petitioners have not alleged any actual injury, and their complaint rests on the abstract injury suffered as a result of the alleged violation of their constitutional rights.

The Court has also recognized that a plaintiff must demonstrate a "real and immediate" threat of future harm to establish standing for injunctive relief. See Monroe v. Board of Commissioners of Jackson, 391 U.S. 450 (1968). The petitioners have not made such a showing, and their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are therefore moot.

Furthermore, the Court has held that a case is not rendered non-justiciable simply because a plaintiff seeks only nominal damages. See Murdock v. Pennsylvania, 319 U.S. 105 (1943). However, in this case, the petitioners' claim for nominal damages is not sufficient to overcome the mootness of their other claims.

The petitioners' attempt to distinguish their case from Flanigan's on the ground that it involves an as-applied challenge to the policy is unavailing. The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the precedent set in Flanigan's, and the petitioners have not shown that their case falls within any of the exceptions discussed in that case.

In Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District, 393 U.S. 503 (1969), the Court held that students' First Amendment rights are not shed at the schoolhouse gate. However, this case is distinguishable from Tinker, as the petitioners here have not demonstrated a concrete injury or a real and immediate threat of future harm.

The Court has also recognized that a plaintiff must have standing to bring a constitutional challenge. See Jones v. Opelika, 316 U.S. 584 (1942). The petitioners have not demonstrated standing, as they have not alleged any actual injury or shown a real and immediate threat of future harm.

In Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), the Court held that a released-time program for religious instruction did not violate the First Amendment. Similarly, in this case, the revision of the school policy has rendered the petitioners' constitutional challenge moot.

In Millsaps College v. City of Jackson, 275 U.S. 129 (1927), the Court held that a tax exemption for a college did not impair the obligation of a contract. While this case is not directly on point, it demonstrates the Court's willingness to consider the specific facts and circumstances of a case in determining whether a constitutional challenge is justiciable.

In Robert Zwicker v. James Boll, 391 U.S. 353 (1968), the Court dismissed a case as moot, holding that the plaintiffs' claims were no longer "live." Similarly, in this case, the petitioners' claims are no longer "live" due to the revision of the school policy.

In Board of Ed. of Central School Dist. No. 1 v. Allen, 392 U.S. 236 (1968), the Court considered a constitutional challenge to a state law requiring the loaning of textbooks to parochial students. While this case is not directly on point, it demonstrates the Court's willingness to consider constitutional challenges to government policies and laws.

For the reasons discussed above, the Eleventh Circuit correctly dismissed the petitioners' constitutional challenge as moot. The revision of the school policy rendered the petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot, and their claim for nominal damages is insufficient to save their otherwise moot constitutional challenge.
