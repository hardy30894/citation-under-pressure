ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Sixth Amendment bars use of Bryant’s prior tribal-court convictions as predicates under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Tribal-court proceedings are not subject to the Sixth Amendment. Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (“[T]he Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.”). Congress’s plenary authority over Indian affairs permits statutes such as § 117(a) to treat tribal convictions as valid predicates. United States v. Lara, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004); United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978).

Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), confirms that an uncounseled conviction valid when rendered may be used in a later prosecution. There the Court held that “a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction” that “did not violate the Sixth Amendment” could enhance a subsequent sentence. Id. at 746-47 (citing Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367 (1979)). The same principle governs recidivist statutes that define elements rather than sentencing factors. United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008) (“[R]ecidivist statutes penaliz[e] only the last offense committed.”). Because Bryant’s tribal convictions complied with the Indian Civil Rights Act and therefore did not violate the Sixth Amendment when entered, their later use under § 117(a) raises no constitutional objection.

Alabama v. Shelton, 535 U.S. 654 (2002), reinforces the distinction between an invalid sentence and a valid conviction. Shelton invalidated only the incarcerative portion of an uncounseled judgment while leaving the conviction intact. Id. at 661-62. Tribal convictions that result in imprisonment remain valid under the ICRA and may therefore serve as predicates.

Comity principles likewise counsel against invalidating tribal judgments for lack of procedures the Constitution does not impose on tribal courts. Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987); Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978). Imposing a Sixth Amendment overlay would “intrude[] needlessly on tribal self-government.” Id.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule rests on an erroneous extension of In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), to a context Nichols expressly left untouched. Nichols permits use of valid uncounseled convictions at both sentencing and, a fortiori, in recidivist prosecutions. 511 U.S. at 747-48. Section 117(a) therefore comports with the Sixth Amendment.
