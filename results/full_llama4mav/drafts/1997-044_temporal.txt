The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are intended to provide a fresh start for honest debtors, but they do not shield those who have committed fraud. The question presented here is whether individuals who commit fraud can discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded, under the Bankruptcy Code. The answer is no.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." The language of this provision is clear: debts obtained by fraud are not dischargeable. As the Supreme Court held in Brown v. Felsen, 442 U.S. 127 (1979) is not available, we look to earlier cases such as Forsyth v. Vehmeyer, 177 U.S. 177 (1900), which established that a discharge in bankruptcy does not release a bankrupt from debts that were obtained through fraudulent means.

In this case, the debtor, Edward S. Cohen, committed actual fraud by charging his tenants excessive rents in violation of the local rent control ordinance. The Bankruptcy Court found that Cohen had made an implicit representation to his tenants that the rents he charged were lawful, and that this representation was false. This finding is supported by the principle that a debtor's silence or failure to disclose material information can constitute a false representation. See Stewart v. Emerson, 52 N.H. 301 (1872).

The Court of Appeals correctly held that the debt Cohen owed to his tenants was nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) because it was obtained by actual fraud. The award of treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is also nondischargeable. The reasoning in Brown v. Felsen is again not available; however, the principle that a debt obtained through fraud is not dischargeable has been long-standing. See Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704 (1877).

In Neal, the Supreme Court held that a debt obtained through fraud is not dischargeable, even if the fraud was not intentional. The Court stated that "the frauds included in the portion of the clause, 'obtained by fraud,' are those which in fact involve moral turpitude or intentional wrong." Id. at 709. Cohen's actions, as found by the Bankruptcy Court, involved intentional wrong and moral turpitude, and therefore the debt he owes to his tenants is not dischargeable.

The award of treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is also nondischargeable because it is a debt that was obtained by Cohen's actual fraud. As the Supreme Court held in Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), a debt that is founded on fraud is not dischargeable in bankruptcy.

In conclusion, the debt Cohen owes to his tenants, including the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, is not dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code because it was obtained by actual fraud. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
