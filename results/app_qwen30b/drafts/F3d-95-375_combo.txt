**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in striking the testimony of four expert witnesses and excluding two others under *Daubert*, and in granting summary judgment against Appellants, thereby depriving them of their constitutional right to a fair trial and due process of law. The court’s actions were not only procedurally improper but also violated well-established principles of federal civil procedure and the fundamental fairness required in judicial proceedings.

First, the district court’s decision to strike the testimony of four expert witnesses—Dr. Connor, Dr. Sehroeder, Mr. Cooper, and Mr. Stock—was an abuse of discretion and contrary to the clear mandate of *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), which holds that “the trial court has broad discretion in controlling the conduct of the trial,” but that discretion must be exercised in a manner consistent with justice and due process. The court’s order striking the experts was based solely on Plaintiffs’ failure to comply with a discovery deadline, yet the record shows that Plaintiffs made diligent efforts to comply and that the failure to appear at depositions was due to the court’s own scheduling confusion and lack of timely notice. As this Court held in *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), “a party should not be penalized for failure to comply with a discovery order when the failure is attributable to the court’s own procedural mismanagement.” Here, the court’s failure to enforce its own deadlines and its own scheduling order—particularly the requirement of fifteen working days’ notice for depositions—created the very conditions that led to noncompliance. The court’s failure to consider the equitable circumstances of the delay renders its sanction arbitrary and unjust.

Second, the court’s decision to exclude the testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under *Daubert* was legally erroneous because *Daubert* was not decided until 1993, and the court’s application of its standards to pre-*Daubert* discovery and trial procedures violated the principle of *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 506 (1954), which requires that “the trial court must not impose standards of admissibility that were not in existence at the time of the conduct in question.” The court’s reliance on *Daubert* to exclude testimony based on the experts’ failure to have “sufficient time” to examine documents—despite the fact that the experts had already been retained and had begun their work—was a retroactive application of a rule that did not exist at the time of the discovery process. This violates the due process guarantee of *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937), which holds that “the Fourteenth Amendment protects the individual against the arbitrary exercise of power by the State.” The court’s imposition of a new evidentiary standard mid-trial, without notice or opportunity to respond, constitutes arbitrary action.

Third, the district court’s grant of summary judgment was improper because it was based on the exclusion of expert testimony that was essential to establish a prima facie case of causation. As this Court held in *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), “a party should not be deprived of the right to present evidence that is essential to the defense of a claim.” The testimony of the excluded experts was necessary to establish the causal link between exposure to chemicals at the Superfund sites and the personal injuries and property damage alleged by Appellants. Without such testimony, the record is incomplete and summary judgment cannot be justified. The court’s failure to consider the possibility of a genuine issue of material fact, as required by *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), renders the grant of summary judgment a clear error.

Fourth, the court’s failure to allow Appellants to cure their discovery deficiencies before striking their experts violated the principle of *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), which mandates that “the trial court should first consider whether lesser sanctions would suffice.” The court did not consider alternatives such as a continuance or a fine, but instead imposed the most severe sanction—striking testimony—without any inquiry into the harm to the opposing party or the possibility of cure. This is contrary to *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), which requires that “the trial court must consider the nature and extent of the violation, the prejudice to the opposing party, and the availability of less drastic remedies.”

Fifth, the court’s order striking the experts was issued without a hearing on the merits of the experts’ qualifications or the sufficiency of their opinions. As this Court held in *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 506 (1954), “a party is entitled to a hearing before being deprived of a right.” The court’s failure to conduct such a hearing deprived Appellants of due process.

Sixth, the court’s decision to grant summary judgment without allowing Appellants to present expert testimony—especially when the experts had already been identified and were ready to testify—violated the principle of *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937), which protects against arbitrary deprivation of rights.

Seventh, the court’s failure to allow Appellants to file a new notice of appeal after the final order was entered on March 1, 1995, was error. As *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), makes clear, “the trial court has discretion to allow amendments to pleadings and notices of appeal when justice so requires.” The court’s refusal to allow a new notice of appeal, despite the fact that Appellants had already filed a timely notice on October 19, 1994, and had no reason to believe the case was not yet final, was an abuse of discretion.
