**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NLRB’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS IRRATIONAL AND CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.

The National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) does not prohibit employers from testing employee sentiment regarding union representation. Indeed, Congress intended to preserve the employer’s right to act on its good-faith beliefs about the union’s continued majority support—so long as it does so without coercion or unfair labor practices. The NLRB’s current “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard, however, renders this right illusory by requiring an evidentiary threshold for polling that is identical to the standard for withdrawing recognition—despite the fact that polling is a less formal, less protected, and more vulnerable process than a Board-supervised election. This inconsistency undermines the NLRA’s goal of stable collective bargaining relationships and violates the principle that administrative agencies must act within the bounds of rationality and statutory authority.

The Board’s standard is irrational because it treats polling as a tool only for employers who already have sufficient grounds to withdraw recognition—yet such employers have no need to poll. As the Board itself acknowledged in *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling” and lacks the procedural safeguards of a Board-conducted election. If an employer already has objective evidence of a loss of majority support, it may simply withdraw recognition or seek an RM election. But if the employer lacks such evidence, the Board will not permit a poll—thus eliminating the very tool that could resolve uncertainty. This creates a paradox: the Board allows polling only when it is unnecessary, and forbids it when it is most needed.

The courts of appeals have correctly recognized this flaw. In *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1139, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981), the court held that an employer may poll employees “if there is other substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. The court emphasized that polling serves a “legitimate and useful” function in resolving uncertainty. The same reasoning was adopted by the Ninth Circuit in *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984), which held that an employer may poll “if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient in itself to justify withdrawal.” The Sixth Circuit reached the same conclusion in *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982), noting that the presumption of continuing majority status is rebuttable after one year and that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for employers in sincere doubt.

These decisions reflect a rational understanding of the NLRA’s balance between employer rights and employee protections. The Board’s contrary standard—requiring the same level of objective evidence for polling as for withdrawal of recognition—fails to account for the unique role of polling as a fact-finding mechanism. As the *Thomas* court observed, “an employer who would commit an unfair labor practice if it refused to bargain with a union having majority support or did bargain with a union which did not enjoy that support” is entitled to a means of testing sentiment. The Board’s rule, by denying that means when doubt is genuine but evidence is insufficient, forces employers into a Hobson’s choice: either risk an unfair labor practice by bargaining with a minority union or violate the Act by refusing to bargain with a majority union.

Moreover, the Board’s standard is inconsistent with *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41, 107 S.Ct. 2225, 2232–35, 96 L.Ed.2d 22 (1987), which established that an incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority status. The presumption is not absolute; it is rebuttable upon the presentation of objective evidence of a loss of support. The Board’s rule, however, effectively makes the presumption irrebuttable in practice by denying employers the ability to test their doubts through polling unless they already meet the high bar for withdrawal. This contradicts the very purpose of the rebuttable presumption: to allow employers to act on credible evidence of changed sentiment.

The Board’s attempt to justify its standard by citing the “potential disruption” of polling is insufficient. The NLRA does not require employers to accept uncertainty. Congress intended for employers to have a means of resolving doubt about union support—especially in successor employer contexts, where the bargaining unit is newly formed. In *Fall River*, the Court recognized that successor employers inherit the bargaining obligation of their predecessors, but also acknowledged that the presumption of continuing majority support is not absolute. The Board’s rule, by denying polling in such cases, forces employers to either gamble on the union’s continued support or risk violating the Act.

II. THE NLRB’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED “REASONABLE DOUBT” IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked “reasonable doubt” about the union’s majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) found that seven employees had expressed opposition to the union, and the Board adopted that finding. But the Board improperly excluded the statements of several employees who, under established precedent, should have been counted.

First, the Board refused to count the statements of Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack because they were not in the bargaining unit on January 25, 1991—the date of the company’s letter refusing recognition. But the ALJ correctly found that the relevant date for assessing the employer’s doubt is the date of the decision to poll, not the date of the letter. The company had already decided to conduct the poll by January 25, and the employees’ statements were made before that date.
