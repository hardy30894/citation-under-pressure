**ARGUMENT**

A conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication as a threat. The district court’s instruction permitting conviction on an objective “true threat” standard, and the Third Circuit’s affirmance under *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), cannot be reconciled with the First Amendment.

In *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003), this Court defined true threats as “those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” The requirement that the speaker “means to communicate” an intent to threaten is a subjective one; it focuses on the defendant’s own understanding and purpose, not on how a reasonable observer might interpret the words. An objective standard that asks only whether a reasonable person would foresee the statement as threatening therefore fails to satisfy the constitutional definition.

Section 875(c) must be read in light of that constitutional floor. The statute criminalizes transmission of “any threat to injure the person of another.” Because the First Amendment protects all but true threats, and because true threats require the speaker’s subjective intent, the government must prove that intent beyond a reasonable doubt. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding—that a subjective standard would inadequately protect against the fear of violence—overlooks the settled principle that “the constitutional guarantees of free speech and free press do not permit a State to forbid or proscribe advocacy of the use of force or of law violation except where such advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action.” *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (per curiam). The same limitation applies to threats.

The jury instructions here allowed conviction without any finding that Elonis intended his posts as threats. That error was not harmless. The record contains repeated statements by Elonis that he was “just letting you know that it’s illegal for me to say I want to kill my wife” and that he was “not actually saying it.” These disclaimers bear directly on subjective intent. Under the correct standard, the jury could have acquitted.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
