**Argument**

Peguero is not entitled to habeas relief because the sentencing court’s omission did not prejudice him. Long before 1970, this Court made clear that collateral relief is reserved for errors that amount to a denial of fundamental rights, not for every technical mistake in the proceedings. See *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (a motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 does not reach “all claimed errors in conviction and sentencing,” but only “a narrow range of injuries”); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947) (collateral review is not a substitute for appeal and cannot be used to bypass ordinary appellate process). Where, as here, the defendant already knew of his appellate right and deliberately chose not to pursue it, the sentencing court’s failure to give the Rule 32 advisement caused no injury cognizable on habeas.

The federal rules are designed to secure fair procedure, but not every violation automatically voids a conviction or sentence. This Court has repeatedly held that procedural rules should be applied in light of their purpose and the absence of prejudice. See *McNabb v. United States*, 318 U.S. 332, 341 (1943) (the Court will not “allow[] a procedural device to nullify the policy” it was meant to serve); *Lustgarden v. United States*, 389 U.S. 560, 561 (1968) (per curiam) (collateral relief is unavailable absent a showing that the asserted error affected substantial rights). That principle controls here. Rule 32’s advisement requirement exists to ensure that defendants are informed of their right to appeal. When the record establishes that the defendant already possessed that knowledge, the rule’s purpose has been fully served, and there is no basis for setting aside the sentence.

Peguero’s claim fails for the additional reason that habeas relief generally requires a showing that the alleged error caused the loss of a meaningful right. In *Smith v. O’Grady*, 312 U.S. 329, 334 (1941), the Court held that due process is violated when a defendant pleads guilty without understanding the charge and consequences, because the lack of information undermines the validity of the plea itself. But *Smith* turned on ignorance and resulting unfairness. The Court did not suggest that every omission by the sentencing judge warrants relief regardless of actual knowledge. Likewise, *Walker v. Johnston*, 312 U.S. 275, 286 (1941), recognizes habeas as a remedy for unlawful detention, not for harmless departures from preferred procedure.

Here, the District Court found after an evidentiary hearing that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing and elected not to appeal. That finding is fatal to his claim. A defendant who is already aware of his appellate right suffers no deprivation from the court’s failure to repeat that information. The omission did not mislead him, deprive him of counsel, or foreclose review. It merely failed to restate a right he already possessed. Under *Hill*, such an error is not the sort of “fundamental defect” cognizable on collateral attack. 368 U.S. at 428.

The court of appeals correctly treated the issue as one of harmless error. This Court’s pre-1970 decisions permit affirmance when the record shows that the procedural lapse did not affect the fairness or outcome of the proceeding. See *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946) (an error warrants reversal only if it had a “substantial and injurious effect or influence”); *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86-87 (1963) (the question is whether the error mattered in light of the whole record). Because Peguero knew of his appeal right, the omission had no effect whatever on his ability to seek review. There is therefore no prejudice to remedy.

Nor does the policy underlying appellate notice support habeas relief in these circumstances. The right to appeal is important, but it is not self-executing where the defendant already understands it and chooses not to act. The Constitution guarantees due process, not redundant warnings. See *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver is “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”). If Peguero knowingly declined to appeal, his inaction was a waiver, not a loss caused by judicial omission.

In short, precedent before 1970 draws a clear line: collateral relief is unavailable for nonprejudicial procedural errors. Peguero knew of his right to appeal, chose not to exercise it, and therefore cannot show that the District Court’s failure to advise him caused any cognizable injury. The judgment should be affirmed.
