**ARGUMENT**

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that non-federal lands “shall not be subject to regulations applicable solely to public lands within such units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). Its text does not divest the National Park Service of authority to enforce generally applicable regulations, such as the hovercraft prohibition, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), on lands within the boundaries of National Park System units.

The “starting point in discerning congressional intent is the existing statutory text.” *Amoco Prod. Co. v. Village of Gambell*, 480 U.S. 531, 548 (1987). That text distinguishes regulations “applicable solely to public lands” from those of broader application. The hovercraft ban falls in the latter category; it governs all lands and waters administered by the Service, not merely those owned by the United States. *See* 36 C.F.R. § 1.2(a). Because the regulation is not “applicable solely to public lands,” § 103(c) leaves it undisturbed.

This reading accords with the settled principle that “laws have no extra-territorial operation” only when Congress so intends, *United States v. Pac. & Arctic Ry. & Nav. Co.*, 228 U.S. 87, 106 (1913), and that federal regulatory authority over navigable waters within federal reservations remains intact absent an express contrary command, *Colorado v. Toll*, 268 U.S. 228, 231 (1925). ANILCA contains no such command. To the contrary, § 1319 expressly preserves “existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to … exercise licensing or regulatory functions.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon’s contrary construction “is foreclosed by the plain text of the statute.” *Sturgeon v. Masica*, 768 F.3d 1066, 1077 (9th Cir. 2014). That holding rests on the statute’s unambiguous language and requires affirmance.
