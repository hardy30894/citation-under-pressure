**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that, in rare and exceptional cases, a district court may enhance the lodestar fee award when supported by specific evidence demonstrating both superior performance and exceptional results. This conclusion is compelled by Supreme Court precedent, including *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), and *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986), which permit upward adjustments to the lodestar only in such extraordinary circumstances. The dissent’s insistence that such enhancements are categorically barred misreads and misapplies these decisions.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the lodestar—hours reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate—is “presumed to be the reasonable fee.” *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897, 104 S.Ct. at 1549. However, the Court has never held that this presumption is absolute. On the contrary, *Blum* explicitly acknowledged that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” Id. at 901, 104 S.Ct. at 1550 (emphasis added). This language, though phrased as dicta, was not mere speculation. It was a reaffirmation of the principle that the lodestar is not a ceiling, but a starting point, and that “where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee.” *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 435, 103 S.Ct. 1933, 1940 (1983). The Court in *Blum* did not reverse the fee enhancement because the quality of representation or results were irrelevant; it reversed because the district court failed to provide specific evidence that the lodestar did not reflect the quality of service rendered. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549.

This distinction is critical. The Supreme Court has never ruled that quality of performance and results are irrelevant to fee awards. Rather, it has held that enhancements based on those factors are permissible only when supported by specific evidence in the record. *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565, 106 S.Ct. at 3098 (“upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts”). The Court has repeatedly cited *Blum* with approval, reaffirming that the lodestar is not a “fixed formula” and that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” but that “the Court leaves open the possibility of an extraordinary case.” *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 566, 106 S.Ct. at 3099 (emphasis added).

The district court in this case did not rely on mere opinion or subjective admiration. It made detailed findings, grounded in its own 58 years of legal experience and firsthand observation of counsel’s performance throughout a complex, multi-year litigation. The court noted that plaintiffs’ counsel “demonstrated extraordinary skill, dedication, and professionalism,” and that the resulting injunction was “comprehensive and far-reaching,” addressing systemic failures in Georgia’s foster care system. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). These findings were not “testimony” in the evidentiary sense, but rather the district judge’s evaluation of the record—precisely the kind of assessment the Supreme Court has entrusted to trial judges. *See id.* at 1290 (“The district court is in the best position to evaluate trial-level advocacy”).

The dissent’s claim that the district court’s findings were unreviewable and violated due process is unfounded. The court did not act ex parte; it issued a detailed order with findings of fact and conclusions of law. The defendants had full opportunity to respond and were not deprived of notice or a hearing. The dissent’s concern about “unidentified” comparisons is misplaced—such comparisons are not required by law. What is required is that the district court provide a reasoned explanation for its enhancement, which it did. *See id.* at 1286–1290.

Moreover, the Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with the holdings of every other circuit that has considered the issue. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits have all affirmed fee enhancements based on exceptional performance and results when supported by specific evidence. *See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794–95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999). The existence of a circuit consensus confirms that the law is not in flux, and that the Eleventh Circuit’s interpretation is not an aberration.

Finally, the dissent’s assertion that *NAACP v. Evergreen* and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery* bar enhancement is incorrect. Those cases did not hold that enhancements are impermissible; they held that district courts must apply the correct standard and provide adequate findings. *NAACP*, 812 F.2d at 1336–37; *Norman*, 836 F.2d at 1306. The panel in this case properly applied that standard. The district court’s findings were detailed, specific, and supported by the record. The enhancement was not arbitrary or capricious.

In sum, the Supreme Court has consistently permitted upward adjustments to the lodestar in rare and exceptional cases
