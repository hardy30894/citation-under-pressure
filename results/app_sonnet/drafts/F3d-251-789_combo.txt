ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT'S FINDING OF CONSENT MUST BE REVERSED BECAUSE THE RECORD LEAVES THIS COURT WITH A DEFINITE AND FIRM CONVICTION THAT A MISTAKE WAS MADE.

Although factual findings are reviewed for clear error, that standard does not immunize findings that are unsupported by the record. A finding is "clearly erroneous when although there is evidence to support it, the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). Where, as here, the trial court's finding rests not on live credibility determinations but on documentary evidence, deposition testimony, and undisputed sequences of events, the reviewing court sits in as favorable a position as the trial court to assess whether the finding can be sustained. Orvis v. Higgins, 180 F.2d 537 (2d Cir. 1950). The same deference this Court affords findings grounded in observed testimony does not extend equally to inferences drawn from an undisputed documentary and testimonial record that, on its face, cuts against the finding reached below. See McAllister v. United States, 348 U.S. 19 (1954) (reviewing court must examine whether record evidence actually supports the trial court's ultimate finding).

II. MENA, AS THE PARTY ASSERTING CONSENT, BORE A HEAVY BURDEN TO PROVE A CLEAR, UNEQUIVOCAL, AND KNOWING RELINQUISHMENT OF GONZALEZ-CABALLERO'S CUSTODIAL RIGHTS.

Consent to relinquish a fundamental right is not to be casually inferred. "A waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). This principle governs here with special force, because the right at stake—a mother's custody of her child—is not a mere procedural entitlement but an interest of the highest personal magnitude. See May v. Anderson, 345 U.S. 528 (1953) (recognizing that custody determinations implicate rights of transcending importance to both parent and child). Where the consequence of finding a waiver is the permanent separation of mother and child, courts must allocate the risk of an ambiguous record against the party urging that waiver occurred, not against the parent whose rights are extinguished by it. Speiser v. Randall, 357 U.S. 513 (1958). Mena, having asserted consent as his defense to the return petition, bore the burden of proving that Gonzalez-Caballero knowingly and unconditionally relinquished her custodial rights over Danelsy. The district court's finding reverses that burden by treating equivocal, contradictory evidence as sufficient proof of an intentional and complete abandonment of a mother's rights to her child.

III. THE UNDISPUTED RECORD DEMONSTRATES, AT MOST, A CONDITIONAL AND TEMPORARY ARRANGEMENT, NOT AN UNEQUIVOCAL RELINQUISHMENT OF CUSTODY.

Gonzalez-Caballero's own account, even where internally inconsistent, never varies on one point: any arrangement by which Danelsy traveled with Mena was tied either to a two-week return or to Mena's promise to help Gonzalez-Caballero immigrate so that she could reassume custody. Her conduct immediately following Danelsy's departure is entirely inconsistent with the district court's finding of unconditional consent. Within a day of Mena's failure to call as promised, Gonzalez-Caballero sought out Panamanian child-welfare authorities and police; days later she approached the Judge Advocate General liaison and the Institute for Children; weeks later she petitioned the Office of the President, radio stations, and the Children's Commission, ultimately obtaining government assistance to travel to the United States to recover her daughter. This pattern of urgent, sustained, and escalating efforts to recover Danelsy is not the conduct of a mother who has intentionally and knowingly relinquished her child. Custody arrangements resting on ambiguous or contested consent must be construed narrowly, particularly where subsequent conduct contradicts the claimed relinquishment. See New York ex rel. Halvey v. Halvey, 330 U.S. 610 (1947) (custody arrangements remain subject to reexamination in light of the child's welfare and are not frozen by prior agreement); Ford v. Ford, 371 U.S. 187 (1962) (custody dispositions are inherently provisional and subject to modification, undermining any claim that an informal arrangement finally settled the matter); Kovacs v. Brewer, 356 U.S. 604 (1958) (courts must closely scrutinize purported custody agreements before treating them as binding waivers of a parent's rights).

IV. ANY CONSENT WAS EXPRESSLY CONDITIONED ON MENA'S PROMISE TO ASSIST GONZALEZ-CABALLERO'S IMMIGRATION, AND THAT CONDITION WAS NEVER FULFILLED.

Even accepting the district court's characterization that Gonzalez-Caballero agreed to Danelsy's temporary removal, her own testimony establishes that any such agreement was conditioned upon Mena helping her immigrate to the United States so she could reassume custody. Mena did not fulfill that condition; he instead directed her to arrange her own immigration through her sister and later required Immigration and Naturalization Service intervention before providing any assistance at all. A conditional consent that is never performed cannot support a finding of unconditional, completed relinquishment of custody. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938) (waiver requires intentional and complete relinquishment of a known right, not partial or conditional acquiescence).

For these reasons, the district court's finding of consent is clearly erroneous and should be reversed.
