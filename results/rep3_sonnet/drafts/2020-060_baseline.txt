# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 107 Remains Available Because the 2004 Consent Decree Did Not Resolve CERCLA Liability

CERCLA provides two distinct avenues for recovering environmental remediation costs: a six-year cost-recovery action under Section 107, 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a), and a three-year contribution action under Section 113(f), *id.* § 9613(f). This Court has recognized that these provisions serve different purposes and apply to different categories of plaintiffs. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 163 n.3 (2004) (noting that Section 107 and Section 113 provide "clearly distinct" remedies). Section 113(f)(3)(B) is triggered only when a party "has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement." 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that the 2004 Consent Decree—arising from a Clean Water Act enforcement action—satisfied this threshold.

### A. The Consent Decree Resolved Clean Water Act Liability, Not CERCLA Liability

The plain text of Section 113(f)(3)(B) requires resolution of liability for a "response action" as that term is defined under CERCLA itself. This Court has emphasized that "CERCLA is not designed to be all things to all people," *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 62 (1998) (internal quotation marks omitted), and its remedial scheme cannot be triggered by unrelated regulatory regimes absent clear congressional intent. The Consent Decree here settled EPA's claims that Guam violated the Clean Water Act, 33 U.S.C. §§ 1251 *et seq.*, by discharging pollutants without a permit—not any claim brought under CERCLA's framework.

This distinction matters because Congress crafted CERCLA's contribution provisions with specificity, "to avoid the additional confusion and complexity that would arise" from conflating distinct statutory schemes. *Key Tronic Corp. v. United States*, 511 U.S. 809, 815 (1994). The Consent Decree's own language—that it was entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam"—confirms that no CERCLA liability was resolved. While the court below dismissed this disclaimer as insufficient standing alone, this Court has recognized that settlement terms must be construed according to what the parties actually agreed to resolve, not what a court retroactively construes them to encompass. *See Cooper Industries*, 543 U.S. at 168 (contribution rights arise only from the specific mechanisms Congress created).

### B. The Contribution Bar's Structural Incentives Confirm Section 107 Availability

Section 113(f)(2) provides that a settling party "shall not be liable for claims for contribution regarding matters addressed in the settlement." 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(2). This provision exists to encourage settlement by protecting settling parties from downstream contribution suits. *See Cooper Industries*, 543 U.S. at 167 (describing Section 113(f)(3)(B) as creating "settlement incentives"). But this protective mechanism presupposes that the underlying settlement actually resolved CERCLA liability in the first instance—otherwise, non-settling PRPs receive a windfall shield without any corresponding CERCLA-based obligation having been fulfilled.

Guam's remediation of the Ordot Dump was compelled by Clean Water Act obligations, not a CERCLA settlement. To hold otherwise stretches Section 113(f)(3)(B) beyond its statutory text and undermines the "cost internalization" purpose that animates CERCLA's liability scheme. *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States*, 556 U.S. 599, 602 (2009) (describing CERCLA's goal of ensuring "that the costs of such cleanup efforts" fall on responsible parties).

## II. Even If Section 113(f)(3)(B) Applies, Guam's Section 107 Claim Should Proceed Because the Navy Bears Primary Responsibility

This Court has long recognized that CERCLA imposes liability according to principles of fairness and actual responsibility for contamination. *See United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. at 56 & n.1 (describing CERCLA's liability scheme as targeting those who caused the contamination). The facts here are extraordinary: the Navy—not Guam—constructed and operated the Ordot Dump for nearly fifty years, depositing military waste, munitions, and chemicals including DDT and Agent Orange, continuing this practice even after relinquishing formal sovereignty over the island following passage of the Guam Organic Act in 1950.

Guam inherited an environmental catastrophe created by federal military operations during a period when Guam lacked meaningful self-governance. The equitable principles underlying CERCLA's contribution scheme—apportioning liability according to "such equitable factors as the court determines are appropriate," 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(1)—cannot be read to permit the federal government to escape responsibility for contamination it caused simply because Guam, acting under EPA compulsion, entered a Clean Water Act consent decree addressing unrelated permitting violations.

This Court should not construe CERCLA's contribution provisions in a manner that produces the "harsh" result the D.C. Circuit itself acknowledged: leaving Guam to bear the entire cost of contamination caused by decades of Navy waste disposal, while the Navy escapes liability entirely due to a technical reading of an unrelated settlement's timing effects. Congress intended CERCLA to ensure that "responsible parties foot the bill," *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010)—not to immunize the actual polluter through statute-of-limitations technicalities triggered by an unrelated regulatory settlement.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and hold that Guam's Section 107 cost-recovery action against the United States remains timely.
