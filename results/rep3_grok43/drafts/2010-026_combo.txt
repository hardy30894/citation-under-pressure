The Ninth Circuit erred in engrafting a “predominantly internal” gloss onto Exemption 2. The statute exempts only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). Where the words of a statute are plain, the duty of interpretation does not arise. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Courts may not add language the Congress omitted. United States v. Missouri Pacific R. Co., 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929).

The phrase “related solely” is restrictive, not expansive. Helvering v. City Bank Farmers Trust Co., 296 U.S. 85, 89 (1935). Documents whose primary purpose is facility planning and public-safety analysis do not fall within that narrow compass merely because they also serve internal administrative ends. The court below therefore exceeded its authority by rewriting the exemption.

Even if internal use were relevant, the Navy’s own declarations establish that the ESQD materials are prepared to comply with external safety regulations and to inform surrounding communities. Such dual-purpose records are not “related solely” to personnel practices. See United States v. Morgan, 313 U.S. 409, 422 (1941) (administrative materials must be examined for their actual function, not their label).

The same principle of narrow construction governs claims of privilege in analogous disclosure contexts. United States v. Reynolds, 345 U.S. 1, 6 (1953); Chicago & Southern Air Lines, Inc. v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 333 U.S. 103, 111 (1948). FOIA exemptions are to be read no more broadly. Jencks v. United States, 353 U.S. 657, 668 (1957). The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test inverts that rule.

The legislative choice to limit Exemption 2 to personnel matters is deliberate. Palermo v. United States, 360 U.S. 343, 349-50 (1959). Courts may not enlarge it under the guise of preventing circumvention. Bowman Dairy Co. v. United States, 341 U.S. 214, 220 (1951). Because the withheld arc maps do not satisfy the statutory text, they must be disclosed.
