The question presented in this case is whether a state prisoner's procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim. The respondent argues that it cannot.

The Supreme Court has long held that a habeas corpus petitioner must demonstrate cause and prejudice to excuse a procedural default. In Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court established that a petitioner's failure to comply with a state procedural rule can bar federal habeas corpus review unless the petitioner can show cause for the default and prejudice resulting from it. The Court has since reaffirmed this principle, holding that a petitioner's failure to raise a claim in a timely manner can result in procedural default, unless the petitioner can demonstrate cause and prejudice. See, e.g., Daniels v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443 (1953).

The Court has also addressed the issue of what constitutes "cause" to excuse a procedural default. In Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), the Court held that a showing of cause requires a demonstration that the default was the result of some external factor, such as interference by state officials or a showing that the factual or legal basis for the claim was not reasonably available to counsel. 

Here, Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim was itself procedurally defaulted. Allowing this defaulted claim to serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim would undermine the principles of procedural default and the cause-and-prejudice standard established by the Court.

The respondent submits that the Court should hold that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim. This holding is consistent with the principles established in Fay v. Noia and Brown v. Allen, and ensures that habeas corpus petitioners are held to the same standards of procedural compliance as other litigants. By requiring petitioners to demonstrate cause and prejudice for procedural defaults, the Court can maintain the integrity of the habeas corpus process and prevent the erosion of state procedural rules. 

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
