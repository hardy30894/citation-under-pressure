**ARGUMENT**

**A federal appellate court conducting plain-error review may consult the entire record—not merely the evidence placed before the jury—to determine whether an unpreserved error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) permits an appellate court to correct a “plain error that affects substantial rights” even though the defendant raised no objection below. This Court’s decisions construing that Rule establish a four-part test: there must be (1) an error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affects substantial rights, and (4) that “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993) (quoting *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936)). Nothing in *Olano* confines a court’s inquiry into the third and fourth prongs to the four corners of the trial evidence submitted to the jury. To the contrary, *Olano* explains that the burden of establishing prejudice under Rule 52(b) “normally” requires the defendant to demonstrate that the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings”—a showing that necessarily depends on the record as a whole, since only by reviewing everything that occurred can a court assess what likely would have happened absent the error. *Id.* at 734.

This Court reinforced that record-wide approach in *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002). There, in the context of an unobjected-to Rule 11 error, the Court held that a reviewing court is not limited to the plea colloquy itself but may look to “the whole record” to determine whether the error affected the defendant’s substantial rights. *Id.* at 59. The Court reasoned that limiting review to a single transcript or isolated portion of proceedings would produce “absurd” results, since a defendant’s knowledge or understanding—an issue directly analogous to the knowledge issue in *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019)—can often be demonstrated by evidence developed at other points in the proceedings. *Vonn*, 535 U.S. at 74–75. If the “whole record” is the proper universe of evidence for evaluating a Rule 11 plain-error claim, there is no principled basis for restricting a *Rehaif* plain-error claim to only the evidence introduced to the jury.

*United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74 (2004), confirms this record-wide framework and supplies the controlling prejudice standard. The Court held that a defendant seeking reversal for plain error must show “a reasonable probability that, but for the error,” the outcome of the proceeding would have been different. *Id.* at 76, 83. That standard—borrowed from cases evaluating prejudice under Rule 11 and other contexts—is inherently retrospective and probabilistic; it asks what a rational factfinder would likely have concluded had the error not occurred. Answering that question requires an appellate court to consider all record evidence bearing on the omitted element, not merely the portion the prosecution happened to present to the jury under a mistaken understanding of the law. *Id.* at 81–83.

Applying these principles to *Rehaif* errors, this Court has already recognized that the knowledge-of-status element will rarely be seriously disputed because a defendant’s knowledge that he was previously convicted of a crime punishable by more than a year’s imprisonment is a fact peculiarly within his own knowledge and typically overwhelming on this record. *Rehaif* itself did not address plain-error review, but it did not disturb the traditional plain-error framework that this Court had already constructed in *Olano*, *Vonn*, and *Dominguez Benitez*. Nor does *Rehaif* purport to convert the substantial-rights inquiry into a jury-record-only test; it simply clarifies the elements the government must prove going forward. See *Rehaif*, 139 S. Ct. at 2200. Where, as here, the defendant stipulated in a plea agreement that he was “punishable as an Armed Career Criminal” based on five prior felony convictions, and where he was previously sentenced to lengthy imprisonment on those convictions, the entire record—including the undisputed fact of multiple prior felony convictions and years of actual imprisonment—forecloses any “reasonable probability” that a properly instructed jury would have reached a different verdict on the knowledge element. *Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. at 83.

This Court’s decision in *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625 (2002), likewise supports respondent’s position. There, the Court held that an *Apprendi* error in charging drug quantity did not seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings because “the evidence that the drug conspiracy involved at least 50 grams of cocaine base was ‘overwhelming’ and ‘essentially uncontroverted.’” *Id.* at 633. The Court reached that conclusion by examining the trial record as a whole—not by confining itself to whether the indictment or jury instructions specifically referenced the omitted element. The same approach governs here: where the record demonstrates that a defendant indisputably knew of his felon status, the omission of that element from the jury instructions does not undermine the fairness or integrity of the proceedings.

Because Rule 52(b) plain-error review has always entailed a holistic assessment of the trial record’s effect on the outcome, and because this Court’s decisions in *Olano*, *Vonn*, *Dominguez Benitez*, and *Cotton* uniformly authorize appellate courts to consult the entire record in performing that assessment, the Eleventh Circuit correctly considered petitioner’s undisputed prior felony convictions and sentence of imprisonment in concluding that any *Rehaif* error did not affect his substantial rights or the fairness of his trial.
