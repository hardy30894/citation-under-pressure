The Court should reject Bond’s challenge for a straightforward reason: she lacks standing to invoke the Tenth Amendment. The Supreme Court in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority held that private parties “absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” 306 U.S. 118, 143-44, 59 S.Ct. 366, 83 L.Ed. 543 (1939). That rule controls here. Bond is a private criminal defendant, not a State or state officer, and she seeks to vindicate a structural federalism objection that belongs to the sovereigns whose authority is allegedly displaced. Under Tennessee Electric, she may not do so.

The Court of Appeals correctly treated Tennessee Electric as binding. A federal court must decide jurisdictional questions before reaching the merits. Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 523 U.S. 83, 101-02, 118 S.Ct. 1003, 140 L.Ed.2d 210 (1998). Standing is jurisdictional, so if Bond lacks standing, her Tenth Amendment claim ends there. Id.; see also Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490, 498-99, 95 S.Ct. 2197, 45 L.Ed.2d 343 (1975). The Third Circuit therefore correctly resolved standing before addressing the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229.

Bond’s position also conflicts with the Supreme Court’s understanding of the Tenth Amendment as a limitation that protects the States’ reserved powers. In New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144, 181-82, 112 S.Ct. 2408, 120 L.Ed.2d 120 (1992), the Court explained that the Constitution’s structure protects state sovereignty by prohibiting Congress from commandeering the States. Similarly, in Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898, 919-20, 117 S.Ct. 2365, 138 L.Ed.2d 914 (1997), the Court reaffirmed that the Tenth Amendment safeguards “the Constitution’s division of authority between federal and state governments.” But those cases were brought by States or state officers, not by private defendants. They confirm that the injury the Tenth Amendment guards against is institutional, not personal.

The circuit authority Bond invokes does not control. Some courts have allowed private parties to raise Tenth Amendment arguments, see Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis, 185 F.3d 693, 703-04 (7th Cir. 1999), cert. denied, 528 U.S. 1116, 120 S.Ct. 934, 145 L.Ed.2d 813 (2000); Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. U.S. Dep’t of Energy, 666 F.2d 1359, 1368 n.16 (11th Cir. 1982), but others have rejected that approach in light of Tennessee Electric. See United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); Oregon v. Legal Services Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); Brooklyn Legal Services Corp. v. Legal Services Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006), cert. denied, 128 S.Ct. 44, 169 L.Ed.2d 11 (2007); Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005), cert. denied, 548 U.S. 904, 126 S.Ct. 2968, 165 L.Ed.2d 951 (2006); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004), cert. denied, 543 U.S. 874, 125 S.Ct. 88, 160 L.Ed.2d 124 (2004). The better view, and the one grounded in Supreme Court precedent, is that private litigants may not assert a Tenth Amendment defense in the absence of state participation. Tennessee Electric, 306 U.S. at 143-44.

Nor can Bond avoid standing by reframing her challenge as an attack on Congress’s enumerated powers. The complaint remains the same: § 229 allegedly intrudes on the States’ police power. A defendant cannot create standing simply by labeling a structural federalism claim as an enumerated-powers objection. See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560-61, 112 S.Ct. 2130, 119 L.Ed.2d 351 (1992). Bond must show a personal, judicially cognizable injury from the alleged constitutional defect, and she cannot. Her injury flows from her criminal conduct and conviction, not from any denial of a right the Tenth Amendment secures to her personally. See Valley Forge Christian College v. Americans United for Separation of Church & State, Inc., 454 U.S. 464, 474-75, 102 S.Ct. 752, 70 L.Ed.2d 700 (1982).

Because Bond lacks standing to assert this claim, the Court should affirm without reaching the merits of her Tenth Amendment argument. Tennessee Electric, Steel Co., and the decisions following them foreclose her attempt to litigate a federalism objection that belongs, if at all, to the States.
