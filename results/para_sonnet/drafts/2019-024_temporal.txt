# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF SECTION 1113(2) REQUIRES SUBJECTIVE AWARENESS, NOT MERE ACCESS TO INFORMATION

The statute bars suit brought more than three years after "the earliest date on which the plaintiff *had actual knowledge* of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) (emphasis added). This Court has long recognized that when Congress uses a term of art with settled meaning, courts must give it that meaning. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952) ("[W]here Congress borrows terms of art . . . it presumably knows and adopts the cluster of ideas that were attached to each borrowed word."). "Actual knowledge" is such a term, and it has never meant constructive or imputed knowledge. Rather, it denotes a state of mind—genuine, subjective awareness of a fact. *See Spies v. United States*, 317 U.S. 492, 497 (1943) (distinguishing actual knowledge of wrongdoing from mere means of knowing).

Congress's own drafting choices confirm this reading. As originally enacted, ERISA contained *two* triggers for the limitations period: a three-year period running from "actual knowledge," and a separate three-year period running from the date a report was filed "from which [the plaintiff] could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(a)(2)(B) (1976). By providing distinct language for constructive knowledge, Congress demonstrated that it understood the difference between what a plaintiff actually knew and what a plaintiff *could have* known had she read the available materials. Interpreting "actual knowledge" to encompass information merely made available to a plaintiff would collapse this distinction and render the actual-knowledge clause interchangeable with the constructive-knowledge clause—a result this Court's interpretive principles forbid. Statutes must be construed, where possible, so that no clause is rendered "insignificant" or superfluous. *Silverthorne Lumber Co. v. United States*, 251 U.S. 385 (1920) (declining to construe a statute in a manner that would strip meaning from its terms); *see also United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955) (courts have a "duty to give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute").

Congress's 1987 repeal of the constructive-knowledge provision, leaving only the actual-knowledge clause standing, reinforces rather than undermines this reading. When Congress amends a statute by deleting language, courts presume the deletion was deliberate and meaningful. *See United States v. Wise*, 370 U.S. 405, 411 (1962) (noting that Congress's amendment of statutory language reflects an intentional choice to alter legal consequences). Having eliminated the constructive-knowledge trigger, Congress left plaintiffs subject only to the six-year statute of repose, absent genuine subjective knowledge. That a defendant fiduciary disclosed documents on a website does not establish that a plaintiff *read* them, any more than mailing a letter establishes that the recipient opened it.

## II. TREATING DISCLOSURE AS EQUIVALENT TO KNOWLEDGE CONFLATES DUTY TO DISCLOSE WITH ACTUAL AWARENESS

Sulyma's position—and the Ninth Circuit's holding—effectively substitutes "could have known" for "actual knowledge," a substitution this Court has resisted in analogous contexts. In *Union Pacific R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190 (1913), and similar cases construing notice provisions, this Court distinguished between what a party is charged with as a matter of law and what a party actually perceived. The distinction matters because the two concepts serve different purposes: constructive knowledge protects defendants from stale claims regardless of the plaintiff's diligence, while actual knowledge protects plaintiffs who, through no fault attributable to bad faith, remain unaware of a violation despite a defendant's technical compliance with disclosure obligations.

This Court's fraud-based limitations jurisprudence illustrates the point. In *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946), the Court explained that equitable tolling doctrines exist precisely because a defendant's compliance with formal disclosure requirements does not necessarily impart genuine understanding to the plaintiff. The same logic applies here: an ERISA fiduciary's compliance with disclosure regulations, standing alone, cannot establish that a beneficiary actually knew the contents of what was disclosed. To hold otherwise would allow fiduciaries to immunize themselves from the three-year period merely by burying critical information in lengthy summary plan descriptions—exactly the kind of formalistic compliance this Court has refused to treat as satisfying substantive statutory requirements. *See SEC v. C. M. Joiner Leasing Corp.*, 320 U.S. 344, 351 (1943) (looking past formal compliance to substance in construing remedial securities legislation).

## III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT'S OWN PRECEDENTS CONFIRM THAT ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE REQUIRES A FACT-SPECIFIC INQUIRY INTO SUBJECTIVE AWARENESS

Even under the Ninth Circuit's two-step framework—first isolating the underlying breach, then inquiring "when the plaintiff had 'actual knowledge' of the alleged breach or violation"—the cases petitioner's opponent relies upon confirm that actual knowledge has never been satisfied by disclosure alone. In each case cited below the courts of appeals confronted plaintiffs who were direct parties to the challenged transaction or who received individualized notice of it—not situations where information sat undisturbed on a website.

The distinction tracks this Court's broader teaching that "knowledge" imports a state of mind that must be proven, not presumed from opportunity. *See Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 270–71 (requiring proof of actual mental state where statute uses subjective mens rea language, rather than allowing inference from mere possibility of awareness). Sulyma's uncontroverted testimony that he did not read, recall, or become aware of the disclosures describing hedge fund and private equity allocations therefore created a genuine dispute of material fact—precisely the kind of factual conflict that forecloses summary judgment. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944) (summary judgment inappropriate where testimony creates a genuine question for the trier of fact, even if that testimony seems improbable).

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed insofar as it correctly requires subjective, actual knowledge rather than constructive knowledge to trigger § 1113(2)'s three-year period.
