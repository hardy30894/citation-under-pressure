The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precludes OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). However, the Coast Guard's authority to regulate vessels is not coextensive with OSHA's authority to regulate workplaces. While the Coast Guard has authority to regulate certain aspects of vessel safety, its regulations do not comprehensively address the working conditions of all employees on vessels.

The Fifth Circuit's reliance on Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. In Clary, the court held that OSHA regulations did not apply to the working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation, but it did not address the specific issue of uninspected vessels. 609 F.2d at 1122. Moreover, the court's broad language in Clary was not necessary to its holding and is therefore dicta. See id. at 1121-22.

Furthermore, the Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels are limited to specific areas, such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation systems. 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq; 46 C.F.R. §§ 26.01 et seq. These regulations do not demonstrate the "comprehensive regulation and supervision of seamen's working conditions" that the Fifth Circuit found in Donovan. 720 F.2d at 826. In fact, the Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels are more limited than those for inspected vessels, suggesting that Congress did not intend for the Coast Guard to have exclusive jurisdiction over uninspected vessels.

The Fifth Circuit's holding creates an anomaly, where workers on uninspected vessels are not protected by either OSHA or comprehensive Coast Guard regulations. This result is inconsistent with the purpose of the Act, which is to provide a safe and healthy work environment for all employees. See 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). OSHA's jurisdiction over uninspected vessels is necessary to fill the regulatory gap left by the Coast Guard's limited authority.

In Gannett v. Merchants' & Miners' Transp. Co., the court held that the Coast Guard's authority to regulate certain aspects of vessel safety did not preclude OSHA's jurisdiction over other aspects of workplace safety. 279 F. 863, 866 (D. Md. 1922). Similarly, in this case, OSHA's jurisdiction over uninspected vessels is not precluded by the Coast Guard's limited authority to regulate certain aspects of vessel safety.

Therefore, OSHA has jurisdiction under the Act to issue citations to uninspected vessels, and the Court of Appeals' holding to the contrary should be reversed.
