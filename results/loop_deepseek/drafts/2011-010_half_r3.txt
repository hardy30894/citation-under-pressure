**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) coverage to workers injured on land. The text of OCSLA, its structure, and this Court’s precedents compel the conclusion that the situs of the injury must be on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

**I. The Plain Language of OCSLA Imposes a Geographic Situs Requirement**

OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision states that LHWCA benefits extend to the “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” defines not merely the source of the injury but the geographical locus of the covered event. This Court has long held that “the language of the statute is the starting point for its interpretation,” and when the text is clear, judicial inquiry ends. *Packard v. Banton*, 264 U.S. 140, 145 (1924); *see also Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (where the language is plain, “the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms”).

The statutory phrase “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” cannot bear the Ninth Circuit’s construction. The word “occurring” modifies “injury,” establishing a direct link between the physical location of the injury-producing event and the shelf. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). If Congress had intended to cover all injuries with any connection to shelf operations, it would have said so—as it did in other provisions of OCSLA. *Compare* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (extending state law to “artificial islands and fixed structures”), *with* § 1333(b) (limiting coverage to injuries “occurring as the result of operations conducted on” the shelf). The deliberate distinction in phrasing demonstrates congressional intent to confine compensation coverage to the shelf itself. *Cf. United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939) (Congress’s use of different language in related provisions indicates a different meaning was intended).

**II. This Court’s Precedents Confirm That OCSLA Coverage Turns on Situs**

In *Rodgers v. United States*, 339 U.S. 992 (1950), this Court reversed a judgment that had applied OCSLA to an injury on shore, holding that the Act “does not extend the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act to injuries occurring on land.” The reasoning of *Rodgers* is dispositive: OCSLA’s compensation provision is tied to the physical place of injury, not merely the nature of the employment.

Similarly, in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986), this Court stated that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA's coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Acknowledging that § 1333(b) “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement,” the Court identified that situs requirement as a geographic limitation. *Id.* at 219 n.2. That statement—though dicta—reflects the consistent understanding of this Court. *See also United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 35-36 (1947) (the submerged lands and waters are subject to the paramount rights and dominion of the United States within the defined shelf area).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. *Curtis* conflicts with this Court’s instruction that OCSLA’s geographic limitation governs. *See Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 423 (1985). In *Herb’s Welding*, this Court observed that “the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to the OCSLA's incorporation of the LHWCA.” *Id.* at 423. That geographic limitation bars compensation for injuries occurring onshore.

**III. A Situs-of-Injury Requirement Is Consistent with the Statutory Scheme**

OCSLA was enacted to govern activities “on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The Act’s purpose is to establish federal jurisdiction over the shelf’s natural resources, not to create a nationwide workers’ compensation program for offshore workers. *See United States v. Ray*, 423 F.2d 16, 19 (5th Cir. 1970). Extending OCSLA to onshore injuries would disregard the Act’s limited territorial reach. *Cf. Skiriotes v. Florida*, 313 U.S. 69, 75 (1941) (statutory construction must respect jurisdictional boundaries).

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test—requiring only a connection between the injury and shelf operations—improperly expands OCSLA beyond its text. This Court has rejected such open-ended standards when Congress has spoken clearly. *See United States v. Brown*, 333 U.S. 18, 25-26 (1948) (refusing to enlarge a statute beyond its plain terms). Workers’ compensation statutes, while remedial, are still subject to the plain meaning of their terms. *See Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 7 (1946).

**IV. Applying the Correct Situs Standard, Valladolid’s Onshore Injury Is Not Covered**

Juan Valladolid was killed at an onshore oil-processing facility located on the California coast. Because his injury did not occur on the outer Continental Shelf, it falls outside § 1333(b). The Ninth Circuit’s remand for application of its “substantial nexus” test was error.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
