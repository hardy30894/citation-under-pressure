The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment secures to persons within a State "the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const. amend. XIV. Its central command is that no State shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." *The Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36, 81 (1873). The Clause was adopted to prevent "hostile and discriminating legislation against" a particular class. *Id.* at 81. It therefore operates only when state action distinguishes among classes of persons. *Strauder v. West Virginia*, 100 U.S. 303, 310 (1880) (the amendment "ordain[s] that the law in the States shall be the same for the black as for the white").

This Court has consistently required a plaintiff to show membership in a class singled out for adverse treatment. In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), the petitioners prevailed because the challenged ordinance was "applied and administered" so as to deny permits "to all Chinese subjects" while granting them to others. *Id.* at 374. The discrimination was "directed against" an identifiable class. *Id.* at 368. Similarly, in *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27 (1885), the Court upheld a municipal ordinance because it did not "discriminate[] between persons of the same class." *Id.* at 32. Arbitrary or unequal enforcement that does not rest on class distinctions falls outside the Clause's protection. *See Gulf, C. & S.F. Ry. Co. v. Ellis*, 165 U.S. 150, 155 (1897) (legislation must operate alike upon all persons "similarly situated").

Grace Olech's complaint alleges only that the Village demanded an easement larger than that required of other property owners. She pleads no membership in any class or group. Under the authorities above, such an allegation states no claim under the Equal Protection Clause. The decision below, which recognized a cause of action for a "class of one," therefore cannot be reconciled with the text and original understanding of the Clause.
