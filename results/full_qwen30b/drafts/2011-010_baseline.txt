**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits to employees injured “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This provision does not create a federal workers’ compensation regime for injuries occurring anywhere on land simply because they are *related* to offshore operations. Rather, it applies only where the injury occurs *on the outer Continental Shelf itself*—that is, on the submerged lands seaward of state jurisdiction, or on fixed structures erected thereon. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits recovery for injuries occurring onshore even when the injury site bears no functional or geographic connection to the shelf, misreads the statute and undermines the carefully drawn boundary between federal and state jurisdiction over maritime labor.

### I. THE OCSLA’S WORKERS’ COMPENSATION PROVISION APPLIES ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF

The plain language of § 1333(b) requires that the injury occur “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase “operations conducted on” is not a mere procedural detail—it is a jurisdictional threshold. The outer Continental Shelf is defined as “all submerged lands lying seaward and outside of the area of lands beneath navigable waters.” 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The term “on” in this context refers to physical location, not causal connection. Congress did not say “resulting from operations *related to*” or “arising out of” offshore activities. It said “conducted on,” which necessarily implies a geographic locus.

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s decision in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), where the Court emphasized that OCSLA’s coverage is “determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Id. at 219. The Court’s footnote in *Tallentire*—which noted that § 1333(b) “makes compensation . . . payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act” only when the injury results from operations on the shelf—was not dictum in the sense of being irrelevant. It was a *contextual* observation that the statute’s coverage is tied to the *situs* of the operation, not merely its *causal connection* to the shelf. The Court did not suggest that an injury on land could qualify under § 1333(b) simply because it was “but for” an offshore operation. To the contrary, the Court’s focus on *locale* as the “principal” determinant of coverage confirms that the injury must occur in the geographic domain Congress designated.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test, which allows recovery for injuries occurring onshore if there is a “but for” causal link to offshore operations, transforms the statute into a broad federal liability regime with no geographic limits. This reading is inconsistent with the statutory text and the Court’s repeated insistence that OCSLA’s reach is defined by *situs*. See *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985) (holding that a worker injured on a platform in state waters was not entitled to OCSLA benefits because the platform was not on the outer Continental Shelf). The Court in *Herb’s Welding* rejected the idea that a causal connection alone suffices. It held that “the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA.” Id. at 422. The same principle applies here: the geographic limitation is not a mere formality—it is the *core* of the statute’s design.

### II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S “SUBSTANTIAL NEXUS” TEST CONFLICTS WITH THE LHWCA’S SITUS REQUIREMENT

Even if the OCSLA were read to extend coverage beyond the shelf, the LHWCA itself requires that the injury occur on a “maritime situs.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that La Conchita qualifies as a “maritime situs” because it is used to consolidate scrap metal from offshore platforms is incorrect. The Supreme Court has made clear that a facility is a maritime situs only if it is “used as an integral part of longshoring operations.” *Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978). The “functional relationship” test requires consideration of proximity to navigable waters, physical access to vessels, and whether the site is devoted primarily to maritime commerce.

La Conchita fails every prong of this test. It is separated from the ocean by a highway and railroad tracks, has no pier or dock, and is three miles from the nearest pier used for loading offshore equipment. The scrap metal was not moved directly from vessel to land; it was unloaded at the Casitas Pass Pier by third-party longshoremen, transported by third-party truckers, and dumped at La Conchita—where it sat for up to two years before being hauled away. The facility’s primary function is the processing of crude oil slurry, a non-maritime activity. *Herb’s Welding I*, 470 U.S. at 422–24. Its use as a dumping ground for scrap metal does not convert it into a maritime situs.

The Ninth Circuit’s analogy to *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69 (1979), is inapposite. In *Pfeiffer*, the injured workers were engaged in the “intermediate steps of moving cargo between ship and land transportation” on a dock—clearly a maritime situs. Id. at 82–83. Here, the movement of scrap metal from the pier to La Conchita was not part of the maritime transportation chain. The maritime activity ended at the pier. The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to stretch the LHWCA’s situs requirement to include onshore facilities used for non-maritime purposes undermines the statutory boundary between maritime and land-based employment.
