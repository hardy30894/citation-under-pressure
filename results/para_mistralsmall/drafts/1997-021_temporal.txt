**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling employees is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll employees only when it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), is arbitrary and contrary to the Act. The Board’s own precedents recognize that polling is a legitimate means for an employer to ascertain employee sentiment, *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967), yet the Board has imposed a standard that renders polling virtually useless. *Id.* at 1061-62. This is irrational because the Board permits an employer to withdraw recognition or seek an RM election under the same standard, *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. N.L.R.B.*, 482 U.S. 27, 41 n.8 (1987), yet denies the employer the lesser intrusive tool of polling. The Board’s approach is inconsistent with the Act’s policy favoring stable collective-bargaining relationships, *N.L.R.B. v. Insurance Agents’ Int’l Union*, 361 U.S. 477, 488 (1960), as it discourages employers from using polling to avoid premature withdrawal of recognition or unnecessary RM elections.

The Board’s standard also conflicts with the Act’s deference to employer speech. *N.L.R.B. v. Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. 469, 477 (1941) (“[T]he Board has no power to compel anyone to speak against his will.”). The Board’s rule effectively penalizes employers for seeking to ascertain employee sentiment through polling, thereby chilling legitimate employer speech. *See generally N.L.R.B. v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 617 (1969) (recognizing employer free speech rights in the context of union recognition disputes). The Board’s approach is thus inconsistent with the Act’s protection of employer and employee rights.

**II. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a “good-faith reasonable doubt” is unsupported by substantial evidence.**

The Board erred in finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt as to the union’s majority status. The record contains substantial objective evidence raising such doubt:

1. **Employee Statements.** Seven employees expressed anti-union sentiments, including statements that the union was not worth the dues and that employees did not need union representation. *See generally N.L.R.B. v. Dan River Mills, Inc.*, 274 F.2d 381, 384 (4th Cir. 1960) (employee statements critical of union may support reasonable doubt). The Board improperly discounted these statements, erroneously requiring that employees explicitly state they oppose union representation. *See N.L.R.B. v. Wyman-Gordon Co.*, 269 F.2d 191, 194 (1st Cir. 1959) (statements expressing dissatisfaction with union may suffice).

2. **Unit Composition.** The bargaining unit was reduced from 45 to 32 employees, and all 32 were former Mack employees. *Fall River Dyeing*, 482 U.S. at 36-37 (successor employer inherits bargaining obligation). The Board ignored the fact that the presumption of majority support is weaker in a newly constituted unit. *See N.L.R.B. v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 780 (1990) (discussing presumption’s strength in context of unit changes).

3. **Poll Results.** The secret ballot poll revealed 19 employees opposed to the union and 13 in favor. *Struksnes*, 165 N.L.R.B. at 1063 (poll results may corroborate reasonable doubt). The Board improperly dismissed the poll’s significance, despite its procedural safeguards, including supervision by a neutral third party. *Id.*

The Board’s conclusion that this evidence did not raise a reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. *Universal Camera Corp. v. N.L.R.B.*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951) (substantial evidence standard requires more than a scintilla). The Board’s decision is arbitrary and capricious, as it ignores clear indicia of employee disaffection. *N.L.R.B. v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 131 (1944) (Board’s factual findings must be supported by substantial evidence).

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling is irrational and inconsistent with the Act. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should reverse the Board’s order and remand with instructions to dismiss the complaint.
