**ARGUMENT**

**A federal appellate court applying plain-error review may examine the whole record, not merely the evidence placed before the jury, to determine whether an unpreserved error affected substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.**

**I. The Plain-Error Standard Was Designed to Permit Review of the Full Record.**

This Court's plain-error doctrine originates in *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157 (1936), which held that appellate courts may notice unpreserved errors "if the errors are obvious, or if they otherwise seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Id.* at 160. That formulation—later codified in Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—does not confine the appellate inquiry to the four corners of the trial transcript. It asks a broader, record-wide question: whether, in light of everything that occurred and everything known about the case, the conviction should stand. An inquiry into "fairness, integrity, or public reputation" cannot sensibly be answered by artificially blinding the court to undisputed facts that appear elsewhere in the record simply because they were not recited to the jury.

*Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189 (1943), reinforces this understanding. There, Justice Frankfurter cautioned that the plain-error power should be exercised "sparingly," but he did not suggest that its exercise be confined to the jury's four walls; rather, the inquiry was framed as one committed to the sound discretion of appellate courts examining the case as a whole. *Id.* at 200.

**II. Harmless-Error Precedent Confirms That Reviewing Courts Must Look to the Whole Record.**

The closely related harmless-error doctrine—which shares its purpose and often its analytical method with plain-error review—has long required courts to consider the entire record rather than isolated snippets of trial testimony. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), this Court explained that a reviewing court must determine, "after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole," whether "the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error." *Id.* at 765. That standard is incompatible with a rule limiting review to only the evidence a jury heard; it requires consideration of "all that happened," a phrase this Court chose deliberately to capture the entirety of the proceedings.

*Bihn v. United States*, 328 U.S. 633 (1946), applied this same whole-record approach, examining the full course of the proceedings—not merely the jury instructions or the specific testimony at issue—to decide whether an error was harmless. *Id.* at 638–39. Likewise, in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), this Court held that even constitutional errors may be found harmless where the reviewing court is satisfied "beyond a reasonable doubt" that the error did not contribute to the verdict, an inquiry this Court has since described as requiring assessment of "the whole record." *Id.* at 24. And in *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85 (1963), the Court likewise directed lower courts to ask "whether there is a reasonable possibility that the evidence complained of might have contributed to the conviction," a question that necessarily draws on the totality of what the record reveals about the strength of the government's case. *Id.* at 86–87.

There is no principled reason why the plain-error inquiry into "substantial rights" and the "fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings," *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160, should employ a narrower record than the harmless-error inquiry into "substantial rights" under the same statutory text now embodied in Rule 52. Both doctrines serve the same purpose: preventing reversal where the error made no real difference to the outcome or to the public's confidence in the proceedings.

**III. This Court's Practice Confirms That Undisputed Facts Outside the Jury's Purview May Be Considered.**

*Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), illustrates the point directly. There, the Court confronted a conviction obtained without a jury instruction on a mental-state element the government was later required to prove—the same structural posture presented here. Rather than treating the omission as automatically fatal, the Court examined the full circumstances of the case, including undisputed facts about the defendants' conduct, to assess the significance of the error. *Id.* at 107–113 (plurality opinion). That approach recognized that some errors, however real, do not warrant automatic reversal when the record as a whole leaves no serious doubt about the outcome.

Similarly, in *Lutwak v. United States*, 344 U.S. 604 (1953), the Court concluded that errors in the proceedings did not warrant reversal because the record, taken as a whole, established the defendants' guilt so convincingly that no different result could reasonably have obtained. *Id.* at 619–20. And in *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78 (1935), the Court evaluated prosecutorial misconduct not in isolation but "in light of the record as a whole," recognizing that the ultimate question—whether the defendant received a fair trial—cannot be answered by reference to a single instruction or a single item of unpresented evidence. *Id.* at 89.

**IV. Restricting Review to the Trial Record Would Undermine the Purpose of Plain-Error Review.**

A rule barring appellate courts from considering undisputed record facts—such as a defendant's own stipulations, prior convictions, or plea colloquy admissions—would sever the plain-error inquiry from the very purpose *Atkinson* articulated: safeguarding the "fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." 297 U.S. at 160. Where the record leaves no realistic possibility that a properly instructed jury would have reached a different result, reversal serves no such purpose. The decisions of this Court from *Atkinson* through *Kotteakos*, *Screws*, *Bihn*, *Lutwak*, *Berger*, *Fahy*, and *Chapman* uniformly instruct reviewing courts to look to the record as a whole. The court below correctly did so here.
