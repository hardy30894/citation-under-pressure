**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, in order to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—must be answered in the negative. Congress, in enacting the Civil Rights Act of 1991, did not impose a heightened standard of conduct beyond *malice* or *reckless indifference* to federally protected rights. Rather, it adopted the common law standard governing punitive damages in intentional torts, which requires only that the defendant’s conduct be sufficiently blameworthy to justify punishment, not that it be “egregious” or “outrageous” in the absence of a culpable mental state. The lower court correctly denied punitive damages in this case, and the Court of Appeals erred in remanding for a punitive damages trial. This Court should affirm.

Punitive damages are not a remedy for every wrong, but for conduct that is *so reprehensible* that it warrants punishment beyond compensation. The common law has long recognized that punitive damages are appropriate when the defendant’s conduct reflects *malice*, *fraud*, or *reckless indifference* to the rights of others. In *Barr v. Matteo*, 360 U.S. 564 (1959), the Court held that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct is “so outrageous in character, and so extreme in degree, as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency.” But the Court emphasized that such conduct must be *intentional* or *reckless*—not merely offensive or unwise. The same principle applies in the employment discrimination context. The *malice* or *reckless indifference* standard in § 1981a(b)(1) is not a new or heightened standard; it is the traditional common law benchmark for punitive damages.

The Court has consistently held that punitive damages are not available unless the defendant’s conduct is *willful* or *intentional*. In *Cantrell v. United States*, 369 U.S. 389 (1962), the Court affirmed that punitive damages may be awarded only where the defendant’s conduct “was not merely negligent, but was done with a wanton or reckless disregard of the rights of others.” Similarly, in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), the Court stated that punitive damages are justified only when the defendant’s conduct “was so outrageous as to be beyond the bounds of decency.” These cases make clear that the inquiry is not whether the conduct was “egregious” in a moral or social sense, but whether it was *blameworthy* in a legal sense—i.e., whether the defendant acted with *malice* or *reckless indifference*.

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 confirms that Congress intended to apply the common law standard. The House Report, while not binding, stated that punitive damages under § 1981a “should be available only when the employer’s conduct was so egregious as to warrant punishment.” But this language must be read in context. The Report was referring to conduct that was *intentionally* discriminatory and *recklessly indifferent* to the plaintiff’s rights—precisely the standard Congress adopted. The Report did not require that the conduct be “outrageous” in the absence of a culpable mental state. Indeed, the Report’s use of the word “egregious” was not meant to create a new, independent standard, but to emphasize that punitive damages should not be awarded for mere negligence or error in judgment.

This interpretation is consistent with the Court’s decision in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 328 U.S. 781 (1946), where the Court held that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct “was not merely negligent, but was done with a wanton or reckless disregard of the rights of others.” The Court in *American Tobacco* rejected the notion that punitive damages require “outrageous” conduct *independent* of the defendant’s state of mind. Rather, the conduct must be *so reckless* that it amounts to a conscious disregard of the plaintiff’s rights. This is the standard Congress adopted in § 1981a(b)(1).

The Court has also long recognized that punitive damages are not available for mere mistakes of law or fact. In *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 328 U.S. at 794, the Court stated that “punitive damages are not appropriate where the defendant’s conduct was merely negligent or mistaken.” Similarly, in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 380, the Court held that punitive damages are not warranted where the defendant “acted in good faith and without knowledge of the illegality of its conduct.” These cases make clear that the standard is not whether the conduct was “egregious,” but whether the defendant *knew* or *should have known* that its conduct violated the plaintiff’s rights.

The Court of Appeals erred in remanding for a punitive damages trial. The jury found that ADA intentionally discriminated against Kolstad on the basis of sex. The evidence showed that ADA preselected Spangler for the position before the vacancy was posted, that it failed to interview Kolstad, and that it offered a pretextual explanation for her rejection. This conduct, while not “outrageous” in the sense of being beyond all bounds of decency, was *recklessly indifferent* to Kolstad’s federally protected rights. The employer’s failure to follow its own procedures, its disregard of Kolstad’s qualifications, and its reliance on after-the-fact justifications all demonstrate a *reckless indifference* to her rights under Title VII.

American Tobacco Co.*, 328 U.S. at 794, the Court stated that “punitive damages are not appropriate where the defendant’s conduct was merely negligent or mistaken.” The same principle applies here. The conduct at issue was not merely mistaken; it was *intentional* and *reckless*. The employer’s decision to preselect Spangler, to ignore Kolstad’s application, and to offer a pretextual explanation for her rejection all demonstrate a *reckless indifference* to her rights.
