**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Patent Issue Is Not "Substantial" Under the "Arising Under" Test**

The Supreme Court has held that a federal court may exercise jurisdiction over a state-law claim only if the federal issue is "necessary to the determination of the state-law claim." *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). Furthermore, the issue must be "substantial." *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Here, the patent issue is merely a legal conclusion about the *effect* of a prior sale, not a dispute over the validity of the patent itself. *New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 270 Neb. 264, 336 (2005). The Court has warned against allowing state plaintiffs to open the "arising under" door by simply pleading a federal issue. *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936). Because the patent issue is not a "substantial" dispute regarding the validity, construction, or effect of the patent law, it does not trigger exclusive federal jurisdiction.

**II. Federalizing Malpractice Claims Disturbs the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities**

The Supreme Court has refined the "arising under" test to include a federalism analysis. In *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005), the Court held that federal jurisdiction exists only if it "will not disturb any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." The Federal Circuit’s approach in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), ignores this requirement. These cases federalize malpractice claims, which is not what Congress intended. Allowing federal courts to decide state-law malpractice claims based on patent law intrudes on the traditional competence of state courts and creates a jurisdictional imbalance.

**III. The Patent Issue Is Incidental, Not Necessary, to the Malpractice Claim**

The California Court of Appeal has held that to the extent that the subject matter of patent law is relevant to the determination of the professional negligence claim, it does not present a question of patent law that is "substantial." *E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 189 Cal.App.4th 1140, 1152 (2010). Here, the malpractice claim is supported by theories that do not depend on patent law or the resolution of patent issues. *Danner, Inc. v. Foley & Lardner, LLP*, No. 09-1220-JE, 2010 WL 2608292, at *5 (D. Or. Mar. 15, 2010). The patent issue is merely a background fact that does not necessitate federal adjudication. Therefore, the state court is the proper forum for this dispute.
