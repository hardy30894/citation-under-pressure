**ARGUMENT**

**I. PUNITIVE DAMAGES UNDER TITLE VII REQUIRE CONDUCT THAT IS "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS," INDEPENDENT OF THE EMPLOYER'S STATE OF MIND.**

This Court has long held that punitive damages are an extraordinary remedy, reserved for conduct that is "egregious" or "outrageous." *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1851) ("Punitive damages ... are allowed ... in cases of malicious mischief, or gross and outrageous insults, or where the defendant acts wantonly, or with such gross negligence as to evince a reckless disregard of the plaintiff's rights."); *Harlow v. Cowan*, 18 F. Cas. 300, 302 (C.C.D. Mass. 1858) (No. 6,074) ("Punitive damages may be awarded where the act is done with malice, or in reckless disregard of the plaintiff's rights.").

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that punitive damages are not merely a multiplier of compensatory damages but are instead a penalty imposed for "deliberate wrongdoing." *Day*, 54 U.S. at 371. In *Barrett v. Van Pelt*, 268 U.S. 85, 91 (1925), the Court explained that punitive damages are awarded "not to compensate the plaintiff for his injuries, but to punish the defendant for his conduct and to deter him and others from similar wrongdoing in the future." This principle was reaffirmed in *Gore v. Turner*, 56 U.S. 173, 180 (1853), where the Court held that punitive damages are appropriate only where the defendant's conduct is "so gross and outrageous as to justify the imposition of additional punishment."

**II. THE "MALICE" OR "RECKLESS INDIFFERENCE" STANDARD UNDER TITLE VII DOES NOT ABROGATE THE REQUIREMENT OF "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS" CONDUCT.**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 amended Title VII to permit punitive damages where the employer acts "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). However, this statutory language does not eliminate the traditional requirement that the conduct itself be "egregious" or "outrageous." *See generally* 1 *Sutherland Statutory Construction* § 50.03 (1943) ("A statute should be construed in light of the common law unless the statute clearly indicates a different intent.").

This Court has long held that the terms "malice" and "reckless indifference" describe the *state of mind* of the defendant, not the *nature of the conduct*. *See, e.g.,* *Harlow*, 18 F. Cas. at 302 ("Malice" means "the intentional doing of a wrongful act without just cause or excuse."); *Barrett*, 268 U.S. at 91 ("Reckless indifference" requires "a conscious choice of a course of action with knowledge of the probable consequences."). The conduct itself must still be "egregious" or "outrageous" to justify punitive damages. *Day*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Gore*, 56 U.S. at 180.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY DENIED PUNITIVE DAMAGES BECAUSE THE ADA'S CONDUCT WAS NOT "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS."**

The evidence in this case does not support an award of punitive damages. The ADA's decision to promote Spangler over Kolstad, while discriminatory, was not "egregious" or "outrageous." The ADA followed its internal procedures, considered the qualifications of both candidates, and made a business decision based on perceived deficiencies in Kolstad's experience. *See* *Barrett*, 268 U.S. at 91 (Punitive damages are not warranted where the defendant's conduct, while wrongful, is not "gross and outrageous.").

Moreover, the ADA's conduct did not evince a "reckless disregard" for Kolstad's rights. The ADA's decision was based on a good-faith (if mistaken) assessment of the candidates' qualifications, not on a conscious choice to disregard her rights. *See* *Harlow*, 18 F. Cas. at 302 (Punitive damages are not available where the defendant's conduct, while wrongful, is not "wanton" or "reckless.").

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The ADA's conduct, while discriminatory, was not "egregious" or "outrageous," and thus punitive damages are not available under Title VII.
