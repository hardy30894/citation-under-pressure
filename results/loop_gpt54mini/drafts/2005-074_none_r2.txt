Fernandez-Vargas respectfully submits that § 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act does not reach aliens who reentered the United States before the statute became effective, and that applying it to him would be impermissibly retroactive.

The Court has long applied a strong presumption against retroactive legislation. In United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399, 413 (1806), the Court stated that a statute is not to be construed to operate retrospectively absent clear legislative intent. The same rule appears in Shwab v. Doyle, 258 U.S. 529, 534-35 (1922), United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co., 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926), and United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co., 276 U.S. 160, 162-63 (1928). Congress must speak plainly before courts will infer that it meant to disturb settled expectations or attach new consequences to completed conduct. See Hassett v. Welch, 303 U.S. 303, 314 (1938); Greene v. United States, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964).

That presumption controls here. Fernandez-Vargas illegally reentered in 1982, long before § 241(a)(5) took effect in 1997. His relevant conduct was therefore complete years before Congress created the reinstatement regime. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a law is retroactive when it imposes new burdens based on acts already done. See Mathews v. Kidder, Peabody & Co., 161 U.S. 625, 629-30 (1896); Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304, 314-16 (1945). Section 241(a)(5) does exactly that. It converts a prior deportation order and past illegal reentry into an automatic ground for reinstatement, while eliminating any chance to seek relief that might otherwise have been available under prior law.

The government may contend that the statute regulates only Fernandez-Vargas’s continued unlawful presence after 1997. But the Court has rejected such formalism where the legal consequences flow from completed conduct. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 269-70 (1994), the Court explained that a statute has retroactive effect when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” Earlier decisions say the same. See White v. United States, 191 U.S. 545, 554 (1903); Railroad Commission v. Northern Pacific Railway Co., 216 U.S. 579, 584-85 (1910). Here, the event that triggers reinstatement is the pre-1997 illegal reentry. Congress did not merely regulate future conduct; it attached new disabilities to a past act and thereby changed the legal significance of Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 entry.

The unfairness is especially acute because Fernandez-Vargas married a United States citizen and sought to adjust status under the law as it then existed. The Court has long recognized that legislation should not be construed to destroy settled expectations or revive old liabilities absent unmistakable language. See Ogden v. Blackledge, 2 Cranch 272, 275 (1805); Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 2 Gall. 105, 139-40 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.); Welch v. Henry, 305 U.S. 134, 147-48 (1938). Section 241(a)(5), as applied here, would foreclose that adjustment opportunity and leave Fernandez-Vargas subject to reinstatement solely because of conduct completed long before Congress enacted the statute.

Nor can the government avoid retroactivity by describing Fernandez-Vargas’s later residence as a continuing violation. In Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988), the Court reaffirmed that retroactive statutes are disfavored and that authority to reach back must be clear. In Kaiser Aluminum & Chemical Corp. v. Bonjorno, 494 U.S. 827, 837-38 (1990), the Court likewise refused to infer retroactive application without clear congressional direction. The statute here contains no unmistakable statement that Congress meant to subject pre-enactment reentrants to the new, harsher regime.

Reading § 241(a)(5) prospectively is also the better construction because it harmonizes the statute with the traditional rule that doubts are resolved against retroactive interpretation. See Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) at 413; Shwab, 258 U.S. at 534-35; Magnolia Petroleum, 276 U.S. at 162-63; Greene, 376 U.S. at 160. That reading preserves the distinction between aliens who reentered after IIRIRA and those, like Fernandez-Vargas, who had already done so and acted in reliance on prior law.

Accordingly, the Court should hold that § 241(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date. At minimum, applying the statute to Fernandez-Vargas would be impermissibly retroactive under Heth, Shwab, St. Louis, Magnolia Petroleum, Hassett, Greene, Mathews, Chase Securities, Landgraf, White, Northern Pacific, Bowen, and Kaiser.
