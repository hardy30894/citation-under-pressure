**ARGUMENT**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent obtained by” limits the exception to the sum actually procured through the fraud; it does not reach additional statutory multiples or penalties. See *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir. 1991) (punitive damages “did not constitute a debt obtained by fraud”).

The $31,382.50 in excessive rents constitutes the only sum “obtained by” the implied representation that the rents complied with the ordinance. The treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are not compensation for money obtained; they are a statutory sanction imposed after the fact. Because those multiples were not received by the debtor, they fall outside the statutory text.

This reading accords with the Code’s “fresh start” policy, which permits discharge of all debts except those Congress expressly preserved for “the honest but unfortunate debtor.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286–87 (1991) (quoting *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934)). Punitive awards, even when triggered by fraudulent conduct, are not debts “obtained by” fraud and therefore remain dischargeable.

The contrary view adopted below—that any judgment traceable to fraud is nondischargeable—imports language Congress omitted. Had Congress intended to except all liability arising from fraud, it would have used broader phrasing, as it did in neighboring subsections. *See, e.g.*, 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(6). The narrower text of § 523(a)(2)(A) must be respected. *See Kawaauhau v. Geiger*, 523 U.S. 57, 62 (1998) (interpreting parallel exception narrowly to preserve the fresh-start principle).

Accordingly, the $62,765 in treble damages, together with attorney’s fees and costs awarded solely under the state statute, are dischargeable. The judgment below should be reversed to that extent.
