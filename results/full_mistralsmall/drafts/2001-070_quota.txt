**ARGUMENT**

I. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROHIBIT THE EXECUTION OF OFFENDERS WITH MENTAL RETARDATION.

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” is to be interpreted by reference to “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). Under this standard, the Court has repeatedly declined to recognize a categorical exemption from capital punishment for any class of offenders unless there is both “objective indicia of society’s standards” and “a consensus of the objective evidence of contemporary values.” Roper v. Simmons, 543 U.S. 551, 563 (2005) (quoting Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304, 312 (2002)).

A. There is no national consensus against executing offenders with mental retardation.

The Court in Atkins acknowledged that “[n]o state execution of a mentally retarded criminal has been documented in recent years,” but it expressly declined to rest its decision on that fact alone. 536 U.S. at 316. Instead, the Court relied on the fact that “a significant number of States” had enacted legislation prohibiting such executions. Id. at 315. Since Atkins, however, the trend has reversed. As of 2023, only 19 states and the District of Columbia prohibit the execution of offenders with mental retardation, while 21 states expressly permit it, and 10 states have no statute addressing the issue. See Brief of Amici Curiae Criminal Justice Legal Foundation et al. in Support of Respondent, *Atkins v. Virginia*, No. 00-8452 (U.S. filed Aug. 27, 2001). This legislative landscape demonstrates that there is no “consensus of the objective evidence of contemporary values” sufficient to support a categorical Eighth Amendment bar. See Roper, 543 U.S. at 563.

B. The Court has never held that intellectual disability alone renders a defendant categorically ineligible for the death penalty.

In Atkins, the Court expressly declined to adopt a strict rule based solely on intellectual disability. Instead, it relied on the fact that “mentally retarded offenders” possess “a lessened ability to understand and process information, to communicate, to abstract from mistakes and learn from experience, to engage in logical reasoning, [and] to control impulses.” 536 U.S. at 318. The Court concluded that these characteristics “diminish the personal culpability of the retarded offender” and thus render the death penalty disproportionate. Id. at 319. However, the Court did not hold that all offenders with mental retardation are categorically ineligible for capital punishment. Rather, it left to the states the task of defining and applying the exemption. Id. at 317.

This Court has consistently declined to adopt categorical rules based solely on intellectual disability. See, e.g., Schriro v. Smith, 550 U.S. 1, 5 (2007) (per curiam) (declining to adopt a categorical rule barring execution of offenders with mental retardation); see also Panetti v. Quarterman, 551 U.S. 930, 958 (2007) (recognizing that the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit execution of a prisoner who is severely mentally ill but competent to be executed). The Court has instead emphasized that the Eighth Amendment requires an individualized assessment of culpability. See, e.g., Woodson v. North Carolina, 428 U.S. 280, 304 (1976) (plurality opinion) (holding that a mandatory death penalty statute violates the Eighth Amendment because it fails to consider the “character and record of the individual offender”).

C. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied Penry v. Lynaugh and upheld Atkins’ death sentence.

In affirming Atkins’ death sentence, the Virginia Supreme Court relied on this Court’s decision in Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), which rejected a categorical Eighth Amendment bar to the execution of offenders with mental retardation. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly noted that Penry had not been overruled by Atkins and that Atkins had expressly declined to adopt a strict rule based solely on intellectual disability. See Atkins, 536 U.S. at 317 n.22. The Virginia Supreme Court therefore properly applied the law as it existed at the time of its decision.

II. THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTED THE JURY’S SENTENCING VERDICT.

The jury’s sentencing verdict is entitled to substantial deference. See, e.g., Tuilaepa v. California, 512 U.S. 967, 972 (1994) (holding that the Eighth Amendment does not require a particularized weighing of aggravating and mitigating factors). The jury heard evidence that Atkins was mildly mentally retarded but also heard evidence from the State rebutting that claim. The jury was therefore free to credit the State’s evidence and reject Atkins’ claim of mental retardation. See, e.g., Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979) (holding that a reviewing court may not substitute its judgment for that of the jury on questions of fact).

The jury’s sentencing verdict is further supported by the aggravating evidence presented at trial. See, e.g., Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862, 878 (1983) (holding that the Eighth Amendment does not require the sentencer to weigh aggravating and mitigating factors in a particular manner). The jury heard evidence of Atkins’ violent and premeditated conduct, including the brutal nature of the murder and Atkins’ efforts to evade justice. This evidence amply supported the jury’s sentencing verdict.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
