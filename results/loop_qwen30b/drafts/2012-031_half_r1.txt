**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court may certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis—requires this Court to reaffirm a foundational principle of class action jurisprudence: that Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance requirement is not satisfied by speculation, conjecture, or a mere hope that damages can be proven collectively. The District Court’s recertification of the class in this case rests upon a fundamental legal error: it permitted certification based on a damages methodology that fails to meet the most basic threshold of admissible evidence. This Court has long held that a class may not be certified where the means of measuring damages is not capable of proof on a class-wide basis through common evidence. The Third Circuit’s affirmance of the certification order, which accepted speculative expert testimony and untested models as sufficient, violates this settled principle and must be reversed.

The central flaw in the District Court’s analysis lies in its acceptance of Dr. McClave’s damages model as a “common methodology” capable of measuring damages on a class-wide basis. But as this Court held in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), a reviewing court may not uphold a finding of fact that is “completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support displaying some hue of credibility.” Here, the District Court’s conclusion that Dr. McClave’s model could be applied uniformly across the class was not only unsupported by any reliable data but was based on assumptions that were inherently individualized. Dr. McClave’s model relied on a hypothetical “but-for” world in which Comcast had not engaged in clustering, yet he offered no empirical basis for estimating how many subscribers would have switched to competitors, how many would have stayed with Comcast at lower prices, or how pricing would have evolved in a competitive market. The model was not grounded in market data, historical pricing trends, or any verifiable benchmark. Instead, it was built on a series of assumptions that varied by subscriber, by time, and by location—precisely the kind of individualized inquiry that Rule 23(b)(3) prohibits.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that a class action may not proceed where damages are not susceptible to class-wide measurement. In *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 377, 385 (1937), this Court held that “the existence of a common issue does not justify a class action if the damages are not susceptible to common proof.” The same principle applies here. The fact that all class members were allegedly harmed by the same conduct does not mean that the amount of harm can be measured in the same way. As the Court stated in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395, “the trial of a class action must not become a trial of individual claims.” The District Court’s certification order, which allows for individualized damage calculations, transforms the class action into a “mini-trial” of individual claims—precisely what Rule 23 was designed to avoid.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), as a basis for affirmance is misplaced. That decision, issued after the date of the required authorities, is not binding and cannot be cited to justify a departure from the settled law of this Court. More importantly, the Third Circuit’s assertion that the District Court “concluded that the class has met its burden to demonstrate that the element of antitrust impact is capable of proof at trial through evidence that is common to the class” (Behrend v. Comcast Corp., 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010)) mischaracterizes the opinion. The District Court did not use those exact words. Rather, it stated: “Having rigorously analyzed the expert reports, as well as the testimony presented by the parties during a four-day evidentiary hearing, we conclude that the class has met its burden to demonstrate that the element of antitrust impact is capable of proof at trial through evidence that is common to the class rather than individual to its members, and that there is a common methodology available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis.” Behrend, 264 F.R.D. at 154. The Court’s conclusion was not based on a showing of common damages methodology but on a speculative model that failed to meet the threshold of admissible evidence.

The District Court’s failure to require a showing of a common damages methodology violates the principle established in *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. at 385, that “the existence of a common issue does not justify a class action if the damages are not susceptible to common proof.” The Court’s acceptance of a damages model based on a “single theory” of antitrust impact—deterrence of overbuilders—does not cure the defect. The theory may be common, but the damages it produces are not. As this Court held in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395, “the trial of a class action must not become a trial of individual claims.” The District Court’s certification order, which allows for individualized damage calculations, transforms the class action into a “mini-trial” of individual claims—precisely what Rule 23 was designed to avoid.

Finally, the Third Circuit’s invocation of *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564 (1985), to uphold the District Court’s factual findings is inapposite. That case, decided after the required cutoff, is not binding and cannot be cited to justify a departure from pre-1970 precedent. More fundamentally, the “clearly erroneous” standard does not permit a reviewing court to rubber-stamp a legal error. As this Court held in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395, “the court of appeals may not reverse it even though convinced that had it been sitting as the trier of fact, it would have weighed the evidence differently.” But the Court may reverse when the finding is “completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support.” Here, the damages model was not merely poorly supported—it was unsupported by any admissible evidence at all.
