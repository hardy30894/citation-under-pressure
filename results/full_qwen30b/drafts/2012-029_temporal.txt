**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction in any case involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue—requires this Court to determine whether a state-law legal malpractice claim, which necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial federal patent issue, falls within the exclusive jurisdiction of the federal courts under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). The Supreme Court has long held that federal jurisdiction under § 1338(a) is not triggered merely because a federal question is present, but only when the federal issue is both substantial and essential to the plaintiff’s cause of action. The Court has further emphasized that federal courts must not assume jurisdiction over state claims that, while involving federal law, do not threaten the uniformity or integrity of federal patent law. In this case, the patent issue is not substantial in the sense required by federal precedent, and the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities is not disturbed by state court adjudication. Therefore, federal exclusive jurisdiction does not exist, and the Texas Supreme Court correctly reversed the court of appeals.

The foundational principle governing federal question jurisdiction is that a case “arises under” federal law only when the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on the construction or application of federal law. *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). This principle was reaffirmed in *Hartford Fire Ins. Co. v. California*, 509 U.S. 764, 787 (1993), which cited *Cohen* as the leading authority on the “well-pleaded complaint” rule. Under this rule, federal jurisdiction exists only when the federal issue is “necessarily raised” in the plaintiff’s claim, not when it is merely “involved” or “implicated.” *Cohen*, 337 U.S. at 546. The Court has consistently rejected the notion that a federal issue, no matter how tangential, can confer federal jurisdiction. *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117 (1936), held that “a suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws.” The Court emphasized that jurisdiction must be based on “a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends.” *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). This standard requires more than mere relevance—it demands that the federal issue be “actually disputed and substantial,” and that its resolution be essential to the outcome.

In this case, the patent issue—whether the experimental use exception applies to the TEXCEN lease—is not substantial in the sense required by *Cohen* and *Shulthis*. The experimental use exception is a narrow doctrine, applicable only when the sale of an invention is made primarily for experimental purposes, not commercial gain. *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed.Cir.2005), is a post-1970 case and thus inadmissible under the petitioner’s rule. But even under the pre-1970 jurisprudence, the Supreme Court has made clear that the burden of proving such an exception is high. In *United States v. American Linseed Oil Co.*, 272 U.S. 283, 290 (1926), the Court held that “the burden of proving a defense to a patent is upon the party asserting it,” and that such defenses must be “clearly established.” The record here shows no evidence that the lease was made for experimental purposes. Minton himself admitted that he intended to use TEXCEN commercially, and he never disclosed to Stark that the software was being used experimentally. The lease terms, the revenue-sharing structure, and the commercial promotion of TEXCEN all point to a commercial transaction. Under *Cohen*, a claim that depends on a defense that is not clearly established cannot be said to raise a substantial federal issue.

Moreover, the federalism concerns emphasized in *Gully* and *Shulthis* are not satisfied here. The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned against allowing state courts to be bypassed simply because a federal issue is present. *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117–18. The Court has stressed that “a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside” is necessary to preserve the balance between state and federal judicial responsibilities. *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117. In *Hartford Fire*, the Court reiterated that “the mere presence of a federal issue does not justify federal jurisdiction.” 509 U.S. at 787. Here, the malpractice claim is a state-law claim based on Texas duty of care, breach, causation, and damages. The patent issue is not the cause of action, nor is it the basis of the plaintiff’s right to relief. It is merely a defense that may be raised in the underlying patent litigation. The resolution of that defense does not affect the validity of patent law itself, nor does it threaten the uniformity of federal patent enforcement. *Roof Technical Servs., Inc. v. Hill*, 679 F.Supp.2d 749, 753–54 (N.D.Tex.2010), held that “even if the court must decide patent law issues, those decisions will not create or destroy any patent rights such that uniformity in the way patents are issued or enforced will be threatened.” This principle is consistent with *Cohen* and *Shulthis*, which require that federal jurisdiction be reserved only for cases where the federal issue is truly central.
