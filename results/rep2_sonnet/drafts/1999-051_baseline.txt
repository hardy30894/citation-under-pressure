**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Defaulted Ineffective-Assistance Claim May Serve as Cause Because *Murray v. Carrier* Requires Only That the Claim Be Fairly Presented, Not That It Escape Every State Procedural Bar**

This Court held in *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), that "the exhaustion doctrine ... generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default." *Id.* at 489. That rule is rooted in exhaustion, not in a freestanding procedural-default requirement layered atop the cause inquiry itself. The concern animating *Carrier* was that a federal habeas court should not be the first tribunal to adjudicate an ineffective-assistance claim; the state courts must have had the opportunity to correct any constitutional error by appointed counsel. *Id.* Carpenter satisfied that concern precisely: he presented his ineffective-assistance claim to the Ohio Court of Appeals through an application to reopen his direct appeal, and to the Ohio Supreme Court on further appeal. The state courts thus had a full opportunity to consider whether appellate counsel unreasonably omitted the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. That the state courts declined to reach the merits because the application was untimely does not deprive the claim of the "independent" character *Carrier* requires; it remains a claim raised, briefed, and presented to the state judiciary as such.

**II. Layering a Second Cause-and-Prejudice Requirement Onto the Ineffective-Assistance Claim Would Produce an Unworkable Infinite Regress Inconsistent With the Equitable Nature of the Doctrine**

The cause-and-prejudice standard is an equitable one, "grounded in concerns of comity and finality," *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 730 (1991), not a rigid procedural trap designed to foreclose review of constitutional violations whenever a petitioner's lawyer stumbles twice instead of once. If a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause unless the petitioner first shows cause and prejudice for defaulting *that* claim, and if that showing of cause must itself be another attorney-error claim, the petitioner is caught in an infinite regression that this Court has never sanctioned. Nothing in *Carrier*, *Coleman*, or *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72 (1977), suggests that the cause-and-prejudice standard was meant to be self-replicating in this fashion. To the contrary, *Reed v. Ross*, 468 U.S. 1 (1984), recognized that this Court has "focused on whether the prisoner has a legitimate excuse for failing to raise" the claim earlier, *id.* at 13, not on whether every antecedent layer of default has itself been separately excused through an independent showing of cause and prejudice.

**III. Petitioner's Rule Would Undermine the Sixth Amendment Values *Carrier* Sought to Protect**

*Carrier* recognized that "[i]neffective assistance of counsel ... is cause for a procedural default" precisely because the Sixth Amendment guarantees effective representation, and a lawyer's unreasonable failure to raise a claim should not be visited upon the client who had no personal role in the omission. 477 U.S. at 488. That rationale applies with equal force whether or not the state courts entertained the ineffective-assistance claim on procedural or substantive grounds. Carpenter's underlying claim — that appellate counsel was constitutionally deficient for failing to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence supporting a conviction obtained on a guilty plea — goes to the very reliability of the criminal proceeding. Petitioner's proposed rule would allow a State's own procedural timing rule, applied to the ineffective-assistance claim, to erect an absolute bar to review of the underlying constitutional violation, even though the state courts were fully informed of the claim and even though the District Court and the Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit both found that counsel's derelictions caused actual prejudice. Such a result cannot be reconciled with this Court's repeated admonition that procedural default doctrine exists to encourage compliance with state procedures, not to insulate constitutional violations from any judicial review. *See Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 750.

**IV. At a Minimum, the Courts Below Correctly Found Cause and Prejudice on This Record**

Even if this Court concludes that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim must independently satisfy cause and prejudice before it can excuse a separate default, Carpenter has made that showing. The default of his ineffective-assistance claim arose from the Ohio courts' application of a timeliness rule to an application filed by new counsel promptly after Carpenter obtained representation distinct from trial and appellate counsel. The unavailability of effective assistance on direct appeal — precisely the constitutional deficiency alleged — is itself an external impediment satisfying cause under *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488, and the District Court's finding of prejudice, affirmed by the Court of Appeals, reflects that but for counsel's omission, the sufficiency claim would have been raised and considered on direct review.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
