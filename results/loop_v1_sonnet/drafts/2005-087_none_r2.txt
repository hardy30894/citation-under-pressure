**ARGUMENT**

This Court should affirm because Arizona's rule confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry falls well within the "wide latitude" the Constitution affords the States in defining criminal responsibility, and because that rule works no violation of fundamental fairness under the Due Process Clause. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 800–01 (1952).

**I. Due Process Permits States Wide Latitude to Define the Boundaries of the Insanity Defense and to Channel Mental-Disease Evidence Accordingly.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess broad authority to define both the elements of crimes and the defenses available to negate them, including the doctrines governing criminal responsibility for mental illness. In *Leland*, the Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, explaining that "the process of formulating a rule of criminal responsibility... is one in which the legislature of each State has wide latitude." 343 U.S. at 800–01. The Court further held that due process does not compel any State to adopt a particular test of insanity, and that a State's chosen formulation offends the Constitution only if it violates a "fundamental principle of liberty and justice." *Id.* at 798 (quoting *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934)).

If a State may constitutionally place the entire burden of proving insanity on the defendant, it follows that a State may also determine the procedural channel through which evidence of mental disease enters the trial. Arizona's rule—which confines psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry rather than permitting it to defeat the mens rea element directly—is precisely the sort of substantive and procedural line-drawing that *Leland* commits to state judgment.

This conclusion is reinforced by *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), in which the plurality declined to constitutionalize any single doctrine of criminal responsibility, cautioning that the Court should be "reluctant to take this step in the absence of some indication... that a change in that direction is required," and that "traditional common-law concepts of personal accountability" remain properly the province of state law. *Id.* at 535–36. Arizona's channeling rule reflects exactly the kind of traditional, considered legislative judgment that *Powell* leaves undisturbed.

Nor is this approach a modern innovation. The insanity defense as historically understood, dating to *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the accused—not as a discrete evidentiary tool for negating particular elements of the offense on a fact-by-fact basis. Arizona's rule preserves that traditional distinction rather than departing from it.

This Court has, moreover, already sustained the exclusion of analogous categories of psychiatric evidence offered to negate intent. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the Court upheld a trial court's refusal to instruct that evidence of mental deficiency could reduce murder to manslaughter by negating premeditation, recognizing "the practical difficulties of ingrafting the doctrine of partial responsibility onto our jurisprudence" and declining to displace the jurisdiction's chosen approach. *Id.* at 475–76. If the exclusion of such evidence to negate premeditation was constitutional in *Fisher*, Arizona's considerably narrower rule—which still allows the same evidence to be weighed on insanity—is a fortiori permissible.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Leland* and *Powell*, Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a reasonable procedural regulation of evidence. Due process does not require the constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules; it demands only that such rules not violate "some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Snyder*, 291 U.S. at 105. Nor does the Fourteenth Amendment "assure uniformity of judicial procedure"; States remain free "to abolish old and to create new remedies... in the exercise of a wide discretion." *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 110–11 (1908).

This Court applied these principles to an analogous state evidentiary limitation in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), sustaining Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence over the objection that the rule improperly influenced the jury's assessment of guilt. The Court explained that "the process of adjudication... normally requires the drawing of lines," and that state evidentiary rules deserve deference absent a showing that they render the trial fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 562–64. Arizona's rule is, if anything, less restrictive than the one sustained in *Spencer*: it does not exclude Clark's psychiatric evidence altogether but directs it to the historically established channel for such evidence—the insanity defense.

The touchstone remains whether "the absence of that fair play" so infects the trial as to work a denial of due process. *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941). Here, the trial court admitted the full range of Clark's expert psychiatric testimony and considered it in adjudicating his insanity claim. Nothing in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), addressing the burden of proving insanity under federal law, suggests that the Constitution requires evidence relevant to establishing one defense to be automatically admissible, unconstrained, for the distinct purpose of negating the prosecution's proof of intent.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's psychiatric evidence was fully heard and weighed by the factfinder on the question the law assigns it to answer: whether he was insane at the time of the offense. Arizona simply declined to permit that same evidence, freed from the burdens attending the insanity defense, to also defeat the mens rea element the State must independently prove. That channeling judgment falls squarely within the "wide latitude" this Court has long afforded the States in structuring criminal responsibility, *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 800, and offends no principle "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people" as to be fundamental, *Snyder*, 291 U.S. at 105.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
