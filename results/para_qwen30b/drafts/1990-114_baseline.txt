**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE EXEMPT AS “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL”

The central issue before this Court is whether Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for state court judges violates the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. § 621–634. The petitioners, appointed state judges under Missouri’s Non-Partisan Court Plan, argue that they are “employees” entitled to ADEA protection and that their forced retirement at age seventy constitutes unlawful age discrimination. This argument fails because Congress explicitly excluded from the ADEA’s coverage “any appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The petitioners are such appointees.

This Court has long recognized that the judiciary is a co-equal branch of government with distinct institutional responsibilities, including the power to interpret law, resolve disputes, and shape legal doctrine. *Marbury v. Madison*, 1 Cranch 137, 177 (1803) (“It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is”). In performing these duties, judges necessarily exercise discretion, judgment, and policy-making authority—hallmarks of the “policymaking level” exception. As the First Circuit observed in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), “policymaking” is “indisputably a part of the function of judging” because judicial decisions often fill “interstices of authority” in constitutions, statutes, and precedent. Indeed, the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that judicial rulings create binding legal precedent and influence public policy across society. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) (“[T]he Constitution requires that all persons be treated equally under the law”); *Roe v. Wade*, 410 U.S. 113, 153 (1973) (invalidating state abortion laws based on constitutional privacy principles).

The petitioners contend that judges merely “apply settled law,” but this view ignores the reality of judicial decisionmaking. Even in routine cases, judges must interpret ambiguous statutes, reconcile conflicting precedents, and determine the scope of constitutional rights—all inherently policymaking functions. As the Eighth Circuit correctly noted, “the reality of judging is that it is not always—or perhaps not even usually—merely a matter of mechanically applying settled, unambiguous principles of law.” *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 604 (8th Cir. 1990). Moreover, Missouri judges exercise significant supervisory and administrative authority over the state court system. Under Article V, Section 4 of the Missouri Constitution, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, chief judges of appellate divisions, and presiding judges of circuit courts have broad powers to adopt rules of practice, supervise court operations, and resolve internal disputes among judges. *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985); *In re Rules of Circuit Court of Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 757 S.W.2d 590 (Mo. 1988). These responsibilities are not clerical or ministerial; they are quintessential policymaking roles.

The petitioners’ argument—that the “policymaking level” exception applies only to executive and legislative appointees—is implausible and contradicted by the statute’s structure. If Congress intended to exclude all judicial appointees from the exemption, then no judge—appointed or elected—would fall within any ADEA exception. Yet Congress explicitly exempted “elected” officials from the ADEA’s reach, see 29 U.S.C. § 630(f), suggesting that it understood the judiciary as a distinct entity subject to different treatment. To read the “policymaking level” exception narrowly to exclude judges would render the exemption meaningless in many states where judges are appointed. Such a reading defies the principle that exemptions should not be interpreted in ways that produce absurd or unintended results. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (“Where two constructions are possible, one leading to absurdity, the other to reasonableness, the latter must prevail”).

Furthermore, the legislative history cited by the petitioners does not support their position. The Joint Explanatory Statement notes that Congress intended to exempt “cabinet officers” and “persons with comparable responsibilities at the local level,” but this language refers to the *type* of responsibility—not the branch of government. It does not exclude judges who perform similar functions. Indeed, the EEOC itself has acknowledged that appointed judges are not covered by the ADEA when they serve in policymaking capacities. See *EEOC Opinion Letter to Rep. Claude Pepper* (Apr. 7, 1987), reprinted in EEOC Compliance Manual (BNA) at N:1001, n.2 (“a state judge who is appointed by the governor or legislature but must appear on a ballot before the general electorate ... would be excepted from the term ‘employee’ under the ADEA as a ‘person elected to public office’”). This consistent administrative interpretation reinforces the conclusion that judges are not “employees” under the ADEA.

Thus, the ADEA does not apply to appointed Missouri judges. Their exclusion is not only statutory but also constitutionally sound, given the unique role of the judiciary in our federal system.
