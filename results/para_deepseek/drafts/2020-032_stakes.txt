## ARGUMENT

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. This case is moot because Georgia Gwinnett College (“GGC”) repealed the challenged policies during litigation, and petitioners’ claim for nominal damages cannot revive an otherwise moot constitutional challenge.

### I. The Repeal of the Challenged Policies Renders This Case Moot

A case becomes moot “when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013) (quoting *Murphy v. Hunt*, 455 U.S. 478, 481 (1982) (per curiam)). The voluntary cessation of challenged conduct moots a case unless there is a “reasonable expectation” that the conduct will recur. *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000). Here, GGC unambiguously terminated its “Freedom of Expression Policy” and “Student Code of Conduct” provisions, replacing them with policies that permit expressive activity anywhere on campus without a permit. The district court found “no reasonable basis to expect that [GGC] will return to” the prior policies. Petitioners do not challenge that finding on appeal. Accordingly, their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are unquestionably moot. *See City of Erie v. Pap’s A.M.*, 529 U.S. 277, 287 (2000) (repeal of ordinance moots challenge to its constitutionality).

### II. Nominal Damages Cannot Save an Otherwise Moot Constitutional Challenge

Petitioners argue that their request for nominal damages preserves a live controversy. But this Court has long held that “a plaintiff cannot maintain a declaratory or injunctive action unless he or she can demonstrate a real and immediate threat of future injury.” *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 109 (1998). Nominal damages are a “technical” remedy that “serve[] only to vindicate the deprivation of a right.” *Flanigan’s Enters., Inc. of Ga. v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1263 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc). They do not create a continuing case or controversy when the challenged policy is no longer in effect and no ongoing injury exists. *See Lewis v. Continental Bank Corp.*, 494 U.S. 472, 480 (1990) (mootness turns on whether “the parties continue to have a ‘personal stake in the outcome of the lawsuit’” (quoting *Los Angeles v. Lyons*, 461 U.S. 95, 101 (1983))).

This Court has never held that a bare claim for nominal damages, unaccompanied by any continuing violation or live dispute, can defeat mootness. To the contrary, in *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court allowed a nominal-damages claim to proceed only because the challenged action—exclusion from Congress—was a completed past wrong that could not be undone by injunctive relief. *Id.* at 496–500. Here, by contrast, the challenged policies were repealed before any final judgment, and petitioners seek only to litigate the constitutionality of a defunct policy. That is precisely the kind of “abstract” dispute that Article III forbids. *See Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona*, 520 U.S. 43, 67 (1997) (mootness applies when “the controversy is no longer ‘live’”).

The court of appeals correctly applied *Flanigan’s*, which held that “a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.” 868 F.3d at 1253. That holding is consistent with this Court’s precedent. A contrary rule would allow plaintiffs to resurrect any moot challenge simply by tacking on a request for nominal damages, undermining the case-or-controversy requirement.

### III. Petitioners Failed to Plead Compensatory Damages

Petitioners contend that their complaint sought compensatory damages, but the district court correctly rejected that argument. The complaint’s prayer for relief requested only “nominal damages.” The references to “monetary damages” and “damages in an amount to be determined by the evidence” are ambiguous at best. Critically, petitioners alleged no “actual injury” such as monetary loss, physical pain, or emotional distress, which is required for compensatory damages under § 1983. *See Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000). The complaint mentions “injury” only twice and never specifies any concrete harm. Thus, the only damages sought were nominal.

### IV. The District Court Properly Denied Leave to Amend

Petitioners sought leave to amend only in a response to a motion to dismiss, not by a proper motion. This Court has held that “[w]here a request for leave to file an amended complaint simply is imbedded within an opposition memorandum, the issue has not been raised properly.” *Rosenberg v. Gould*, 554 F.3d 962, 967 (11th Cir. 2009). The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying such an informal request. Moreover, petitioners never specified what additional factual allegations they would add to support compensatory damages. *See Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC*, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018) (plaintiff must “set forth the substance of the proposed amendment”).

### Conclusion

Because the challenged policies were repealed and petitioners seek only nominal damages for a past violation, this case is moot. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
