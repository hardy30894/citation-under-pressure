The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the revision of the challenged policies during litigation rendered this action moot. When a defendant ceases the conduct giving rise to a request for declaratory or injunctive relief, no live controversy remains. Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895) (“The duty of this court, as of every other judicial tribunal, is to decide actual controversies by a judgment which can be carried into effect, and not to give opinions upon moot questions or abstract propositions.”). The same principle applies once the policies at issue have been superseded and the plaintiffs have graduated or otherwise lack any continuing stake in their enforcement. United States v. Alaska S.S. Co., 253 U.S. 113, 116 (1920).

A bare request for nominal damages does not alter the analysis. The Constitution limits federal courts to “cases” and “controversies,” a limitation that excludes adjudication of abstract questions divorced from any concrete injury. Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth, 300 U.S. 227, 240-241 (1937) (“A justiciable controversy is thus distinguished from a difference or dispute of a hypothetical or abstract character.”). Where, as here, the complaint alleges no actual loss, the claim for nominal damages seeks only a declaration that past conduct violated the Constitution—an advisory opinion the federal courts have no power to render. Chicago & Grand Trunk Ry. Co. v. Wellman, 143 U.S. 339, 345 (1892).

The district court therefore properly dismissed the action. Because the policies have been revised and no compensable injury was ever pleaded, nothing remains for judicial resolution.
