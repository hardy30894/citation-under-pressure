ARGUMENT

The Civil Service Reform Act channels judicial review of Merit Systems Protection Board decisions to district court whenever an employee alleges that a prohibited basis for the action was discrimination. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). That command is not defeated when the Board concludes the action lies outside its jurisdiction.

Federal-question jurisdiction extends to any case in which the plaintiff asserts a right under federal law, even if the claim ultimately fails. Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678, 681-682 (1946). The same principle governs review of administrative orders that rest on jurisdictional determinations. Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 54-55 (1932). Once a complaint presents a substantial federal question, the district court possesses authority to decide it. Id. at 64.

Discrimination claims arising under federal civil-rights statutes are quintessential federal questions. Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954). When such a claim is joined with a challenge to an adverse employment action, the entire controversy falls within the district court's original jurisdiction. See NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 31 (1937) (holding that constitutional and statutory claims arising from the same transaction are heard together).

Administrative agencies possess only the powers conferred by statute. FCC v. Pottsville Broadcasting Co., 309 U.S. 134, 142 (1940). A Board determination that it lacks jurisdiction therefore resolves a threshold legal question, not the merits of the discrimination allegation. That legal question remains subject to judicial correction in the forum Congress designated for mixed cases. Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro, 349 U.S. 48, 51 (1955).

The requirement of substantial-evidence review does not alter the forum. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). District courts routinely apply that standard to administrative records. Heikkila v. Barber, 345 U.S. 229, 234 (1953). Uniformity of interpretation is achieved by appellate review, not by routing every jurisdictional dismissal to a single specialized court. St. Joseph Stock Yards Co. v. United States, 298 U.S. 38, 51 (1936).

Because Perry's complaint alleged that discrimination rendered his retirement and suspension involuntary, the case is a mixed case within the meaning of § 7702(a)(1). Review therefore lies in district court.
