## Argument

### I. No inmate has a protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings themselves.

The Due Process Clause is not triggered by clemency proceedings simply because a prisoner hopes for mercy. In **Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981)**, the Court held that a prisoner seeking commutation had no protected liberty interest in the Board’s discretionary clemency decision. The Court explained that “pardon and commutation decisions” have “not traditionally been the business of courts” and are “rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” **Id. at 464**. That principle controls here.

Ohio’s clemency process is likewise discretionary. As in **Dumschat**, the State has created a mechanism for mercy, not an entitlement to it. A procedure that leaves the ultimate decision to executive grace does not create a constitutionally protected liberty interest. See **Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 464–67**. Nor does the fact that the prisoner faces a death sentence change the analysis. The Court has repeatedly distinguished between ordinary procedural protections in the criminal process and later executive clemency, which remains a matter of grace. See **Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard, 523 U.S. 272, 280–85 (1998)** (plurality opinion); **id. at 289–90 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment)**.

Woodard’s reliance on **Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12 (1956)** and **Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985)** is misplaced. Those cases involved appellate review that was an integral part of the adjudicatory process. **Evitts, 469 U.S. at 393–94**. Clemency is different. It comes after conviction and sentence are final; it is not a stage in adjudicating guilt or innocence. The Court in **Dumschat** rejected the notion that discretionary clemency becomes constitutionally protected merely because it may affect the duration of confinement. **452 U.S. at 464–65**. The same reasoning defeats any claim that Ohio’s clemency procedures themselves create a protected life or liberty interest.

### II. Offering a voluntary clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.

The Fifth Amendment prohibits compulsion. It does not forbid a State from offering a prisoner the chance to speak voluntarily in an effort to obtain clemency. The Ohio procedure at issue does not require Woodard to be interviewed, does not require him to answer questions, and does not condition clemency on waiving the privilege against self-incrimination. On its face, the procedure provides only the option of a voluntary interview. A voluntary choice does not amount to unconstitutional compulsion. See **Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308, 316–20 (1976)**.

The Court’s decision in **Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420 (1984)** confirms that the privilege is violated only by compelled testimonial self-incrimination. A person who voluntarily speaks without invoking the privilege is not coerced simply because silence may have practical consequences. **Id. at 434–39**. Likewise, in **Baxter**, the Court held that adverse consequences flowing from a choice to remain silent did not themselves constitute unconstitutional compulsion. **425 U.S. at 318–20**. So too here: a prisoner may choose to participate in a clemency interview or decline to do so. The Constitution does not transform that choice into compulsion.

Justice O’Connor’s controlling concurrence in **Woodard** is especially clear on this point. She concluded that Ohio’s process did not violate the Fifth Amendment because it did not force the inmate to speak and did not compel waiver of the privilege. **Woodard, 523 U.S. at 289–90 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment)**. At most, the procedure offered a voluntary opportunity to plead for mercy, which is not the kind of coercion the Fifth Amendment forbids.

### III. The court of appeals erred in constitutionalizing clemency.

The court below effectively treated clemency as if it were another round of criminal adjudication. That conflicts with **Dumschat** and the Court’s later decision in **Woodard** itself. Executive clemency is a matter of grace, not of right. **Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 464–67**. While the Due Process Clause may forbid wholly arbitrary state action in some extreme circumstances, it does not require judicial management of clemency procedures or convert a voluntary interview into a constitutional violation. **Woodard, 523 U.S. at 289–90 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment)**.

Because Woodard has no protected liberty interest in the clemency process itself, and because the interview option does not compel self-incrimination, the judgment below should be reversed.
