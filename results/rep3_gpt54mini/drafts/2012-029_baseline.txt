**ARGUMENT**

Federal courts do **not** have exclusive jurisdiction over every case that merely contains a patent issue. Congress gave the federal courts exclusive jurisdiction only over civil actions “arising under” the patent laws. 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected an expansive reading of that phrase, holding that a case arises under federal patent law only when “a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). Even then, jurisdiction exists only if the federal issue is “actually disputed and substantial” and can be heard “without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).

Minton’s malpractice suit does not satisfy that standard. His claim is a state-law tort action governed by Texas malpractice principles, not a federal patent cause of action. To prevail, he must prove duty, breach, causation, and damages under Texas law. *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., Inc.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). Although a “case within a case” may require examination of the underlying patent dispute, that embedded issue does not convert the entire suit into one arising under patent law. *Schaeffer v. O’Brien*, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied). The Supreme Court has long warned that a suit does not arise under federal law merely because it “really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting” federal law. *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912).

The patent question here is not substantial in the jurisdictional sense. In *Grable*, the Court emphasized that the relevant inquiry is not whether a federal issue is present, but whether it is substantial to the federal system as a whole. 545 U.S. at 313-14. That principle reflects the Court’s concern that federal jurisdiction should not be opened by “the mere presence of a federal issue in a state cause of action.” *Merrell Dow Pharms. Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 813 (1986). Minton’s malpractice suit asks only whether his former lawyers negligently failed to raise an available defense in a past patent case. That inquiry does not determine the validity of any patent, alter the scope of patent law for future cases, or require the federal courts to speak with one voice on a recurring issue of patent doctrine. See *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 315.

Nor would exercising exclusive federal jurisdiction respect the federal-state balance. Legal malpractice is a “special concern” of the States, which “have a special responsibility for maintaining standards among members of the licensed professions.” *Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. v. Manning*, 578 U.S. 374, 386 (2016) (describing the limits of federal jurisdiction); see also *Gunn v. Minton*, 568 U.S. 251, 264 (2013). A contrary rule would federalize a broad class of state malpractice, contract, fiduciary-duty, and negligence actions whenever they happen to require a court to consult patent law in resolving causation or damages. That would markedly expand federal jurisdiction beyond anything Congress authorized in § 1338(a). See *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808-10.

This Court should reject any rule that makes federal jurisdiction exclusive whenever patent law appears anywhere in the case. The Supreme Court has held that patent-related state claims belong in state court unless they necessarily raise a disputed and substantial federal issue whose federal adjudication is consistent with the congressional division of labor between state and federal courts. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314; *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808-09. Minton’s malpractice claim does not meet that test. The Texas courts therefore had subject-matter jurisdiction. The judgment dismissing the case should be reversed.
