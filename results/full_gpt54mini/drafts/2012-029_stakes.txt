Federal courts do **not** have exclusive jurisdiction over every case that merely touches patent law. Under the Supreme Court’s controlling cases, a state-law claim falls within § 1338 only when patent law creates the cause of action or when the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of patent law. Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988). Even then, the embedded patent issue must be “actually disputed and substantial,” and federal jurisdiction must not disturb the congressionally approved balance between state and federal courts. Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).

That standard is not met here. Minton’s claim is a Texas legal-malpractice action. The elements are those of state professional-negligence law: duty, breach, causation, and damages. Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., Inc., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). To prove causation, Minton must show a “case within a case.” Schaeffer v. O’Brien, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied). Although patent law may supply one link in that chain, the claim remains one for legal malpractice, not one “arising under” the patent laws. Christianson, 486 U.S. at 808–09.

This Court has repeatedly rejected efforts to convert ordinary state causes of action into federal patent cases merely because patent issues appear in the background. In Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian, the Court explained that federal jurisdiction does not exist simply because a federal question is “lurking in the background”; rather, the federal issue must be part of the plaintiff’s own statement of his claim. 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936). And in Shulthis v. McDougal, the Court held that a suit does not arise under federal law unless it “really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect” of federal law. 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Minton’s malpractice claim fails that test because the real dispute is whether his lawyers negligently handled the earlier litigation under Texas professional standards.

Grable confirms that federal jurisdiction is reserved for a “special and small category” of state claims. Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh, 547 U.S. 677, 699 (2006). In Grable, the federal issue was a pure question of the meaning of a federal tax notice statute, actually disputed, and important to the federal government’s own enforcement scheme. 545 U.S. at 314–15. By contrast, patent law here is only evidence of causation in a malpractice suit. The Court in Gunn v. Minton later made this precise point, holding that although the patent issue was necessarily raised and actually disputed, it was not substantial in the relevant sense because its resolution would be important only to the parties, not to the federal system as a whole. 568 U.S. 251, 260–64 (2013). The Court further held that allowing state courts to resolve such embedded patent questions would not upset the federal-state balance because state courts adjudicate these issues only “in a hypothetical sense” and their decisions have no effect on future patent validity. Id. at 263–64.

That reasoning defeats exclusive federal jurisdiction. A patent issue is not “substantial” merely because it may require a court to apply patent law correctly in a single malpractice case. Empire Healthchoice, 547 U.S. at 700–01. Nor does the need for uniformity in patent law create exclusivity where Congress has not said so. Gunn, 568 U.S. at 264. Section 1338 gives federal courts exclusive jurisdiction over patent cases, but not over every state case that happens to reference a patent rule. Christianson, 486 U.S. at 808–09.

Indeed, federal exclusivity here would sweep too broadly. If every malpractice claim that required proving a lost patent defense belonged only in federal court, then state courts would be stripped of ordinary authority over professional negligence whenever the underlying representation involved patent matters. That result would contradict Grable’s warning that federal jurisdiction must not disturb the balance of judicial responsibilities. 545 U.S. at 314. It would also expand § 1338 beyond the Court’s careful limitation in Christianson. 486 U.S. at 808–09.

Because Minton’s claim arises under state law, and because the embedded patent issue is not substantial in the federal-jurisdiction sense, federal courts lack exclusive jurisdiction. The judgment should be reversed. Gunn v. Minton, 568 U.S. 251 (2013).
