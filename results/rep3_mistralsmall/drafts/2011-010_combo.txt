**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF.

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) provides that the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) applies “with respect to disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The plain text of this provision requires that the injury occur “on the outer Continental Shelf.” The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that coverage extends to injuries occurring on land where there is a “substantial nexus” to shelf operations—is unsupported by the statutory text and conflicts with this Court’s longstanding interpretation of similar jurisdictional statutes.

This Court has repeatedly held that statutory grants of admiralty jurisdiction are limited to injuries occurring on navigable waters. In *The Plymouth*, 3 Wall. 20, 18 L.Ed. 125 (1865), the Court construed the predecessor to the modern LHWCA, which extended coverage to injuries “upon the navigable waters of the United States.” *Id.* at 21. The Court held that the statute did not cover injuries occurring on land, even if the employee was engaged in maritime employment. *Id.* at 22-23. Similarly, in *Grant Smith Porter Ship Co. v. Rohde*, 257 U.S. 469, 42 S.Ct. 157, 66 L.Ed. 321 (1922), the Court reaffirmed that the LHWCA’s coverage is limited to injuries occurring on navigable waters. *Id.* at 476-77. These cases establish that Congress intended jurisdictional statutes to apply only to injuries occurring in the specified location—here, the outer Continental Shelf.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test also conflicts with *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 105 S.Ct. 1498, 84 L.Ed.2d 402 (1985), which held that OCSLA coverage is limited to injuries occurring on the shelf or its adjacent waters. *Id.* at 424-25. The Court reasoned that the OCSLA’s incorporation of the LHWCA was expressly limited to “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” *Id.* (quoting 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b)). The Court further noted that the OCSLA’s coverage is “geographically limited” and does not extend to injuries occurring in state waters. *Id.* at 424. The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of coverage to land-based injuries is inconsistent with this clear geographic limitation.

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), which adopted a situs-of-injury requirement for OCSLA claims, is more consistent with the statutory text and this Court’s precedent. *Id.* at 362. The Fifth Circuit held that an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on the outer Continental Shelf or its adjacent waters, in addition to satisfying the “but for” test. *Id.* This interpretation ensures that OCSLA coverage is limited to the shelf itself, as Congress intended.

Finally, the Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. The Third Circuit’s “but for” test conflicts with the plain text of § 1333(b) and this Court’s precedent. *Id.* at 810-11. The Third Circuit’s approach would extend OCSLA coverage to virtually any injury that could be remotely connected to shelf operations, rendering the statutory text meaningless.

II. THE LHWCA DOES NOT COVER INJURIES OCCURRING ON LAND, EVEN IF THE EMPLOYEE IS ENGAGED IN MARITIME EMPLOYMENT.

The LHWCA extends coverage to employees injured “upon the navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). This Court has consistently held that the LHWCA’s coverage is limited to injuries occurring on navigable waters. In *The Plymouth*, 3 Wall. at 22-23, the Court held that the predecessor to the LHWCA did not cover injuries occurring on land, even if the employee was engaged in maritime employment. Similarly, in *Grant Smith*, 257 U.S. at 476-77, the Court reaffirmed that the LHWCA’s coverage is limited to injuries occurring on navigable waters.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Valladolid* conflicts with this precedent. The court held that La Conchita, a land-based facility, was not a maritime situs under the LHWCA. *Valladolid*, 6933 at 6959-61. The court correctly applied the “functional relationship” test from *Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137 (9th Cir. 1978), which requires that the facility be “used as an integral part of longshoring operations.” *Id.* at 141. The court found that La Conchita was not an “adjoining area” within the meaning of § 903(a) because it was three miles from the nearest pier and not adjacent to any maritime facilities. *Valladolid*, 6933 at 6959-61.

This Court’s decision in *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69, 100 S.Ct. 328, 62 L.Ed.2d 225 (1979), does not alter this analysis. *Pfeiffer* held that warehouse employees who handled cargo within a port were engaged in maritime employment. *Id.* at 83. However, the Court did not address the situs requirement because the injuries occurred on a dock and a pier, which are indisputably maritime situs. *Id.* at 71. The Ninth Circuit correctly distinguished *Pfeiffer* in *Valladolid*, noting that the handling of scrap metal at La Conchita did not involve “moving cargo directly from ship to land transportation.” *Valladolid*, 6933 at 6960.

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test conflicts with the statutory text and this Court’s precedent. Similarly, the LHWCA does not cover injuries occurring on land, even if the employee is engaged in maritime employment. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that La Conchita was not a maritime situs under the LHWCA. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision and reinstate the Benefits Review Board’s denial of benefits.
