**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE TAX COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT MERUELO FAILED TO ESTABLISH A BONA FIDE INDEBTEDNESS RUNNING DIRECTLY TO HIM.**

A. **The Tax Court Misapplied the "Bona Fide Indebtedness" Standard Under § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i).**

To claim a basis increase under § 1366(d)(1), a shareholder must demonstrate that the corporation is indebted to *him* and that the indebtedness is "bona fide." Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The Tax Court held that Meruelo failed to prove a direct loan from himself to Merco, instead finding that the funds flowed through intermediaries. This was error. The Eleventh Circuit has long held that "the form of the transaction is not controlling" where the substance demonstrates a bona fide debtor-creditor relationship. *Perlman v. Comm'r*, 44 T.C. 150, 156 (1965), *aff'd*, 376 F.2d 45 (3d Cir. 1967). Here, the record shows that Meruelo personally borrowed funds and directed their transfer to Merco through affiliates. The substance of the transaction—Meruelo’s personal liability on the loan and his direct economic outlay—satisfies the regulatory standard.

B. **Meruelo’s Economic Outlay Was Real and Direct.**

The Tax Court also erred in concluding that Meruelo failed to make an "actual economic outlay." The regulation requires only that the shareholder suffer an "out-of-pocket" loss. *See* Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). In *Oren v. Comm'r*, 357 F.3d 897 (8th Cir. 2004), the Eighth Circuit upheld a shareholder’s basis increase where funds were routed through a partnership, reasoning that the shareholder’s personal liability and direct contribution satisfied the economic-outlay requirement. *Id.* at 901. Similarly, in *Selfe v. United States*, 778 F.2d 769 (11th Cir. 1985), the Eleventh Circuit recognized that a shareholder’s personal guarantee of a corporate loan, coupled with an economic outlay, suffices to establish basis. *Id.* at 772. Meruelo’s personal borrowing and directed transfers to Merco constitute an unmistakable economic outlay, just as in *Oren* and *Selfe*.

C. **The Tax Court Ignored the Presumption of Bona Fide Indebtedness.**

Courts presume that corporate indebtedness to shareholders is bona fide unless the Commissioner shows it is a sham. *See, e.g., Litton Bus. Sys., Inc. v. Comm'r*, 61 T.C. 367, 377 (1973), *aff'd*, 501 F.2d 1096 (9th Cir. 1974). The Tax Court failed to apply this presumption here. Instead, it treated the intermediated transfers as fatal to Meruelo’s claim. But the Eleventh Circuit has recognized that "a shareholder’s basis may be increased by loans made by the shareholder to the corporation, even if the funds are routed through intermediaries." *See Selfe*, 778 F.2d at 772. The Commissioner presented no evidence that the transactions were shams; thus, the presumption should have applied in Meruelo’s favor.

II. **THE TAX COURT’S RULING CONTRADICTS THE PASS-THROUGH PRINCIPLE OF SUBCHAPTER S.**

A. **Subchapter S’s Pass-Through Regime Requires a Flexible, Substance-Over-Form Approach.**

Subchapter S was designed to avoid double taxation by passing corporate items directly to shareholders. *Bufferd v. Comm'r*, 506 U.S. 523, 525 (1993). The Tax Court’s rigid insistence on a direct loan ignores this purpose. In *Trevino v. Comm'r*, 91 T.C. 646 (1988), the Tax Court allowed a shareholder to increase basis where funds were advanced through a partnership, emphasizing that "the economic reality of the transaction" controls. *Id.* at 652. Similarly, in *Hargis v. Comm'r*, 70 T.C. 113 (1978), the court permitted basis increases for loans routed through intermediaries, rejecting a formalistic approach. The Tax Court’s ruling here departs from these precedents and undermines Subchapter S’s pass-through mechanics.

B. **The Tax Court’s Formalism Defeats the Purpose of § 1366(d)(1).**

Section 1366(d)(1) allows shareholders to deduct losses to the extent of their basis, which includes indebtedness *to them*. The Tax Court’s holding that Meruelo’s basis cannot include the $9 million because the debt ran to intermediaries rather than him directly frustrates this statutory purpose. As the Supreme Court has cautioned, "taxpayers are entitled to arrange their affairs to minimize taxes," so long as the transactions have economic substance. *Gregory v. Helvering*, 293 U.S. 465, 469 (1935). The Tax Court’s ruling penalizes Meruelo for structuring transactions in a manner consistent with Subchapter S’s pass-through design.

III. **THE TAX COURT ERRED IN DISALLOWING BASIS FOR THE INTERMEDIATE TRANSFERS.**

A. **The Intermediated Transfers Were Direct Economic Outlays by Meruelo.**

The Tax Court’s refusal to recognize the $9 million as basis because the debt ran to affiliates rather than Meruelo directly conflicts with *Oren*, 357 F.3d at 901, and *Selfe*, 778 F.2d at 772. In both cases, courts upheld basis increases where shareholders routed funds through intermediaries but bore the economic risk. Here, Meruelo personally borrowed the funds and directed their use for Merco’s benefit. The substance of the transaction—his personal liability and out-of-pocket loss—satisfies the regulatory standard.

B. **The Tax Court’s Ruling Ignores the "Net Worth" Theory of Basis.**

Courts have long recognized that a shareholder’s basis may include funds advanced by intermediaries if the shareholder bears the economic risk. *See, e.g., Perry v. Comm'r*, 54 T.C. 1293 (1970), *aff'd*, 470 F.2d 103 (1st Cir. 1972). The Tax Court’s formalistic approach here disregards this principle. Meruelo’s personal guarantee of the loan and his economic outlay through the intermediaries should suffice to establish basis under *Perry* and its progeny.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Tax Court erred in disallowing Meruelo’s claimed basis increase. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s opinion.
