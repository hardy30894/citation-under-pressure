**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NLRB’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS IRRATIONAL AND CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.

The National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) does not prohibit employers from testing employee sentiment regarding union representation. Indeed, Congress recognized that employers may have a legitimate interest in determining whether an incumbent union continues to enjoy majority support—particularly in the context of a successor employer assuming operations from a predecessor. But the NLRB’s rigid “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard, which requires an employer to possess objective evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition *before* conducting a poll, renders the poll itself a legally meaningless exercise. This standard is not only irrational but also inconsistent with the NLRA’s purpose of promoting stable collective bargaining relationships.

The Board’s rule creates a paradox: an employer may not conduct a poll unless it already has sufficient grounds to withdraw recognition, yet the sole purpose of the poll is to *determine* whether such grounds exist. As the Board itself acknowledged in *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989), polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling,” and thus should be used sparingly. But if the employer must already have “objective indications” of a loss of majority support to justify polling, then the poll serves no purpose beyond confirming a conclusion already reached. This undermines the very rationale for allowing polling in the first place—namely, to provide employers with “more certain, precise information” about employee sentiment than their own subjective doubts can supply. Id. at 1063.

The courts of appeals that rejected the Board’s standard did so not out of judicial overreach, but because the Board’s rule rendered polling “useless” and “anomalous.” See *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984). If an employer cannot poll without already having a basis to withdraw recognition, then the poll becomes a mere formality—no different from simply refusing to bargain. The Board’s insistence on the same evidentiary threshold for polling as for withdrawal of recognition thus collapses the distinction between the two actions, defeating the purpose of the poll as a tool for *testing* union support.

Moreover, the Board’s standard is inconsistent with the NLRA’s framework. Section 8(a)(5) requires employers to bargain with the union if it is the representative of a majority of employees. But the Act does not require employers to accept a union’s claim of majority status without question. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41, 107 S.Ct. 2225, 2232–35, 96 L.Ed.2d 22 (1987). The presumption of continuing majority status is rebuttable, and the burden of rebuttal lies with the employer. Yet the Board’s standard effectively shifts the burden back to the employer by requiring it to prove a loss of majority support *before* it can even test that claim. This is not administrative efficiency—it is a substantive bar to lawful employer action.

The Board’s own reasoning in *Texas Petrochemicals* confirms the irrationality of its position. It stated that polling is “potentially disruptive,” yet allows it only when the employer already has a basis to withdraw recognition—meaning the disruption has already occurred. Id. at 1061. If the employer has already decided to withdraw recognition, why conduct a poll at all? The only logical answer is that the poll is meant to *avoid* the risk of bargaining with a minority union, which would violate § 8(a)(2). But the Board’s standard makes that risk unavoidable: if the employer lacks sufficient evidence to justify withdrawal, it cannot poll, and thus cannot avoid the risk. The Board’s rule thus forces employers into a Hobson’s choice: either risk an unfair labor practice by bargaining with a minority union, or violate the Act by refusing to bargain without a proper basis.

The Board’s standard is not only irrational—it is also inconsistent with the statutory scheme. The NLRA permits employers to conduct Board-supervised elections (RM elections) to test union support, and the Board has long held that such elections are the preferred method. But the Board applies the *same* evidentiary standard for polling as for RM elections, despite the fact that RM elections are conducted under strict procedural safeguards. This creates an absurd result: an employer may not conduct a poll without sufficient evidence to justify an RM election, but may conduct an RM election only if it has such evidence. The Board’s rule thus makes polling *less* accessible than a formal election, which is contrary to the Act’s goal of facilitating employee choice.

The Board’s standard is not merely flawed—it is arbitrary and capricious. It fails to account for the practical realities of successorship and the legitimate need for employers to assess employee sentiment in the wake of a change in ownership. The Board’s insistence on a “reasonable doubt” standard that requires objective evidence of a loss of majority support *before* polling is not a reasonable interpretation of the NLRA. It is a policy choice disguised as legal doctrine.

II. THE NLRB’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED “REASONABLE DOUBT” IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked “reasonable doubt” about the union’s majority status rests on a selective and erroneous interpretation of the record. The ALJ and the Board discounted key statements from employees who had expressed clear opposition to the union, while ignoring the cumulative weight of evidence suggesting a significant loss of support.

First, the Board erred in refusing to count the statements of Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack because they were not in the bargaining unit on January 25, 1991—the date of the company’s letter refusing recognition. But the Board’s use of that date as a cutoff is arbitrary. The company’s decision to conduct the poll was made *after* January 25, and the employees’ statements were made *before* the sale. The relevant inquiry is whether the employer had a good-faith basis for doubt *at the time it decided to poll*, not at the time it sent a letter. The statements of these employees, made during the transition period, were relevant to the employer’s assessment of employee sentiment.
