**Argument**

A state prisoner may not use a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. The rule follows directly from the Court’s settled default doctrine. Federal habeas review is barred when a state prisoner has failed to comply with a state procedural rule, unless the prisoner shows both “cause” and “prejudice.” *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). Cause must be something external to the defense, not merely another constitutional claim that itself has never been properly preserved. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). The Court has repeatedly explained that attorney error can constitute cause only when the petitioner had a constitutional right to counsel in the proceeding where the error occurred. Id. at 488-89; *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 752-54 (1991).

That principle controls here. In *Coleman*, the Court held that because there is no constitutional right to counsel in state postconviction proceedings, attorney error in those proceedings cannot serve as cause to excuse a default. 501 U.S. at 752-57. The Court emphasized that “because petitioner had no right to counsel to pursue his appeal in state habeas, any attorney error that led to the default of petitioner’s claims in state habeas cannot constitute cause to excuse the default.” Id. at 757. The same logic applies when the asserted cause is a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim. If that claim was itself not fairly presented in state court, it cannot supply cause unless the petitioner first establishes cause and prejudice for the default of the ineffective-assistance claim itself. *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 488-89; *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 750.

The Court’s later decisions confirm that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim is not a free-standing key that unlocks otherwise barred federal review. In *Edwards v. Carpenter*, 529 U.S. 446, 453 (2000), the Court held that “a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim only if the prisoner can satisfy the cause-and-prejudice standard with respect to the ineffective-assistance claim itself.” That holding resolves the question presented and requires reversal of any rule, like the one applied below, that treats a defaulted ineffectiveness claim as automatically sufficient cause. See also *Smith v. Murray*, 477 U.S. 527, 533-34 (1986) (cause must justify the failure to comply with the state procedure that produced the default).

Respondent Carpenter cannot satisfy that standard. His claim that appellate counsel should have challenged the sufficiency of the evidence was not preserved in state court because his application to reopen his appeal was dismissed as untimely. Under Ohio’s procedural rules, that default is an independent and adequate state ground. See *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 729-30. A federal court therefore may not reach the sufficiency claim unless Carpenter first shows cause and prejudice for the untimeliness of the ineffective-assistance claim itself. *Edwards*, 529 U.S. at 453; *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 489.

Nor does the Sixth Circuit’s contrary approach fit the Court’s cause doctrine. A prisoner cannot circumvent default by recasting a lawyer’s omission as both the cause and the merits of a separate habeas claim. The Court has rejected precisely that kind of bootstrapping. In *Carrier*, the Court held that “the exhaustion doctrine . . . generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default.” 477 U.S. at 489. That requirement ensures that state courts have the first opportunity to address the alleged attorney error and prevents federal courts from excusing defaults based on claims that were themselves never properly exhausted. Id.; *O’Sullivan v. Boerckel*, 526 U.S. 838, 844-45 (1999).

Finally, even if Carpenter could show cause, he still would have to prove actual prejudice from the underlying sufficiency claim. *Wainwright*, 433 U.S. at 87. The record does not establish such prejudice. Carpenter pleaded guilty, received the sentence authorized by state law, and failed to present the sufficiency issue on direct review. Federal habeas relief is not available merely because a petitioner can imagine a different appellate strategy. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 689-91 (1984). And when the alleged ineffectiveness is itself defaulted, the petitioner must clear the added hurdle imposed by *Edwards*. 529 U.S. at 453.

The judgment should be reversed.
