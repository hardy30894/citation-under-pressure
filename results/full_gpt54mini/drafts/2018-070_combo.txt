Parker Drilling Management Services, Ltd. v. Brian Newton presents a straightforward question of statutory interpretation: whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) borrows state law only when federal law is silent, or instead whenever state law is merely “applicable” and not inconsistent with federal law. The statute, its structure, and this Court’s pre-1970 precedent confirm the former. State law becomes federal law on the Outer Continental Shelf only as surrogate law filling a gap in federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A).

OCSLA was enacted to create a body of law for fixed structures on the shelf because Congress understood that existing maritime law would not supply a complete rule of decision. Congress declared that the Constitution and laws of the United States extend to fixed installations on the shelf “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). But Congress immediately qualified the borrowing of adjacent state law: only “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law are those state laws “declared to be the law of the United States.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The natural reading of that language is gap-filling, not concurrent supplementation. See United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286, 293-94 (1958) (upholding assimilation of state law where Congress adopted state criminal law for federal enclaves as federal law); Paul v. United States, 371 U.S. 245, 263-65 (1963) (describing incorporation of state law into federal enclaves as congressional adoption, not direct state authority).

This Court’s decisions construing federal enclaves reinforce that Congress incorporates state law only where federal law does not already occupy the field. In Mater v. Holley, 200 F.2d 123, 125-26 (5th Cir. 1952), the court explained that enclave law derives from congressional adoption of state law in the absence of applicable federal provision. Likewise, James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94, 99-100 (1940), held that state safety regulations continued to govern a federal enclave only because Congress had not supplied a contrary federal rule. The premise of those cases is that state law is absorbed as federal law to avert legal vacuums, not to coexist with a complete federal scheme.

That understanding is also consistent with Sola Electric Co. v. Jefferson Electric Co., 317 U.S. 173, 176-77 (1942), which held that state policy cannot defeat federal law once Congress has spoken, and Clearfield Trust Co. v. United States, 318 U.S. 363, 366-67 (1943), which recognized that federal law controls where the federal government has created a national rule. OCSLA follows the same design: federal law governs, and adjacent state law enters only when needed to make the federal regime operative. See United States v. Standard Oil Co., 332 U.S. 301, 309-10 (1947) (federal interests justify creation of federal rules when Congress has established a federal enclave or program).

Nor does the phrase “applicable and not inconsistent” authorize wholesale adoption of every state statute touching the same subject. This Court has repeatedly understood analogous language to require a threshold inquiry into whether the federal law leaves room for assimilation. In Williams v. United States, 327 U.S. 711, 717-18 (1946), the Court treated state law as incorporated into federal enclave law only where federal law was deficient. And in United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. at 293, the Court emphasized that Congress adopted state law as federal law “for use in federal enclaves” to avoid gaps in the criminal code. Those decisions would make little sense if state law applied whenever it was merely nonconflicting with a preexisting federal statute.

Newton’s contrary view would invert OCSLA’s structure. If every state wage-and-hour rule applied so long as it did not conflict with the Fair Labor Standards Act, then the Act’s separate federal standards would no longer be the operative source of law on the shelf; state law would be. But OCSLA does not leave workers subject to a patchwork of state regulatory overlays whenever a state rule is “applicable.” It instead incorporates state law only “to the extent” it is needed and only when it is “not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That is the familiar model of federal enclave law, not concurrent state regulation. See Pacific Coast Dairy, Inc. v. Department of Agriculture of California, 318 U.S. 285, 294-95 (1943); Collin v. Smith, 447 F. Supp. 676, 686-87 (N.D. Ill. 1978), aff’d, 578 F.2d 1197 (7th Cir. 1978) (explaining enclave incorporation as dependent on congressional authorization).

The better reading preserves Congress’s choice to make federal law primary on the Outer Continental Shelf while borrowing only those state rules necessary to prevent a statutory gap. Because the FLSA supplies the governing federal wage-and-hour standards, California’s additional meal-period and wage-penalty provisions are not assimilated as surrogate federal law merely because they concern the same subject matter. The judgment below should be reversed.
