The Supreme Court's decision in Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 528 (2015), held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) is unconstitutionally vague. The Court's reasoning in Johnson applies retroactively to Welch's case, rendering his sentence under the ACCA invalid.

The ACCA defines a "violent felony" as a crime that has as an element the "use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another," or is one of several enumerated offenses, or "otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another." 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B). The residual clause, which includes crimes that "otherwise involve[ ] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another," was held to be unconstitutionally vague in Johnson. The Court reasoned that the clause failed to give people adequate notice of the conduct it punished and invited arbitrary enforcement. Johnson, 576 U.S. at 537-38.

The Eleventh Circuit's decision in Welch's case relied on the residual clause to classify his Florida strong arm robbery conviction as a "violent felony." However, in light of Johnson, this classification is no longer valid. The Eleventh Circuit's analysis under the categorical approach, as seen in United States v. Lockley, 632 F.3d 1238, 1246 (11th Cir.2011), and United States v. Alexander, 609 F.3d 1250, 1253 (11th Cir.2010), is now undermined by the Supreme Court's holding in Johnson.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has held that a new rule of constitutional law applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it is a "substantive rule or a watershed rule of criminal procedure implicating the fundamental fairness and accuracy of the criminal proceeding." See Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004) (citing Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, 311 (1989)). The rule announced in Johnson is a substantive rule, as it narrows the scope of the ACCA and affects the underlying conduct that is punishable under the statute. See Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 620 (1998). As such, it applies retroactively to Welch's case.

Additionally, the Court's decision in McNeill v. United States, 563 U.S. 816 (2011), emphasizes the importance of applying the version of state law that the defendant was convicted of violating when determining whether a prior conviction qualifies as a "violent felony." In Welch's case, the Florida law at the time of his conviction in 1996 did not require a level of force sufficient to satisfy the federal statute. See McCloud v. State, 335 So.2d 257, 258-59 (Fla.1976).

The Eleventh Circuit's decision in United States v. Chitwood, 676 F.3d 971 (11th Cir.2012), and United States v. Schneider, 681 F.3d 1273 (11th Cir.2012), which applied the residual clause to similar cases, are now abrogated by Johnson. The Court's holding in James v. United States, 550 U.S. 192 (2007), and Sykes v. United States, 564 U.S. 1 (2011), which interpreted the residual clause, are also no longer controlling.

In conclusion, the rule announced in Johnson v. United States applies retroactively to Welch's case, and his sentence under the ACCA should be vacated. The Eleventh Circuit's decision affirming Welch's sentence is no longer valid in light of Johnson, and the case should be remanded for resentencing.
