ARGUMENT

**I. The district court improperly resolved genuine factual disputes against Erdman and applied the wrong standard for counting FMLA eligibility hours.**

Summary judgment is a drastic remedy that should be granted only when no genuine issue of material fact exists. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). All reasonable inferences must be drawn in favor of the nonmovant. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 467 (1962). That rule is particularly important here because the district court’s eligibility determination turned on a factual dispute: whether Erdman accumulated the 1,250 hours required by the Family and Medical Leave Act. The court credited Nationwide’s assertion that Erdman’s extra hours were “unapproved” and disregarded her testimony and that of her former supervisor, Patty Sarno. A jury, not the court at summary judgment, should resolve that conflict. *United States v. Diebold, Inc.*, 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962).

**II. The FMLA’s eligibility threshold is a remedial requirement that must be liberally construed, and “hours worked” is defined by principles drawn from the Fair Labor Standards Act.**

Congress enacted the FMLA to provide job security for employees who need leave for serious family or medical reasons. The statute is remedial, and remedial labor legislation is construed liberally to effectuate its purposes. *A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling*, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945); *Kirschbaum v. Walling*, 316 U.S. 517, 524 (1942). The FMLA’s eligibility standard, measured by an employee’s accumulated hours, should therefore be interpreted in a way that does not defeat the statute’s protective purposes. The natural source of guidance is the FLSA, which defines “hours worked” as the time an employee is suffered or permitted to work for the employer. That principle is well established in this Court’s and the Supreme Court’s FLSA jurisprudence. *Armour & Co. v. Wantock*, 323 U.S. 126, 132 (1944); *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 139–40 (1944).

Where the employer knowingly allows work, that time counts, regardless of whether it was formally scheduled or approved. *Armour*, 323 U.S. at 132; *Skidmore*, 323 U.S. at 139–40. And when an employer fails to keep accurate records of all hours worked, “the employee has carried his burden if he proves that he has in fact performed work for which he was improperly compensated,” with ambiguities resolved against the employer. *Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co.*, 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946). Those principles should govern here.

**III. Erdman presented sufficient evidence of qualifying hours to defeat summary judgment.**

**A. The extra hours Erdman worked and for which Sarno authorized payment or “comp” time are hours worked.**

Erdman testified that Sarno regularly authorized payment for extra work or allowed her to accrue “comp” time. Sarno confirmed that Erdman was a reliable employee who worked outside the office and that those hours were approved. Nationwide does not deny that it paid for those hours. On this record, a reasonable juror could find that Nationwide suffered and permitted Erdman to work, and that those hours must count toward the eligibility threshold. *Armour*, 323 U.S. at 132; *Skidmore*, 323 U.S. at 139–40. The district court instead treated Getgen’s later characterization of those hours as “unapproved” as conclusive. But Getgen’s assertion contradicts Sarno’s account and creates a material factual dispute. That dispute cannot be resolved at summary judgment. *Sartor*, 321 U.S. at 627.

**B. Nationwide cannot retroactively erase hours it previously authorized, paid, or accepted.**

By February 2003, Getgen told Erdman she could no longer use extra hours as comp time. Even assuming Nationwide could change its policy prospectively, it could not retroactively convert hours already worked and already paid into “unapproved” hours for purposes of defeating Erdman’s eligibility. Equitable estoppel bars a party from denying the effect of its prior assurances or course of conduct upon which another has relied. *Glus v. Brooklyn Eastern District Terminal*, 359 U.S. 231, 232–33 (1959). Nationwide accepted the benefit of Erdman’s labor and compensated her for it; it cannot now disavow those hours merely because Erdman’s FMLA request made her eligibility important.

**C. Any ambiguity concerning the total hours Erdman worked must be resolved in her favor.**

Nationwide controlled the timekeeping records, and its records necessarily failed to capture the additional hours Sarno approved and Erdman worked. Under *Anderson*, an employer that fails to record all time suffered or permitted cannot benefit from that failure; the employee’s credible testimony may establish hours, and uncertainties are construed against the employer. *Anderson*, 328 U.S. at 687–88. Erdman’s testimony, corroborated by Sarno, is sufficient to raise a triable issue that she exceeded 1,250 hours.

**IV. Erdman’s retaliation claim likewise should have survived.**

Erdman announced her need for FMLA leave, submitted the required request, and was fired within two weeks. The proximity, combined with Nationwide’s shifting explanations and its hasty new policy barring comp time, is strong circumstantial evidence of retaliation. Employees who invoke rights under remedial labor statutes are protected from retaliation for doing so. *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. NLRB*, 313 U.S. 177, 193 (1941) (recognizing the power to remedy discrimination against employees because of protected activity). *Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp. v. NLRB*, 301 U.S. 1, 33–34 (1937), similarly underscores that the effective enforcement of employees’ statutory rights depends on preventing discriminatory discharge. Nationwide’s purported reasons for firing Erdman are disputed, and a jury must decide whether those reasons were pretextual.

**V. Conclusion**

The district court erred by granting summary judgment to Nationwide on Erdman’s FMLA interference and retaliation claims. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Erdman, creates genuine issues of material fact concerning whether she accumulated the requisite hours and whether Nationwide fired her for requesting FMLA leave. This Court should reverse and remand for trial.
