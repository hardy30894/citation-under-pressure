The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones, rather than limiting damages to the portion of profits attributable to the infringing design elements. The design patents at issue relate to specific components of Samsung's smartphones, such as the graphical user interface and the shape of the phone. Under 35 U.S.C. § 289, damages for design patent infringement are limited to the infringer's "total profit." However, this provision must be read in conjunction with the principle that damages should be apportioned to reflect the value of the infringing design.

In Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641 (1915), the Supreme Court held that damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the profit attributable to the infringing design. The Court stated that "the act does not contemplate that damages shall be assessed against the infringer for the profit derived by him from the sale of other parts or products not covered by the design." Id. at 646. Similarly, in Schnadig Corp. v. Gaines Mfg. Co., 620 F.2d 1166 (6th Cir. 1980), the Sixth Circuit held that damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the profit attributable to the infringing design, rather than the entire product.

The Federal Circuit's decision in this case is inconsistent with these precedents. By awarding damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones, the court effectively allowed Apple to recover for profits attributable to non-infringing components, such as the phone's hardware and software. This is contrary to the principle that damages should be apportioned to reflect the value of the infringing design. See, e.g., Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120 (1884) (holding that damages for patent infringement should be limited to the profit attributable to the infringing invention).

Furthermore, the Federal Circuit's decision is also inconsistent with the Supreme Court's decision in Power Integrations, Inc. v. Fairchild Semiconductor International, Inc., 136 S. Ct. 762 (2016), which held that damages for patent infringement should be based on the "smallest salable patent-practicing unit." In this case, the smallest salable patent-practicing unit is the component that incorporates the infringing design, not the entire smartphone.

The Federal Circuit's decision also ignores the principle that damages for patent infringement should be reasonable and not excessive. See, e.g., Rite-Hite Corp. v. Kelley Co., 56 F.3d 1538 (Fed. Cir. 1995) (en banc). Awarding damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones is unreasonable and excessive, as it includes profits attributable to non-infringing components.

In addition, the decision is contrary to the principle that patent damages should be based on the value that the infringing feature adds to the product. See, e.g., Lucent Techs., Inc. v. Gateway, Inc., 580 F.3d 1301 (Fed. Cir. 2009). In this case, the value of the infringing design elements should be apportioned from the overall value of the smartphone.

The Federal Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the case remanded for a determination of damages based on the profit attributable to the infringing design elements. This is consistent with the principles established in Dowagiac, Schnadig, and other cases, and ensures that damages for design patent infringement are reasonable and apportioned to reflect the value of the infringing design.

In Ericsson, Inc. v. D-Link Systems, Inc., 773 F.3d 1201 (Fed. Cir. 2014), the Federal Circuit held that damages for patent infringement should be based on the "value that the patented invention adds to the product." Id. at 1226. Similarly, in VirnetX, Inc. v. Cisco Systems, Inc., 767 F.3d 1308 (Fed. Cir. 2014), the Federal Circuit held that damages for patent infringement should be based on the "smallest salable unit." Id. at 1326.

By affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits, the Federal Circuit has failed to follow these precedents and has allowed Apple to recover excessive damages. The decision should be reversed to ensure that damages for design patent infringement are reasonable and apportioned to reflect the value of the infringing design.
