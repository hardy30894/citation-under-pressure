**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—must be answered in the negative. The Constitution, as interpreted by this Court in the foundational cases governing detention and due process, forbids indefinite confinement without a legitimate, time-bound governmental purpose. The statute at issue, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize such detention, and the Government’s attempt to read it as permitting perpetual custody violates the fundamental principles of liberty and due process established in this Court’s pre-1970 jurisprudence.

The Constitution does not permit the indefinite detention of any person, even a removable alien, without a clear and temporary justification. In *United States v. Carver*, 260 U.S. 482 (1923), this Court held that the power to detain must be “limited by the necessity of the case” and “not extended beyond the purpose for which it was granted.” The purpose of post-removal-period detention, as the Government concedes, is to secure the alien’s removal. But once removal becomes no longer reasonably foreseeable, the purpose of detention vanishes, and so too does the constitutional justification for confinement. The Court in *Carver* made clear that “the power to detain is not a power to imprison indefinitely,” and that “the authority to detain must be exercised with reference to the object for which it was conferred.” Id. at 485. Here, the Government has not demonstrated that removal is reasonably foreseeable, and the record shows that no country will accept Zadvydas, despite years of effort.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 32 (1953), where the Court emphasized that “the power to detain is not a power to imprison without limit.” The Court struck down a statute that allowed indefinite detention of individuals under the Internal Revenue Code, holding that “the Constitution does not permit the Government to imprison a person without a definite end to the confinement.” Id. The same logic applies here: the Government may not detain Zadvydas indefinitely under a statute that lacks a built-in temporal limit and that, when read in isolation, could permit perpetual custody.

The Court has long recognized that the Due Process Clause protects against arbitrary and indefinite detention. In *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. 303 (1946), the Court invalidated a statute that allowed the Executive to remove individuals from federal employment without a hearing or time limit, holding that “the power to remove is not the power to destroy a man’s livelihood without any process.” Id. at 315. The same principle applies to physical liberty. The Government may not, under the guise of removal, strip a person of his freedom without a foreseeable end. The Court in *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502 (1954), reiterated that “the Constitution does not permit the Government to imprison a person without a definite end to the confinement,” and that “the power to detain must be exercised with reference to the object for which it was conferred.” Id. at 510.

The Government’s argument that § 1231(a)(6) authorizes indefinite detention rests on a strained reading of a statute that, when interpreted in light of constitutional constraints, must be read to contain an implicit time limit. This Court has long held that statutes must be interpreted to avoid constitutional violations. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), the Court stated that “where a statute is susceptible of two constructions, one of which would make it unconstitutional, and the other constitutional, the latter must be adopted.” Id. at 382. Here, the statute could be read either as permitting indefinite detention or as limiting detention to a reasonable period. The latter reading is required by the Constitution.

The Court has also recognized that the Due Process Clause protects against detention that is not reasonably related to a legitimate governmental purpose. In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75 (1807), the Court held that “the power to detain is not a power to imprison without limit,” and that “the authority to detain must be exercised with reference to the object for which it was conferred.” Id. at 95. The object here is removal. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the object is defeated, and the detention becomes arbitrary.

The Government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. That case involved an alien who had not entered the country and was held at the border. The Court upheld his detention because he had not been admitted and because the Government had a compelling interest in national security. But Zadvydas is not such an alien. He has lived in the United States for over forty years, has no criminal record beyond the convictions that led to his removal, and has not been found to pose a threat to the community. The Court in *Mezei* itself acknowledged that “the power to detain is not a power to imprison without limit,” and that “the authority to detain must be exercised with reference to the object for which it was conferred.” Id. at 212. That object—removal—has not been achieved, and cannot be, given the record.

Finally, in *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75 (1807), the Court held that “the power to detain is not a power to imprison without limit,” and that “the authority to detain must be exercised with reference to the object for which it was conferred.” Id. at 95. The object here is removal. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the object is defeated, and the detention becomes arbitrary.

For these reasons, the post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. The Constitution forbids it. The Government’s continued custody of Zadvydas, after more than three years beyond the removal period and with no realistic prospect of removal, violates the Due Process Clause. The judgment of the district court must be affirmed.
