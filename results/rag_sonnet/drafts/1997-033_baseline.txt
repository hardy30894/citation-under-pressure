# ARGUMENT

## THE PLAIN LANGUAGE OF § 1407(a) PROHIBITS A TRANSFEREE COURT FROM ASSIGNING A CASE TO ITSELF FOR TRIAL

Section 1407(a) provides that any action transferred to a district for coordinated pretrial proceedings "shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). This is not permissive language subject to judicial override; it is a mandatory command. The word "shall" admits of no exception for a transferee judge's own view that a self-transfer under § 1404(a) would be more efficient. Congress vested the power to remand exclusively in "the panel," not in the individual transferee judge, and it specified the *only* mechanism by which remand may be avoided—suggestion of remand by the transferee court to the Panel itself, not unilateral action under an entirely different statutory provision.

Section 1404(a), by contrast, was designed for an altogether different purpose. This Court has repeatedly described § 1404(a) as a codification of forum non conveniens principles applicable to venue transfers between coordinate district courts in ordinary civil litigation. *See* Pope v. Atlantic Coast Line Railroad, 345 U.S. 379 (1953) (Section 1404(a) "provides for the transfer of such actions to a more convenient forum" and, "by its very terms, speaks to federal courts" addressing "that federal forum in which a lawsuit has been initiated"). Section 1404(a) contemplates a transfer *from* the forum where suit was filed *to* a district "where it might have been brought," Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335 (1960)—not a self-serving redesignation by a transferee court retaining a case it received only for pretrial purposes under an entirely separate multidistrict statute.

Nothing in this Court's § 1404(a) jurisprudence suggests that the statute authorizes a court to transfer a case to itself. Every case construing § 1404(a) that this Court has decided has involved genuine transfers between two different districts, with the transferee court exercising no antecedent custody of the case. *See* Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612 (1964) (transfer from Pennsylvania to Massachusetts); Ferens v. John Deere Co., 494 U.S. 516 (1990) (same); Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585, 364 U.S. 19 (1960) (transfer from New Orleans to Memphis); Stewart Organization, Inc. v. Ricoh Corp., 487 U.S. 22 (1988) (transfer from Alabama to New York). The statute's structure presupposes two distinct courts—a transferor and a transferee—not a single court purporting to "transfer" a case to itself. Judge Kozinski's dissent below correctly recognized that this "unlawful practice" traces to the Second Circuit's summary treatment in Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and that courts adopting it have candidly admitted, as did the District of New Hampshire, that "there is nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district ... for purposes of trial." 61 F.3d 911 (quoting In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971)).

The Ninth Circuit's own memorandum candidly acknowledged that petitioners' position rested on "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate," yet excused the district court's departure from that mandate solely because Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) "explicitly authorizes" self-transfer and because a scattering of circuit precedent had blessed the practice. But an agency rule cannot override an unambiguous statutory command, and the accretion of erroneous precedent does not transform an unlawful practice into a lawful one. As Judge Kozinski observed, "[s]eldom have so many been so consistently wrong."

## SECTION 1407(a)'S REMAND MANDATE REFLECTS A DELIBERATE CONGRESSIONAL CHOICE THAT MUST BE RESPECTED

This Court has long recognized that venue and transfer statutes reflect considered policy judgments that courts may not disregard for reasons of convenience. In Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335 (1960), this Court refused to permit a transferee court to exercise power under § 1404(a) that the statute did not confer, notwithstanding that the transferee forum might have been more convenient, because "the Northern District of Illinois was not a place where the action 'might have been brought'" within the meaning of the statute. The Court did not permit practical considerations to override the statutory text. So too here: whatever efficiencies a transferee court might perceive in retaining a case for trial, § 1407(a)'s remand mandate is unambiguous, and Congress—not the judiciary—determines when policy considerations justify deviation from an ordinary venue rule. *See* Kilpatrick v. Texas & Pacific Railway Co., 337 U.S. 75 (1949) (recognizing that venue transfer determinations must follow the statutory scheme Congress enacted, notwithstanding overlapping litigation).

Moreover, this Court has treated the appellate transfer power under § 1404(a) as an "extraordinary action" justified only by "extraordinary circumstances," Koehring Co. v. Hyde Construction Co., 382 U.S. 362 (1966), underscoring that transfer authority under § 1404(a) is to be exercised sparingly and only within its intended sphere—not stretched to swallow the carefully bounded remand mechanism of § 1407(a).

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
