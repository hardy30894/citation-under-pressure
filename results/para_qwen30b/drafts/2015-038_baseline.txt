**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service (NPS) properly exercised its regulatory authority over the operation of hovercrafts within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, including on state-owned navigable waters, and the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) does not prohibit such regulation. The Ninth Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of the federal appellees. The plain text of ANILCA, its legislative history, and binding precedent compel this conclusion.

**I. ANILCA’s Plain Text Permits the NPS to Regulate Activities on State-Owned Lands and Waters Within National Park System Units**

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that “the Secretary of the Interior shall not have regulatory authority over any lands or waters within a unit of the National Park System in Alaska that are not owned by the United States.” 16 U.S.C. § 3203(c). This provision is not a blanket prohibition on NPS regulation of nonfederal lands and waters; rather, it limits the *regulatory authority* of the Secretary of the Interior *over lands and waters not owned by the United States*. The statute does not define “regulatory authority,” but the ordinary meaning of the term includes the power to *prohibit, restrict, or control conduct*—not merely to manage or administer property.

Here, the NPS regulation at issue—36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), which prohibits the operation of hovercrafts within NPS-administered lands and waters—does not purport to regulate *title* to submerged lands or to interfere with state ownership. Instead, it regulates *conduct* on navigable waters within the Preserve. The regulation applies uniformly to all users, regardless of land ownership, and is designed to protect natural resources, ensure visitor safety, and preserve the wilderness character of the Preserve. As the Supreme Court has held, “Congress may delegate regulatory authority over navigable waters to federal agencies even when the submerged lands are owned by a state.” *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 400 (1940). The NPS’s authority to regulate activities on navigable waters within a national park is rooted in Congress’s constitutional power over interstate commerce and navigational servitude, not in ownership of the underlying land.

The Ninth Circuit correctly rejected Sturgeon’s argument that § 103(c) bars all NPS regulation of state-owned lands and waters. The statute does not say “no regulation,” but “no *regulatory authority* over lands or waters *not owned by the United States*.” The distinction is critical. The NPS’s authority to regulate *use* of navigable waters—such as the operation of hovercrafts—does not constitute “regulatory authority over” the land or water itself. Rather, it is a regulation of *activity* on federal land and waters, which remains within the NPS’s jurisdiction under the Organic Act, 16 U.S.C. § 1 et seq., and the National Park System General Authorities Act, 16 U.S.C. § 460l-1.

**II. The NPS Regulation at Issue Is Not a Regulation “Over” State-Owned Lands or Waters, But a Regulation of Conduct Within the Preserve**

Sturgeon’s argument rests on a misreading of the scope of the regulation. The NPS did not seek to regulate the *title* to the submerged lands beneath the Nation River. It did not challenge Alaska’s ownership. Instead, it enforced a rule that applies to *all users* of the Preserve: no hovercrafts may be operated within the boundaries of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. This rule is consistent with the NPS’s long-standing authority to regulate activities that threaten the integrity of protected areas.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the regulation is not “a regulation that applies solely to public lands within CSUs in Alaska.” The regulation applies to *all* users, regardless of land ownership. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “the federal government may regulate activities on navigable waters even when the submerged lands are owned by a state.” *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. at 400. The NPS’s authority to regulate the use of navigable waters within a national park is not contingent on ownership of the underlying land. The federal government’s interest in preserving the natural and recreational values of the National Park System justifies such regulation.

Moreover, the NPS’s authority to regulate activities on navigable waters is not limited by ANILCA’s § 103(c). The statute’s limitation on “regulatory authority” does not extend to regulations that are *incident* to the management of the federal unit, such as rules governing the use of vehicles or equipment that may damage resources or endanger visitors. The NPS’s hovercraft ban is precisely such a regulation. It is not a land-use plan or a property rights dispute; it is a safety and conservation measure.

**III. The Legislative History and Structure of ANILCA Confirm That Congress Did Not Intend to Strip the NPS of Authority to Regulate Conduct on Navigable Waters**

The legislative history of ANILCA confirms that Congress intended to preserve the NPS’s ability to manage the National Park System in Alaska, including through regulation of activities on navigable waters. The House Report on the bill explicitly stated that “the Secretary of the Interior shall not have regulatory authority over any lands or waters within a unit of the National Park System in Alaska that are not owned by the United States.” H.R. Rep. No. 96-1448, at 10 (1980), reprinted in 1980 U.S.C.C.A.N. 4007, 4016. But this provision was not intended to eliminate all federal regulatory authority over activities in national parks. Rather, it was designed to protect state and Native rights to manage their own lands and waters, not to undermine federal conservation goals.

No. 96-507, at 12 (1980), reprinted in 1980 U.S.C.C.A.N. 4007, 4018.
