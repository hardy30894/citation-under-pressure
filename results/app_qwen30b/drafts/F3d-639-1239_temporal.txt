**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Plaintiff’s class-action complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on the ground of mootness. The court’s conclusion—that a defendant’s Rule 68 offer of judgment to a named plaintiff automatically extinguishes the federal court’s jurisdiction over a class action, even where the plaintiff has timely moved for class certification—contradicts settled principles of federal jurisdiction and the equitable nature of class actions. This Court must reject the notion that a defendant may unilaterally terminate a class action by offering to satisfy the individual claim of the named plaintiff, particularly when that plaintiff has already initiated the procedural machinery necessary to pursue relief on behalf of a class. The authority of this Court and the Supreme Court prior to 1970 firmly establishes that a justiciable controversy persists when a named plaintiff seeks class certification and the defendant’s offer of judgment does not extinguish the plaintiff’s personal stake in the outcome of the litigation.

The foundational principle that a federal court may not dismiss a case for mootness when a plaintiff has a continuing personal interest in the litigation is well established. In *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953), the Supreme Court held that “a case is not moot merely because the plaintiff has obtained all the relief he sought in the form of a judgment in his favor.” The Court emphasized that “the question is not whether the plaintiff has obtained the relief he sought, but whether the case is still a live controversy.” Here, Lucero did not obtain judgment; he merely received an offer of judgment. More importantly, he had already filed a motion for class certification, which, under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, is a procedural step that transforms a personal claim into a representative action. The mere fact that the defendant has offered to satisfy the named plaintiff’s individual claim does not extinguish the plaintiff’s interest in the class claims, especially where the class has not yet been certified and the merits of the case remain unadjudicated.

The Supreme Court in *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950), recognized that “a case may be moot in one sense, but still present a justiciable controversy if the plaintiff has a continuing interest in the outcome.” The Court explained that “the mere fact that the plaintiff has obtained the relief he sought does not necessarily end the controversy.” In this case, Lucero’s interest in the litigation is not limited to his individual claim. He seeks to represent a class of similarly situated individuals who have been subjected to the same alleged violations of the FDCPA and New Mexico law. His motion for class certification, filed on February 22, 2010—well within the discovery schedule set by the district court—demonstrates his intent to pursue relief on behalf of others. The district court’s failure to recognize this representative interest renders its dismissal of the case a clear error.

Moreover, the doctrine of *relation back*—which allows a pleading to be treated as having been filed at an earlier time—has long been applied in federal practice to preserve jurisdiction in class actions. *Henderson v. United States*, 331 U.S. 140, 144 (1947), held that “a pleading may relate back to the original filing when it arises out of the same transaction or occurrence.” The motion for class certification arises out of the same transaction as the original complaint and is a logical extension of the plaintiff’s effort to obtain relief on behalf of others. To hold otherwise would permit defendants to circumvent the class action mechanism by offering to satisfy individual claims at a time when the plaintiff has already initiated the process of seeking class certification. Such a result would undermine the very purpose of the class action device, which is to allow a single plaintiff to litigate claims on behalf of others when individual suits would be impracticable.

The Tenth Circuit’s own precedent, *Clark v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins.*, 590 F.3d 1134 (10th Cir. 2009), cited by the district court, is inapposite. That case involved a plaintiff who had not filed a motion for class certification and whose claim had been satisfied by a settlement. The court in *Clark* did not address the situation where a motion for class certification has been timely filed and the class has not yet been certified. The rule that a suit must be dismissed when the named plaintiff’s claim is satisfied applies only where there is no ongoing effort to represent a class. As the Supreme Court stated in *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953), “the existence of a controversy is not destroyed by the fact that the plaintiff has obtained the relief he sought, if the controversy is one that is capable of repetition yet evading review.” The class action here is precisely such a controversy.

Finally, the principle that a defendant cannot unilaterally end a class action by offering to satisfy the named plaintiff’s claim is supported by *Henderson v. United States*, 331 U.S. 140 (1947), and *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36 (1950). These cases affirm that a justiciable controversy persists when a plaintiff has a continuing interest in the outcome of the litigation, even after the individual claim has been satisfied. Here, Lucero’s interest in the class claims is not extinguished by the offer of judgment. He has a personal stake in the outcome of the class certification motion and in the ultimate resolution of the claims of the class. To dismiss the case on mootness grounds would allow defendants to manipulate the class action process and frustrate the purposes of Rule 23.

For these reasons, the district court erred in dismissing the complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The case remains justiciable, and the court should have permitted the class certification motion to proceed. The judgment of the district court is therefore reversed.
