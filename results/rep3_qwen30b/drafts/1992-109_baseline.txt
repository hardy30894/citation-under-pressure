**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and its reversal must be affirmed. The record here reveals substantial evidence of mental impairment—suicidal ideation, severe depression, and the use of four psychotropic medications—combined with monosyllabic responses and a desire to prevent mitigation, all of which created a good faith doubt about Moran’s capacity to make a reasoned choice. Under *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), and *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960), a defendant must possess not only a rational understanding of the proceedings but also the ability to make a deliberate, informed decision among available legal alternatives. That higher standard applies with particular force when a defendant waives the right to counsel and pleads guilty—rights so fundamental that their waiver demands “utmost solicitude” from the court. *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973).

The state court’s failure to apply the correct standard was not merely error—it was constitutional infirmity. The Supreme Court has long recognized that competency to stand trial is not equivalent to competency to waive counsel or plead guilty. *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172; *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966). The former requires only a “rational and factual understanding” of the proceedings and the ability to assist counsel. *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402. The latter demands a “reasoned choice” among alternatives, including the decision to forgo counsel, to forgo mitigation, and to accept the consequences of a death sentence. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981). This distinction is not academic. As the Ninth Circuit correctly observed, “competency to waive constitutional rights requires a higher level of mental functioning than that required to stand trial.” *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 268.

The state court’s postconviction finding of competency was fatally flawed because it rested on psychiatric evaluations conducted solely to assess competency to stand trial—evaluations that never addressed the “reasoned choice” standard. Dr. O’Gorman’s report, for example, concluded only that Moran “is knowledgeable of the charges,” “can assist his attorney,” and “is fully cognizant of the penalties.” *O’Gorman Report* at 2. These observations, while relevant to *Dusky* competency, are insufficient to establish the higher threshold required for a knowing, voluntary, and intelligent waiver of counsel and a guilty plea. The report says nothing about Moran’s ability to weigh the consequences of waiving mitigation, to understand the strategic implications of self-representation, or to resist the influence of medication. Indeed, the report acknowledges Moran’s “difficulty with his memory” and his “depression,” *id.* at 1, and notes he “may not make the effort necessary to assist counsel,” *id.* at 3—factors that undermine, not support, a finding of reasoned choice.

Similarly, Dr. Jurasky’s evaluation, though more detailed, was limited to the *Dusky* standard. It addressed Moran’s ability to understand the charges and to communicate with counsel, but not his capacity to make a deliberate decision to forgo counsel and to prevent the introduction of mitigating evidence. The state court’s reliance on these reports to conclude that Moran was competent to waive counsel and plead guilty was therefore legally erroneous. As the Ninth Circuit held, “the state court applied the wrong legal standard.” *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 269. That error cannot be cured by a postconviction hearing that applies the same incorrect standard. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963). A state court’s finding of competency is not entitled to deference when it is based on an incorrect legal standard. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 857.

Moreover, the record itself, viewed through the proper lens, fails to support a finding of competency. Moran had attempted suicide just months before the plea hearing. He was taking four medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—all of which affect cognition, mood, and motor function. *Phenobarbitol* is a sedative; *Dilantin* and *Inderal* are known to cause confusion and lethargy; *Vistaril* is used to treat anxiety and may impair judgment. The court’s failure to inquire further when Moran admitted he was on medication—despite the obvious risk of cognitive impairment—was a constitutional failure. *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 885 (9th Cir. 1986) (“We must indulge in every reasonable presumption against waiver of constitutional rights.”).

Finally, Moran’s conduct at the plea hearing—monosyllabic responses, refusal of standby counsel, and a stated desire to prevent any mitigation—suggests not reasoned choice, but emotional distress and self-destructive impulse. The court’s acceptance of such a plea without a hearing to assess competency to make a reasoned choice violated due process. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969) (“The constitutional right to a fair trial is meaningless unless the defendant is capable of participating in it.”).
