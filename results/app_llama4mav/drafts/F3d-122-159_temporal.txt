## Argument

The Bureau of Prisons' ("BOP") denial of Victor M. Roussos' eligibility for early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) was improper because it was based on an overly broad interpretation of the term "crime of violence." The BOP's Program Statement 5162.02, which classifies Roussos' offense as a crime of violence due to the sentencing court's two-level enhancement for possession of a firearm, is inconsistent with the congressional statute and relevant case law.

The statute at issue, 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), allows for a reduction in sentence for prisoners convicted of nonviolent offenses who complete a treatment program. The term "nonviolent offense" is not defined in the statute, but the BOP regulations define it by referencing the term "crime of violence" as used in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). Section 924(c)(3) defines a crime of violence as an offense that is a felony and either has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person or property of another, or involves a substantial risk that physical force may be used in the course of committing the offense.

The Supreme Court has interpreted the term "crime of violence" in United States v. Chapman, 330 U.S. 767 (1947), where it held that a crime of violence requires more than mere possession of a firearm. In Chapman, the Court considered whether a conviction for possessing a firearm after being convicted of a crime of violence constituted a crime of violence. The Court ultimately held that it did not, as the statute at issue did not require proof of the use or attempted use of physical force.

Similarly, in United States v. Patten, 183 F.2d 69 (3d Cir. 1950), the Third Circuit Court of Appeals held that a conviction for conspiracy to commit a crime of violence did not necessarily constitute a crime of violence. The court noted that the conspiracy statute did not require proof of the use or attempted use of physical force, and that the underlying crime was not necessarily a crime of violence.

In this case, Roussos was convicted of conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846, which does not require proof of the use or attempted use of physical force. While the sentencing court enhanced his sentence for possession of a firearm, this enhancement does not render the underlying offense a crime of violence. As the Third Circuit held in Patten, a conspiracy conviction is not necessarily a crime of violence, and the BOP's Program Statement 5162.02 is overly broad in classifying Roussos' offense as such.

Furthermore, the BOP's interpretation is inconsistent with the congressional intent behind 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). The statute is intended to provide an incentive for prisoners to complete treatment programs, and the BOP's overly broad interpretation of "crime of violence" undermines this purpose. As the Supreme Court held in United States ex rel. Marcus v. Hess, 317 U.S. 537 (1943), administrative interpretations that are inconsistent with congressional intent are not entitled to deference.

Therefore, the BOP's denial of Roussos' eligibility for early release was improper, and he is entitled to relief. The district court's order denying his habeas petition should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
