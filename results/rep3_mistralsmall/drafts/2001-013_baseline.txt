**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY AND HEALTH ACT (“OSH ACT”) APPLIES TO THE MR. BELDON BECAUSE IT IS A “WORKPLACE” UNDER SECTION 4(a).**

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the OSH Act does not apply because the MR. BELDON is not a “workplace” under section 4(a) is erroneous. Section 4(a) of the OSH Act provides that it applies “with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The Act defines “workplace” as “any factory, plant, establishment, construction site, or other area, workplace or environment where work is performed by an employee.” 29 U.S.C. § 652(10). The MR. BELDON plainly falls within this definition. It is an “establishment” where Mallard’s employees performed work, and it is undisputed that the explosion occurred during the course of their employment. *See generally* Marshall v. Daniel Constr. Co., 563 F.2d 707, 710 (5th Cir. 1977) (holding that a construction site was a “workplace” under the OSH Act).

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. Those cases addressed whether the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction preempted OSHA’s authority over seamen’s working conditions, not whether the vessel was a “workplace.” The Fifth Circuit in *Clary* explicitly stated that its holding was limited to the preemption issue: “We do not reach the question whether the OSH Act applies to the working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation.” 609 F.2d at 1122 n.3. Thus, those cases do not support the conclusion that the MR. BELDON is not a “workplace.”

**II. SECTION 4(b)(1) OF THE OSH ACT DOES NOT PREEMPT OSHA’S JURISDICTION BECAUSE THE COAST GUARD HAS NOT EXERCISED EXCLUSIVE AUTHORITY OVER OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY AND HEALTH STANDARDS ON UNINSPECTED VESSELS.**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act does not apply “with respect to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Fifth Circuit erred in concluding that the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is exclusive. The Coast Guard’s jurisdiction over uninspected vessels is limited to specific safety regulations, such as those governing lifesaving equipment and fire extinguishers. *See* 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. It does not extend to comprehensive occupational safety and health standards for workers on uninspected vessels.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary* and *Donovan* is again misplaced. Those cases involved seamen working on vessels in navigation, where the Coast Guard’s authority is more comprehensive. *See Clary*, 609 F.2d at 1121; *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826. The MR. BELDON, however, is an uninspected vessel engaged in drilling operations in territorial waters, not a vessel in navigation. The Coast Guard’s authority over such vessels is not exclusive, and OSHA’s jurisdiction is not preempted.

The Supreme Court has recognized that preemption under section 4(b)(1) requires a clear indication that Congress intended to delegate exclusive authority to another agency. *See Gade v. Nat’l Solid Wastes Mgmt. Ass’n*, 505 U.S. 88, 98 (1992). Here, there is no such indication. The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels are narrow and do not address the comprehensive occupational safety and health standards at issue in this case. *See* 29 C.F.R. § 1910.12(a) (excluding vessels from OSHA coverage only when “specifically regulated under another section of this chapter”). Thus, OSHA’s jurisdiction is not preempted.

**III. THE FIFTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION CREATES AN UNREASONABLE DISTINCTION BETWEEN INSPECTED AND UNINSPECTED VESSELS, WHICH CONTRADICTS THE OSH ACT’S BROAD PROTECTIVE PURPOSE.**

The Fifth Circuit’s decision creates an arbitrary distinction between inspected and uninspected vessels, undermining the OSH Act’s goal of ensuring safe workplaces. The Act’s coverage is broad, and its protections should not turn on whether a vessel is inspected by the Coast Guard. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 651(b) (declaring the Act’s purpose is to “assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions”). The Fifth Circuit’s decision leaves workers on uninspected vessels without OSHA’s protections, despite the fact that they face the same hazards as workers on inspected vessels.

The Supreme Court has cautioned against interpretations of the OSH Act that would leave workers unprotected. *See Martin v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n*, 499 U.S. 144, 156 (1991) (holding that the Act should be construed to effectuate its remedial purpose). The Fifth Circuit’s decision does the opposite, creating a loophole that deprives workers on uninspected vessels of essential safety protections.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Fifth Circuit’s decision and hold that OSHA has jurisdiction over the MR. BELDON. The judgment of the Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission should be affirmed.
