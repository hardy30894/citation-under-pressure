The Qualifications Clauses of Article I set forth the exclusive requirements for service in Congress. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969). Those clauses reflect the Framers’ deliberate choice to create a uniform national standard rather than permit state variation. Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1 (1964). Because the power to regulate the qualifications of members of Congress is not among the powers reserved to the states, any state attempt to enlarge those qualifications is void. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819).

The constitutional design contemplates that representatives and senators shall be chosen by the people of the several states according to federal rules alone. Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186 (1962). State-imposed term limits operate as additional qualifications that the Constitution itself does not contain. Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533 (1964). Such limits therefore exceed the authority retained by the states under the Tenth Amendment. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824).

Congressional elections are federal functions whose integrity the Constitution protects against state interference. United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299 (1941). The states may regulate the “times, places and manner” of holding elections, but that power does not extend to redefining eligibility for office. Ex parte Yarbrough, 110 U.S. 651 (1884). Any contrary rule would produce a patchwork of qualifications inconsistent with the national character of the legislative branch. Cooley v. Board of Wardens, 53 U.S. (12 How.) 299 (1852).

The people of Arkansas therefore lacked authority to render ineligible those candidates who have served the number of terms specified in Amendment 73. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court declaring Section 3 unconstitutional should be affirmed.
