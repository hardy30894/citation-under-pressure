ARGUMENT

The National Labor Relations Act protects employees' right to choose or reject representation while imposing on employers a duty to bargain only with a union enjoying majority support. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33 (1937). An incumbent union's majority status is not irrebuttable; an employer may test that status when objective facts create reasonable doubt. Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 805 (1945). The Board's requirement that an employer possess "objective reasonable doubt" before polling therefore must be measured against the statutory command that representation questions be resolved by employee free choice. NLRB v. Falk Corp., 308 U.S. 453, 459 (1940).

The Board's standard collapses the distinct statutory avenues of withdrawal, RM election, and polling into a single, insurmountable threshold. This conflation is inconsistent with the Act's structure, which distinguishes between an employer's unilateral action and its request for an election or poll. Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944). Requiring the same quantum of proof for an internal poll that the Board demands for withdrawal of recognition renders the poll a nullity, contrary to the principle that administrative rules must effectuate rather than frustrate congressional policy. NLRB v. Pennsylvania Greyhound Lines, Inc., 303 U.S. 261, 265 (1938).

The Board's factual finding that Allentown lacked reasonable doubt is likewise unsupported. Substantial-evidence review demands that the record as a whole contain evidence sufficient to support the agency's conclusion. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Here the record contains uncontradicted statements from multiple unit employees expressing opposition to the union, statements made before any poll was announced. Such contemporaneous expressions constitute objective indicia that the union had lost majority support. NLRB v. Babcock & Wilcox Co., 351 U.S. 105, 113 (1956). The Board improperly discounted these statements by applying an evidentiary filter nowhere authorized by the Act. See Local 357, International Brotherhood of Teamsters v. NLRB, 365 U.S. 667, 677 (1961).

Because the Board's standard is irrational and its factual determination rests on less than substantial evidence, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
