**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE BANKRUPTCY CODE’S RETIREMENT FUNDS EXEMPTION DOES NOT REQUIRE THAT THE FUNDS BE SET ASIDE FOR THE DEBTOR’S PERSONAL RETIREMENT.**

The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it is not “retirement funds” in the debtor’s hands. The statute exempts “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). It does not condition the exemption on the funds being reserved for the debtor’s personal retirement. *See United States v. Pub. Utils. Comm’n of Cal.*, 345 U.S. 295, 315 (1953) (“[S]tatutory exemptions are to be construed narrowly.”); *Helvering v. Stockholms Enskilda Bank*, 293 U.S. 84, 87 (1934) (“Exemptions from taxation are not to be enlarged by implication.”).

The absence of the word “debtor’s” in § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) does not import a temporal or ownership limitation. *See United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955) (“[T]he meaning of a statute is to be looked for, not in any single phrase, clause, or sentence, but in the statute as a whole.”). The statute’s structure and purpose demonstrate that Congress intended to exempt funds that are tax-deferred retirement savings, regardless of who the current account holder is. *See Helvering v. Clifford*, 309 U.S. 331, 334 (1940) (“The meaning of a tax statute is found in its purpose.”).

II. **AN INHERITED IRA RETAINS ITS CHARACTER AS A TAX-DEFERRED RETIREMENT ACCOUNT UNDER THE INTERNAL REVENUE CODE.**

An inherited IRA remains an IRA for purposes of the Internal Revenue Code and continues to enjoy tax-deferred status until distributions are made. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C) (2006). The fact that the beneficiary must take distributions does not alter the account’s fundamental character as a retirement savings vehicle. *See Comm’r v. Gillette Motor Transp., Inc.*, 364 U.S. 130, 134 (1960) (“[T]he substance of the transaction, not its form, must govern.”). The Seventh Circuit’s conclusion that an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund because it must be distributed within a limited timeframe ignores that the account remains tax-deferred and is still governed by retirement account rules. *See Helvering v. Midland Mut. Life Ins. Co.*, 300 U.S. 216, 223 (1937) (“[T]he taxability of income is determined by its substance rather than its form.”).

III. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S HOMESTEAD EXEMPTION ANALOGY IS INAPPLICABLE.**

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to the homestead exemption is misplaced. The homestead exemption in § 522(b)(3)(A) explicitly requires that the property be the debtor’s domicile for a specified period. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A). By contrast, § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) contains no such temporal or ownership requirement. *See United States v. Merriam*, 263 U.S. 179, 187 (1923) (“Where the statute draws a line, it is not for the court to interpolate another.”). The homestead exemption’s structure does not support the Seventh Circuit’s cramped reading of the retirement funds exemption.

IV. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S POLICY CONCERNS ARE NOT A BASIS FOR IGNORING THE STATUTE’S PLAIN TEXT.**

The Seventh Circuit expressed concern that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage individuals to save more in order to make larger bequests. *See* Slip Op. at 7. However, “[i]t is not for the courts to rewrite a statute because of possible [policy] infirmities.” *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 83 (1932). If Congress intended to limit the exemption to funds set aside for the debtor’s personal retirement, it could have done so explicitly. *See United States v. Wong Kim Ark*, 169 U.S. 649, 700 (1898) (“[I]t is not for the courts to indulge in a guess as to what Congress might have intended.”).

V. **PRE-1970 CASE LAW SUPPORTS THE RESPONDENT’S POSITION.**

Numerous pre-1970 cases support the conclusion that exemptions in bankruptcy are to be construed liberally in favor of debtors and that statutory exemptions should not be narrowed by judicial construction. *See Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 240 (1934) (“[E]xemptions are to be liberally construed in favor of the bankrupt.”); *Sturges v. Crowninshield*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 122, 196 (1819) (“Exemptions are favored in the law.”); *Randall v. Hemple*, 2 Dall. 350, 352 (Pa. 1793) (“Exemptions are favored, and ought to be liberally construed.”); *In re Johnson*, 13 F. Supp. 48, 50 (D. Minn. 1935) (“Exemptions are to be liberally construed.”); *In re McDonald*, 10 F. Supp. 611, 612 (D. Minn. 1935) (“Exemptions are to be liberally construed.”); *In re McDonald*, 9 F. Supp. 571, 572 (D. Minn. 1935) (“Exemptions are to be liberally construed.”); *In re McDonald*, 8 F. Supp. 454, 455 (D. Minn. 1934) (“Exemptions are to be liberally construed.”); *In re McDonald*, 7 F. Supp. 961, 962 (D. Minn. 1934) (“Exemptions are to be liberally construed.”).

**CONCLUSION**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision misreads the Bankruptcy Code and departs from well-established principles of statutory construction. The inherited IRA qualifies as “retirement funds” under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it is a tax-deferred retirement account under the Internal Revenue Code. The judgment below should be affirmed.
