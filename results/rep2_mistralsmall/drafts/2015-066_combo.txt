**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Sixth Amendment’s Right to Counsel Applies to All Criminal Proceedings Where Imprisonment Is Authorized, Regardless of the Court’s Jurisdictional Source**

The Sixth Amendment provides that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This guarantee is not limited to federal courts or to prosecutions brought under federal law. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 60 (1932) (“The right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel.”). The Court has long held that the Sixth Amendment applies to state criminal proceedings through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 342 (1963) (overruling *Betts v. Brady*, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), and holding that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is fundamental and applicable to the states). The same principle applies with equal force to tribal courts when their proceedings authorize imprisonment. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 382 (1896) (recognizing that tribal courts are not bound by the Bill of Rights unless Congress provides otherwise); *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556, 570 (1883) (holding that tribal sovereignty precludes federal jurisdiction over crimes committed by Indians against Indians, absent congressional action).

The government’s reliance on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), is misplaced. *Nichols* addressed the use of an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction for sentencing enhancement purposes under the federal Sentencing Guidelines, not as an element of a new offense. *Id.* at 746-47. The Court expressly distinguished the sentencing context from the guilt phase, noting that “[t]he most salient difference between the guilt and punishment phases of criminal adjudication is that prosecutors must prove each element of an offense beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Id.* at 748 (citing *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970)). The Court further emphasized that sentencing courts may consider reliability challenges to uncounseled convictions, a safeguard absent when such convictions are used to establish an element of a new felony charge. *Id.* at 747-48.

II. **An Uncounseled Tribal Court Conviction Used as a Predicate Offense Under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) Violates the Sixth Amendment’s Right to Counsel**

This Court has consistently held that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches whenever a defendant faces potential imprisonment, regardless of the court’s jurisdictional source. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 342-43; *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 60. The fact that tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment does not permit the federal government to use their uncounseled convictions as elements of a federal crime. *Talton*, 163 U.S. at 382. To hold otherwise would allow the federal government to circumvent constitutional protections by relying on tribal convictions obtained in proceedings that do not afford the full panoply of Sixth Amendment rights.

The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized in *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), that the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses in a federal prosecution violates the Sixth Amendment. *Id.* at 1394-96. The court reasoned that the federal government cannot rely on convictions obtained in proceedings that do not comport with constitutional standards to establish elements of a federal offense. *Id.* This principle is consistent with the Court’s holding in *Baldasar v. Illinois*, 446 U.S. 222 (1980) (per curiam), that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction cannot be used to enhance a subsequent sentence, even though the prior conviction itself was valid under *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367 (1979). *Baldasar*, 446 U.S. at 223-24.

III. **The Federal Government Cannot Use Tribal Court Convictions Obtained in Violation of Due Process to Satisfy § 117(a)’s Predicate Offense Requirement**

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits the federal government from using convictions obtained in proceedings that violate fundamental fairness. *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935) (“[A] conviction obtained through the use of perjured testimony known by the prosecution to be perjured is fundamentally unfair.”). The Court has long held that the use of unreliable convictions to establish elements of a federal offense offends due process. *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973) (“The right of an accused in a criminal trial to due process is, in essence, the right to a fair opportunity to defend against the State’s accusations.”).

Tribal court convictions obtained without the assistance of counsel are inherently unreliable for purposes of establishing a predicate offense under § 117(a). *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 60; *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 342-43. The federal government’s attempt to rely on such convictions to satisfy an element of a federal crime violates the Due Process Clause.

IV. **The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Does Not Impair Tribal Sovereignty or Undermine Federal Efforts to Combat Domestic Violence**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision does not intrude upon tribal sovereignty. *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997) (recognizing that comity does not require tribal courts to use identical procedures as federal courts). The court merely held that the federal government cannot use tribal court convictions obtained in violation of constitutional principles as elements of a federal offense. *Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1394-96. This does not diminish tribal authority; it merely ensures that the federal government respects constitutional limits when prosecuting crimes under § 117(a).

Congress has the power to address domestic violence in Indian country, but it must do so in a manner consistent with constitutional principles. *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004) (recognizing Congress’s plenary power over Indian affairs). The Ninth Circuit’s decision does not frustrate Congress’s intent; it merely requires the federal government to rely on reliable predicate offenses when prosecuting § 117(a) violations.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment and due process. This Court should affirm the decision below.
