The question presented is whether an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act. The plain language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) requires only that the employer engage in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." This statutory text is dispositive, as it clearly sets forth the standard for awarding punitive damages.

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 indicates that Congress drew upon existing law, including 42 U.S.C. § 1981, which has long recognized that punitive damages serve to deter and punish egregious conduct. The Supreme Court has observed that punitive damages are awarded "for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant," Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), and are available when a defendant acts with a "bad motive, or so recklessly as to imply a disregard of social obligations," Milwaukee & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489, 493 (1875). This principle is further supported by cases such as Philadelphia, W. & B.R. Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202 (1858), and Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), which discuss the concept of malice and the role of punitive damages in deterring wrongful conduct.

The Restatement of Torts, reflecting the common law as of 1939, provides that punitive damages are available when a defendant's conduct is "outrageous, because of ... evil motive or ... reckless indifference to the rights of others," Restatement of Torts § 908(2) (1939). This standard aligns with the language of § 1981a(b)(1) and is consistent with the principles established in cases such as Scott v. Donald, 166 U.S. 193 (1897), and Rood v. Wallace, 109 F. 924 (8th Cir. 1901).

In the present case, the District Court er. in dismissing Kolstad's claim for punitive damages. The jury's finding of intentional discrimination, combined with evidence of the ADA's reckless indifference to Kolstad's federally protected rights, is sufficient to support an award of punitive damages. The ADA's preselection of Spangler for the promotion, in violation of its own procedures, and its disregard for Kolstad's efforts to discuss the position, demonstrate a level of malice or reckless indifference that warrants consideration of punitive damages. As established in cases such as Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), and Milwaukee & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489 (1875), such conduct is sufficient to support an award of punitive damages.

The evidence presented at trial, including the ADA's actions and statements, was sufficient to support a finding of punitive damages under the standard established by Congress in § 1981a(b)(1). The ADA's conduct demonstrates a level of malice or reckless indifference that is consistent with the principles established in cases such as Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), and Scott v. Donald, 166 U.S. 193 (1897).

In conclusion, the plain language of § 1981a(b)(1), combined with the legislative history and relevant case law, supports the conclusion that an employer's conduct does not need to be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings on Kolstad's punitive damages claim.
