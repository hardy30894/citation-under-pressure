**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Circuit’s Decision in *Air Measurement* Correctly Recognizes Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction Over State Malpractice Claims That Require Resolution of a Substantial and Necessary Patent Issue.**

The Supreme Court has long held that federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. *See Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988) (holding that § 1338(a) extends federal jurisdiction to cases where "the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law"). This principle applies with equal force to state legal malpractice claims that hinge on the resolution of a patent issue. *See Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1271-73 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (finding exclusive federal jurisdiction over a state malpractice claim where the plaintiff’s right to relief depended on proving patent infringement in the underlying case).

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308 (2005), refined this test, requiring that the federal issue be (1) necessarily raised, (2) actually disputed, (3) substantial, and (4) capable of resolution without disrupting the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Id.* at 314. The Federal Circuit correctly applied this framework in *Air Measurement*, concluding that the patent issue in that case was substantial and necessary to the malpractice claim. *Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1272-73. The same analysis applies here, where Minton’s malpractice claim turns on whether his attorneys negligently failed to raise the experimental use exception—a quintessential question of federal patent law—in the underlying infringement litigation. *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (defining the experimental use exception).

**II. The Texas Supreme Court Erred in Concluding That the Patent Issue in This Case Is Not Substantial or Necessary to the Malpractice Claim.**

The Texas Supreme Court’s decision misapplies *Grable* by minimizing the role of the patent issue in Minton’s malpractice claim. The court acknowledged that the experimental use exception is a "necessary, disputed, and substantial" federal patent issue, yet it dismissed the claim as non-justiciable under federal law. *See* slip op. at 12. This conclusion ignores the Supreme Court’s admonition that federal jurisdiction exists where a state-law claim "necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue." *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314.

The patent issue here is not merely incidental; it is the linchpin of Minton’s malpractice claim. Without resolving whether the experimental use exception applied in the underlying litigation, Minton cannot establish that his attorneys’ alleged negligence proximately caused his injury. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004) (requiring proof of proximate cause in legal malpractice claims). The Texas court’s attempt to distinguish *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), is unavailing, as those cases squarely control this issue. *See Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1273 ("[W]here establishing patent infringement is a necessary element of a malpractice claim stemming from alleged mishandling of earlier patent litigation, the issue is substantial and contested, and federal resolution of the issue was intended by Congress, federal courts possess exclusive ‘arising under’ jurisdiction of the malpractice claim.").

**III. The Federal Circuit’s Precedent Is Binding and Consistent with Supreme Court Jurisprudence.**

The Texas Supreme Court’s reliance on non-patent cases to cabin federal jurisdiction is misplaced. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that § 1338(a) confers exclusive federal jurisdiction over cases "arising under" patent law, regardless of whether the claim is state- or federal-based. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808-09. The Federal Circuit’s decisions in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept* faithfully apply this principle, and this Court should defer to that court’s specialized expertise in patent law. *See United States v. Dubilier Condenser Corp.*, 289 U.S. 178, 199 (1933) (recognizing the Federal Circuit’s "special competence" in patent matters).

The dissent’s suggestion that the Federal Circuit has ignored *Grable* is incorrect. *See* slip op. dissent at 3. The Federal Circuit has explicitly applied *Grable*’s framework in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept*, concluding that the patent issues in those cases were substantial and necessary to the malpractice claims. *See Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1272-73; *Immunocept*, 504 F.3d at 1288-89. The Fifth Circuit has likewise adopted this reasoning. *See USPPS, LLC v. Avery Dennison Corp.*, 647 F.3d 274, 282 (5th Cir. 2011) (applying *Air Measurement*’s analysis).

**Conclusion**

The Texas Supreme Court’s decision conflicts with controlling Supreme Court and Federal Circuit precedent. This Court should reverse and hold that federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over Minton’s malpractice claim, as the patent issue is substantial, necessary, and capable of resolution without disrupting the federal-state balance.
