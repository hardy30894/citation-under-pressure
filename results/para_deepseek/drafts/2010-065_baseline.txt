# ARGUMENT

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. Bond lacks standing to challenge 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds because that Amendment reserves unenumerated powers to the States, not to individuals, and Bond seeks to vindicate the sovereign interests of a third party—Pennsylvania—rather than any right of her own.

## I. The Tenth Amendment Protects the Sovereignty of the States, Not Individual Rights

Article III standing requires a plaintiff to allege "an invasion of a legally protected interest" that is "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent." *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992). A plaintiff "must assert his own legal rights and interests, and cannot rest his claim to relief on the legal rights or interests of third parties." *Warth v. Seldin*, 422 U.S. 490, 499 (1975). These requirements apply with equal force to criminal defendants, for courts must decide jurisdictional questions before reaching the merits. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't*, 523 U.S. 83, 94 (1998).

The Tenth Amendment provides that "[t]he powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." U.S. Const. amend. X. By its terms, the Amendment is a structural provision allocating authority between the federal government and the States. It "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered." *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). It confers no substantive right on any individual.

This Court applied that principle in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). There, private utilities argued that the TVA's sale of electricity violated the Tenth Amendment. The Court held that, because the States had not objected, the utilities, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the amendment." *Id.* at 144. The Court thus recognized that the Tenth Amendment's guarantee belongs to the States, not to private litigants.

The courts of appeals have applied *Tennessee Electric* to hold uniformly that private parties cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment. *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005); *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004). The two decisions Bond may invoke are outliers that either predate consideration of *Tennessee Electric* or have since been disavowed, and neither involved a criminal defendant. *See Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693, 703-04 (7th Cir. 1999); *Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. U.S. Dep't of Energy*, 666 F.2d 1359, 1368 n.16 (11th Cir. 1982).

## II. Bond's Conviction Does Not Give Her a Personal Stake in the States' Sovereign Interests

Bond's conviction is undoubtedly an injury. But the injury she alleges is not to her own liberty; it is to "the delicate balance between the federal and state governments." She complains that § 229 invades the sovereign prerogatives of Pennsylvania. That is precisely the kind of third-party claim the standing doctrine forbids.

The criminal context does not alter the analysis. The defendant in *Hacker* raised a Tenth Amendment challenge to her federal conviction, and the Eighth Circuit held she lacked standing because the Amendment protects the sovereignty of the States rather than any individual right. 565 F.3d at 525-27. The First Circuit reached the same conclusion, explaining that "the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state's policy of acquiescence." *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35.

Bond is not without recourse. If she believes § 229 exceeds Congress's enumerated powers, she may raise an Article I challenge in the ordinary course. But that is not the claim she has made. Her claim is that the statute offends federalism—and federalism's beneficiary is the State of Pennsylvania, not Carol Anne Bond. Pennsylvania has not complained, and for good reason: Bond's conduct is precisely the kind of local criminal activity that state law routinely addresses.

## III. The Phrase "or to the People" Does Not Create Individual Standing

Bond may argue that the Tenth Amendment's final phrase, "or to the people," gives her a personal right. It does not. The people are the ultimate sovereign from whom all governmental power derives; that phrase preserves the residual sovereignty of the people collectively, not a personal entitlement to challenge federal statutes. The Tenth Amendment is a "truism" confirming the Constitution's structure of limited, enumerated federal powers. *Darby*, 312 U.S. at 124. A criminal defendant who wishes to argue that Congress exceeded an enumerated power may do so. But she may not repackage that argument as a Tenth Amendment claim belonging to the States and thereby manufacture standing that Article III denies.

The court of appeals correctly held that Bond lacks standing to raise her Tenth Amendment challenge. The judgment should be affirmed.
