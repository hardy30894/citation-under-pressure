The Due Process Clause does not impose a heightened “reasoned choice” standard for determining a defendant’s competence to plead guilty or waive counsel. The governing test remains whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam); see also Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 378 (1966).

This Court has never required a distinct, more demanding formulation when the defendant seeks to forgo trial. In Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), the Court held that a guilty plea must be voluntary and intelligent, yet it measured those qualities by reference to the same capacity to understand the proceedings and consult with counsel that Dusky demands. Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), likewise recognized a right of self-representation without announcing any elevated mental-function test; the decision turned on the defendant’s knowing and voluntary relinquishment of counsel, not on a “reasoned choice” among sentencing alternatives.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—that competence to waive rights requires “the capacity for reasoned choice among the alternatives available”—finds no support in these precedents. That formulation conflates the distinct inquiries of competence and the validity of a waiver. Once a defendant is competent under Dusky, the separate constitutional requirements that any waiver be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent are satisfied by the familiar colloquy and record mandated by Boykin and Faretta. Imposing an additional substantive standard would effectively constitutionalize a higher threshold that this Court has declined to adopt.

The record here confirms that Moran satisfied the Dusky standard at the time of his plea and waiver. Two court-appointed psychiatrists examined him weeks earlier and concluded he possessed the ability to understand the charges and assist counsel. The state post-conviction court credited those findings. Because the Due Process Clause does not demand more, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
