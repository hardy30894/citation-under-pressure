**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court Erred in Applying the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) Because Furlow’s 2016 South Carolina Conviction for Distribution of Crack Cocaine Does Not Qualify as a “Serious Drug Offense” Under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii)

The ACCA’s “serious drug offense” enhancement applies only to convictions for offenses “involving . . . distributing . . . a controlled substance . . . for which a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more is prescribed by law.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). This statutory language is not self-executing; rather, courts must determine whether the *underlying state offense* meets the ACCA’s threshold based on the *maximum penalty prescribed by law*, not the actual sentence imposed. See *Mathis v. United States*, 579 U.S. 102, 107 (2016) (“The ACCA’s ‘serious drug offense’ definition turns on the maximum penalty prescribed by law, not the sentence actually imposed.”).

Here, Furlow was convicted in South Carolina in 2016 of “distribution of crack cocaine,” a violation of S.C. Code § 44-53-350. The South Carolina statute provides that a first offense for distribution of crack cocaine is a felony punishable by up to ten years in prison. See S.C. Code § 44-53-350(B). However, the ACCA requires a “maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more,” and the Supreme Court has made clear that “a sentence of exactly ten years” does not satisfy the ACCA’s threshold. *United States v. Johnson*, 576 U.S. 531, 535 (2015) (holding that a state offense with a maximum penalty of ten years is not a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA because the statute must prescribe a penalty “of ten years or more,” and “more” means greater than ten). The Court emphasized that “the ACCA’s ‘ten years or more’ language is not satisfied by a maximum of exactly ten years.” Id. at 536.

The Fourth Circuit has followed *Johnson* in holding that a state offense with a maximum penalty of ten years does not qualify as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA. *United States v. Williams*, 875 F.3d 215, 220 (4th Cir. 2017) (holding that a North Carolina offense with a maximum penalty of ten years did not qualify under the ACCA). Similarly, in *United States v. Smith*, 857 F.3d 178, 183 (4th Cir. 2017), the court rejected a conviction with a ten-year maximum as insufficient under the ACCA, citing *Johnson* and emphasizing that “the ACCA’s ‘ten years or more’ language requires a penalty exceeding ten years.” See also *United States v. Brown*, 845 F.3d 148, 152 (4th Cir. 2017) (same).

Furlow’s 2016 South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine carries a maximum penalty of ten years, which falls short of the ACCA’s requirement. Therefore, it cannot serve as a predicate offense under the ACCA. The district court’s contrary conclusion was error.

II. The District Court Erred in Applying the Career Offender Provision Because Furlow’s 2008 Georgia Convictions for First-Degree Arson Do Not Qualify as “Crimes of Violence” Under USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2)

Under the Guidelines, a “crime of violence” includes “arson.” USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). However, the Supreme Court has held that the term “arson” in the Guidelines must be interpreted in light of the *generic* definition of the offense, not the broad sweep of state statutes. *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 599 (1990) (“The generic, or ‘ordinary,’ meaning of arson is the intentional burning of a building or structure.”). The Court has further clarified that “arson” under the Guidelines requires the *intentional* and *unlawful* burning of a structure with the *intent to damage or destroy it*. *United States v. Johnson*, 576 U.S. 531, 538 (2015) (citing *Taylor*, 495 U.S. at 599).

In this case, Furlow was convicted in Georgia of two counts of first-degree arson under Ga. Code § 16-7-10(a). That statute defines first-degree arson as “the malicious burning of a building or structure.” Ga. Code § 16-7-10(a). The term “malicious” is defined in Georgia law as “done intentionally or with reckless disregard for the safety of others.” Ga. Code § 16-1-6(1). This statutory language is broader than the generic definition of arson, as it includes reckless conduct, which does not satisfy the *intentional* element required by *Taylor* and *Johnson*.

The Supreme Court has held that a conviction under a statute that criminalizes both intentional and reckless conduct cannot qualify as a “crime of violence” under the ACCA or the Guidelines if the statute is not limited to intentional conduct. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 538 (“A conviction under a statute that criminalizes both intentional and reckless conduct cannot qualify as a ‘crime of violence’ unless the statute is limited to intentional conduct.”). Smith*, 857 F.3d 178, 184 (4th Cir. 2017) (holding that a North Carolina arson conviction under a statute criminalizing both intentional and reckless conduct did not qualify as a “crime of violence” under the Guidelines).
