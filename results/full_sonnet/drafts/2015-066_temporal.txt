**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings, and Bryant's Tribal Convictions Were Therefore Not Obtained in Violation of Any Constitutional Right to Counsel.**

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel constrains the federal government and, through incorporation, the States. It has never bound Indian tribes, whose criminal jurisdiction over their own members rests on a sovereignty that predates the Constitution. In *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376 (1896), this Court held that the constitutional Bill of Rights does not govern the internal proceedings of tribal courts, explaining that the powers exercised by tribal governments "existed prior to the Constitution of the United States." *Id.* at 384. Because tribal sovereignty is "not operated upon by the Constitution," *id.*, a tribal-court conviction obtained without counsel is not a conviction "obtained in violation of" the Sixth Amendment—it is simply not subject to the Amendment at all.

This conclusion follows directly from the unique constitutional status of tribes recognized since *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556 (1883), which respected tribal self-governance over "the internal and social relations of tribal life," *id.* at 571, and *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375 (1886), which described tribes as "a separate people, with the power of regulating their internal and social relations." *Id.* at 381–82. A conviction rendered by a body outside the Constitution's reach cannot be deemed constitutionally "infirm," because there is no constitutional baseline against which to measure it. The premise of the Ninth Circuit's rule—that Bryant's tribal convictions would have been unconstitutional had they occurred in state or federal court—proves nothing, because they did not occur in state or federal court, and the Sixth Amendment supplies no metric for tribal proceedings.

**II. Using a Valid Prior Conviction to Establish Recidivist Status Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment Because the Federal Prosecution Punishes Only the Instant Offense, for Which Bryant Received Full Constitutional Protection.**

Even apart from the inapplicability of the Sixth Amendment to tribal proceedings, this Court has long recognized that recidivist statutes do not punish the defendant for his prior conduct; they punish him for his present offense, aggravated by his demonstrated persistence in unlawful conduct. In *McDonald v. Massachusetts*, 180 U.S. 311 (1901), the Court explained that in a habitual-offender scheme "the punishment is for the last offence committed, and it is rendered more severe in consequence of the situation in which the party has placed himself by his own conduct." *Id.* at 313. *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616 (1912), reaffirmed that recidivist statutes rest on "the persistency of the offender," not a re-litigation of the earlier crimes. *Id.* at 631. Section 117(a) does the same: it makes tribal recidivism an element bearing on the defendant's status, not an occasion to reopen or validate the prior tribal proceedings themselves.

Because the prior convictions serve only an evidentiary, status-defining function, the constitutional protections that matter are those afforded in the proceeding where guilt of the *charged* offense is actually adjudicated—here, the federal prosecution under § 117(a), in which Bryant was represented by counsel throughout. This Court's recidivism jurisprudence confirms that due process is satisfied so long as the defendant receives notice and an opportunity to contest the recidivist allegation in that proceeding. *Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448 (1962), held that a defendant charged as a habitual offender "must receive reasonable notice and an opportunity to be heard relative to the recidivist charge," *id.* at 452, not a fresh right to counsel in the predicate proceedings themselves. *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), likewise upheld the use of prior convictions to establish habitual-offender status, recognizing the state's "substantial interest" in dealing with recidivism through such proof. *Id.* at 556, 567. Bryant received exactly the process those cases require: counsel, notice, and a full opportunity to contest the § 117(a) charge, including the fact and validity of his tribal convictions.

**III. Imposing a Sixth Amendment Counsel Requirement on Tribal Proceedings as a Condition of Their Use in Federal Court Disregards the Limited, Proceeding-Specific Scope of the Right to Counsel.**

*Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), described the right to counsel as securing the assistance of counsel "in all criminal prosecutions" conducted by the federal courts, *id.* at 462—a right defined by, and confined to, the proceeding in which the government seeks a conviction against the accused. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), extended that guarantee to state felony prosecutions because, "in our adversary system of criminal justice, any person haled into court, who is too poor to hire a lawyer, cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided." *Id.* at 344. Both decisions speak to the proceeding in which the individual's liberty is directly at stake in that forum. Neither purports to reach backward and invalidate, for use in an entirely separate sovereign's later prosecution, a conviction rendered by a tribunal to which the Sixth Amendment never applied. Extending *Gideon* and *Zerbst* in that fashion would convert a right tied to a particular adversarial proceeding into a freestanding rule of evidentiary disqualification untethered from any actual constitutional violation—precisely the result the Ninth Circuit reached, and precisely the result this Court's precedents do not support.
