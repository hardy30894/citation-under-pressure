ARGUMENT

I. WARN Act Rights May Not Be Extinguished by an Unsupervised Private Release.

The district court erred in treating the General Waiver and Release as a complete bar to Appellants’ claims under the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101-2109. WARN, like the Fair Labor Standards Act, confers rights that exist not merely for the private benefit of the employee but to vindicate a broader public interest — here, in ensuring that workers and their communities receive advance notice of mass layoffs so they can plan for the transition. The Supreme Court has long recognized that statutory protections of this kind cannot be bargained away without independent oversight. In Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O’Neil, 324 U.S. 697 (1945), the Court held that an employee’s FLSA rights could not be waived by private settlement because doing so would allow “the allowing of waivers . . . [to] nullify the purposes of the Act.” Brooklyn Savings Bank, 324 U.S. at 707. The Court reaffirmed this principle in Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight System, Inc., 450 U.S. 728 (1981), explaining that rights created by remedial labor legislation designed to protect a class of workers as a whole are not subject to private waiver through collective bargaining or otherwise. WARN serves precisely this kind of collective, community-oriented purpose, and the sixty days of notice it requires cannot be surrendered through a release negotiated and executed within days of a plant-closing announcement, without any court or agency approval of the settlement's adequacy.

II. Even Assuming WARN Claims Are Waivable, the Releases Here Were Not Knowing and Voluntary.

Even if this Court concludes that WARN claims may be released under appropriate circumstances, the releases at issue fail under the "totality of the circumstances" standard this Circuit applies to determine whether a waiver of statutory employment rights was knowing and voluntary. See Pierce v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co., 65 F.3d 562, 571 (7th Cir. 1995) (adopting a multi-factor totality-of-the-circumstances test, including the clarity of the agreement, the employee's education and business experience, the amount of time the employee had to consider the agreement, and whether the employee was encouraged to consult an attorney). The factors relevant to that inquiry — drawn from cases such as Bormann v. AT&T Communications, Inc., 875 F.2d 399, 403 (2d Cir. 1989), and adopted in this Circuit in Pierce — include the amount of time the employee had to consider the waiver, whether the employee had input into its terms, and whether the consideration given exceeded what the employee was already entitled to receive. Here, drivers eligible for the ten-week package were forced to decide, simultaneously with an unrelated job-rebid decision, in a matter of days, whether to surrender all WARN rights, with no opportunity for individualized negotiation. This truncated timeline mirrors the compressed circumstances that other courts have found insufficient to support a valid waiver of statutory rights. See Runyan v. National Cash Register Corp., 787 F.2d 1039, 1044 (6th Cir. 1986) (en banc) (emphasizing that adequate time for reflection is central to a knowing and voluntary waiver).

Moreover, the Supreme Court's decision in Oubre v. Entergy Operations, Inc., 522 U.S. 422 (1998), confirms that when a release fails to satisfy the statutory or judicially imposed requirements for a valid waiver, the employee's acceptance of severance benefits does not itself ratify an otherwise defective release; nor must the employee tender back the consideration received before suing. Oubre, 522 U.S. at 426–27 ("The employer cannot ratify a defective waiver merely by paying the employee no more than the employer already owed."). The same logic applies with equal force to WARN: the fact that the Appellants accepted severance pay does not cure the coercive, compressed process by which DHL extracted their releases, particularly where — as with the ten-week plan — the payments were expressly conditioned on relinquishing rights the workers did not yet know they possessed. See D.H. Overmyer Co. v. Frick Co., 405 U.S. 174, 185–86 (1972) (a waiver of important legal rights must be made "voluntarily, intelligently and knowingly").

III. DHL Violated WARN's Sixty-Day Notice Requirement and Cannot Invoke the Unforeseeable Business Circumstances Exception.

Independent of the release issue, DHL's conduct itself violated WARN. The statute requires sixty days' advance written notice of a plant closing or mass layoff. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). DHL's November 10, 2008 announcement, followed by a January 30, 2009 cessation of domestic service, did not provide the required notice to the affected Chicagoland facilities, which had already been shedding jobs since August — well before any public announcement. This Court has recognized that the "unforeseeable business circumstances" exception under 29 U.S.C. § 2102(b)(2)(A) is narrow and fact-intensive, turning on whether the circumstances were "not reasonably foreseeable" at the time notice would otherwise have been required. Roquet v. Arthur Andersen LLP, 398 F.3d 585, 588 (7th Cir. 2005). Similarly, the Fourth Circuit in Burns v. AAF-McQuay, Inc., 166 F.3d 292, 296 (4th Cir. 1999), instructs that the exception applies only where a sudden, dramatic, and unexpected business circumstance caused the layoff. DHL's decision followed a sustained, months-long deterioration of its domestic operations, not a sudden, unforeseeable event, and the record — including layoffs beginning in August 2008 — establishes that the closing was foreseeable well before the sixty-day window began.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded.
