The Bankruptcy Act of 1898 excepts from discharge only those debts “created by [the debtor’s] fraud, embezzlement, misappropriation, or defalcation while acting as an officer or in any fiduciary capacity.” Bankruptcy Act of 1898, ch. 541, § 17a(2), 30 Stat. 544, 550. This Court has long held that such exceptions “should be confined to those plainly expressed,” Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915), and that “the fraud referred to in that section means positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong,” Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877).

The statute reaches only the money or property “obtained by” the fraud. Where a debtor has secured a sum through misrepresentation, the exception operates solely upon that sum. Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176, 193 (1904). Additional awards imposed by statute or judgment—whether denominated treble, exemplary, or punitive—stand outside the language of the exception. They are not money “obtained by” the fraud; they are penalties assessed after the fact. See Forsyth v. Vehmeyer, 177 U.S. 177, 181 (1900) (limiting nondischargeability to the debt actually procured by deceit).

The fresh-start policy of the Act reinforces this limit. A discharge “releases the honest debtor from his debts” and affords “a new opportunity in life,” Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 236 U.S. 549, 554–55 (1915). Allowing statutory multipliers to survive discharge would enlarge the exception beyond its text and frustrate that purpose.

The rent overcharges here were the only sums obtained by the alleged misrepresentation. The treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs awarded under state law are separate sanctions. Because they were not “obtained by” fraud, they fall outside § 17a(2) and are dischargeable.

The judgment below should be reversed to the extent it declares the non-compensatory award nondischargeable.
