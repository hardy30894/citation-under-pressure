# ARGUMENT

## A CONVICTION UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) REQUIRES PROOF THAT THE DEFENDANT SUBJECTIVELY INTENDED HIS STATEMENT AS A THREAT

### A. The Presumption in Favor of Scienter Requires Reading a Subjective Intent Element into Section 875(c)

This Court has long recognized that "the requirement of some mens rea for a crime is firmly embedded in the maxims of the common law." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Where a statute does not specify a mental state, courts must "read into the statute only that mens rea which is necessary to separate wrongful conduct from otherwise innocent conduct." *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994). Section 875(c) criminalizes transmission of "any communication containing any threat," but is silent as to the state of mind the speaker must possess. The Third Circuit filled this silence with an *objective* negligence standard—asking only whether a reasonable person would foresee that his words would be interpreted as threatening. That construction collapses the distinction between one who intends to threaten and one who is simply careless or misunderstood, thereby criminalizing the latter along with the former.

This Court's decision in *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600 (1994), instructs that mens rea requirements are especially critical where, absent such a requirement, "a defendant who was mistaken as to the legal consequences of his otherwise knowing conduct" could be convicted for conduct he did not know was wrongful. *Id.* at 614–15. Elonis's Facebook posts—rap-influenced, hyperbolic, and posted to a public forum where he say he intended only to vent—present precisely the risk *Staples* identified: criminal liability without any showing that the speaker intended to threaten.

### B. This Court's True-Threats Jurisprudence Requires Subjective Intent

The Third Circuit relied on its own precedent in *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), holding a statement is a "true threat" whenever "a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted . . . as a threat." App. Op. at 2. But this objective standard cannot survive this Court's decision in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003).

In *Black*, this Court defined "true threats" as "statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." *Id.* at 359 (emphasis added). The word "means" is a term of subjective purpose—it asks what the speaker intended to communicate, not what a hypothetical reasonable listener might have perceived. This Court went on to hold that a Virginia cross-burning statute violated the First Amendment because it presumed intent to intimidate from the act of burning a cross alone, without requiring the State to prove that the particular defendant burned the cross with the intent to threaten. *Id.* at 363–66. If the First Amendment forbids presuming subjective intent to intimidate from a symbolic act, it necessarily forbids convicting a defendant of making a "threat" without any proof he intended to threaten.

This concern for chilling protected speech runs throughout this Court's First Amendment precedents. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), the Court reversed a conviction under a threat statute where the defendant's statement about the President was "a kind of very crude offensive method of stating a political opposition," holding that the statute "must be interpreted with the commands of the First Amendment clearly in mind." *Id.* at 707. And in *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46 (1988), this Court emphasized that "the sort of robust political debate encouraged by the First Amendment is bound to produce speech that is critical of those who hold public office," *id.* at 51, counseling against a rule of liability that punishes speech based on how it is received rather than what the speaker meant.

An objective, reasonable-speaker standard converts § 875(c) into precisely the kind of strict-liability speech regulation that this Court's precedents forbid. Under the Third Circuit's rule, a speaker who is understood as threatening—regardless of what he actually intended—commits a federal felony. That standard punishes negligent communication, not intentional threats, and sweeps within its reach protected expression such as rap lyrics, dark humor, and hyperbole that a reasonable observer might, mistakenly, construe as a threat.

### C. The Objective Standard Cannot Be Reconciled with the Statute's Structure or This Court's Mens Rea Precedents

Every other subsection of § 875 requires a showing of intent to extort, see 18 U.S.C. § 875(a)-(b), (d), and there is no textual basis for concluding that Congress intended subsection (c) alone to dispense with a scienter requirement respecting the threatening nature of the communication. This Court has cautioned against interpretations that would "criminalize a broad range of apparently innocent conduct." *Liparota v. United States*, 471 U.S. 419, 426 (1985). The objective standard applied below does exactly that: it transforms § 875(c) from a statute punishing those who intend to instill fear into one punishing careless, misunderstood, or artistically ambiguous speech.

Requiring subjective intent does not leave threats unpunished. The jury may still infer intent from context, repetition, and surrounding circumstances, just as intent is proven in any other criminal case. *See Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 274-76. What subjective intent forecloses is a conviction resting solely on how a statement was received, untethered to what the defendant meant to convey.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial under proper instructions requiring the jury to find that Elonis subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats.
