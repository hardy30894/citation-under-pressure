The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore Act coverage only where the injury occurs on the outer Continental Shelf. The statute’s text limits benefits to “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This language imposes a geographic limitation on the place of injury, consistent with the principle that “Congress has power to alter and extend the maritime law” only within defined bounds. Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205, 215 (1917).

Congress enacted the Longshore Act against a background of strict situs requirements. The Court has repeatedly held that state workmen’s compensation laws apply to injuries occurring on land, while federal maritime remedies govern only injuries on navigable waters. State Industrial Comm’n v. Nordenholt Corp., 259 U.S. 263, 275 (1922); Grant Smith-Porter Ship Co. v. Rohde, 257 U.S. 469, 477 (1922). These decisions establish that the place of injury determines the applicable compensation regime. Knickerbocker Ice Co. v. Stewart, 253 U.S. 149, 160 (1920).

When Congress extended Longshore Act coverage to the outer Continental Shelf, it preserved this geographic distinction. The extension applies only to operations conducted on the Shelf itself. Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 38 (1932) (noting that the Longshore Act “is limited to injuries occurring upon the navigable waters”). Later decisions confirmed that coverage turns on the location where the injury is sustained. Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc., 314 U.S. 244, 247 (1941); Calbeck v. Travelers Insurance Co., 370 U.S. 114, 126 (1962).

The same principle controls here. An injury sustained at an onshore facility three miles from the coast does not occur “on the outer Continental Shelf.” Nacirema Operating Co. v. Johnson, 396 U.S. 212, 218 (1969) (emphasizing that “Congress intended to provide a uniform compensation system for maritime employees injured on navigable waters”). Allowing recovery for land-based injuries would expand the statute beyond its text and undermine the situs limitation that has governed maritime compensation since Jensen.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test finds no support in the statute or in the decisions interpreting analogous provisions before 1970. The judgment should be reversed.
