# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT COMMAND STATES TO ADMIT EXPERT MENTAL-STATE EVIDENCE TO NEGATE MENS REA.

The Fourteenth Amendment leaves to the States the authority to define the elements of criminal offenses and to determine the relevance of particular evidence to those elements. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952). In *Leland*, this Court upheld Oregon's requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting the claim that due process required the State to bear the burden on that issue. *Id.* at 799-800. The Court expressly recognized that "the defense of insanity is separate from the issue of specific intent." *Id.* at 795. Arizona has made the same judgment: evidence of mental disease or defect is relevant to the affirmative defense of insanity, not to the *mens rea* element.

This Court has long distinguished between whether a defendant formed the requisite intent and whether he was so mentally disordered that he should be excused from responsibility. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 487 (1895) (holding that the presumption of sanity "may be overcome by proof that the accused is insane" but that the government bears no burden to prove sanity absent sufficient evidence). That distinction reflects a substantive judgment: a defendant who acts intentionally but under delusion may be blameless, while a defendant who cannot form intent is not guilty as defined. A State may constitutionally enforce that distinction by limiting the evidentiary avenue through which mental illness may be presented.

The States possess broad authority to define rules of evidence governing criminal trials. *Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 475-76 (1948) (recognizing that "the trial court has [wide] discretion" in evidentiary rulings and that "the wisdom of the policy" behind evidentiary rules "is for the legislature to determine"). Arizona's decision to confine expert mental-state evidence to the insanity defense is a permissible exercise of that authority.

## II. ARIZONA'S RULE DOES NOT RELIEVE THE STATE OF ITS BURDEN TO PROVE MENS REA.

The State bore and met its burden of proving that Eric Clark intentionally killed the officer. The rule excluding expert mental-state evidence from the *mens rea* determination did not shift or reduce that burden. It merely reflected Arizona's determination that mental disease or defect, however severe, is not evidence that a defendant lacked the cognitive capacity to form intent—it is evidence that he should be excused from punishment for his intentional act.

This Court has consistently held that States may define the mental element of a crime without reference to abnormal mental conditions. In *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 583 (1884), the Court observed that a defendant's "mental condition may be so disordered that he cannot form a criminal intent," but recognized that the State may treat that condition as an excuse rather than as a negation of an element. In *Hotema v. United States*, 186 U.S. 413, 418 (1902), the Court upheld a jury instruction requiring the defendant to prove "irresistible impulse" to the satisfaction of the jury, even though the government retained the burden on general intent. These cases refute the notion that due process requires a unitary treatment of all mental-state evidence.

The trial court's discretion to exclude evidence that is irrelevant or misleading is well established. *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 463 (1946) (per curiam) ("the trial court has a wide discretion in determining the admissibility of evidence"). Arizona's rule is a permissible exercise of that discretion, codified by the legislature.

## III. THE STATES HAVE LONG BEEN PERMITTED TO STRUCTURE THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN INSANITY AND MENS REA.

Since the early days of the Republic, the States have exercised broad authority to define the relationship between mental illness and criminal responsibility. In *Salinger v. United States*, 272 U.S. 542, 545 (1926), this Court reaffirmed the common-law rule that "the burden of proving insanity" rests on the defendant "and that such burden is not satisfied unless the proof is sufficient to establish it." The rule Clark challenges is merely the converse: evidence insufficient to prove insanity may not be repurposed to negate *mens rea*.

The foundational case of *M'Naghten's Case*, 10 Cl. & F. 200, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (H.L. 1843), which has shaped American insanity law, established that the defense of insanity asks whether the defendant knew the nature and quality of his act or knew that it was wrong. That inquiry is distinct from the question whether the defendant intended to commit the act. Arizona's rule faithfully preserves that distinction.

In *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 532 (1968), this Court declined to impose a constitutional rule requiring States to treat chronic alcoholism as a defense to public intoxication. The Court emphasized that "the criminal law" is not "powerless to punish" conduct "committed under circumstances which would normally be deemed criminal." *Id.* So too here: Arizona may punish a defendant who intentionally kills, even if his intent was influenced by mental illness. The State may address such illness through the insanity defense rather than by fracturing the *mens rea* element.

## IV. CLARK'S PROPOSED RIGHT WOULD UNDERMINE THE STATE'S CAREFULLY BALANCED INSANITY SCHEME.

Arizona requires a defendant asserting insanity to prove by clear and convincing evidence that he was unable to appreciate the wrongfulness of his conduct. Allowing the same evidence to negate *mens rea* would permit a defendant to circumvent that heightened burden and the specialized adjudication of insanity. This Court has never held that due process requires such a result. To the contrary, in *Leland*, the Court upheld a scheme requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt—a standard *more* demanding than Arizona's—without any separate channel for the evidence in the *mens rea* context. 343 U.S. at 798-99.

The distinction between *mens rea* and insanity is "not a constitutional afterthought" but a reflection of the State's sovereign judgment about the limits of criminal responsibility. *Powell*, 392 U.S. at 535. The Due Process Clause does not require Arizona to dismantle that judgment.

## CONCLUSION

Because Arizona's rule respects the distinct roles of *mens rea* and insanity, does not relieve the State of its burden of proof, and reflects a balanced legislative judgment rooted in centuries of common law, it does not violate the Due Process Clause. The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
