**ARGUMENT**

**THE PAT-FRISK OF SOARES VIOLATED THE FOURTH AMENDMENT BECAUSE THE OFFICERS LACKED SPECIFIC AND ARTICULABLE FACTS GIVING RISE TO A REASONABLE BELIEF THAT SOARES WAS ARMED AND DANGEROUS**

The Fourth Amendment protects "[t]he right of the people to be secure in their persons ... against unreasonable searches and seizures." A pat-frisk is a search within the meaning of that guarantee. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 16 (1968). In Terry, the Supreme Court held that a police officer may conduct a limited search for weapons only where the officer "is able to point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." Id. at 21. The Court was explicit that the justification for a frisk is distinct from the justification for a stop: even where an investigatory stop is lawful, an officer may not automatically frisk the person stopped. Id. at 27. Rather, "the officer must be able to point to particular facts from which he reasonably inferred that the individual was armed and dangerous." Sibron v. New York, 392 U.S. 40, 64 (1968).

Sibron, decided the same day as Terry, is directly instructive here. The Court reversed a conviction obtained through a frisk because the officer's testimony revealed no facts specific to the defendant suggesting he carried a weapon; the officer had observed only that the defendant spoke with several known addicts. Id. at 62-64. The Court explained that "[t]he police officer is not entitled to seize and search every person whom he sees on the street ... simply because he suspects that a person may be engaged in some sort of criminal activity." Id. at 64. The same principle controls this case. The record establishes no fact particular to Soares indicating he was armed.

First, the "furtive movement" the officers observed was not attributable to Soares individually. Officer Reid testified that both the driver and the front-seat passenger moved their hands—the driver toward the door, the front-seat passenger toward the console—while no movement was observed from the back-seat passenger. This generalized, simultaneous movement by multiple occupants of a car, without any showing tying a movement toward a weapon-like location specifically to Soares, cannot supply the individualized suspicion Terry requires. See Terry, 392 U.S. at 21 n. 18 (emphasizing that the officer's belief must be evaluated in light of "his own experience" as applied to the particular suspect before him). An inference of danger drawn from the conduct of the vehicle's other occupants does not become a lawful basis to frisk Soares merely because he happened to be sitting nearby. Cf. Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 479 (1963) (probable cause "must be founded upon facts which, in the judgment of the court, would make him ... entertain an honest and strong suspicion" specific to the individual searched).

Second, Soares's agitation, rocking, and profane protest that the stop was racially motivated do not supply the missing individualized suspicion. The Terry Court was careful to root the frisk of the defendant in that case in objective conduct consistent with casing a store for a robbery, observed over a period of time, combined with the officer's reasonable inference that men who behave that way "are frequently armed with a weapon." Terry, 392 U.S. at 28. Nothing of that kind occurred here. An arrestee's verbal hostility toward police, without more, is not among the facts Terry or Sibron recognized as indicative of concealed weaponry. Indeed, Sibron itself rejected an attempt to justify a frisk based on the suspect's conduct and words directed at the officer, holding that such generalized unease does not substitute for particularized fact. Sibron, 392 U.S. at 62-63.

Third, to the extent the government relies on the lateness of the hour and the characterization of the area as "high crime," such considerations, standing alone, do not establish reasonable suspicion that a particular occupant of a lawfully stopped car is armed. The Terry standard demands facts "specific" to the individual, not merely facts about the neighborhood or the hour. Terry, 392 U.S. at 21. Cf. Beck v. Ohio, 379 U.S. 89, 91 (1964) (probable cause, like reasonable suspicion, "must be judged... at the moment the arrest was made," based on facts within the officer's own knowledge, not general assumptions about locale). The stop itself was justified by the equipment violation; but the escalation to a frisk of Soares required its own independent, particularized justification, which the government cannot supply from atmospheric factors alone.

Because the officers lacked specific and articulable facts particular to Soares that reasonably warranted a belief he was armed and dangerous, the frisk exceeded the narrow authority recognized in Terry and Sibron. The gun recovered from Soares's person is therefore the fruit of an unconstitutional search. See Wong Sun, 371 U.S. at 484-88 (evidence obtained as the direct result of an unlawful search must be excluded as "fruit of the poisonous tree"). The exclusionary rule announced in Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961), applies with full force to this federal prosecution, and the handgun must be suppressed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's denial of Soares's motion to suppress should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to suppress the handgun and vacate the conviction.
