The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that a plaintiff's failure to read or recall disclosed information can create a genuine dispute of material fact as to whether the plaintiff had "actual knowledge" of an ERISA breach or violation under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The statute's plain language requires only that the plaintiff have "actual knowledge" of the breach or violation, not that they have read or understood the disclosed information.

The Ninth Circuit's decision is inconsistent with this Court's precedent and the decisions of other circuits. In Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), the Ninth Circuit held that "actual knowledge" is triggered by the plaintiff's knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law. Similarly, in Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co., 916 F.2d 548 (9th Cir. 1990), the court held that the plaintiff had "actual knowledge" of the breach when they were informed of the transaction, even if they did not understand its implications.

Other circuits have also held that "actual knowledge" under § 1113(2) requires only that the plaintiff have knowledge of the underlying facts, not that they have read or understood the disclosed information. See, e.g., Gluck v. Unisys Corp., 960 F.2d 1168, 1177 (3d Cir. 1992) ("actual knowledge" requires "knowledge of all relevant facts"); Martin v. Consultants & Administrators, Inc., 966 F.2d 1078, 1086 (7th Cir. 1992) ("actual knowledge" requires "knowledge of the transaction or conduct constituting the breach").

In this case, Intel disclosed all relevant information about the investments to Sulyma through various documents and website disclosures. Sulyma's failure to read or recall this information does not create a genuine dispute of material fact as to whether he had "actual knowledge" of the alleged breach. The district court correctly granted summary judgment to Intel, and the Ninth Circuit erred in reversing that decision.

The Ninth Circuit's decision will undermine the purpose of § 1113(2) by allowing plaintiffs to delay filing suit indefinitely, even after being provided with full disclosure of the relevant facts. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that "actual knowledge" under § 1113(2) is triggered by the disclosure of the relevant facts, regardless of whether the plaintiff reads or understands that information.
