**Argument**

The First Amendment protects speech unless it is a "true threat." A "true threat" requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten. The objective standard applied by the Third Circuit creates a "chilling effect" on speech and fails to account for the First Amendment's protection of artistic expression and private venting.

The Supreme Court has long held that speech is not protected if it is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action. In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444 (1969), the Court held that the government may only punish speech that is "directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action" and is "likely to incite or produce such action." This standard requires a subjective intent to incite lawless action. Therefore, a "true threat"—which is a subset of speech directed to imminent lawless action—must also require a subjective intent to threaten.

The Court in *Gillespie v. New York*, 236 U.S. 730 (1915), emphasized that statutes punishing threats must be applied to "actual threats" and not "mere criticism." The Court noted that the statute must be "not so broad as to permit the punishment of the innocent." An objective standard, which punishes speech based on how a reasonable person might interpret it, risks punishing innocent speech. "Actual threats" implies a specific intent to communicate a threat, not merely a statement that a reasonable person might misinterpret.

Finally, the objective standard allows the government to punish speech based on the "fear of disturbance" which is the "fear of the mob." In *Whitney v. California*, 274 U.S. 357 (1927), Justice Brandeis warned that the law should protect speech to prevent the "fear of the mob" from silencing dissent. An objective standard permits the government to punish speech based on the subjective fear of the listener, which is exactly what the First Amendment seeks to prevent. Therefore, a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten.
