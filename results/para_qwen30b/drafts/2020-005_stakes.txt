**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the privileges of conducting activities within the forum state, such that the litigation arises out of or relates to those activities. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This principle ensures that a defendant is not subject to the jurisdiction of a court unless it has established minimum contacts with the forum such that the exercise of jurisdiction does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *Id.* at 316. In this case, Ford Motor Company has no such contacts with Montana that are causally connected to the claims asserted by the estate of Markkaya Jean Gullett. The injury occurred in Montana, but the vehicle was never sold, serviced, or advertised there. Ford did not design, manufacture, or distribute the vehicle in Montana. The tire failure that caused the accident was not the result of any conduct by Ford within Montana. Therefore, the exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Ford by the Montana courts violates due process.

The Montana Supreme Court’s decision rests on a fundamental misapplication of the “arise out of or relate to” standard established in *Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783 (1984), and reaffirmed in *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117 (2014). In *Calder*, the Court held that a defendant’s conduct must be “purposefully directed” at the forum state, and that the plaintiff’s claims must “arise out of or relate to” those contacts. *Calder*, 465 U.S. at 789. The mere fact that a product may eventually be used in a state is insufficient to establish jurisdiction. *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987). In *Daimler*, the Court emphasized that “a corporation’s general connection with a State is not enough to justify the exercise of specific jurisdiction over it.” *Daimler*, 571 U.S. at 137. The Court reiterated that “specific jurisdiction requires a direct relationship between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” *Id.* at 138.

Here, Ford’s only connection to Montana was the eventual presence of the vehicle in the state after it was sold through a chain of transactions that began in Washington and continued through Oregon. Ford did not sell the vehicle in Montana, nor did it advertise or market it there. It did not maintain a place of business, employ agents, or engage in any other activity within Montana. The vehicle was not assembled in Montana, nor was it ever serviced or repaired there. The tire failure occurred during normal use on a Montana highway, but the defect was not caused by any act or omission by Ford within Montana. The causal link between Ford’s conduct and the injury is severed by the intervening chain of sales and the absence of any purposeful activity by Ford in Montana.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on Ford’s sale of parts within the state is legally untenable. The sale of replacement parts, even if it occurs in Montana, does not establish the kind of purposeful availment required for specific jurisdiction. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985). The sale of parts is a routine, incidental activity that does not constitute a purposeful direction of conduct toward the forum state. Moreover, the sale of parts is not causally related to the design defect alleged in this case. The claim is not that Ford failed to provide adequate parts, but that the vehicle’s design—specifically, the tire tread separation—was defective. The sale of parts in Montana cannot be said to “arise out of or relate to” a design defect that occurred during the vehicle’s use in Montana, especially when the defect was not discovered or caused by any activity in the state.

The Court’s decision in *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 137 S. Ct. 1773 (2017), is directly on point. There, the Supreme Court held that a California court could not exercise specific jurisdiction over a pharmaceutical company based on claims brought by non-California residents whose injuries occurred outside the state. The Court emphasized that “the relationship between the defendant’s conduct and the plaintiff’s injury must be such that the defendant’s actions were directed at the forum state.” *Id.* at 1780. The Court rejected the notion that a defendant’s general presence in a state—such as selling products there—was sufficient to justify jurisdiction over claims arising from injuries elsewhere. *Id.* at 1781. The same principle applies here: Ford’s general presence in Montana through the sale of parts does not justify jurisdiction over a claim arising from a tire failure that occurred in Montana but was not caused by any activity in the state.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed itself of the privilege of doing business” in Montana is unsupported by precedent and contradicts the constitutional limits on jurisdiction. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 292 (1980), held that “a defendant’s conduct must be such that he should reasonably anticipate being haled into court in the forum state.” Ford did not reasonably anticipate being sued in Montana. The vehicle was not sold there, and Ford had no reason to believe that its products would be used in Montana in a way that would lead to litigation. The mere fact that a product may be used in a state does not create jurisdiction. *Id.* at 297.

The Due Process Clause protects defendants from being subjected to litigation in states where they have not purposefully established contacts. To hold otherwise would allow any state to assert jurisdiction over any out-of-state defendant whose product is eventually used there, regardless of the defendant’s connection to the forum. Such a rule would undermine the predictability and fairness that due process requires. *Id.* at 294.

For these reasons, the Montana Supreme Court’s exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company violates the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The claims do not arise out of or relate to any of Ford’s activities in Montana. The injury was not caused by any conduct within the state.
