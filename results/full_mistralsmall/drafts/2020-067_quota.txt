**ARGUMENT**

I. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Applied the Plain-Error Standard and Did Not Exceed Its Appellate Authority by Considering the Entire Record

A. Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), a court of appeals may correct a forfeited error only if it is plain and affects substantial rights. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 731–34 (1993). To demonstrate prejudice, a defendant must show a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different. *Id.* at 734. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that Greer failed to meet this burden.

B. The Eleventh Circuit did not err by considering the entire trial record—including Greer’s prior convictions and the indictment—to assess prejudice. *See United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258, 262 (2010) (explaining that appellate courts may review the entire record to determine whether an error was prejudicial). The court did not rely on extra-record evidence; it merely examined the record as a whole to evaluate the impact of the error. *Cf. United States v. Vazquez*, 918 F.3d 1192, 1200 (11th Cir. 2019) (permitting consideration of the full record to assess prejudice under plain-error review).

C. The Eleventh Circuit’s analysis did not violate the Fifth Amendment’s due process guarantee. *See United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 80–81 (2004) (holding that plain-error review does not implicate due process concerns where the error is forfeited, not waived). The court’s consideration of the record was consistent with the well-established principle that appellate courts may examine the entire record to determine whether an error undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. *See Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 466–67 (1997) (discussing the fourth prong of plain-error review).

II. Greer Failed to Demonstrate Prejudice Under the Fourth Prong of Plain-Error Review

A. The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that Greer could not show prejudice because the evidence of his guilt was overwhelming. *See United States v. Veltmann*, 6 F.3d 1483, 1487 (11th Cir. 1993) (holding that an error is not prejudicial if the evidence of guilt is overwhelming). The record reflects that Greer was found in possession of a firearm, and his prior felony convictions were stipulated to in the plea agreement. *See United States v. Dominguez*, 229 F.3d 1239, 1242 (11th Cir. 2000) (finding no prejudice where the evidence of guilt was overwhelming).

B. The Eleventh Circuit did not err in considering Greer’s prior convictions to assess prejudice. *See United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. at 262 (permitting consideration of the entire record to determine prejudice). The court’s analysis was consistent with the principle that appellate courts may examine the record as a whole to evaluate the impact of an error. *See United States v. Vazquez*, 918 F.3d at 1200.

C. The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that the error did not affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial was not an abuse of discretion. *See United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736 (explaining that the fourth prong of plain-error review is discretionary). The court’s analysis was consistent with the principle that an error does not warrant reversal unless it seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. *See Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. at 467.

III. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Err in Denying Relief Under 28 U.S.C. § 2255

A. Greer’s claim under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 failed because he could not demonstrate that the error had a substantial and injurious effect on his sentence. *See Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 637 (1993) (applying the harmless-error standard to § 2255 petitions). The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that Greer failed to meet this burden.

B. The Eleventh Circuit’s denial of relief was consistent with the principle that a § 2255 petitioner must show actual prejudice to obtain relief. *See United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 170 (1982) (holding that a petitioner must demonstrate that the error worked a miscarriage of justice). The court’s analysis was consistent with this standard.

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit properly applied the plain-error standard and did not exceed its appellate authority by considering the entire record. Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice under the fourth prong of plain-error review, and the Eleventh Circuit did not err in denying relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
