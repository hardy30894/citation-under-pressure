## ARGUMENT

### A. Constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951

The appellant, Dominic G. Pearson, challenges the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951, arguing that Congress exceeded its authority under the Commerce Clause in enacting this statute. The Hobbs Act, codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1951, prohibits robbery or extortion that affects interstate commerce. Mr. Pearson contends that Congress lacked the constitutional authority to regulate such local crimes.

The Commerce Clause grants Congress the power to regulate commerce among the states. U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3. The Supreme Court has long recognized that Congress's authority under the Commerce Clause extends to activities that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce. In United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), the Court held that Congress may regulate intrastate activities that have a close and substantial relation to interstate commerce. The Court emphasized that the power to regulate commerce is not limited to transactions that occur entirely within interstate commerce but also extends to local activities that affect it. Id. at 119.

In United States v. Wrightwood Dairy Co., 315 U.S. 110 (1942), the Court further clarified the scope of Congress's Commerce Clause authority, stating that "the commerce power is not confined in its exercise to the regulation of commerce among the states" but also extends to local activities that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce. Id. at 119. The Court has also upheld the constitutionality of statutes regulating local activities that affect interstate commerce, such as the Fair Labor Standards Act, which was challenged in Darby.

Applying these principles to the Hobbs Act, it is clear that Congress had the authority to enact § 1951. The statute regulates robbery and extortion that affect interstate commerce, which is within the scope of Congress's Commerce Clause authority. The evidence presented at trial, including the testimony of Mr. Peterson, the owner of Mr. Goodcents, established that the robbery affected interstate commerce, as the stolen money would have been used to purchase goods produced outside of Kansas. This connection to interstate commerce brings the Hobbs Act within the ambit of Congress's constitutional authority.

The appellant's challenge to the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951 is therefore without merit. The statute is a valid exercise of Congress's Commerce Clause authority, as it regulates local activities that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce. The conviction should be upheld.

### B. Refusal to Allow Tardy Alibi Defense

Mr. Pearson also argues that the district court erred in refusing to allow him to submit a tardy alibi defense. However, the record shows that Mr. Pearson had ample opportunity to present his alibi defense through other means, including his testimony about being at the mall jewelry store having his watch repaired. The district court's decision to exclude the alibi defense was within its discretion, and Mr. Pearson has not shown that the exclusion was prejudicial.

### C. Sufficiency of the Evidence

The appellant also challenges the sufficiency of the evidence supporting his conviction. However, the testimony of Courtney Martin, Deborah Meyer, and Angela Starks, among others, provided sufficient evidence to support the conviction. The jury was entitled to weigh the credibility of these witnesses and consider the evidence presented.

### D. Jury Instructions on Felony Murder

Mr. Pearson argues that the district court erroneously instructed the jury on the elements of felony murder. However, the instructions given by the district court accurately reflected the law, and Mr. Pearson has not shown that the instructions were prejudicial.

### E. Refusal to Submit Lesser Included Offense Instructions

Finally, Mr. Pearson argues that the district court erred in refusing to submit his requested lesser included offense instructions to the jury. However, the district court's decision was within its discretion, and Mr. Pearson has not shown that the refusal was prejudicial.

For the foregoing reasons, the appellant's conviction should be upheld.
