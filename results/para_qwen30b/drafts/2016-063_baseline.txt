**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—is one of first impression in this Court and of profound consequence to federal employees seeking redress for unlawful discrimination. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in this case, which holds that such dismissals are reviewable only in the Federal Circuit, is irreconcilable with the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596 (2012), and with the statutory text, structure, and purpose of the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA). The Court should reverse.

### I. *Kloeckner* Overruled the D.C. Circuit’s Precedent in *Powell* and Eviscerated the Jurisdictional/Procedural Distinction

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), to uphold the Federal Circuit’s exclusive jurisdiction over jurisdictional dismissals is no longer tenable in light of *Kloeckner*. In *Kloeckner*, the Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on a *procedural* ground—such as untimeliness—judicial review lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit. 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Court did so not merely to resolve a circuit split, but to ensure that employees alleging discrimination are not deprived of the district court forum Congress intended for mixed cases. Id. at 604–605.

The D.C. Circuit attempts to distinguish *Kloeckner* by asserting that the case involved only a “procedural” dismissal, while Perry’s case involved a “jurisdictional” one. But this distinction is not only legally unsound—it is doctrinally unsustainable in light of *Kloeckner*. The Court in *Kloeckner* explicitly recognized that the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is not a matter of judicial invention, but one rooted in the statutory framework. The Court emphasized that the key inquiry under § 7702(a)(1)(A) is whether the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” 133 S. Ct. at 604. In *Kloeckner*, the employee was affected by a removal—clearly an appealable action—despite the untimeliness of her appeal. The Court held that the procedural defect did not strip the case of its mixed-case character. Id.

The same logic applies here. Perry was affected by a suspension and retirement—actions that are appealable to the MSPB if not voluntary. 5 U.S.C. § 7512(1). The Board’s dismissal was not based on a lack of jurisdiction over the *action*, but on a factual determination that the action was *voluntary*. That determination, however, is not a jurisdictional bar—it is a merits-based inquiry into the voluntariness of the employee’s acceptance of the action. As the Federal Circuit has recognized, “the Board’s jurisdiction depends on whether the action was voluntary,” and such a determination “is not a jurisdictional issue in the traditional sense.” *Conforto v. Merit Sys. Prot. Bd.*, 713 F.3d 1113, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013). The Board’s jurisdiction is not defeated by the employee’s voluntary acceptance; rather, the Board must *determine* whether the acceptance was truly voluntary. That determination is not a jurisdictional threshold—it is a factual issue that goes to the merits of the claim.

Thus, *Kloeckner* did not merely address procedural dismissals; it redefined the scope of what constitutes a “mixed case” under § 7702(a)(1)(A). The Court made clear that the statutory inquiry is not whether the Board *has* jurisdiction, but whether the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). In both *Kloeckner* and this case, the employee was affected by an appealable action. The only difference is that in *Kloeckner*, the Board dismissed on timeliness grounds; here, it dismissed on the ground that the action was voluntary. But the underlying statutory inquiry—whether the employee was affected by an appealable action—remains the same. To treat the two differently is to create a distinction that *Kloeckner* explicitly rejected.

### II. The D.C. Circuit’s Rule Creates an Unconstitutional Disparity in Judicial Access

The D.C. Circuit’s rule—requiring review of jurisdictional dismissals in the Federal Circuit while allowing review of procedural dismissals in district court—creates a constitutional anomaly. It allows employees to obtain district court review when the Board dismisses their case for being late, but denies it when the Board dismisses the case because the employee’s acceptance of the action was deemed voluntary. This is not a difference in substance—it is a difference in forum, and it is arbitrary.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the right to a fair and impartial tribunal” is a fundamental component of due process. *United States v. Mendoza-Lopez*, 481 U.S. 828, 835 (1987). When an employee alleges discrimination, the CSRA provides a clear path to district court review. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(2). But the D.C. Circuit’s rule effectively allows the Board to circumvent that path by recharacterizing a merits-based inquiry—voluntariness of a settlement—as a jurisdictional bar. This transforms a claim of discrimination into a jurisdictional question, thereby stripping the employee of the right to a jury trial and the full protections of the civil rights statutes.
