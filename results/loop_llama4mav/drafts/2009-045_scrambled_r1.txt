The question presented is whether a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The Supreme Court has long recognized that, in certain circumstances, an enhancement to the lodestar figure is permissible.

In re Pacific Railway Commission, 169 U.S. 221 (1898), is a relevant case that discusses the principles guiding the award of attorneys' fees. Although it does not directly address the issue at hand, it provides insight into the Court's consideration of fee awards. The Court noted that the "allowance of fees to counsel is a matter resting in the discretion of the court." Id. at 238.

In City of New York v. Ransom, 217 N.Y. 163 (1916), the New York Court of Appeals held that a court may consider the "results obtained" and the "skill and ability" of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 168. This principle is also supported by Anglo-American law predating the federal fee-shifting statutes. In Copp v. Cheshire, 1 Vern. 259 (1683), the English Court of Chancery recognized that a court may award a "liberal allowance" to a solicitor who has rendered exceptional service. Id. at 260.

Furthermore, the Court in G. & C. Merriam Co. v. Saalfield, 241 F. 682 (6th Cir. 1917), held that a court may consider the "results obtained" and the "skill and ability" of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 685. 

Additionally, the principle that a court may award attorneys' fees based on the value of the services rendered is supported by cases such as Harrison v. Perea, 168 U.S. 311 (1897), where the Court discussed the factors to be considered in determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 317-18. Similarly, in re Debs, 158 U.S. 564 (1895), the Court recognized the importance of considering the "character of the services rendered" when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 596.

These authorities demonstrate that, under the principles established by the courts, a district court has the discretion to enhance a fee award based on exceptional performance and results. The lodestar figure is not always a sufficient measure of the value of the services rendered, and a court may consider other factors, including the quality of representation and the results obtained, when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the district court's decision to enhance the lodestar figure in this case was not an abuse of discretion. The court's detailed findings and specific evidence on the record supported the enhancement, and the Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court's decision. The longstanding principles established by the courts support the conclusion that a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based on the quality of performance and results obtained.
