**ARGUMENT**

**I. Missouri's Constitutional Authority to Fix Qualifications for Its Own Judges Must Not Be Displaced Absent a Clear and Unambiguous Command from Congress.**

The power of a State to define the qualifications of the officers who exercise its sovereign judicial authority lies at the core of state sovereignty. This Court has long recognized that the structure of a State's judicial system, including the tenure and qualifications of its judges, is a matter for the State itself to decide. In *Missouri v. Lewis*, 101 U.S. 22 (1880), the Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment does not compel uniformity among the several branches or courts a State chooses to establish, explaining that the Constitution leaves to each State the organization of "the tribunals it may deem necessary for its own government." *Id.* at 31. That principle applies with full force here: Missouri's decision, embodied in its own Constitution, to require judges to retire at seventy is a quintessential exercise of the State's authority to organize its judiciary.

Because the mandatory retirement provision touches so directly upon this core sovereign function, the ADEA should not be read to override it unless Congress has spoken with unmistakable clarity. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against construing federal statutes to intrude upon matters of traditional state authority absent an unambiguous expression of congressional intent. In *Jerome v. United States*, 318 U.S. 101 (1943), the Court refused to read a federal statute to reach conduct traditionally regulated by the States, cautioning that federal statutes touching sensitive areas of state authority should not be presumed to displace state prerogatives without a clear signal from Congress. The same restraint applies here. Nothing in the text of the ADEA's "policymaking level" exception, 29 U.S.C. § 630(f), suggests that Congress contemplated—much less clearly intended—to strip Missouri of its constitutional power to retire its own judges.

Nor does anything in the structure of American government suggest that Congress must have intended such an intrusion. This Court has recognized that States retain wide latitude to decide how their officers, including judicial officers, shall be chosen and how long they shall serve. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the Court observed that the privileges of holding and continuing to hold state office are creatures of state law, not guarantees secured by the federal Constitution. And in *Sailors v. Board of Education of Kent County*, 387 U.S. 105 (1967), the Court upheld a State's choice to fill local offices by appointment rather than election, recognizing the State's broad discretion to design the mechanisms by which its officers obtain and lose their positions. If a State may choose whether its officers are elected or appointed, it follows that a State may likewise choose when their service ends. Missouri's judges—whether selected under the Missouri Plan or through partisan election—occupy policymaking positions of the highest order within Missouri's judicial branch, and Congress gave no clear indication that it intended the ADEA to override the State's chosen limitation on their tenure.

**II. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Provision Easily Satisfies Rational-Basis Review Under the Equal Protection Clause.**

Even apart from the ADEA, Missouri's retirement provision comfortably survives equal protection scrutiny. Age is not a suspect classification, and a State's regulation of the qualifications for holding public office is subject only to rational-basis review. Under that deferential standard, a classification "having some reasonable basis does not offend" the Equal Protection Clause "merely because it is not made with mathematical nicety or because in practice it results in some inequality." *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911). The Constitution "does not require that things different in fact ... be treated in law as though they were the same," and it is "no requirement of equal protection that all evils of the same genus be eradicated or none at all." *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949).

This Court has repeatedly sustained state-imposed occupational qualifications against equal protection challenge so long as a rational relationship to a legitimate state interest could be discerned. In *Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Commissioners*, 330 U.S. 552 (1947), the Court upheld a State's regulatory scheme governing the licensing of harbor pilots, deferring to the State's judgment about who was fit to exercise a position demanding exacting skill and public trust. Similarly, in *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483 (1955), the Court reaffirmed that a State "may do so," even if the law imperfectly fits the problem the legislature identified, because it is not the province of the judiciary to "sit as a super-legislature to weigh the wisdom" of state economic and social regulation. *Id.* at 488. And in *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961), the Court explained that "the constitutional safeguard is offended only if the classification rests on grounds wholly irrelevant to the achievement of the State's objective." *Id.* at 425.

Missouri's decision to require judicial retirement at seventy rests on grounds anything but irrelevant. A mandatory retirement age reasonably accounts for the possibility that advancing age will erode the faculties essential to judging, ensures orderly succession, and avoids the indignity of case-by-case competency hearings for aging jurists. Missouri need not prove these judgments empirically correct; it need only articulate a rational basis, which it has plainly done. Finally, because no petitioner possesses a fundamental right to continued public employment, see *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886 (1961) (no constitutional entitlement to public employment), strict scrutiny is inapplicable, and the provision must be sustained.
