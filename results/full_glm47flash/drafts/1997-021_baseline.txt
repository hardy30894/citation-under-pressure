**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling is Irrational and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s standard for allowing employers to poll employees regarding union support is irrational because it treats polling with the same evidentiary rigor as withdrawing recognition, despite the fact that polling lacks the procedural safeguards of a Board-supervised election. Under the National Labor Relations Act, an incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). An employer may overcome this presumption by demonstrating objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status. *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). The Board permits an employer to conduct a poll only when it has “sufficient objective evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition from the union.” *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984).

This standard is inconsistent with the statutory scheme. Polling is a less disruptive and less reliable method of determining employee sentiment than a Board-conducted election. *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have rejected the Board’s approach, holding that polling requires a lower standard—“substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support”—because it lacks the procedural protections of an RM election. *Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 867; *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1299; *A.W. Thompson, Inc.*, 651 F.2d 1143, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981). By requiring the same high threshold for polling as for withdrawing recognition, the Board renders polling a “useless” tool for employers who have sincere doubts but insufficient evidence to justify a complete withdrawal of recognition. *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1297. This creates an anomaly where an employer can conduct an unsupervised poll with fewer safeguards than a Board election, a result that cannot be reconciled with the Act’s goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships.

**II. The Board’s Factual Finding of “No Reasonable Doubt” is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence in the Record.**

The Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status is not supported by substantial evidence. *Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 868. *Fall River* establishes that a successor employer inherits the presumption of majority status for the bargaining unit. 482 U.S. at 36-41. However, the presumption is rebuttable. Here, the record contains substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support. The Board found that only seven employees made statements repudiating the union. However, the record also includes statements from Mike Ridgick, who told a manager during a job interview that there was “no need for a union” after being told the company would be non-union; Dennis Marsh, who expressed dissatisfaction with union dues; and Kermit Bloch, who reported that the entire night shift opposed the union. The Board’s refusal to credit these statements, finding them equivocal or unverified, is arbitrary and capricious. *Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 868. The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith doubt is not supported by the record.
