# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEXT OF SECTION 289 REQUIRES THAT DAMAGES BE CALCULATED BASED ON THE "ARTICLE OF MANUFACTURE" TO WHICH THE PATENTED DESIGN IS APPLIED, WHICH MAY BE A COMPONENT RATHER THAN THE ENTIRE PRODUCT

Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that an infringer "shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit" for applying a patented design "to any article of manufacture." 35 U.S.C. § 289. This Court's precedents construing analogous patent damages provisions establish that the "article of manufacture" to which profits attach must be the article that embodies the infringement—not necessarily the entire commercial product sold to consumers.

This Court's decision in *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), is instructive. There, addressing profits recoverable for design patent infringement on carpet patterns, the Court recognized that profits must be tied to the design itself, not to the whole carpet as a commodity, explaining that a patentee could not recover the "entire profit" on goods where the patented design was but one contributing feature among many to the article's marketability. The Court's reasoning in the companion case, *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), similarly cautioned against damages awards untethered from the actual contribution of the patented design to the profits realized.

This principle finds further support in *Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros.*, 234 F. 79 (2d Cir. 1916), and analogous utility patent cases construing the general damages provisions of the Patent Act. In *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), this Court held that where a patent covers only an improvement or component of a larger machine, "the patentee must show in what particulars his improvement has added to the usefulness of the machine or contrivance" and must apportion damages accordingly, because "the patentee must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features." *Id.* at 121. Although *Garretson* construed the general damages provision rather than § 289's total-profits remedy, its apportionment logic illuminates what Congress meant by "article of manufacture"—the relevant unit of analysis is the thing to which the design is actually applied, not an artificially expanded commercial product.

## II. THE STRUCTURE OF SECTION 289 DEMONSTRATES THAT "ARTICLE OF MANUFACTURE" CANNOT BE EQUATED AUTOMATICALLY WITH THE ENTIRE END PRODUCT

The phrase "article of manufacture" in § 289 must be read consistently with its use throughout the design patent statutes, including 35 U.S.C. § 171, which permits patents on designs for articles of manufacture. This Court has long recognized that a single commercial product may comprise multiple "articles of manufacture" for patent purposes. In *Untermeyer v. Freund*, 58 F. 205 (2d Cir. 1893), the court recognized that a design patent could cover a distinct component of a composite article, and that the infringer's profit calculus must correspond to that component.

The logic of *Root v. Railway Co.*, 105 U.S. 189 (1882), reinforces this reading. There, this Court held that a patentee's recovery must be limited to profits attributable to the patented improvement, refusing to permit recovery of profits on an entire structure merely because the patented invention was incorporated into it. The Court explained that unless the patented feature drove the entirety of the demand for the product, the plaintiff could not "make the profits of the whole business the measure of his damages." *Id.* at 203. This same principle applies with full force to § 289's "total profit" language: "total profit" refers to the total profit on the relevant article of manufacture—the component embodying the design—not the total profit on every product into which that component happens to be incorporated.

## III. LIMITING DAMAGES TO THE RELEVANT COMPONENT ACCORDS WITH THE PURPOSE OF DESIGN PATENT LAW AND AVOIDS UNJUST WINDFALLS

The purpose of patent damages has consistently been understood as compensatory rather than punitive. In *Livingston v. Woodworth*, 15 How. (56 U.S.) 546 (1853), this Court emphasized that patent remedies are designed to make the patentee whole for the value of what was actually taken, not to permit recovery disproportionate to the invention's contribution. Awarding a design patentee 100% of an infringer's profits on an entire multi-component smartphone—where the patented design covers only the shape of a housing or a grid of icons—would contravene this settled principle by awarding profits attributable to hundreds of unpatented, independently valuable technological components: processors, wireless radios, touchscreen sensors, and operating-system software.

This concern was directly addressed in *Blake v. Robertson*, 94 U.S. 728 (1877), where this Court refused to permit recovery of the whole profit on complex machinery when only a portion of the machine embodied the patented invention, holding that the plaintiff must demonstrate that "the profits or damages are to be calculated on the whole machine" only where the patented feature constitutes the basis for the customer demand for the entire machine. *Id.* at 733. No such showing was made, or could be made, here: Samsung's smartphones generate profit from myriad functional features wholly unrelated to the shape of the phone's corners or the grid arrangement of icons on a screen.

## IV. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT'S CATEGORICAL RULE CANNOT BE RECONCILED WITH THIS COURT'S APPORTIONMENT JURISPRUDENCE

The decision below treated the "article of manufacture" as coextensive with the end product sold to consumers as a matter of law, foreclosing any inquiry into whether the patented design applied only to a component. That categorical approach cannot be squared with this Court's repeated instruction that apportionment questions are factual and case-specific. *Garretson*, 111 U.S. at 121; *Root*, 105 U.S. at 203. The proper approach, consistent with *Dobson* and *Blake*, requires a factfinder to identify the relevant "article of manufacture" by examining the scope of the patented design, the structure of the accused product, and whether the components are sold separately or are conceptually distinct—then to calculate the infringer's total profit on that article alone.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that where a design patent applies only to a component of a multi-component product, § 289 damages must be limited to the infringer's profit on that component, and should remand for further proceedings consistent with that standard.
