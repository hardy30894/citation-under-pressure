**ARGUMENT**

A state prisoner may use a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel as cause to excuse the default of an underlying constitutional claim when the prisoner demonstrates that the default of the ineffectiveness claim itself was not attributable to deliberate bypass. See Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977); Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986).

In Carrier, this Court held that “the existence of cause for a procedural default must ordinarily turn on whether the prisoner can show that some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule.” 477 U.S. at 488. Ineffective assistance of counsel that rises to the level of a Sixth Amendment violation constitutes such an external factor. Id. at 488. Nothing in Carrier requires that the ineffectiveness claim itself have been presented and preserved in state court before it may be invoked as cause. To the contrary, the opinion recognizes that a claim of attorney error may itself be defaulted, yet still serve as cause if the prisoner can separately establish cause and prejudice for that default. Id. at 492.

Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), confirms rather than undermines this principle. Coleman reiterated that “a petitioner may establish cause by showing that he received ineffective assistance of counsel,” but added that the ineffectiveness claim must itself meet the cause-and-prejudice standard if it was defaulted. Id. at 753-54. The Court did not hold that a defaulted ineffectiveness claim is categorically unavailable as cause; it held only that the prisoner must satisfy the ordinary cause-and-prejudice test with respect to the ineffectiveness claim. Id. at 754.

Here, Carpenter’s application to reopen his direct appeal was dismissed as untimely. The record contains no indication that Carpenter or his post-conviction counsel deliberately bypassed the state deadline. Under Carrier and Coleman, the default of the ineffectiveness claim may therefore be excused upon a showing of cause and prejudice, allowing the claim to serve as cause for the default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The Sixth Circuit correctly applied this framework when it examined whether Carpenter’s appellate counsel rendered constitutionally ineffective assistance and whether that deficiency prejudiced the direct appeal.

The State’s contrary rule—that a defaulted ineffectiveness claim can never constitute cause—would erect an additional, extra-statutory barrier to federal review not required by Sykes, Carrier, or Coleman. Such a rule would also conflict with the equitable nature of the cause-and-prejudice doctrine, which “is not intended to supplant the ‘deliberate bypass’ standard” but to accommodate instances in which counsel’s error, rather than the prisoner’s own choice, produced the default. Carrier, 477 U.S. at 492.

Accordingly, a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim when the prisoner satisfies the cause-and-prejudice standard as to the ineffectiveness claim itself.
