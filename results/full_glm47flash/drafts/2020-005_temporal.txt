**Argument**

**I. The Due Process Clause requires that a claim "arise out of or relate to" the defendant's contacts with the forum.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires that a state court have specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only if the defendant’s contacts with the forum state are such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This requirement is satisfied only when the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum and the claim "arises out of or relates to" those activities. *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 251 (1958).

Here, the claim does not arise out of Ford’s activities in Montana. The plaintiff’s claims are for a design defect in the vehicle’s tires, which caused a tread separation and subsequent rollover. The only contact Ford had with Montana was the sale of parts. However, the claim for a design defect does not arise from the sale of parts; it arises from the design of the vehicle itself. *Hanson* requires a "nexus" between the claim and the defendant’s contacts. 357 U.S. at 251. The sale of parts in Montana is too attenuated to create such a nexus. The claim is not about the quality or performance of the parts sold in Montana, but about the safety of the vehicle’s design, which was determined in Kentucky and sold in Oregon prior to the accident. Therefore, the claim does not "arise out of" Ford’s contacts with Montana.

**II. The "stream of commerce" theory is insufficient to establish specific jurisdiction where the claim does not arise from the defendant's activities in the forum.**

The Montana Supreme Court relied on the "stream of commerce" theory to find specific jurisdiction, reasoning that Ford’s sale of parts in Montana availed it of the privilege of doing business there. However, the "stream of commerce" theory is a subset of the "arising out of" test established in *Hanson*. *Hanson* explicitly states that the claim must "arise out of or relate to" the defendant’s contacts with the forum. 357 U.S. at 251. The "stream of commerce" theory is only valid if the claim arises from the defendant’s activities in the state.

In this case, Ford’s sale of parts in Montana was a general commercial activity, not a specific targeting of Montana consumers for the specific product defect that caused the accident. The claim is for a design defect that occurred in the manufacturing and assembly process in Kentucky. The fact that Ford sold parts in Montana does not mean that the claim "relates to" those sales. The claim is about the vehicle’s safety, not the parts sold. The Montana court’s reasoning conflates the defendant’s general presence in the state with the specific requirement that the claim arise from the defendant’s activities. *Hanson* requires a more direct connection than the mere presence of the product in the stream of commerce. 357 U.S. at 251. Because the claim does not arise from Ford’s activities in Montana, the state court lacks specific personal jurisdiction.
