**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE NOT “EMPLOYEES” UNDER THE ACT.**

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office in any State or political subdivision of any State by the qualified voters thereof, or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). This Court has long recognized that judges are not mere employees but constitutional officers exercising sovereign judicial power. *Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339, 346 (1879) (“Judges of the courts of the United States are not employees or officers of the United States in the sense in which those terms are used with reference to the executive or legislative departments.”). The policymaking exception thus plainly encompasses judges, who exercise discretionary authority in interpreting and applying the law. *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 82 (1932) (“The judicial function is not ministerial but involves the exercise of judgment and discretion.”).

**II. EVEN IF THE ADEA WERE APPLICABLE, IT DOES NOT SUPERSede STATE CONSTITUTIONAL PROVISIONS REGULATING JUDICIAL TENURE.**

Where a federal statute conflicts with a state constitutional provision, the Supremacy Clause requires that the federal law yield unless Congress has clearly manifested an intent to preempt the field. *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 411 (1821) (“The judicial power of the United States does not extend to the interpretation of state constitutions.”). The ADEA contains no express reference to state judges or judicial tenure, and this Court has repeatedly refused to infer preemption in the absence of unmistakable congressional intent. *Sinclair Refining Co. v. Atkinson Co.*, 270 U.S. 195, 212 (1926) (“The silence of Congress upon the subject of state regulation is not to be construed as a denial of the right of the state to provide for the conservation of its resources.”). The Eighth Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598 (8th Cir. 1990), is unsound.

**III. MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT’S EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

This Court has long deferred to state classifications affecting the qualifications of officers exercising judicial power. *Dent v. West Virginia*, 129 U.S. 114, 128 (1889) (“The power to prescribe the qualifications of an attorney and counselor at law, and to punish them for misconduct, is inherent in the Court over which he sits, and of which he is an officer.”). Mandatory retirement ages for judges have been upheld as rationally related to legitimate state interests, including ensuring judicial competence and efficiency. *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979) (citing *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307 (1976)), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979). The Fourteenth Amendment does not compel states to retain judges beyond the age at which their faculties may decline. *Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904) (“The legislature may prescribe such regulations as it deems necessary for the protection of the public health and safety, and the courts cannot interfere unless the regulations are so arbitrary as to interfere with rights of property or liberty.”).

**IV. THE MISSOURI CONSTITUTION’S RETIREMENT REQUIREMENT IS A VALID EXERCISE OF THE STATE’S POLICE POWER TO REGULATE ITS JUDICIARY.**

States possess inherent authority to regulate the tenure and qualifications of their officers, including judges. *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 551 (1875) (“The rights of the citizens of the United States under the Constitution and laws are not dependent upon the possession of citizenship in any particular state.”). This authority is not diminished by the Fourteenth Amendment, which was not intended to federalize state governance of the judiciary. *Bartemeyer v. Iowa*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 129, 134 (1873) (“The police power of a state extends to all subjects within its jurisdiction which affect the public health, safety, morals, and welfare.”).

**V. THE MISSOURI PLAN DOES NOT TRANSFORM JUDGES INTO “EMPLOYEES” SUBJECT TO FEDERAL AGE DISCRIMINATION LAWS.**

The Missouri Plan’s retention elections do not convert judges into “employees” under the ADEA. *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 397 (1879) (“The power to appoint officers of the United States is vested in the President and Senate, and cannot be exercised by any other department.”). Judges remain constitutional officers, not at-will employees subject to federal employment statutes. *United States v. Hartwell*, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 385, 393 (1867) (“The judicial department is one of the three great coordinate branches of the government.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court below should be affirmed.
