# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF SECTION 1113(2) REQUIRES SUBJECTIVE KNOWLEDGE, NOT MERE ACCESS TO INFORMATION

Section 1113(2) bars suit brought more than "three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The word "actual" is not surplusage. This Court has repeatedly instructed that statutes must be interpreted to give effect to every word, and that Congress's choice of "actual" knowledge—rather than knowledge a plaintiff should have had, or constructive knowledge derived from availability of documents—must be respected. *TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 31 (2001) (declining to read a general discovery rule into a statute where Congress used the term "knowledge" and noting that Congress "explicitly incorporated a discovery rule" only where it intended one). Congress knew how to draft a constructive-knowledge standard: the original 1974 version of ERISA's limitations provision paired "actual knowledge" with a separate constructive-knowledge trigger tied to the filing of reports "from which [the plaintiff] could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(a)(2)(B) (1976) (repealed 1987). Congress's deliberate 1987 repeal of that constructive-knowledge provision, leaving only "actual knowledge" in the statute, confirms that Congress rejected an objective, reasonable-diligence standard in favor of a subjective one. This Court has long held that when "Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another," the omission is presumed intentional. *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983).

The ordinary meaning of "actual knowledge" is possession of a fact in one's mind, not access to a fact one has not read or has forgotten. As this Court explained in a related context, "actual knowledge" is possession of facts sufficient to alert a reasonable person, but critically, it requires that the person *possess* those facts. *Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma* itself squarely presents this issue: an ERISA participant who is furnished a disclosure but does not read it, or reads it and forgets, does not thereby "know" the contents in any ordinary sense of that word. This Court has cautioned against conflating "actual" knowledge with "constructive" knowledge in analogous statutory contexts. *See Rotkiske v. Klemm*, 589 U.S. ___, 140 S. Ct. 355, 360–61 (2019) (rejecting a discovery rule not found in the statutory text of the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, and observing that courts "have no authority to rewrite" a limitations provision "based on the belief that Congress must have intended something broader").

## II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT'S PRIOR CASE LAW CORRECTLY DISTINGUISHES KNOWLEDGE OF A TRANSACTION FROM KNOWLEDGE OF ILLEGALITY, BUT DOES NOT SUPPORT DISREGARDING WHETHER THE PLAINTIFF EVER RECEIVED THE INFORMATION

Petitioner does not contend that a plaintiff must know his employer violated the law—only that he must actually know the facts constituting the breach. This distinction, drawn by the Ninth Circuit itself in *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991–92 (9th Cir. 1985), is sound: the limitations period "is triggered by the [defendant's] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law." *Id.* at 991–92. Petitioner's position is fully consistent with *Blanton*, *Meagher v. International Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), and *Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co.*, 916 F.2d 548 (9th Cir. 1990)—in each of those cases the plaintiff was either a party to the transaction or was specifically and personally informed of it. The problem here is not that Sulyma lacked legal sophistication; it is that the summary judgment record, construed in his favor, shows he never actually absorbed the substantive facts at all. Sulyma testified without contradiction that he was "unaware that the monies that [he] had invested through the Intel retirement plans had been invested in hedge funds or private equity," and did "not recall seeing any documents" alerting him to that fact. That testimony creates a genuine dispute as to whether he ever possessed—as opposed to merely had opportunity to obtain—the relevant knowledge.

## III. AN OBJECTIVE "SHOULD HAVE KNOWN" STANDARD WOULD IMPERMISSIBLY REWRITE THE STATUTE AND UNDERMINE ERISA'S REMEDIAL PURPOSES

This Court has cautioned against judicially engrafting equitable or objective gap-fillers onto detailed statutory limitations schemes that Congress has calibrated with precision. *See Rotkiske*, 140 S. Ct. at 360. ERISA's fiduciary provisions were designed to "provide a panoply of remedies for participants and beneficiaries" and to ensure fiduciaries are held to exacting standards, *Massachusetts Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Russell*, 473 U.S. 134, 146 (1985) (quoting legislative history), and this Court has recognized ERISA's fiduciary duties as "the highest known to the law." *Tibble v. Edison Int'l*, 575 U.S. 523, 528 (2015) (quoting *Donovan v. Bierwirth*, 680 F.2d 263, 272 n.8 (2d Cir. 1982)). Permitting disclosure alone—without proof the participant actually read and retained the disclosed facts—to trigger the shortened three-year bar would convert every routine plan disclosure into a limitations trap, eviscerating the six-year backstop Congress separately provided in § 1113(1) for cases lacking actual knowledge. Such a construction cannot be reconciled with Congress's decision to preserve "actual" knowledge as a distinct, narrower trigger.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed, and this Court should hold that "actual knowledge" under § 1113(2) requires that the plaintiff be subjectively aware of the facts constituting the breach, not merely that such facts were disclosed to him.
