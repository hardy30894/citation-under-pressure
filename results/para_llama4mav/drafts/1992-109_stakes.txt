The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that a defendant is competent to waive counsel or plead guilty only if he has the capacity for "reasoned choice" among the alternatives available to him (Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); Evans v. Raines, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986)). This standard is more stringent than the standard for competency to stand trial, which requires only that a defendant have a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and be capable of assisting his counsel (Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975); Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960)).

The trial court's failure to apply the "reasoned choice" standard to Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty plea was a critical error. The state court's post-conviction ruling, which relied on psychiatric evaluations conducted to determine Moran's competency to stand trial, did not cure this error (Moran v. Godinez, 972 F.2d 263, 272 (9th Cir. 1992)). The psychiatric evaluations did not assess Moran's capacity for "reasoned choice," and therefore did not provide a sufficient basis for determining his competency to waive counsel and plead guilty (Id. at 272-73).

Furthermore, the record raises significant doubts about Moran's competency to waive counsel and plead guilty. Moran had attempted suicide just a few months prior to the plea hearing, and was taking multiple medications at the time of the hearing (Id. at 266-67). The trial court's failure to inquire further into Moran's mental state and medication regimen was a clear error (Id. at 267). Given these circumstances, the trial court should have had a good faith doubt about Moran's competency to waive counsel and plead guilty, and should have held a hearing to evaluate his competency (Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211, 214-15 (9th Cir. 1973)).

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court's post-conviction ruling did not constitute a valid finding that Moran was competent to waive his constitutional rights (Moran v. Godinez, 972 F.2d at 272). The state court applied the wrong legal standard, relying on the psychiatric evaluations conducted to determine Moran's competency to stand trial rather than the "reasoned choice" standard required for a valid waiver of constitutional rights (Id.). Therefore, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
