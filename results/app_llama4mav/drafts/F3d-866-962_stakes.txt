## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to BNSF Railway Company on the appellants' retaliation claims under the Federal Railroad Safety Act (FRSA), 49 U.S.C. § 20109. To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under the FRSA, the appellants must show that (1) they engaged in a protected activity, (2) BNSF knew about the protected activity, (3) they suffered an adverse employment action, and (4) the circumstances raise an inference that the protected activity was a contributing factor in the adverse action. See Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co., 768 F.3d 786, 789 (8th Cir. 2014).

The appellants satisfy the first element by reporting the unsafe conditions at the bridge to BNSF officials. See 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C) (protecting an employee's report of a hazardous safety or security condition). The appellants' written statements and recorded statements to BNSF officials, in which they mentioned the lack of side rails and lighting on the bridge, constitute protected activity. See id.; see also Araujo v. N.J. Transit Rail Operations, Inc., 708 F.3d 152, 158 (3d Cir. 2013) (holding that an employee's report of a safety concern to a supervisor is a protected activity under the FRSA).

BNSF's knowledge of the protected activity is established by the fact that the appellants' statements were made to BNSF officials, including Trainmaster Dixon and Claims Representative Murphy. See Kuduk, 768 F.3d at 791 (holding that an employer's knowledge of an employee's protected activity can be established by circumstantial evidence).

The appellants suffered adverse employment actions when BNSF disciplined them for alleged rule infractions. See 49 U.S.C. § 20109(d)(2)(A) (defining an adverse action as a "discharge, demotion, suspension, reprimand, or retaliatory investigation"). The discipline imposed on the appellants, including Foster's termination, Kline's record suspension, and Snyder's formal reprimand, constitutes adverse employment actions.

The circumstances raise an inference that the protected activity was a contributing factor in the adverse actions. The temporal proximity between the appellants' protected activity and the adverse actions supports this inference. See Araujo, 708 F.3d at 158 (holding that a short period between the protected activity and the adverse action can establish a causal connection). The appellants reported the unsafe conditions at the bridge in April 2012, and BNSF disciplined them in March 2013, after a lengthy investigative hearing.

Furthermore, the inconsistencies in BNSF's explanation for the discipline imposed on the appellants raise an inference of pretext. See Heim v. BNSF Ry. Co., 849 F.3d 723, 727 (8th Cir. 2017) (holding that an employer's inconsistent explanations for an adverse action can support an inference of retaliation). The appellants presented evidence that BNSF's witnesses, Knutstrom and Dixon, contradicted each other and the other crewmembers regarding the crew-change location. This inconsistency, combined with the temporal proximity between the protected activity and the adverse actions, creates a genuine dispute of material fact regarding whether the protected activity was a contributing factor in the adverse actions.

Therefore, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to BNSF, and this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.
