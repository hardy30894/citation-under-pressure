**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE SIXTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT BAR THE USE OF A VALID TRIBAL COURT CONVICTION AS A PREDICATE OFFENSE UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)**

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies only to criminal prosecutions in federal and state courts, not to tribal courts. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 322-23 (1978) (holding that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts because they are not federal or state courts). Tribal courts are governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a), which provides a right to counsel only when the defendant faces imprisonment. *Id.* § 1302(a)(6). Because Bryant’s tribal court convictions were valid under ICRA, they may be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a).

This Court has long recognized that tribal court convictions are valid for federal sentencing purposes. *See United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389, 1394 (9th Cir. 1989) (holding that tribal court convictions are valid predicate offenses for federal sentencing). The Supreme Court has also upheld the use of tribal court convictions in federal prosecutions. *See Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 323-24 (affirming tribal sovereignty to punish offenders). The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, and thus cannot invalidate Bryant’s tribal court convictions.

II. **NICHOLS V. UNITED STATES DOES NOT PROHIBIT THE USE OF UNCOUNSELED TRIBAL COURT CONVICTIONS AS PREDICATE OFFENSES**

In *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), the Supreme Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction could be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines. *Id.* at 746-47. The Court emphasized that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply to uncounseled convictions where no imprisonment is imposed. *Id.* at 741 (citing *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 372 (1979)). *Nichols* did not address the use of tribal court convictions as predicate offenses in a federal prosecution.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is inapposite. *Ant* held that tribal court convictions could not be used as predicate offenses in a federal prosecution because they did not provide a Sixth Amendment right to counsel. *Id.* at 1394-95. However, *Ant* predates *Nichols* and does not account for the Supreme Court’s holding that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts. *See Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 322-23. *Nichols* does not prohibit the use of tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a).

III. **THE USE OF TRIBAL COURT CONVICTIONS AS PREDICATE OFFENSES UNDER § 117(a) DOES NOT VIOLATE THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE**

The Due Process Clause does not require that predicate offenses used in federal prosecutions be obtained with the same procedural safeguards as federal or state convictions. *See Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746-47 (holding that due process is not violated by the use of uncounseled convictions for sentencing enhancements). Tribal court convictions are valid under ICRA, which provides sufficient procedural protections. *See* 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a). The use of these convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) does not violate due process.

IV. **THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION CREATES A CIRCUIT SPLIT AND UNDERMINES CONGRESS’S EFFORTS TO COMBAT DOMESTIC VIOLENCE IN INDIAN COUNTRY**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision conflicts with the Eighth and Tenth Circuits, which have held that tribal court convictions may be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a). *See United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011). The Ninth Circuit’s decision also undermines Congress’s efforts to combat domestic violence in Indian country by limiting the use of tribal court convictions as predicate offenses. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the circuit split and ensure that Congress’s efforts to protect victims of domestic violence are not frustrated.
