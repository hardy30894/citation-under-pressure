**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Government’s Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove That Nieves-Castaño Knew the Weapon Had Automatic-Fire Capability, as Required by *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600 (1994).**

To convict under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant knowingly possessed a machine gun. *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 602 (1994). Critically, *Staples* held that the government must prove the defendant knew the weapon had the characteristics that made it a machine gun—specifically, that it could fire automatically. *Id.* at 619–20. This mens rea requirement reflects the traditional principle that “the defendant must know the facts that make his conduct illegal.” *Id.* at 605.

Here, the government’s evidence fell short. The sole proof of Nieves-Castaño’s knowledge came from her post-arrest statement that she “knew it was an AK-47.” But knowledge that a weapon is an AK-47 does not, without more, establish knowledge that it is a machine gun. Many AK-47 variants are semiautomatic only. As the *Staples* Court recognized, firearms of this general type are “traditionally lawful” and widely owned for legitimate purposes. *Id.* at 615–16. A layperson’s familiarity with a weapon’s make or model does not automatically convey awareness of its technical firing capability.

Moreover, Nieves-Castaño’s statement that she had “once opened the bag, looked in, and observed that there was a rifle in it” does not reveal any examination of the weapon’s firing mechanism. The record contains no evidence that she tested the gun, discussed its capabilities with the owner, or otherwise acquired knowledge of its automatic-fire feature. The government did not present testimony that the weapon’s appearance—externally—would have disclosed to an ordinary observer that it could fire automatically. Indeed, many semiautomatic rifles are visually indistinguishable from their automatic counterparts. *See United States v. Garth*, 188 F.3d 99, 110 (3d Cir. 1999) (distinguishing knowledge of weapon type from knowledge of automatic capability).

Because the government failed to adduce evidence from which a rational jury could infer that Nieves-Castaño knew the rifle could fire automatically, her conviction under § 922(o) must be reversed and the charge dismissed. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979).

**II. The School-Zone Statute, 18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A), Is Unconstitutionally Void for Vagueness.**

The Fifth Amendment requires that criminal statutes “define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited.” *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357 (1983). A statute is void for vagueness if it fails to give fair notice of the prohibited conduct. *Village of Hoffman Estates v. Flipside, Hoffman Estates, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 489, 498 (1982).

Section 922(q)(2)(A) makes it unlawful to possess a firearm “in a school zone,” which is defined as “the grounds of, or within a distance of 1,000 feet from the grounds of, a public, parochial or private school.” 18 U.S.C. § 921(a)(26). The statute provides no method for measuring the 1,000-foot distance. It does not specify whether the distance is to be measured in a straight line, along pedestrian pathways, or by road. It does not indicate whether measurement should be taken from the school’s property line, the building’s footprint, or some other point. This ambiguity leaves ordinary citizens—and law enforcement—without clear guidance on the statute’s reach.

Street-level variations compound the problem. In dense urban areas like the housing project where Nieves-Castaño resided, multiple schools may be nearby. Without a defined measuring technique, individuals cannot reasonably determine whether their location falls within the prohibited zone. “A statute which leaves the public uncertain as to the conduct it prohibits” violates due process. *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921).

Because § 922(q)(2)(A) fails to provide “fair warning” of the conduct it punishes, it is unconstitutionally vague. *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104, 108 (1972). Nieves-Castaño’s conviction under this provision must be reversed.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of conviction should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges.
