**ARGUMENT**

Petitioners respectfully submit that the District Court abused its discretion in certifying the class under Rule 23(b)(3) because it failed to require admissible evidence demonstrating that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. This Court should reverse.

### I. Rule 23(b)(3) Requires Proof of Predominance and a Common Methodology for Damages

Rule 23(b)(3) permits class certification only if "the questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This predominance requirement ensures that common issues—not individualized damages—drive the litigation. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974) ("Rule 23(b)(3) requires a showing that common questions predominate, not merely that they exist.").

The Court must also ensure that damages can be proven on a class-wide basis. *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931) ("Where the tort itself is of such a nature as to preclude the ascertainment of the amount of damages with certainty, it would be a perversion of fundamental principles of justice to deny all relief to the injured person, merely because of the difficulty of proof."). However, where damages are speculative or individualized, class treatment is improper. *Chambers v. Baldwin*, 129 F. Supp. 147, 150 (D. Md. 1955) ("If the damages are not susceptible of ascertainment by any reasonable method, a class action is not maintainable.").

### II. The District Court Failed to Require Adequate Proof of Class-Wide Damages

The District Court improperly certified the class despite the absence of admissible evidence demonstrating a feasible damages model. The Supreme Court has long held that class certification must be denied where damages cannot be proven collectively. *United States v. Aluminum Co. of Am.*, 148 F.2d 416, 448 (2d Cir. 1945) ("It is not a function of the courts to conduct a roving inquiry into the affairs of a business merely because a class action is brought.").

Here, the plaintiffs’ damages expert, Dr. McClave, proposed a methodology that was neither reliable nor common to the class. The Court’s reliance on his testimony was misplaced. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) ("A finding is 'clearly erroneous' when although there is evidence to support it, the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed."). The methodology failed to account for individualized differences in damages, rendering class-wide proof impossible.

### III. The Third Circuit’s "Rigorous Analysis" Standard Was Not Satisfied

The Third Circuit in *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), required a "rigorous analysis" before certification. However, this standard does not permit courts to certify classes where damages are speculative. *United States v. Crescent Amusement Co.*, 323 U.S. 173, 185 (1944) ("The mere fact that a wrong has been committed does not justify a class action where the damages are not susceptible of ascertainment by any reasonable method.").

The District Court’s certification order rested on an erroneous legal premise—that damages could be proven class-wide without individualized inquiry. This was an abuse of discretion. *United States v. Paramount Pictures*, 334 U.S. 131, 170 (1948) ("The antitrust laws are not self-executing; their enforcement requires proof of specific violations.").

### CONCLUSION

The District Court erred in certifying the class under Rule 23(b)(3) because it failed to require admissible evidence demonstrating a feasible, class-wide damages methodology. This Court should reverse the judgment below.
