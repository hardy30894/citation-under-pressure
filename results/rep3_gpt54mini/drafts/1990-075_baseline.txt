**Argument**

The Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. Nor did the Attorney General exceed his authority in assigning temporary scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator.

I. **Congress supplied an intelligible principle for temporary scheduling.**

Article I permits Congress to “obtain[] assistance from coordinate branches” so long as it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of delegated authority. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372, 109 S. Ct. 647, 654, 102 L. Ed. 2d 714 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The Court has repeatedly upheld broad delegations where Congress identified the goal of the delegation, the factors to be considered, and limits on the delegatee’s authority. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374-79, 109 S. Ct. at 655-57; *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 425-27, 64 S. Ct. 660, 667-68, 88 L. Ed. 834 (1944).

Section 811(h) easily satisfies that standard. Congress authorized temporary scheduling only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). It further constrained the Attorney General by requiring consideration of specified factors bearing on abuse and public risk, including “history and current pattern of abuse,” “scope, duration, and significance of abuse,” and “risk to the public health.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3), incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6). Congress also required notice in the Federal Register and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). Finally, Congress sharply limited duration to one year, with a possible six-month extension. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2).

Those limits are more than sufficient under the modern nondelegation cases. In *Mistretta*, the Court upheld a delegation where Congress specified general purposes, factors to consider, and boundaries on discretion. 488 U.S. at 379-79, 109 S. Ct. at 656-57. In *Yakus*, the Court sustained a delegation that allowed executive officials to fix prices pursuant to a statutory standard of “fair and equitable” and the elimination of “inflationary spirals.” 321 U.S. at 420-27, 64 S. Ct. at 664-68. If those delegations were constitutional, so is this one. Congress identified the emergency it sought to prevent, the criteria governing temporary scheduling, the procedural prerequisites, and the temporal limits on the exercise of power.

II. **The fact that temporary scheduling affects criminal liability does not require a different rule.**

Petitioners rely on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), but those cases involved statutes that supplied no meaningful policy or standard. *Panama Refining* invalidated a delegation that “did not declare any policy” and furnished no criterion to guide the President’s action. 293 U.S. at 430, 55 S. Ct. at 247. *Schechter Poultry* similarly turned on the absence of adequate standards. 295 U.S. at 541-42, 55 S. Ct. at 852-53. Section 811(h), by contrast, states a concrete objective—preventing imminent hazards to public safety—and cabins the agency’s discretion with enumerated factors and strict time limits.

Nor does the fact that temporary scheduling carries criminal consequences alter the analysis. The Court has upheld delegations in contexts with significant regulatory and penal consequences. See *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 420-27, 64 S. Ct. at 664-68; *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 785-86, 68 S. Ct. 1294, 1317-18, 92 L. Ed. 1694 (1948). The relevant question is whether Congress supplied an intelligible principle, not whether the delegated authority touches criminal law.

III. **Congress authorized the Attorney General to delegate temporary scheduling functions to the DEA.**

The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator was authorized by statute and proper within the Executive Branch. Congress expressly provided that the Attorney General may “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The DEA Administrator is an officer within the Department of Justice, and the Attorney General’s regulation delegating CSA functions to the Administrator therefore falls squarely within the statutory grant. See 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b).

The Court has recognized that, absent a contrary congressional directive, the head of a department may assign delegated duties to subordinates charged with carrying out the statute. *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 512-17, 94 S. Ct. 1820, 1825-28, 40 L. Ed. 2d 341 (1974), makes clear that the controlling question is congressional intent as expressed in the statute. Here, Congress affirmatively authorized delegation to officers and employees of the Department of Justice. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). That authorization is decisive.

IV. **The Third Circuit correctly upheld the temporary scheduling of Euphoria.**

The Third Circuit properly concluded that § 811(h) contains sufficient standards and that constitutional review is not foreclosed by the statute’s limitation on judicial review. See *Touby v. United States*, 909 F.2d 759, 764-78 (3d Cir. 1990). The court’s reasoning accords with *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109, 97 S. Ct. 980, 986, 51 L. Ed. 2d 192 (1977), which presumes judicial review for constitutional claims absent clear congressional intent to preclude it.

Because Congress lawfully delegated temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General, and because the Attorney General lawfully subdelegated that authority to the DEA Administrator pursuant to 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), petitioners’ convictions should be affirmed.
