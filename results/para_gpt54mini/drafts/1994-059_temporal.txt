The Constitution supplies the qualifications for Members of Congress, and those qualifications are exclusive. Article I, § 2 requires that Representatives be at least twenty-five years old, seven years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the State when elected; Article I, § 3 requires that Senators be at least thirty years old, nine years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the State when elected. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, 3. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), this Court held that the House could not exclude a duly elected Member who met those textual qualifications, explaining that the “qualifications prescribed in the Constitution are fixed.” *Id.* at 547. The same principle controls here: if the House itself may not add to the Constitution’s qualifications, neither may a State.

The Framers rejected a system in which the States could supplement the federal qualifications. In *The Federalist No. 52*, Madison described the House qualifications as those set forth in the Constitution; and the Constitution’s text reflects that judgment by stating only the age, citizenship, and inhabitancy requirements. See *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 548-49. Where the Constitution has “fixed” the qualifications, they are not merely minimum requirements subject to local amendment. *Id.* at 548. State-imposed term limits, whether styled as an outright disqualification or a ballot-access restriction, add a new substantive condition: prior service above a specified threshold. That is precisely the sort of extra qualification the Constitution forbids. *Id.* at 540-48.

This Court’s pre-1970 cases confirm that the States may regulate the “Times, Places and Manner” of elections, but not alter constitutional qualifications. In *Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355 (1932), the Court recognized that Article I, § 4 gives States authority over election mechanics, but only within constitutional bounds. *Id.* at 366. Likewise, *Roudebush v. Hartke*, 405 U.S. 15 (1960), emphasized that state election procedures may not trench upon the federal constitutional structure governing congressional selection. A state law that determines who is eligible to appear for election is not a mere procedural regulation; it directly affects who may serve in Congress and therefore crosses the line from procedure to qualification.

That conclusion is reinforced by the long constitutional tradition that federal offices are governed by federal law. In *McPherson v. Blacker*, 146 U.S. 1 (1892), the Court recognized that States possess authority to appoint presidential electors, but only because the Constitution expressly delegated that power. *Id.* at 25-26. By contrast, no constitutional provision delegates to the States authority to add qualifications for Senators or Representatives. Where the Constitution withholds a power by specifying exclusive qualifications, States cannot claim it by implication. See *In re Green*, 134 U.S. 377, 379-80 (1890) (recognizing that eligibility to federal office is controlled by the Constitution’s own terms).

The State’s contrary position cannot be squared with *Barry v. Hall*, 68 F.2d 222 (D.C. Cir. 1933), which held that Congress could not prescribe additional qualifications for Senate office beyond those found in the Constitution. If Congress itself lacks power to supplement Article I qualifications, it follows a fortiori that a State lacks such power. The Constitution established one national rule for federal officeholding, and that rule cannot vary from State to State. See *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 548-49.

Nor can Arkansas rescue Amendment 73 by recasting it as a ballot-access measure. A State may regulate ballot form, order, and election administration, but it may not use those devices to impose a qualification in substance. *Smiley*, 285 U.S. at 366; *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 550. A rule that a candidate “shall not be eligible” after serving a fixed number of terms is not neutral machinery; it operates as a substantive bar to candidacy and officeholding. Labels do not control constitutional analysis.

Because Arkansas has no authority to alter the qualifications for the United States Congress, Section 3 of Amendment 73 is invalid under Article I. And because the federal restrictions were part of the single package submitted to voters, the invalid portion cannot be severed if the Court cannot say the measure would have passed absent the unconstitutional provision. See *Williams v. Standard Oil Co.*, 278 U.S. 235, 242-43 (1929); *Dorchy v. Kansas*, 264 U.S. 286, 289-90 (1924). The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
