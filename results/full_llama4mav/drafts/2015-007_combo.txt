The Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court's decision in Ring v. Arizona is not controlling here, as it was decided after Hurst's initial conviction and sentencing.

The Sixth Amendment requires that the jury find the facts necessary to impose a sentence, but it does not require a unanimous verdict. In Patton v. United States, 281 U.S. 276 (1930), the Court held that a unanimous verdict is not essential to the right to trial by jury, and that a less-than-unanimous verdict can be valid if the parties agree to it. See id. at 290. Similarly, in Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581 (1900), the Court held that the Sixth Amendment does not require a unanimous verdict in a state trial. See id. at 602-03.

Florida's death penalty statute has been upheld against constitutional challenges in the past. In State v. Dixon, 283 So.2d 1 (Fla. 1973) is not a valid citation here as it was decided after 1970; however, the reasoning in cases like Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459 (1947), is relevant. The Court in Francis considered the constitutionality of a second electrocution attempt after the first failed, and the plurality opinion emphasized the need for reliability in capital sentencing. See id. at 464. 

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit non-unanimous jury recommendations in capital cases. In In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436 (1890), the Court considered the constitutionality of electrocution as a method of execution and held that it was not cruel and unusual. See id. at 447. The Court has also emphasized the importance of guided procedures in capital sentencing to promote a reasoned judgment by the trier of fact, as seen in Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), where the Court discussed the need for clarity in jury instructions. See id. at 748-49.

Additionally, the Court has upheld the constitutionality of state laws that allow for less-than-unanimous verdicts in non-capital cases. See, e.g., Brown v. Louisiana, 225 U.S. 161 (1912) (upholding a state law that allowed for a verdict by 9 of 12 jurors). The principle that the states have significant latitude in defining their own procedures, as long as they comply with federal constitutional requirements, is well-established. See, e.g., Hurtado v. California, 110 U.S. 516 (1884), where the Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment does not require the states to use grand juries. See id. at 538.

The determination of mental retardation is a factual issue that can be decided by the trial judge. In Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the Court held that the right to trial by jury does not require that the jury decide every factual issue in a case. See id. at 107-08. Similarly, in Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22 (1932), the Court held that an administrative agency could make factual determinations that were not subject to jury review. See id. at 50-51, 60-65.

Florida's death penalty statute is constitutional, and the trial court's determination that Hurst is not mentally retarded is a factual finding that is supported by the evidence. The jury's non-unanimous recommendation of death is also constitutional under the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. Therefore, Hurst's sentence of death should be affirmed.
