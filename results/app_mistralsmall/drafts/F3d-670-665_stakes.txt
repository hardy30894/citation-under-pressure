**ARGUMENT**

I. **Equitable Tolling of AEDPA’s Statute of Limitations Is Warranted Where a Petitioner Presents a Credible Claim of Actual Innocence**

This Court has long recognized that a credible claim of actual innocence may warrant equitable tolling of AEDPA’s statute of limitations. *See, e.g., House v. Bell*, 547 U.S. 518, 537 (2006) (noting that "a credible showing of actual innocence" may excuse a procedural default); *Schlup v. Delo*, 513 U.S. 298, 324 (1995) (holding that a "truly persuasive demonstration of 'actual innocence'" may overcome a procedural bar). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the "fundamental miscarriage of justice" exception to procedural default applies where a petitioner can show "by clear and convincing evidence that, but for a constitutional error, no reasonable juror would have found the petitioner guilty." *Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 316 (quoting *Sawyer v. Whitley*, 505 U.S. 333, 336 (1992)).

The Warden’s reliance on *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), is misplaced. *Pace* did not alter the rule that actual innocence alone may justify equitable tolling. Rather, *Pace* addressed the standard for determining when a state court’s adjudication of a claim on the merits bars federal review under AEDPA’s deferential standard of review. *Id.* at 414-15. The Court in *Pace* did not displace the longstanding principle that actual innocence can excuse procedural defaults or toll statutes of limitations. *See also McQuiggin v. Perkins*, 569 U.S. 383, 392 (2013) (reaffirming that "a credible claim of actual innocence" may warrant equitable tolling of AEDPA’s statute of limitations).

Here, Perkins has presented compelling new evidence—three affidavits—that directly undermine the prosecution’s case and point to Jones as the actual perpetrator. The affidavits from Hudson, Louis, and Fleming collectively establish that Jones (1) admitted to the murder, (2) was seen with bloodstained clothing on the night of the crime, and (3) sought to clean bloodstained clothing shortly thereafter. This evidence was not merely "substantially available" at trial; it was unavailable until years later, as the affidavits themselves demonstrate. The district court erred in concluding otherwise.

II. **Reasonable Diligence Is Not a Prerequisite to Equitable Tolling Based on Actual Innocence**

The Warden argues that Perkins must demonstrate reasonable diligence in pursuing his claims to qualify for equitable tolling. However, this Court has never held that diligence is a prerequisite to tolling based on actual innocence. *See, e.g., Hill v. United States*, 473 F.3d 387, 393 (6th Cir. 2007) (recognizing that "actual innocence alone may warrant equitable tolling" without requiring diligence). The Supreme Court has likewise declined to impose a diligence requirement in the actual innocence context. *See Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 324-27 (focusing on the persuasiveness of the innocence claim rather than the petitioner’s diligence).

Moreover, *Pace* does not support the Warden’s position. *Pace* addressed the standard for reviewing state court decisions under AEDPA, not the equitable tolling analysis. 544 U.S. at 414-15. The Court in *Pace* did not suggest that diligence is a prerequisite to equitable tolling based on actual innocence. To the contrary, the Supreme Court has made clear that the "fundamental miscarriage of justice" exception—rooted in actual innocence—does not require diligence. *See Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 324-27.

Here, Perkins’s delay in filing his petition was directly tied to the unavailability of the new evidence. The affidavits were signed years after the crime, and Perkins filed his petition within a reasonable time after discovering their existence. The district court’s diligence requirement conflates two distinct inquiries: whether the petitioner has a credible innocence claim and whether the petitioner has been diligent in pursuing procedural remedies. The former does not depend on the latter.

III. **The District Court Erred in Denying Equitable Tolling**

The district court’s denial of equitable tolling was based on two erroneous conclusions: (1) that Perkins’s new evidence was "substantially available" at trial, and (2) that Perkins failed to pursue his claims with reasonable diligence. Both conclusions are unsupported by the record and misapply the law.

First, the new evidence—particularly the affidavits from Louis and Fleming—was not available at trial. The affidavits were signed years after Perkins’s conviction, and there is no indication that Perkins could have discovered them earlier through due diligence. The district court’s conclusion to the contrary was clearly erroneous.

Second, even if diligence were required, Perkins’s delay was justified by the unavailability of the evidence. The Supreme Court has never held that a petitioner must file a habeas petition immediately upon discovering new evidence; rather, the focus is on whether the petitioner has a credible innocence claim. *See Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 324-27. Here, Perkins filed his petition within a reasonable time after the evidence became available, and the district court’s diligence requirement was an improper legal standard.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in denying Perkins’s petition for equitable tolling based on his credible claim of actual innocence. This Court should reverse the judgment below and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
