**Argument**

The FDCPA regulates “debt collectors,” not creditors collecting their own debts. 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692a(4), 1692a(6), 1692e. The statutory structure makes that distinction central: a “debt collector” is one who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another,” while a “creditor” is a person to whom the debt is owed. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4), (6). This Court has already recognized that the FDCPA’s “natural reading” is directed at those who collect debts “owed ... another,” not at entities collecting their own accounts. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995).

1. **A purchaser collecting for its own account is not a “debt collector” under the Act’s main definition.**  
The Fourth Circuit correctly held that the key inquiry is whether the entity is collecting for itself or for another at the time of the challenged conduct. *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc.*, 817 F.3d 131, 136-39 (4th Cir. 2016). That reading follows the text. The FDCPA’s operative definition covers those who regularly collect debts “owed or due another,” not those who collect debts owed to themselves. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6); *Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 293. As the Eleventh Circuit explained, “a person must regularly collect or attempt to collect debts for others in order to qualify as a ‘debt collector’ under the second definition of the term.” *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1315-16 (11th Cir. 2015). Likewise, the Ninth Circuit has rejected the claim that “owed or due another” means “originally owed or due another.” *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013).

2. **Default at the time of purchase does not transform a buyer into a debt collector.**  
Petitioners’ theory depends on default status. But the statute does not. Section 1692a(6)(F)(iii) excludes certain collectors of debts “not in default at the time it was obtained,” but that exclusion presupposes that the person otherwise falls within the definition of “debt collector.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). The better reading, adopted by the Fourth Circuit, is that default status matters only after one has already qualified under the main definition. *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 138-39. The Fifth Circuit has likewise held that “the plain language of the statute” does not make debt status the touchstone for whether an entity is a debt collector. *Medeiros v. BAC Home Loans Servicing, L.P.*, 798 F.3d 442, 446-47 (5th Cir. 2015). And the Sixth Circuit in *Bridge v. Ocwen Federal Bank, FSB*, 681 F.3d 355, 359 (6th Cir. 2012), while discussing the statutory categories, recognized the mutual exclusivity of creditors and debt collectors.

3. **Congress intended the FDCPA to reach third-party collectors, not creditors on their own accounts.**  
The legislative history confirms the text. The Senate Report explained that the definition of “debt collector” was meant to cover “third persons who regularly collect debts for others,” while excluding “mortgage service companies and others who service outstanding debts for others, so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing.” S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. The Seventh Circuit similarly stated that the Act is directed at those whose “principal business is debt collection,” not ordinary creditors. *Ruth v. Triumph Partnerships*, 577 F.3d 790, 796-97 (7th Cir. 2009). The Third Circuit has likewise explained that creditors and debt collectors are distinct categories under the Act. *FTC v. Check Investors, Inc.*, 502 F.3d 159, 173 (3d Cir. 2007).

4. **Petitioners’ broader reading would distort the statute and expand liability beyond Congress’s design.**  
If merely purchasing defaulted debt made a company a debt collector, then any assignee of defaulted receivables would be regulated as a debt collector even when collecting for itself. That would collapse the statutory distinction between creditor and collector, contrary to *Henson* and *Heintz*. The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned against reading statutes in a way that ignores their text and structure. See *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241-42 (1989). Here, the text already supplies the answer: Santander purchased the loans and then collected them for its own account. It therefore acted as a creditor, not a debt collector, when it engaged in the challenged conduct. *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 139-41.

Because petitioners failed to allege that Santander was collecting debts owed “another” at the time of the alleged misconduct, they failed to plead that Santander was a “debt collector” within 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The judgment should be affirmed.
