ARGUMENT

I. THE BANKRUPTCY COURT LACKED JURISDICTION TO ADJUDICATE A POST-DISCHARGE, POST-CLOSING DEFAULT AND TO CALCULATE THE INTEREST AND COSTS FLOWING FROM IT

A. Bankruptcy Jurisdiction Is Confined to the Administration of the Estate and Enforcement of the Court’s Own Decrees

A bankruptcy court is not a court of general jurisdiction; it is a specialized tribunal whose authority is derived from, and limited to, the administration of the estate before it. This Court’s predecessors have long recognized that although “courts of bankruptcy are essentially courts of equity, and their proceedings inherently proceedings in equity,” Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 240 (1934), that equitable authority does not extend indefinitely. It persists only so long as necessary to complete the administration commenced by the petition, or to enforce an order the bankruptcy court has already entered. Id. Once the estate is fully administered, the case closed, and the debtor discharged, the court’s special jurisdiction over the debtor’s affairs comes to an end. See Case v. Los Angeles Lumber Products Co., 308 U.S. 106, 114–15 (1939) (bankruptcy court’s continuing authority is tied to completion of the plan and enforcement of its own decrees, not to matters arising thereafter).

Kirkland’s Chapter 13 plan was fully administered, the final report filed, the discharge entered, and the case closed in February 2006. The default at issue here, and the interest and collection costs flowing from it, arose only after that closing—when Sallie Mae, and later ECMC, began asserting a post-closing delinquency on claim no. 9. That is not a continuation of the bankruptcy administration; it is a new controversy between a creditor and a former debtor concerning a loan contract, arising wholly outside the four corners of the estate that the bankruptcy court once administered.

B. Adjudicating the Post-Default Interest and Collection Costs Required an Independent Plenary Proceeding, Not Summary Bankruptcy Jurisdiction

This Court’s predecessors have repeatedly distinguished between the bankruptcy court’s summary jurisdiction over property and claims that are part of the estate’s administration, and the plenary jurisdiction required to adjudicate controversies that stand apart from that administration. See MacDonald v. Plymouth County Trust Co., 286 U.S. 263, 266–67 (1932) (summary jurisdiction is confined to matters concerning property in the actual or constructive possession of the bankruptcy court; controversies requiring independent adjudication of rights must proceed plenarily); Cline v. Kaplan, 323 U.S. 97, 98–99 (1944) (summary jurisdiction does not extend to disputes over property or obligations not within the court’s possession or control). The determination of whether, and in what amount, Kirkland defaulted on claim no. 9 after the estate closed—and the calculation of interest and collection fees that flow from that post-closing default under the terms of a promissory note—is precisely the kind of independent contractual dispute that Cline and MacDonald hold falls outside summary bankruptcy jurisdiction.

Nor can the bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction be sustained on the theory that the claim was “core” because it touched upon an allowed proof of claim. See Thompson v. Magnolia Petroleum Co., 309 U.S. 478, 481–83 (1940) (claims requiring adjudication of rights not settled in the claims-allowance process must be pursued independently of the bankruptcy proceeding). The proof of claim filed in 2001 fixed the amount then owed; it did not, and could not, fix the amount of a default that had not yet occurred and would not occur until years after the case was closed.

C. Ancillary Jurisdiction to Enforce a Discharge Does Not Encompass Adjudication of a New, Post-Discharge Default

Kirkland’s adversary proceeding sought only a determination that the discharge order barred collection of the principal already administered through the plan. That request arguably fell within the bankruptcy court’s ancillary jurisdiction “to enforce the discharge which it has granted,” Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. at 239. But the bankruptcy court went further, adjudicating a separate and subsequent default and computing interest and collection costs under the loan instrument itself. That inquiry is not an enforcement of the discharge; it is the litigation of a new contractual controversy of the sort this Court’s predecessors have confined to plenary process. See Harrison v. Chamberlin, 271 U.S. 191, 195–96 (1926) (bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction over controversies is limited to those arising in the course of, and necessary to, the administration of the estate, and does not extend to matters that are independent of that administration).

Jurisdiction cannot be assumed simply because the underlying debt originated in a proceeding once before the bankruptcy court. As this Court’s predecessors have cautioned, “the jurisdiction of a court is to be determined by the case as it stands” at the time the court is asked to adjudicate the controversy, Chicot County Drainage Dist. v. Baxter State Bank, 308 U.S. 371, 376 (1940), and where, as here, the controversy did not exist—and could not have existed—until years after the case was closed, no jurisdictional hook to the extinguished bankruptcy estate remains.

D. The Bankruptcy Court Exceeded Any Residual Equitable Authority It Possessed

Even accepting some residual authority, a bankruptcy court’s equity powers "must be exercised within the confines of" the Bankruptcy Code and the matter properly before it, Pepper v. Litton, 308 U.S. 295, 304–05 (1939); Sampsell v. Imperial Paper & Color Corp., 313 U.S. 215, 219 (1941). Neither party asked the court to adjudicate post-petition interest or collection costs; Kirkland sought only a discharge determination. By resolving an issue never presented and never briefed, the bankruptcy court exceeded the equitable authority these precedents preserve for matters genuinely before it.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
