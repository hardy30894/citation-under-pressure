**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING MOLL’S HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT CLAIM BY FAILING TO CONSIDER THE TOTALITY OF THE ALLEGATIONS**

The district court erred in dismissing Moll’s hostile work environment claim by narrowly construing her allegations to include only “sexually offensive acts” within the limitations period. Hostile work environment claims under Title VII are evaluated under an objective, totality-of-the-circumstances standard. *See Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993) (“[T]he plaintiff must show that the workplace is permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult, that is sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim’s employment.”). This Court has repeatedly held that facially sex-neutral acts may contribute to a hostile work environment if motivated by sex. *See Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002) (“[A] facially neutral comment may contribute to a hostile work environment if it is made in circumstances where a reasonable person would perceive the comment as part of a pattern of harassment based on sex.”).

The district court’s approach contravenes this precedent by artificially segregating Moll’s allegations into discrete categories—“sexually offensive” and “non-sexually offensive”—and ignoring the cumulative effect of all alleged conduct. *See id.* at 374 (“[T]he harassment must be evaluated in the context of the entire record.”). The Supreme Court has cautioned against such a piecemeal analysis, emphasizing that isolated incidents must be considered in their totality. *See Nat’l R.R. Passenger Corp. v. Morgan*, 536 U.S. 101, 115 (2002) (“[S]ingle incidents of discrete discriminatory acts are not actionable if they occur outside the statute of limitations.”). However, the Court did not require that each act be independently actionable; rather, it permitted aggregation of incidents to demonstrate a pattern of discrimination. *Id.* at 117.

Moll’s allegations—including Irving’s repeated intrusive communications, his refusal to permit remote work despite accommodating male colleagues, and exclusion from work-related events—are not merely discrete acts but part of a broader pattern of sex-based disparate treatment. *See id.* at 118 (“[A] series of acts taken together may constitute a hostile environment even if some of the acts, standing alone, would not.”). The district court’s failure to consider these allegations in their totality warrants reversal of the dismissal.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON MOLL’S RETALIATION CLAIM**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Moll’s retaliation claim under Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-3(a), because genuine disputes of material fact remain regarding whether Verizon’s actions were materially adverse. *See Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006) (“A materially adverse action is one that might have dissuaded a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination.”).

Moll presented evidence that Verizon forced her transfer to Syracuse—a significant change in her work conditions—shortly after she filed her EEOC charge. *See id.* at 68-69 (finding a materially adverse action where an employee was transferred to a less desirable position after filing a charge). The district court improperly discounted this evidence, erroneously applying the “sham issue of fact” doctrine to disregard conflicting testimony from non-party witnesses. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 249 (1986) (holding that summary judgment is inappropriate where there is a genuine dispute of material fact). The doctrine does not mandate disregarding all conflicting testimony; rather, it addresses attempts by a party to create a sham issue of fact through self-contradiction. *See id.* at 256. Here, Moll’s evidence, including the timing of the transfer and the lack of justification for differential treatment of male colleagues, raises a triable issue of retaliation.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING MOLL’S MOTION TO COMPEL DOCUMENT PRODUCTION**

The district court abused its discretion in denying Moll’s motion to compel production of documents related to Verizon’s promotion and compensation practices. *See Chudasama v. Mazda Motor Corp.*, 123 F.3d 1347, 1357 (11th Cir. 1997) (standard of review for discovery orders). Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(b)(1), relevant evidence need not be admissible to be discoverable; it need only be reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. *See Oppenheimer Fund, Inc. v. Sanders*, 437 U.S. 340, 351 (1978). Moll’s request for documents pertaining to similarly situated male employees was directly relevant to her equal pay and promotion claims under the EPA and Title VII. *See 29 C.F.R. § 1620.13(a)* (permitting discovery of wage and promotion data to establish discrimination).

The district court’s denial of the motion was erroneous because Verizon failed to demonstrate that the requested documents were irrelevant or unduly burdensome. *See Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(2)(C)*. The documents sought—promotion and compensation records for male Sales Engineers—were central to Moll’s prima facie case of discrimination. *See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973) (plaintiff must show she was qualified for the position and treated less favorably than similarly situated males). The court’s refusal to compel production deprived Moll of critical evidence to oppose summary judgment.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be vacated in its entirety, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s opinion.
