**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Pat-Frisk of Michael Soares Violated the Fourth Amendment’s Reasonableness Requirement.**

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures, and its reasonableness requirement mandates that a warrantless search or seizure be justified by probable cause or a recognized exception. *Beck v. Ohio*, 379 U.S. 89, 91 (1964). A pat-frisk is a search subject to the Fourth Amendment, and it may be conducted only if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). Here, the officers lacked reasonable suspicion to pat-frisk Soares, and the frisk was therefore unconstitutional.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that an officer’s mere hunch or inchoate suspicion is insufficient to justify a pat-frisk. *Rios v. United States*, 364 U.S. 253, 261-62 (1960) (holding that reasonable suspicion must be based on “specific and articulable facts” and not on “inarticulate hunches”). In *Henry v. United States*, 361 U.S. 98, 102 (1959), the Court rejected the government’s argument that a pat-frisk was justified by the suspect’s presence in a high-crime area, noting that “[a]nything less than probable cause” is insufficient. Similarly, in *Brinegar v. United States*, 338 U.S. 160, 175 (1949), the Court held that reasonable suspicion must be grounded in “facts and circumstances within the officer’s knowledge” that would warrant a prudent person in believing a suspect is armed and dangerous.

Here, the officers’ justification for the pat-frisk—Soares’s agitation and erratic behavior—was insufficient to create reasonable suspicion. In *Peters v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 47 (1968), the Court upheld a pat-frisk where the suspect’s behavior included “furtive gestures” and attempts to conceal his hands, but the Court emphasized that such behavior must be “unusual” and “give rise to a reasonable fear of danger.” Soares’s behavior—while certainly confrontational—did not rise to the level of conduct that would cause a reasonable officer to fear for his safety. His verbal abuse and agitation, without more, cannot justify a pat-frisk. *See United States v. Mallides*, 473 F.2d 859, 862 (9th Cir. 1969) (holding that “mere nervousness or agitation” is insufficient to justify a frisk).

Moreover, the officers’ reliance on the high-crime area and the passengers’ “furtive movements” before the stop does not cure the constitutional deficiency. In *Draper v. United States*, 358 U.S. 307, 313 (1959), the Court held that an informant’s tip, combined with the suspect’s presence in a high-crime area, could contribute to probable cause, but the Court cautioned that such factors must be “reliable and specific.” Here, the officers’ observations were not sufficiently reliable or specific to justify a pat-frisk. The passengers’ movements before the stop were brief and ambiguous, and the officers did not observe any conduct that would suggest the presence of a weapon. *See Rios*, 364 U.S. at 262 (holding that “furtive movements” alone are insufficient to justify a frisk).

Finally, the officers’ decision to remove Soares from the car and pat-frisk him was not justified by any exception to the warrant requirement. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the government’s attempts to expand the scope of a traffic stop beyond its justification. *Henry*, 361 U.S. at 102 (holding that a pat-frisk must be justified by reasonable suspicion, not by the fact of a lawful stop). Here, the officers’ justification for the pat-frisk—Soares’s agitation—was a product of the stop itself, not an independent basis for suspicion. The Court has made clear that the Fourth Amendment does not permit officers to expand the scope of a lawful stop based on a suspect’s reaction to the stop. *See Brinegar*, 338 U.S. at 175 (holding that the Fourth Amendment requires that searches and seizures be “strictly tied to and justified by the circumstances which rendered [them] permissible”).

II. **The Evidence Obtained from the Unconstitutional Pat-Frisk Must Be Suppressed.**

The exclusionary rule applies to evidence obtained as a result of an unconstitutional search or seizure, and such evidence must be suppressed to deter future constitutional violations. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961). The Supreme Court has held that the exclusionary rule applies to federal prosecutions, and that evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is inadmissible. *Weeks v. United States*, 232 U.S. 383, 398 (1914). Here, the handgun was obtained as a direct result of the unconstitutional pat-frisk, and it must be suppressed.

The government cannot rely on the “independent source” doctrine or the “inevitable discovery” doctrine to justify the admission of the handgun, as neither doctrine applies here. In *Silverthorne Lumber Co. v. United States*, 251 U.S. 385, 392 (1920), the Court held that evidence obtained as a direct result of an unconstitutional search must be suppressed, even if the government could have obtained the evidence from an independent source. Similarly, in *Nardone v. United States*, 302 U.S. 379, 384 (1937), the Court held that the exclusionary rule applies to evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment, regardless of whether the evidence could have been obtained lawfully.

Here, the handgun was obtained as a direct result of the unconstitutional pat-frisk, and the government has not shown that the handgun would have been discovered through an independent source or inevitable discovery. The officers did not have probable cause to arrest Soares or search him incident to arrest, and the handgun was not in plain view. *See Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 465 (1971) (holding that the plain view doctrine requires that the officer have probable cause to believe that the item is contraband or evidence of a crime). The government’s attempt to justify the pat-frisk based on Soares’s agitation is unavailing, as his agitation was a product of the unconstitutional stop and frisk, not an independent basis for suspicion.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress. The pat-frisk of Soares was unconstitutional, and the handgun obtained as a result of the frisk must be suppressed. The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
