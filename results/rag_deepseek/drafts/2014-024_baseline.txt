**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be a threat. It does. The text of the statute, this Court’s settled mens rea principles, and the First Amendment all compel that conclusion.

**I. The Text of Section 875(c) and Background Mens Rea Principles Require Subjective Intent.**

Section 875(c) prohibits “transmit[ting] in interstate or foreign commerce any communication containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another.” The statute does not specify a mental state, but this Court has long held that “silence as to mens rea is not an invitation to dispense with it.” *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 606 (1994). Rather, “some indication of congressional intent, express or implied, is required to dispense with mens rea as an element of the offense.” *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 438 (1978). Nothing in §875(c) suggests Congress intended to create a strict-liability crime. To the contrary, the term “threat” itself imports a mental state: a communication that the speaker intends to be understood as a serious expression of an intent to harm. This Court has therefore applied a “knowingly” mens rea to each element of a similar statute. *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 70-72 (1994) (reading “knowingly” to apply to the sexually explicit nature of the material). So too here: the government must prove that the defendant knew his communication contained a threat—i.e., that he subjectively intended it to be threatening.

**II. The First Amendment Demands a Subjective Intent Standard.**

The First Amendment prohibits the government from punishing speech unless it falls within a few narrow categories of unprotected expression. One such category is “true threats.” *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). This Court has defined true threats as “those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group.” *Id.* at 359 (emphasis added). The word “means” is dispositive: it requires a subjective intent to threaten. *Black* thus rejected an objective test that would punish speech based on how a reasonable listener would interpret it. *See id.* at 367-68 (striking down statute that allowed jury to infer intent from cross burning alone). The Court explained that the government must prove “that the defendant intended to intimidate.” *Id.* at 367.

This holding is consistent with *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam), where the Court considered “whether a ‘true threat’ was made” by examining the speaker’s intent and context, not merely a reasonable observer’s reaction. The Third Circuit’s objective standard—asking whether a “reasonable speaker” would foresee the statement as threatening—is incompatible with *Black*. By requiring only that a reasonable person (not the actual defendant) would recognize the threat, the standard punishes speech the defendant did not intend to be threatening. That criminalizes protected speech and chills expression that is merely offensive or hyperbolic. *See Dombrowski v. Pfister*, 380 U.S. 479, 486-87 (1965) (recognizing that overbroad statutes “may deter the exercise of First Amendment rights”); *cf. Gormley v. Director, Conn. Dept. of Adult Prob.*, 449 U.S. 1023, 1025 (1980) (White, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (“speech may be ‘annoying’ without losing its First Amendment protection”).

**III. The Government’s Policy Arguments Do Not Justify an Objective Standard.**

The court of appeals reasoned that a subjective-intent requirement would “fail to protect individuals from the fear of violence.” That reasoning conflates the *impact* of speech with the *culpability* of the speaker. The First Amendment does not allow punishment based solely on the listener’s fear; otherwise, any statement that accidentally instills fear could be criminalized. *Black* itself recognized that the state’s interest in protecting against fear is served by punishing those who *mean* to threaten. 538 U.S. at 360. A speaker who does not intend his words as a threat cannot be said to have the mental state necessary for criminal liability or for the forfeiture of constitutional protection. The objective standard would impose criminal liability for negligence or even strict liability, which this Court has consistently rejected when First Amendment rights are at stake. *See X-Citement Video*, 513 U.S. at 72-73.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury be required to find that the defendant subjectively intended his communications as threats.
