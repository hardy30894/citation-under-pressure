**ARGUMENT**

I. The Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that competency to waive counsel or plead guilty demands a higher level of mental functioning than the minimal standard for competency to stand trial. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871 (1988); *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981). This Court has long recognized that the Constitution protects a defendant’s right to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of fundamental rights. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242–43 (1969). To ensure such waivers are valid, courts must assess whether the defendant possesses the capacity for “reasoned choice” among available alternatives. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Evans*, 800 F.2d at 887. This standard reflects the principle that constitutional waivers are not merely mechanical acts but deliberate decisions requiring cognitive and volitional capacity. *See id.*

II. The trial court failed to apply the “reasoned choice” standard and instead relied on evaluations of competency to stand trial, which are insufficient.

The Nevada courts erroneously conflated the standards for competency to stand trial and competency to waive rights. *See Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975) (defining competency to stand trial as the ability to understand proceedings and assist counsel); *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam) (same). Competency to stand trial requires only a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings, whereas competency to waive rights demands the ability to make a reasoned decision. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Evans*, 800 F.2d at 887. The state court’s post-conviction ruling relied exclusively on psychiatric evaluations conducted to assess Moran’s competency to stand trial, not his capacity for reasoned choice. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963) (federal courts must ensure state courts applied correct legal standards).

III. The record contained substantial evidence triggering a duty to inquire into Moran’s competency under the heightened standard.

Due process requires a trial court to initiate a competency hearing whenever it has a “good faith doubt” about the defendant’s competency. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1982), cert. denied, 456 U.S. 946 (1982). Such doubt arises when there is “substantial evidence” of incompetence. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856. Here, Moran’s recent suicide attempt, his monosyllabic responses during the plea colloquy, his stated desire to prevent mitigating evidence from being presented, and his use of multiple medications—including sedatives and antiepileptics—created a duty to inquire. *See Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518 (medication use may undermine competency to waive rights). The trial court’s failure to conduct any inquiry violated due process.

IV. The post-conviction hearing did not cure the error because it applied the wrong standard and occurred too late.

A delayed competency hearing cannot retroactively validate an invalid waiver. *See Creech v. Arave*, 947 F.2d 873, 879 n.5 (9th Cir. 1991) (noting uncertainty whether § 2254(d) presumption applies to competency-to-plead determinations). The state court’s post-conviction ruling erroneously relied on the competency-to-stand-trial standard, and the record does not support a finding of “reasoned choice.” *See Townsend*, 372 U.S. at 315 (federal courts must independently review competency determinations). Moreover, the delay between the plea and the hearing—over three years—further undermined the reliability of any retrospective assessment. *See Miller v. Fenton*, 474 U.S. 104, 117 (1985) (voluntariness determinations require prompt inquiry).

**CONCLUSION**
The Due Process Clause requires trial courts to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights. The Nevada courts failed to do so here, and the record contained substantial evidence triggering a duty to inquire. The Ninth Circuit correctly reversed the denial of Moran’s habeas petition. This Court should affirm.
