# ARGUMENT

## I. FLORIDA'S CAPITAL SENTENCING SCHEME COMPLIES WITH THE SIXTH AMENDMENT AS INTERPRETED BY RING

### A. Ring Does Not Require Jury Unanimity or Elimination of Judicial Factfinding

Petitioner's Sixth Amendment argument rests on a fundamental misreading of *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). This Court held only that "the Sixth Amendment requires that the aggravating factors necessary for imposition of the death penalty must be found by a jury." *Id.* at 609. Florida's scheme satisfies this requirement: a jury sits as factfinder, hears evidence of aggravating and mitigating circumstances, and returns a recommendation. The jury in Hurst's case did exactly this, finding sufficient aggravation to recommend death by a vote of seven to five.

*Ring* addressed Arizona's scheme, under which a judge alone determined the existence of aggravating circumstances after a jury verdict of guilt. *Id.* at 588-89. This Court struck that scheme because it removed the jury entirely from the aggravating-circumstance determination, reasoning that "the Sixth Amendment requires that those circumstances be proved by a jury." *Id.* at 604. Florida's scheme is fundamentally different: it requires jury participation and recommendation, unlike the judge-alone system in Arizona. This Court's holding in *Ring* did not purport to require unanimity in that jury recommendation, nor did it require any particular voting threshold. *Ring* did not disturb *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), which established the operative rule that "any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury." *Id.* at 490. Neither *Apprendi* nor *Ring* addressed the degree of jury consensus required.

### B. This Court's Sixth Amendment Jurisprudence Has Never Imposed a Unanimity Requirement on States as a Matter of Federal Constitutional Law

The Sixth Amendment's jury-trial guarantee, as incorporated against the states, does not mandate unanimous verdicts. In *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404 (1972), this Court upheld convictions returned by nonunanimous juries, holding that unanimity is not a constitutionally required feature of jury trials binding on the states. The plurality reasoned that the "purpose of trial by jury" is fulfilled by requiring "a group of laymen representative of a cross section of the community" to deliberate, without requiring that deliberation to end in unanimous accord. *Id.* at 410. Although *Apodaca* addressed guilt-phase verdicts, its reasoning applies with equal force to sentencing recommendations, which involve the jury's application of community judgment to aggravating and mitigating evidence—precisely the values *Apodaca* found satisfied by nonunanimous verdicts.

Florida's supreme court correctly recognized this distinction, and the dissent's invocation of Florida's "outlier status" among death-penalty states does not transform a policy preference into a constitutional mandate. That other states have chosen unanimity as a matter of legislative grace does not establish that the Sixth Amendment compels it. The dissenting justices' policy concerns, however sincerely held, are properly directed to the Florida Legislature, not to this Court sitting in constitutional review.

## II. FLORIDA'S PROCEDURE FOR ADJUDICATING MENTAL RETARDATION CLAIMS DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT

### A. Atkins Left the Method of Enforcement to the States

In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), this Court held that "death is not a suitable punishment for a mentally retarded criminal," *id.* at 321, but expressly left "to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction." *Id.* at 317. Florida has done precisely that, establishing a three-part test requiring proof of "(1) significantly subaverage general intellectual functioning; (2) concurrent deficits in adaptive behavior; and (3) manifestation of the condition before age eighteen." Fla. Stat. § 921.137(1) (2012).

Petitioner received a full and fair opportunity to litigate this claim. He was afforded an evidentiary hearing during his initial postconviction proceedings, where both a defense expert and a State expert examined him using standardized intelligence testing. The trial court's determination that Hurst failed to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence—a burden this Court has approved for such claims—was not constitutionally infirm merely because a judge, rather than a jury, made the factual determination.

### B. Nothing in Atkins or Ring Requires Jury Adjudication of Mental Retardation

Petitioner's argument conflates two distinct constitutional inquiries: the Eighth Amendment categorical bar recognized in *Atkins*, and the Sixth Amendment jury-trial right addressed in *Ring*. *Atkins* is silent on procedural mechanics, and this Court has never extended *Ring*'s aggravating-circumstance holding to encompass eligibility determinations governing categorical exemptions from capital punishment. Mental retardation, unlike an aggravating circumstance, does not "increase the penalty" within the meaning of *Apprendi*—rather, it operates as an absolute bar that a defendant must affirmatively establish. This structural distinction places Eighth Amendment eligibility determinations outside *Ring*'s Sixth Amendment framework.

The trial court's procedure—permitting the jury to consider mental retardation evidence as mitigation while reserving the categorical bar determination for judicial resolution—reflects a reasonable accommodation of *Atkins*'s mandate. Petitioner's mental health evidence was fully presented to and considered by the jury as mitigation, and separately adjudicated by the trial court as a categorical bar. This dual-track approach satisfies both *Atkins*'s substantive guarantee and Florida's sentencing structure.

## CONCLUSION

Florida's capital sentencing scheme satisfies *Ring*'s requirement that a jury find the aggravating circumstances necessary for a death sentence. Neither the Sixth Amendment nor this Court's precedents require unanimity in that jury recommendation, and neither *Ring* nor *Atkins* requires that mental retardation—a categorical bar rather than an aggravating factor—be submitted to a jury as a matter of federal constitutional law. The judgment below should be affirmed.
