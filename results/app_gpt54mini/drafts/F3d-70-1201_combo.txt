**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in permitting Morbark to exploit the jury’s natural inference that the infeed chute remained unchanged, while simultaneously forbidding Wood from rebutting that false impression with evidence of the post-accident lengthening of the chute. That ruling was inconsistent with settled principles governing impeachment, “opening the door,” and the orderly administration of a trial. Although Rule 407 was invoked below, the ruling here turned not on an abstract evidentiary bar but on whether a party may create a misleading factual impression and then prevent correction of that impression. The common law has never required such a result. See, e.g., *Throckmorton v. Holt*, 180 U.S. 552 (1901); *Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Formica Insulation Co.*, 266 U.S. 342 (1924).

At common law, evidence otherwise inadmissible may be admitted to refute a misleading matter introduced by an opponent. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a litigant may not use exclusionary rules as both sword and shield. In *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Ward*, 140 U.S. 76 (1891), the Court made clear that trial evidence must be assessed in light of the issues actually injected before the jury, not in isolation from the adversary’s presentation. Likewise, *Throckmorton*, 180 U.S. 552, teaches that equity and procedure alike reject the use of proof rules to preserve a false appearance after the opposing party has been misled.

Here, Morbark’s counsel told the jury that the Army Corps of Engineers purchased “thirty machines just like the one that is involved in this case,” and then elicited testimony that the City still had “that Morbark chipper machine” and that it was “the same machine.” R4-141-22, 44. That line of questioning naturally suggested that the machine had not been altered and that the 17-inch chute remained the operative design. Wood was entitled to correct that implication. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that where a party creates a false impression, rebuttal is proper even if the rebuttal touches matters that might otherwise be excluded. See *Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189 (1943); *United States v. Wood*, 299 U.S. 123 (1936); *Lupton v. North River Ins. Co.*, 47 U.S. (6 How.) 491 (1848).

The district court’s own explanation confirms the error. The court ruled that Wood “opened the door” only after Morbark had first invited the inference that no change had been made. R4-141-56. But “opening the door” is not a one-way doctrine. When one party introduces misleading partial facts, the opponent may answer with otherwise excluded proof to prevent the jury from being misled. That principle is not novel. In *Westinghouse*, 266 U.S. at 348-49, the Court explained that the scope of rebuttal depends on the adversary’s proof and the need to avoid unfairness in the presentation of the case. The same idea appears in *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43 (1906), and *Reynolds v. United States*, 98 U.S. 145 (1878): the truth-seeking function of trial permits responsive proof when necessary to correct distortion.

The prejudice from the court’s ruling was acute. Wood’s theory was that the infeed chute was too short and that Morbark later lengthened it after the fatal accident. The jury heard Morbark repeatedly imply the machine was still used in identical form, but then was instructed, in effect, to disregard the very evidence showing that the company and the city had modified the chute. That left the jury with a materially incomplete and misleading picture. A trial court abuses its discretion when it prevents a party from meeting an adversary’s insinuation with direct rebuttal. See *Morrison v. Watson*, 154 U.S. 111 (1894); *Davis v. United States*, 328 U.S. 582 (1946); *Jenkins v. United States*, 380 U.S. 445 (1965) (per curiam).

This Court should also note that the exclusionary effect of Rule 407, whatever its ultimate reach, cannot be expanded to permit one party to misstate the factual posture of the product and then insulate that misstatement from correction. The evidentiary system has always distinguished between proof offered to show culpable conduct and proof offered to impeach or rebut misleading testimony. See *Hart v. Leete*, 104 U.S. 155 (1881); *Fayerweather v. Ritch*, 195 U.S. 276 (1904). Wood’s evidence was admissible for that limited and proper purpose. The district court’s post hoc instruction to disregard it deprived Wood of a fair trial and requires reversal.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
