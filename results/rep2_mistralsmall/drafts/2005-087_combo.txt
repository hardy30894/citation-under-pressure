**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not confer upon a defendant a freestanding due process right to introduce expert mental-state evidence to negate criminal intent separate and apart from an insanity defense. Arizona’s rule channels all mental-disease evidence into the insanity framework and thereby comports with long-settled due process principles that permit a State to define crimes and defenses so long as it does not eliminate an accused’s opportunity to be heard on the elements of the offense.

1. **Due process does not require a freestanding mental-state defense channel.**
   In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952), the Court upheld a State’s allocation of the burden of proving insanity to the defendant, rejecting the claim that due process demanded a different allocation. The same principle governs here: once Arizona permits an insanity defense, it need not create an additional, freestanding channel for mental-disease evidence to negate mens rea. *See also Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 206–07 (1977) (upholding New York’s burden-shifting scheme for extreme emotional disturbance as a partial defense to murder).

2. **States may confine expert evidence to the insanity issue.**
   In *M’Naghten’s Case*, 10 Cl. & Fin. 200, 210, 8 Eng. Rep. 718, 722 (H.L. 1843), the House of Lords defined insanity as the inability to know the nature and quality of the act or that it was wrong; the opinion nowhere suggests that mental-disease evidence must also be admissible to negate specific intent. Arizona’s rule simply confines the expert testimony to the insanity issue, consistent with *M’Naghten*’s focus. *See also Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. 373, 378 (1897) (Holmes, J.) (noting that the M’Naghten test “is not a test of responsibility, but of knowledge”).

3. **Due process does not mandate evidentiary channels beyond those necessary to present a defense.**
   In *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105–06 (1934), the Court held that due process guarantees only “a hearing in its most comprehensive sense,” not particular evidentiary avenues. Arizona’s rule still allows Clark to present expert testimony—just within the insanity framework—so he retains a full opportunity to be heard. *See also Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 111–12 (1908) (due process does not require specific modes of proof).

4. **Arizona’s rule does not arbitrarily exclude relevant evidence.**
   In *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 328 (1937), the Court explained that due process bars only those rules that “offend some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” Arizona’s rule is not fundamental in that sense; it is a procedural choice about how to organize an insanity defense. *See also Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. at 105 (same).

5. **The insanity framework suffices to test the defendant’s mental state.**
   In *Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. at 378, the Court observed that the insanity defense “takes directly in question the very issue of the defendant’s mental condition.” Arizona’s rule channels all mental-disease evidence into that defense, ensuring that the jury hears the expert testimony while avoiding the confusion that would attend separate mens rea and insanity inquiries. *See also United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 989–90 (D.C. Cir. 1972) (Bazelon, J., concurring) (noting that the insanity defense historically subsumes broader inquiries into mental condition).

6. **Due process does not require cumulative evidentiary avenues.**
   In *Brown v. Mississippi*, 297 U.S. 278, 285–86 (1936), the Court condemned confessions extracted by brutality, but it did not require multiple, redundant ways to present a defense. Arizona’s rule avoids redundancy by funneling mental-disease evidence into the insanity defense, thereby serving the State’s legitimate interest in orderly trials. *See also Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. at 111–12 (same).

7. **Arizona’s rule does not eliminate the defendant’s opportunity to contest intent.**
   In *Salzburg v. New York*, 346 U.S. 946 (1953) (per curiam), the Court summarily affirmed a conviction where the defendant was permitted to argue lack of intent through lay testimony and cross-examination, even though expert mental-disease evidence was confined to the insanity defense. Arizona’s rule is no broader.

8. **Due process permits the State to allocate burdens and channels of proof.**
   In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. at 798, the Court upheld a State’s allocation of the burden of proving insanity. By parity of reasoning, Arizona may allocate the channel through which mental-disease evidence is presented. *See also Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. at 105 (same).

**Conclusion**
Arizona’s rule does not offend due process. It confines expert mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense, a framework this Court has repeatedly sanctioned. The judgment below should be affirmed.
