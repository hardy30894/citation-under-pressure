**ARGUMENT**

ERISA’s three-year limitations period begins only “after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). That text requires more than disclosure by the fiduciary; it requires knowledge by the plaintiff. The court of appeals erred by treating Intel’s disclosures as enough, even though Sulyma testified that he was unaware of the challenged investments and did not recall reading the documents Intel says disclosed them. That ruling conflicts with the statutory text and this Court’s decisions requiring courts to give effect to Congress’s chosen limitations trigger. See Heimeshoff v. Hartford Life & Accident Ins. Co., 571 U.S. 99, 107-08 (2013); Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 589 U.S. ___, 140 S. Ct. 768, 776-77 (2020).

Section 1113(2) uses the phrase “actual knowledge,” not “constructive knowledge,” and certainly not “available information.” Congress knew how to draft a constructive-knowledge rule; it did so in ERISA’s original limitations provision and then removed it. See Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 776; Enron Corp. v. Sprint Corp., 342 F. Supp. 2d 627, 643 (S.D. Tex. 2004). The ordinary meaning of “actual knowledge” is knowledge in fact, not knowledge that could have been gained by reading materials made available by another party. See Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 776-77; Black’s Law Dictionary 1090 (11th ed. 2019) (defining “actual knowledge” as “direct and clear knowledge, as distinguished from constructive knowledge”). The court of appeals’ rule collapses the statute into a constructive-knowledge regime, contrary to Congress’s choice. See Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 777-78.

This Court has already rejected the notion that ERISA’s limitations period turns on whether information was disclosed to a participant. In Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, the Court held that “to meet § 1113(2)’s ‘actual knowledge’ requirement, the plaintiff must in fact have become aware of the information” showing the breach. 140 S. Ct. at 777. The Court explained that “disclosure of plan information alone does not trigger ERISA’s statute of limitations.” Id. at 779. A participant who “did not actually read” the documents cannot be charged with actual knowledge merely because the documents were accessible. Id. at 778-79. That is decisive here, because the petition squarely presents the same question: whether Intel’s disclosures started the clock even though Sulyma chose not to read, or could not recall reading, them. Under Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, they did not. 140 S. Ct. at 778-79.

The court of appeals relied on the proposition that knowledge of the transaction, rather than knowledge of illegality, can suffice. But that is beside the point. This Court has recognized that ERISA’s “actual knowledge” requirement means knowledge of the facts constituting the breach, not legal awareness that those facts violate ERISA. See Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 777; Merck & Co. v. Reynolds, 559 U.S. 633, 648-49 (2010). Even under that standard, the plaintiff must actually know the facts, not merely have access to them. See Rotella v. Wood, 528 U.S. 549, 555-56 (2000). Intel’s rule would permit limitations to run before the participant knows anything at all about the alleged breach, so long as the fiduciary placed the relevant facts somewhere the participant could have found them. That is not “actual knowledge.” See Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 777-78.

Nor is Intel’s rule necessary to protect repose. ERISA already supplies a six-year outside limit from “the date of the last action which constituted a part of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(1). This Court has repeatedly recognized that Congress balanced repose and enforcement by pairing a six-year objective limit with a narrower three-year actual-knowledge rule. See Heimeshoff, 571 U.S. at 105-06; Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 776. Intel’s approach would erase that balance by making the three-year period run whenever fiduciaries can prove disclosure, regardless of whether the participant actually learned of the breach.

Because the court of appeals’ decision cannot be reconciled with § 1113(2)’s text or this Court’s interpretation in Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, the judgment should be reversed. The statute bars suit only when the plaintiff actually knew the relevant facts, not when the defendant merely disclosed them. 140 S. Ct. at 777-79.
