**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 to cover documents that are “predominantly internal” and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation finds no support in the text of the Freedom of Information Act or in its consistent interpretation by the courts. This Court should reverse.

**I. Exemption 2 is Narrowly Confined to Trivial Personnel Matters, Not Substantive Operational Data**

The plain language of Exemption 2 permits withholding of matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). From FOIA’s inception, courts have uniformly read this language to cover only minor housekeeping matters—such as parking regulations, lunch hours, and sick leave—that are “not of genuine and significant public interest.” *American Mail Line Ltd. v. Gulick*, 411 F.2d 696, 699 (D.C. Cir. 1969) (citing S. Rep. No. 813, 89th Cong., 1st Sess. 8 (1965)). The legislative history confirms that Exemption 2 was meant to exempt “internal personnel rules and practices which are trivial in nature.” *Id.* at 700. The D.C. Circuit later reiterated that Exemption 2 “applies only to minor housekeeping matters,” not to “the substantive rules of an agency.” *Ackerly v. Ley*, 420 F.2d 1336, 1339 (D.C. Cir. 1969).

The Sixth Circuit has likewise held that Exemption 2 does not reach an agency’s internal manual that “is used in dealing with the public” because such material is not “solely” a personnel rule. *Hawkes v. IRS*, 409 F.2d 1082, 1084 (6th Cir. 1969). The Ninth Circuit itself, in a pre-1970 decision, recognized that Exemption 2 is limited to “matters of a trivial nature.” *Department of the Army v. Rich*, 416 F.2d 1137, 1138 (9th Cir. 1969). District courts have followed suit, holding that the exemption “does not embrace all internal rules but only those relating to personnel.” *Consumers Union of United States v. Veterans Administration*, 301 F. Supp. 796, 800 (S.D.N.Y. 1969); *see also Tuchinsky v. Selective Service System*, 294 F. Supp. 803, 807 (N.D. Ill. 1969) (Exemption 2 “must be narrowly construed” and applies only to matters “solely” internal personnel). The consistent message is that Exemption 2 is a “narrow exception” that excludes “rules and practices that are essentially personnel matters.” *Sharad v. FDIC*, 411 F.2d 1005, 1007 (D.C. Cir. 1969); *see also Rosenbloom v. Department of Justice*, 279 F. Supp. 133, 135 (D. Colo. 1968) (Exemption 2 covers “only those matters related to the internal personnel rules and practices of the agency—not to all internal rules”).

**II. The ESQD Maps Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices”**

The ESQD arc maps are technical safety data used to design ammunition storage facilities and to plan operations. They have nothing to do with personnel management—hiring, firing, leave, or other employee conduct. The Navy’s own description confirms that the maps are “a long term planning tool for the Navy” and are used to “design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities.” App. 12a, 14a. Such substantive operational data cannot be shoehorned into a category Congress reserved for trivial administrative minutiae. As the dissent below recognized, the maps “do not concern the internal personnel rules and practices of the Navy.” App. 25a.

**III. The “Predominantly Internal” Standard Lacks Statutory Basis**

The Ninth Circuit adopted the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc). That test rewrites the statute. Congress used the word “solely,” not “predominantly.” The substitution enlarges the exemption beyond its intended scope. Every pre-1970 case that interpreted Exemption 2 applied the “solely” standard, not a looser “predominantly” gauge. *See, e.g., American Mail Line*, 411 F.2d at 700 (emphasizing that the matter must be “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices”). The Ninth Circuit’s approach effectively nullifies the word “solely” and invites agencies to withhold any document that is “predominantly” internal, regardless of whether it concerns personnel at all.

**IV. The Risk-of-Circumvention Test Does Not Apply Here**

Even if the “predominantly internal” standard were correct, the Navy has not shown that disclosure of the ESQD maps “may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” *Rose* (cited in the opinion below) addressed only law enforcement materials. The maps here are not law enforcement manuals; they are factual data about blast zones. The Navy’s fear that a terrorist could “reverse engineer” the maps is speculative and contradicted by the Navy’s voluntary release of identical maps for the Bangor base. The maps are not classified, and the Navy could have invoked Exemption 1 if it truly believed national security was at stake. *See* App. 30a (dissent). The risk-of-circumvention rationale is a post hoc justification that cannot transform operational safety data into an internal personnel rule.

**V. FOIA Exemptions Must Be Narrowly Constructed**

This Court has long held that FOIA’s exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be “narrowly construed” to effectuate the statute’s purpose of “full agency disclosure.” *Rose* (citing *S. Rep. No. 813*). The Ninth Circuit’s decision flouts this principle by creating a judicial gloss that expands Exemption 2 far beyond its text. The proper remedy is to reverse and remand with instructions to order disclosure of the ESQD maps.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
