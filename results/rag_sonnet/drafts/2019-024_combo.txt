# ARGUMENT

## I. Actual Knowledge Arises When a Fiduciary Furnishes the Participant the Information Describing the Challenged Transaction

Section 1113(2) bars any claim brought more than three years after "the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This Court has long distinguished between the *creation* of a limitations right and the mechanics that trigger it: a limitations provision "does not grant the [claimant] a new right, but is a limitation of the [claimant's] long-established right to sue." *United States v. Wurts*, 303 U.S. 414, 417 (1938). That principle applies with equal force here. Congress did not create a novel cause of action contingent on subjective legal sophistication; it simply set the outer boundary within which an existing cause of action must be pursued.

When Congress repealed the separate constructive-knowledge provision in 1987, it eliminated only the mechanism by which knowledge could be *imputed* from public filings the plaintiff never saw. It did not convert "actual knowledge" into a requirement of introspective certainty about facts the fiduciary itself placed directly before the participant. As in *Unexcelled Chemical Corp. v. United States*, 345 U.S. 59 (1953), where the limitations period was tied to the objective fact "when petitioner violated the statute," the trigger for accrual is an objective event—receipt of the disclosure—not a plaintiff's later, self-reported state of mind.

## II. The Narrow Fraudulent-Concealment Exception Confirms That Disclosure, Not Recollection, Governs

Courts applying analogous limitations statutes have consistently held that tolling for lack of knowledge is available only where the defendant affirmatively hid the relevant facts—not wherever a plaintiff simply failed to absorb information placed in front of him. In *Curtis v. Connly*, 257 U.S. 260 (1921), this Court examined a state statute suspending accrual only where a defendant "shall fraudulently, by actual misrepresentation, conceal from him the existence of the cause of such action." *Id.* at 261. No such concealment is alleged here: Intel's fund fact sheets, summary plan descriptions, and website disclosures affirmatively described the very asset allocations Sulyma now challenges. Similarly, in *Exploration Co. v. United States*, 247 U.S. 435 (1918), the limitations question turned on whether the defendants had concealed the fraud, since "the frauds were concealed until after six years had elapsed from the issuance of the patents." *Id.* at 437. Where, as here, the fiduciary makes full disclosure rather than concealing anything, the rationale for extending the limitations period disappears.

*American Surety Co. v. Pauly*, 170 U.S. 160 (1898), illustrates the same point from the other direction: there, the plaintiff's own pleadings fixed "discovery" to the specific dates on which he learned of the wrongdoing and thereafter "presented to the defendant a claim in writing for the losses occasioned by such acts." *Id.* at 161. Discovery is treated as an objective, provable event tied to notice, not an unfalsifiable assertion of forgetfulness offered years later in litigation.

## III. A Rule Permitting Bare Denials of Recollection to Defeat Undisputed Disclosure Would Undermine the Repose Limitations Periods Exist to Provide

Statutes of limitations exist to bring finality to claims once the underlying facts have been placed within a party's reach. This Court has repeatedly enforced limitations periods strictly against efforts to extend them through post hoc characterizations of ignorance. In *United States v. A.S. Kreider Co.*, 313 U.S. 443 (1941), the Court addressed how a specific statutory limitations period controls over more permissive general limitations rules, reinforcing that Congress's chosen trigger—not a litigant's convenient framing of the facts—must govern. And in *United States v. Goldman*, 277 U.S. 229 (1928), the Court recognized that limitations provisions are to be given their ordinary, practical effect once the triggering facts are established, rather than being reopened based on a party's later account of subjective awareness.

The tolling provision at issue in *Jaben v. United States*, 381 U.S. 214 (1965), likewise shows that this Court examines the *purpose* of a limitations-extending mechanism—there, "the purpose of the tolling provision"—to ensure it is not manipulated to indefinitely extend a period Congress meant to be finite. *Id.* at 224 (Goldberg, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part) (describing majority's reasoning). Permitting a plan participant to defeat summary judgment merely by declaring, without more, that he does not "recall" reading disclosures he indisputably received would transform § 1113(2)'s three-year period into a standardless inquiry incapable of ever being resolved short of trial—precisely the result the doctrine of finality embodied in *Baker v. Cummings*, 181 U.S. 117 (1901), and *Peak v. United States*, 353 U.S. 43 (1957), counsels against. Once a full hearing establishes that the relevant facts were presented to the claimant, as in *Baker*, the matter should be treated as resolved rather than perpetually reopened by an uncorroborated assertion of memory loss.

## CONCLUSION

Because Intel's undisputed disclosures gave Sulyma actual knowledge of the challenged asset allocations more than three years before he filed suit, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
