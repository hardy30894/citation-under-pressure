**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SEARCH WARRANT FAILED TO ESTABLISH A NECESSARY NEXUS BETWEEN THE PLACE TO BE SEARCHED AND THE CRIMES UNDER INVESTIGATION, VIOLATING THE FOURTH AMENDMENT**

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects “the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures.” *Weeks v. United States*, 232 U.S. 383, 392 (1914). A search warrant may only issue upon probable cause supported by “oath or affirmation,” and must particularly describe the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 654 (1961) (citing *Matter of the Application of the United States for a Search Warrant*, 293 U.S. 500, 504 (1935)). The requirement of particularity ensures that the scope of a search is limited to what is reasonably related to the offense under investigation and the place where evidence might be found.

Here, the warrant authorized the search of Joubert’s parents’ home—*a residence not owned or occupied by Joubert*—for evidence of sexual exploitation of children. The affidavit supporting the warrant relied almost entirely on speculative assertions and generalized observations about the conduct of child molesters. It stated that “persons engaged in the molestation and exploitation of minors often maintain possession and/or control of physical or electronic documents pertaining to their victims,” and that such evidence “may confirm or dispel” Joubert’s background, relationships with minors, and travel. But this conclusory language, unsupported by specific facts linking Joubert to the premises, fails to establish a nexus between the place to be searched and the crimes under investigation.

The Supreme Court has long held that a warrant must be supported by facts showing that the evidence sought is likely to be found at the location specified. In *United States v. Rabinowitz*, 339 U.S. 56, 61 (1950), the Court emphasized that “the warrant must describe with particularity the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized,” and that “the facts supporting the warrant must be such as to justify a belief that the items sought are located at the place to be searched.” The affidavit here contained no facts indicating that Joubert had ever lived at his parents’ home, used it as a base of operations, or stored any materials there. The only evidence connecting Joubert to the residence was that he had moved there with the assistance of his son, SJ, and that he had discarded computer components in the home. But the mere act of discarding parts of a computer does not establish that the *completed* computer, or its data, was ever located there. *See United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452, 465 (1932) (holding that a search warrant must be supported by facts showing that the evidence sought is likely to be found at the place to be searched).

Moreover, the affidavit’s reliance on the general behavior of child molesters—“often maintain possession and/or control of physical or electronic documents”—is insufficient to establish probable cause. *See United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. at 465. The mere fact that some offenders keep records does not justify a warrant to search a home where no evidence of possession or use has been shown. The warrant here was not based on facts showing that Joubert had ever used the home to store or access child pornography. The absence of such facts renders the warrant constitutionally defective.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that a warrant may be issued based on “a mere possibility” or “a speculative connection.” *United States v. Rabinowitz*, 339 U.S. at 61. Here, the warrant was issued based on a chain of inferences: Joubert moved to the home → he discarded computer parts → he may have stored data there → he may have used it to exploit minors. This speculative chain fails to meet the standard of probable cause. *See United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. at 465. The warrant, therefore, violated the Fourth Amendment by failing to establish a sufficient nexus between the place to be searched and the crimes under investigation.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN ADMITTING TESTIMONY OF UNCHARGED CHILD MOLESTATION, VIOLATING THE FEDERAL RULE OF EVIDENCE 404(b)**

Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) provides that evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts is not admissible to prove the character of a person in order to show action in conformity therewith. *See Fed. R. Evid. 404(b)*. Such evidence may be admitted only if it is relevant to a material issue other than character, and its probative value outweighs its prejudicial effect. *See United States v. Brawner*, 188 F.2d 177, 180 (D.C. Cir. 1951) (holding that evidence of prior misconduct is inadmissible unless it bears a “clear and substantial” relevance to the issue at trial).

The district court admitted testimony from SJ, Joubert’s son, regarding alleged sexual abuse in the mid-1980s—acts that were not charged and not part of the indictment. This testimony was offered to show Joubert’s “pattern” of conduct, thereby establishing a propensity to molest children. But such evidence is precisely what Rule 404(b) was designed to exclude. *See United States v. Brawner*, 188 F.2d at 180. The mere fact that Joubert allegedly abused one child decades earlier does not prove he abused KC or possessed child pornography in 2002.

Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. at 465.
