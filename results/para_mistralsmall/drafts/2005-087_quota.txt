**ARGUMENT**

Respondent submits that the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not confer upon a defendant a freestanding right to introduce expert mental-state evidence to negate mens rea, separate and apart from the insanity defense. The Arizona Supreme Court’s decision is consistent with this Court’s precedents and those of the state courts of last resort.

First, this Court has long held that the Due Process Clause does not impose a uniform code of criminal procedure upon the States. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952) (“Due process does not require that every conceivable step be taken, at whatever cost, to eliminate the possibility of convicting an innocent man.”). Arizona’s allocation of the burden of proving insanity to the defendant does not offend due process. *Id.* at 802 (“[T]he State is not required by the Federal Constitution to adopt any particular form of procedure in criminal cases so long as it does not appear that the accused has been denied fundamental rights.”). The same principle governs the admissibility of expert evidence: the State may regulate the scope of such evidence so long as it does not deny a fundamental right. *See Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437, 455 (1992) (upholding state procedural rule allocating burden of proof on competency to stand trial).

Second, this Court has repeatedly recognized that States may define the defenses available to criminal defendants. *Martin v. Ohio*, 480 U.S. 228, 233 (1987) (“The Due Process Clause does not mandate that a State create any particular defense to a crime.”). Arizona’s limitation of expert mental-state evidence to the insanity defense is a permissible exercise of that authority. *See id.* at 234 (“The State is entitled to presume that defendants intend the ordinary consequences of their voluntary acts.”). The Court has likewise upheld state rules that restrict the use of psychiatric testimony to specific defenses. *See Shannon v. United States*, 512 U.S. 573, 583 (1994) (upholding federal statute that barred use of psychiatric evidence to negate mens rea in drug prosecutions).

Third, the Arizona Supreme Court’s decision does not contravene *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68 (1985). In *Ake*, this Court held that an indigent defendant has a due process right to psychiatric assistance when his sanity is “a significant factor at trial.” *Id.* at 83. The Court did not, however, recognize a broader right to introduce expert mental-state evidence to negate mens rea. *See id.* at 79 (“We do not decide the extent to which a defendant might need expert assistance . . . on issues other than sanity.”). Arizona provided Clark with expert assistance for his insanity defense; it did not deny him access to psychiatric testimony altogether.

Fourth, this Court has repeatedly deferred to state evidentiary rules that exclude relevant evidence. *See Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 42 (1996) (plurality opinion) (“States are entitled to have their own rules of evidence.”). Arizona’s rule is no different. The exclusion of expert mental-state evidence on mens rea does not violate due process because it is a reasonable regulation of expert testimony. *See Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44, 55 (1987) (“The States’ interest in regulating the admission of evidence is not displaced by the Due Process Clause.”).

Fifth, the Arizona Supreme Court’s decision is consistent with the holdings of other state courts of last resort. *See People v. Skinner*, 39 Cal.4th 749, 770 (2006) (“A defendant may not use psychiatric evidence to negate mens rea unless the Legislature has expressly authorized it.”); *State v. Johnson*, 292 N.W.2d 286, 290 (Iowa 1980) (“The insanity defense is the exclusive vehicle for introducing psychiatric evidence to negate criminal responsibility.”). These decisions reflect the well-established principle that States may confine psychiatric evidence to the insanity defense.

Sixth, the rule applied by the Arizona Supreme Court does not offend the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. *See Pulley v. Harris*, 465 U.S. 37, 41 (1984) (“The Eighth Amendment does not require individualized sentencing in non-capital cases.”). The exclusion of expert mental-state evidence on mens rea does not implicate the Eighth Amendment because it does not affect the defendant’s guilt or punishment; it merely regulates the admissibility of evidence. *See Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 44 (“The Eighth Amendment does not impose a particular mode of evidentiary hearing.”).

Seventh, the Arizona Supreme Court’s decision is consistent with the holdings of the lower federal courts. *See United States v. Pohlot*, 827 F.2d 889, 896 (3d Cir. 1987) (“A defendant may not use psychiatric evidence to negate mens rea unless the insanity defense is at issue.”); *United States v. Bennett*, 818 F.2d 1001, 1005 (9th Cir. 1987) (“The insanity defense is the exclusive vehicle for introducing psychiatric evidence to negate criminal responsibility.”). These decisions reflect the well-established principle that States may regulate the admissibility of expert mental-state evidence.

Eighth, the Arizona Supreme Court’s decision does not deny Clark a fair trial. *See Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554, 563 (1967) (“The Due Process Clause does not require that every relevant fact be proved in every case.”). The exclusion of expert mental-state evidence on mens rea does not render Clark’s trial fundamentally unfair because he was provided with expert assistance for his insanity defense and was not denied the opportunity to present a defense. *See Leland*, 343 U.S. at 802 (“The State is not required by the Federal Constitution to adopt any particular form of procedure in criminal cases so long as it does not appear that the accused has been denied fundamental rights.”).

In sum, the Arizona Supreme Court’s decision is consistent with this Court’s precedents and those of the state courts of last resort. The Due Process Clause does not confer upon a defendant a freestanding right to introduce expert mental-state evidence to negate mens rea, separate and apart from the insanity defense. The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
