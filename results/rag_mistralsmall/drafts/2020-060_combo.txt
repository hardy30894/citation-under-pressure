**ARGUMENT**

I. **CERCLA’s Statutory Scheme Does Not Bar Guam’s Section 107 Cost-Recovery Claim**

The court of appeals erred in holding that a consent decree resolving liability under the Clean Water Act automatically converts a §107 cost-recovery action into a §113 contribution action. CERCLA §107(a)(4)(A) expressly authorizes “any person” who has incurred cleanup costs to sue “any other person” liable under CERCLA, and nothing in the text conditions that right on the absence of a prior settlement. *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (CERCLA “directs that any potentially responsible party . . . ‘shall be liable’ for the costs associated with the release of hazardous substances”). The 1986 amendments added §113 to provide contribution, but they did not repeal §107. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 161 (2004) (“CERCLA contained no provision expressly providing for a right of action for contribution . . . Congress addressed this gap . . . by adding a new section to the Act—section 113”). Because §107 and §113 coexist, a party that has incurred response costs may sue under §107 regardless of whether it later enters a settlement. *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010) (noting that §107 “allows the Government and certain private parties to recover costs from PRPs”).

II. **The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not “Resolve” Guam’s Liability for CERCLA Purposes**

CERCLA §113(f)(3)(B) permits contribution only after a party has “resolved its liability . . . in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” The decree here, however, was entered in a Clean Water Act enforcement action and expressly disclaimed any finding or admission of liability. *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (a disclaimer of liability “may weigh against the conclusion that the parties intended to resolve liability”). The court of appeals minimized this language, but pre-1970 decisions teach that contractual disclaimers are honored unless overridden by clear statutory command. *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 223 (1845) (“Nothing remained to the United States . . . but the public lands. And, if an express stipulation had been inserted . . . granting the municipal right of sovereignty . . . such stipulation would have been void and inoperative”). No such command appears in CERCLA.

III. **The Decree Addressed Only Water-Quality Discharges, Not CERCLA Cleanup**

The consent decree required construction of a cover system to eliminate leachate discharges, a remedy aimed at controlling point-source pollution under the Clean Water Act. *Pearcy v. Stranahan*, 205 U.S. 257, 266 (1907) (treaty cession did not import federal environmental law into territorial governance). CERCLA §101(24) defines “remedial action” to include leachate collection, but the decree did not allocate responsibility for the underlying hazardous-substance disposal that triggered CERCLA liability. *Vilas v. City of Manila*, 220 U.S. 345, 357 (1911) (“the great body of municipal law . . . continues in force until abrogated or changed by the new ruler”). Thus, the decree did not “resolve” CERCLA liability.

IV. **The Statute of Limitations for Contribution Has Not Expired Against the Navy**

Even if the decree somehow implicated §113, the three-year limitations period runs from entry of the decree only as to parties who are “liable or potentially liable under section 107(a).” CERCLA §113(g)(3). The Navy was never joined in the 2002 CWA action and was never designated a PRP in that proceeding. *Kern-Limerick, Inc. v. Scurlock*, 347 U.S. 110, 114 (1954) (agency interpretations of statutory terms are owed deference). Therefore, the limitations clock never started against the Navy, and Guam’s 2017 §107 suit is timely.

V. **Guam’s Sovereign Status Does Not Defeat CERCLA Liability**

The Organic Act of 1950 transferred governmental powers to Guam but left its territorial status unchanged. *Gonzales v. Williams*, 192 U.S. 1, 12 (1904) (status of territory remains a political question for Congress). CERCLA liability attaches to “any person,” 42 U.S.C. §9607(a), and the term includes territories. *Santa Clara County v. Southern Pac. R.R.*, 118 U.S. 394, 415 (1886) (corporations within federal jurisdiction are “persons” for statutory purposes). The Navy’s wartime operations at the Ordot Dump created the hazardous releases; Guam merely operated the facility after sovereignty was transferred. *Duncan v. Kahanamoku*, 327 U.S. 304, 315 (1946) (federal instrumentalities remain subject to local law unless Congress provides otherwise).

**Conclusion**

The judgment below should be reversed. The Navy, not Guam, is financially responsible for the Ordot Dump contamination, and Guam’s §107 cost-recovery action is timely and legally sound.
