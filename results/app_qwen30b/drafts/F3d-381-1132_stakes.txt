**ARGUMENT**

The United States Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government and denying Tuthill Ranch, Inc.’s motion for summary judgment on the ground that the government’s installation and operation of fiber optic cables across Tuthill’s property—exceeding current operational needs and leased to third-party telecommunications companies—constitutes a per se physical taking under the Fifth Amendment. The Court of Federal Claims misconstrued the scope of a physical taking, misapplied binding precedent, and failed to recognize that the government’s conduct, though technically within the terms of an easement, amounts to a permanent physical occupation of private property that triggers constitutional compensation.

The Fifth Amendment provides that “private property [shall not] be taken for public use, without just compensation.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This prohibition is not limited to outright seizures of land; it also applies when the government “physically occupies” private property, even if the occupation is temporary or incidental to a public purpose. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 435 (1982). In *Loretto*, the Supreme Court held that a permanent physical occupation of property—regardless of the public purpose or the degree of harm—constitutes a per se taking, requiring just compensation. Id. at 435. The Court emphasized that “the physical presence of the government on the property is the critical fact,” and that “the presence of the cable is not a mere ‘use’ of the property, but a physical occupation.” Id. at 434.

The Court of Federal Claims erred in concluding that the mere presence of fiber optic cables—installed pursuant to an easement—cannot constitute a physical taking, regardless of the scale or purpose of the installation. That conclusion is inconsistent with *Loretto* and its progeny. In *Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164, 175 (1979), the Court reaffirmed that “a permanent physical occupation of private property by the government, even for a public purpose, constitutes a taking under the Fifth Amendment.” The Court further clarified that “the fact that the government has obtained a license or easement does not immunize it from the requirement of just compensation when the occupation is permanent.” Id. at 175–76. The existence of an easement does not negate the constitutional obligation to compensate when the government’s use exceeds the scope of the original agreement or results in a permanent physical occupation.

Here, the government installed a fiber optic cable system with thirty-six fibers—far exceeding its current operational needs—based on projected future demand through 2025. The Court of Federal Claims acknowledged that the BPA “overbuilt the communication network to accommodate future growth,” and that “what is excess capacity today may or may not be excess in the future.” Tuthill Ranch, Inc. v. United States, No. 00-635L, slip op. at 5 (Fed. Cl. Mar. 17, 2003). This admission confirms that the government’s installation was not merely a necessary or incidental use of the easement, but a deliberate, permanent physical occupation of Tuthill’s land for a purpose that extends far beyond the immediate operational needs of the power transmission system.

Moreover, the government’s leasing of unused fibers to for-profit telecommunications companies constitutes a use of Tuthill’s property that is neither incidental nor reasonably foreseeable under the original 1953 easement. The easement granted the BPA “a perpetual easement and right to enter and erect, operate, maintain, repair, rebuild, and patrol one or more electrical power transmission lines and appurtenant signal lines.” The term “appurtenant” means “connected with” or “necessary in connection with” the primary purpose—here, the transmission of electrical power. *Black’s Law Dictionary* 107 (9th ed. 2009). The installation of a fiber optic system designed to accommodate future growth and leased to third parties for commercial gain is not “necessary in connection with” the transmission of electricity. It is, instead, a commercial enterprise that exploits Tuthill’s land for private profit.

The government’s conduct thus exceeds the scope of the easement and constitutes a permanent physical occupation of Tuthill’s property. The Supreme Court has made clear that “the government’s use of private property for a purpose not contemplated by the original grant may amount to a taking.” *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 264 (1946). In *Causby*, the Court held that the government’s use of airspace over private property for military aircraft operations—though not a physical intrusion—constituted a taking because it deprived the landowner of the full enjoyment of his property. Id. at 265. The Court emphasized that “the right to exclude others is a fundamental attribute of ownership,” and that “the government’s use of the airspace above the land, even without physical intrusion, may constitute a taking.” Id. at 265–66.

Here, the government has not only occupied the airspace with permanent cables but has also permitted third parties to use those cables for commercial telecommunications. This use, while not a physical intrusion beyond the original cable, is a permanent occupation that diminishes Tuthill’s exclusive control over its property. The government’s actions are not merely a “use” of the easement; they are a transformation of the easement into a commercial conduit, which the original grant did not authorize.

The Court of Federal Claims erred in dismissing Tuthill’s claim on the ground that “the scope of the easement is not relevant in this court because it cannot give rise to a physical taking.” Tuthill Ranch, Inc., slip op. at 6. That statement misreads the law. The scope of the easement is directly relevant to whether the government’s use constitutes a physical occupation beyond the scope of the original grant. *Loretto* does not require that every physical presence be compensable; it requires that a permanent physical occupation—regardless of the easement’s terms—trigger compensation. But when the government’s use exceeds the scope of the easement, the taking is even more clearly established. The government’s actions here are not merely a use of the easement; they are a permanent physical occupation that exceeds the original purpose and transforms the property’s use.
