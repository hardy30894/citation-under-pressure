# ARGUMENT

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a reviewing court, in determining whether a *Rehaif* error affected a defendant's substantial rights or the fairness of judicial proceedings under plain-error review, may examine the entire record, including undisputed facts outside the four corners of the trial evidence. This approach follows directly from the text of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) and this Court's longstanding plain-error jurisprudence.

## I. Plain-Error Review Under Rule 52(b) Requires Examination of the Whole Record

Rule 52(b) permits appellate courts to correct "a plain error that affects substantial rights" even absent a timely objection below. Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b). This Court has consistently interpreted this rule as demanding that the defendant "bears the burden of persuasion" to show prejudice. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993). In *Olano*, this Court explained that the plain-error inquiry requires the defendant to demonstrate that the error "affected the outcome of the district court proceedings." *Id.* at 734.

Critically, in *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), this Court squarely rejected the notion that plain-error review is confined to the trial record alone. There, addressing a Rule 11 violation analogous to the Rehaif error here, this Court held that a reviewing court may consult "the whole record" when assessing whether an unpreserved error affected substantial rights. *Id.* at 59. The Court reasoned that "the purpose of Rule 52(b)... is to allow correction of only 'particularly egregious errors,'" and that purpose is best served by permitting courts to consider all record evidence bearing on prejudice, not merely evidence formally presented to the jury. *Id.* at 63 (quoting *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 163 (1982)).

This Court reaffirmed that approach in *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74 (2004), holding that a defendant seeking relief for an unpreserved Rule 11 error must show "a reasonable probability that, but for the error, he would not have entered the plea." *Id.* at 83. The Court's analysis in *Dominguez Benitez* looked beyond the plea colloquy itself to the broader circumstances of the case. *Id.* at 84-85.

## II. Petitioner's Proposed Rule Would Contradict *Rehaif*'s Narrow Holding and This Court's Precedent on Harmless and Plain-Error Review

Petitioner asks this Court to limit appellate review exclusively to what a properly instructed jury considered, ignoring undisputed facts of record—here, petitioner's prior felony convictions and years of incarceration. But this Court has never adopted such an artificial constraint. To the contrary, in *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1 (1999), addressing harmless-error review of an omitted jury instruction, this Court held that reviewing courts must ask "whether the record contains evidence that could rationally lead to a contrary finding," *id.* at 19, necessarily requiring examination of record evidence beyond what the jury was instructed to consider.

*Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), itself provides no basis for departing from this framework. *Rehaif* held only that the Government must prove a defendant knew of his prohibited status under § 922(g). *Id.* at 2200. Nothing in *Rehaif* addressed the separate question of remedy on plain-error review, nor did it purport to override *Olano*, *Vonn*, and *Dominguez Benitez*.

## III. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Applied This Framework to Petitioner's Case

Petitioner's prior felony convictions, resulting in sentences of decades' imprisonment, made it not merely likely but overwhelmingly certain that he knew of his felon status when he possessed the firearm. This Court has recognized that "if a person is a felon, he ordinarily knows he is a felon." *Rehaif*, 139 S. Ct. at 2197. Where, as here, the record conclusively establishes this knowledge, requiring reversal would convert plain-error review into an automatic-reversal rule—precisely what *Olano* forbids. 507 U.S. at 737 ("Rule 52(b) is permissive, not mandatory").

This Court's admonition in *Puckett v. United States*, 556 U.S. 129 (2009), controls: plain-error review exists to ensure "that the punishment of criminal defendants is scrupulously fair," but the doctrine simultaneously respects "the balance struck by the contemporaneous-objection rule." *Id.* at 134. Allowing defendants to obtain reversal despite overwhelming, undisputed evidence of guilt—evidence available in the record but outside the truncated trial presentation necessitated by the Rehaif error itself—would upend that balance and reward gamesmanship rather than correct genuine miscarriages of justice.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit.
