ARGUMENT

Congress may delegate authority to the Attorney General provided it lays down “by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Controlled Substances Act supplies that principle by directing the Attorney General to consider the history, current pattern, scope, duration, and significance of abuse, together with the risk to public health, before temporarily scheduling a substance on an emergency basis. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (1988).

This guidance accords with delegations sustained when Congress has identified the ends to be achieved and the factors to be weighed. See Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 420 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). The same principle appears in Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), where the Court upheld authority to suspend statutory provisions upon a factual determination.

The two instances in which delegations were invalidated are distinguishable. In Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), the statute “did not declare any policy” or establish any “criterion to govern” executive action. In A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 541-42 (1935), Congress supplied “no standards” whatever. Here the enumerated statutory factors and the imminent-hazard limitation provide the requisite criteria.

Congress may likewise authorize subdelegation. In United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc., 272 U.S. 1, 12 (1926), the Court approved the transfer of functions vested in the President to subordinate officers. Earlier decisions confirm that Congress may “commit the details” to those charged with execution. Wayman v. Southard, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 1, 43 (1825). Administrative officers may promulgate rules carrying criminal consequences when acting within statutory bounds. Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904); United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516 (1911).

The temporary scheduling provisions therefore rest on a valid delegation that the Attorney General lawfully transmitted to the Administrator of the Drug Enforcement Administration.
