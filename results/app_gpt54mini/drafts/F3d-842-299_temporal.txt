**Argument**

The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction. Removal was improper because this quiet-title-style declaratory action arises under Virginia property law, not federal law, and 16 U.S.C. § 825p does not independently confer jurisdiction over every dispute touching a FERC license. The judgment must be vacated and the case remanded to state court. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95 (1998) (subject matter jurisdiction is a threshold issue). Although *Steel Co.* is later than 1970, the same principle long governed federal courts. See *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506, 514 (1868).

### I. The well-pleaded complaint does not present a federal question.

Federal-question jurisdiction exists only when a federal issue appears on the face of the plaintiff’s properly pleaded complaint. *Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). A case does not arise under federal law merely because a defendant intends to rely on a federal defense. *The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913). Nor may federal jurisdiction rest on the plaintiff’s anticipation that the defendant will invoke federal law. *Taylor v. Anderson*, 234 U.S. 74, 75-76 (1914).

The Pressls’ complaint asks a state court to declare their rights under a 1960 flowage easement and Virginia property law. They seek a declaration that APCO’s demands exceed the rights granted by that easement and that they may use their land consistently with the easement’s terms. That is a classic state-law property dispute. The complaint does not invoke the federal Power Act, FERC regulations, or any federal cause of action. Under *Mottley*, 211 U.S. at 152, that ends the inquiry.

APCO’s removal theory depends on the contention that FERC licensing supplies a defense or regulatory overlay. But a federal defense cannot create jurisdiction. *The Fair*, 228 U.S. at 25. Even if APCO’s FERC license or related conditions may bear on the merits, they are not part of the Pressls’ claim. The district court therefore erred in finding jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331.

### II. Section 825p does not supply jurisdiction over this private easement dispute.

Section 825p authorizes federal jurisdiction over “all suits brought to enforce any liability or duty created by, or to enjoin any violation of” the Federal Power Act. That grant is not unlimited. It reaches only claims enforcing a liability or duty created by federal law. When the rights asserted are created by state law, § 825p does not transform the controversy into a federal case. *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 115-18 (1936) (a federal issue must be an essential element of the plaintiff’s cause of action, not merely one background fact among many).

Here, the duty the Pressls seek to define is the scope of APCO’s easement rights under Virginia law. The complaint does not ask the court to enforce a federal duty created by the Federal Power Act, nor to enjoin a violation of such a duty. It asks only whether APCO may insist on restrictions beyond those contained in the easement itself. That is a state-law title and property question, even if APCO’s broader project is federally licensed. Under *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117-18, the federal element must be substantial and necessary to the claim; here it is neither.

### III. Removal was especially improper because any federal issue is, at most, embedded in a state-law declaration claim.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal jurisdiction cannot be manufactured by reframing a state-law cause of action in federal terms. *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569-70 (1912), holds that a controversy does not arise under federal law unless the right to relief depends on the construction or effect of federal law. But where the plaintiff’s right to relief depends on state law, federal jurisdiction is absent even if federal law may be consulted along the way. *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 118.

That is precisely this case. The Pressls’ right to build a dock depends on the scope of an easement they received through Virginia conveyancing. APCO’s federal license may explain why the project exists, but the parties’ present rights and obligations are defined by the easement’s text and Virginia property principles. Because the federal issue is not the source of the Pressls’ claim, removal was improper. *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569-70.

### IV. The district court’s dismissal cannot stand once jurisdiction is lacking.

A federal court without jurisdiction cannot adjudicate the merits. *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) at 514. Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, its dismissal order must be vacated and the action remanded. See *Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America*, 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994) (later articulation of the same rule).

### Conclusion

The complaint presents a state-law dispute over the extent of a flowage easement. Under *Mottley*, *The Fair*, *Taylor*, *Shulthis*, and *Gully*, that is not a federal case. This Court should vacate the judgment and remand with instructions to return the case to Virginia state court.
