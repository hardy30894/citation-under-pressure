**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals erred by replacing OCSLA’s statutory situs requirement with a free-ranging “substantial nexus” test. Section 1333(b) extends LHWCA benefits only to disability or death “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain text requires that the injury occur on the shelf itself.

**I. The Statutory Text Anchors Coverage to Injuries on the Shelf**

Every construction begins with the statutory language. *Packard v. Banton*, 264 U.S. 140, 145 (1924). The participle “occurring” immediately follows “injury” and identifies where the injury must happen. If Congress had intended to cover all injuries caused by shelf operations, it would have written “resulting from operations conducted on the shelf” or “sustained in the course of shelf-related employment.” Instead, Congress linked the injury itself to the shelf. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917), instructs that unambiguous statutory language is controlling.

The contrast with the neighboring choice-of-law provision reinforces this reading. Section 1333(a)(2)(A) expressly covers the “subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” Section 1333(b) contains no similar extension to structures or locations beyond the shelf. As this Court recognized in *FTC v. Bunte Bros.*, 312 U.S. 349, 354 (1941), a deliberate difference in statutory phraseology must not be disregarded. Had Congress wished to cover injuries at onshore processing plants, it would have said so.

**II. OCSLA’s Geographic Limits Are Jurisdictional**

OCSLA defines the outer Continental Shelf as submerged lands outside state territorial waters. 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The federal interest protected by OCSLA is confined to that defined geographic area. This Court has consistently treated federal jurisdiction over submerged lands as a geographic question. *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 35-36 (1947); *United States v. Texas*, 339 U.S. 707, 715 (1950). A statute that extends federal law to a discrete offshore zone should not be read to expand that zone to onshore facilities through the loose phrase “substantial nexus.” *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820), warns against widening the reach of a federal enactment beyond the terms of its text.

**III. Remedial Purposes Cannot Override Clear Language**

The Ninth Circuit was influenced by the remedial purpose of workers’ compensation law. But *Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 7 (1946), recognized that the reach of a compensation statute is confined by its express language. The LHWCA itself contains explicit situs requirements, and OCSLA adds a further geographic limitation. Courts are not free to substitute an equitable standard for the enacted one. *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 82 (1932), explains that judicial interpretation must be faithful to the legislature’s chosen language.

**IV. Application to This Case**

Juan Valladolid was killed at an onshore facility near the California coast. That facility is not on the outer Continental Shelf. Because his injury did not occur on the shelf, § 1333(b) does not apply. The court of appeals’ remand for application of a “substantial nexus” test is therefore erroneous. The judgment must be reversed.
