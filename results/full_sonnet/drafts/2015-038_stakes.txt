**ARGUMENT**

**I. ANILCA § 103(c) Defines Which Lands Are "Public Lands" Within Conservation System Units; It Does Not Strip the National Park Service of Authority to Regulate Activity Occurring Within Unit Boundaries.**

Section 103(c) provides that only "lands, waters, and interests therein" title to which is held by the United States shall be deemed "public lands" for purposes of the conservation system unit (CSU) provisions of ANILCA, and that the inclusion of non-federal land within a unit's boundaries "shall not be considered a part of the System unit" until title vests in the United States or the Secretary acquires an interest. But nothing in that text purports to divest the Park Service of *regulatory* authority over conduct occurring on the surface of navigable waters located within the exterior boundaries of a national preserve. As the Ninth Circuit correctly recognized, Sturgeon's hovercraft ban does not apply "solely to public lands within CSUs in Alaska"—it is a regulation of general applicability governing conduct throughout NPS-administered units, wherever title to the underlying bed happens to lie.

Congress said as much elsewhere in the same statute. ANILCA § 1319 provides that "[n]othing in [the statute] shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto." 16 U.S.C. § 3207. That savings clause makes clear that Congress did not intend § 103(c) to work a silent repeal of the Park Service's pre-existing regulatory authority over activity within park boundaries. Had Congress meant to carve non-federal inholdings out from under otherwise-applicable park regulations of general applicability, it knew how to say so "specifically." Id. It did not.

**II. The Park Service's Authority to Regulate Activity Within Unit Boundaries, Including on Waters as to Which the State Holds Title, Is Independently Grounded in the Government's Navigational Servitude and Its Organic Authority to Manage the National Park System.**

Even where a State holds title to the submerged lands beneath a navigable river, the United States retains substantial regulatory authority over activity on the water above it. As Justice Scalia observed in *Alaska v. United States*, 545 U.S. 75 (2005), "[i]f title to submerged lands passed to Alaska, the Federal Government would still retain significant authority to regulate activities in the waters of Glacier Bay by virtue of its dominant navigational servitude, other aspects of the Commerce Clause, and even the treaty power." Id. at 111 (Scalia, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). The same is true here. The Nation River's course through the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve does not immunize hovercraft operation on its surface from otherwise valid federal regulation simply because Alaska may own the riverbed. Ownership of submerged land is not the measure of the Park Service's authority to regulate navigation and recreational conduct within a unit it administers.

That authority is reinforced by Congress's broader grant of power to the Secretary to promulgate rules and regulations necessary to protect Park System resources and to govern the use and enjoyment of the parks. ANILCA left that pre-existing regulatory framework intact except where it "specifically" provided otherwise. 16 U.S.C. § 3207. Petitioner identifies no such specific exception for hovercraft use on state-owned waters.

**III. The Park Service's Interpretation of § 103(c) Is Entitled to Deference and Is Consistent With Congress's Purpose of Ensuring Uniform Administration of the Park System in Alaska.**

Because § 103(c) is, at minimum, ambiguous as to whether it strips the Park Service of regulatory jurisdiction over non-federal inholdings, the agency's longstanding construction—that its general regulations of park-wide applicability, such as the hovercraft ban, apply throughout unit boundaries regardless of subsurface title—warrants deference. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984). Any suggestion that the agency's position shifted over time does not defeat that deference, because "[a]gency inconsistency is not a basis for declining to analyze the agency's interpretation under the Chevron framework." *Nat'l Cable & Telecomms. Ass'n v. Brand X Internet Servs.*, 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005).

Reading § 103(c) as petitioner urges would produce a patchwork of enforcement within a single preserve—full regulatory authority on some stretches of the Nation River and none on others, depending on the vagaries of riverbed title, much of which remains contested or unadjudicated. Congress did not design ANILCA's conservation system units to operate that way. It designated the Yukon-Charley as a unit of the National Park System precisely to ensure integrated management of its resources, wildlife, and visitor use. Petitioner's construction would undermine that integrated scheme every time a navigable waterway happened to run through Alaska public land, notwithstanding the absence of any "specific[]" congressional command to that effect. 16 U.S.C. § 3207.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals as to Sturgeon should be affirmed.
