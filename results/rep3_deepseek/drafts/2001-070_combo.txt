**ARGUMENT**

**I.** **The Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment’s Prohibition Against Cruel and Unusual Punishment.**

The Eighth Amendment must be interpreted in light of evolving standards of decency, *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion), but those standards are measured by “objective indicia” such as state legislation and jury determinations, not by judicial subjectivity. *Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 471 (1947) (Frankfurter, J., concurring); *see also Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 470 (1947) (Frankfurter, J., concurring). At the time of the lower court’s decision, only two states—Georgia and Maryland—had enacted statutes barring execution of mentally retarded persons. Those isolated enactments cannot demonstrate a national consensus. *See Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 378-79 (1910) (holding that the “cruel and unusual” prohibition is informed by “the progress of civilization,” but requiring widespread legislative action to establish an evolving standard). The limited state action here falls far short of the broad consensus required to invalidate a punishment long accepted under the Eighth Amendment. *See id.*

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not categorically forbid execution of those with diminished mental capacity. *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 334 (1989), recognized that mental retardation is a mitigating factor, but it did not hold that the Constitution bars capital punishment for all mentally retarded offenders. The Commonwealth of Virginia’s sentencing scheme, which allows the jury to give full consideration to Atkins’ mental limitations as mitigating evidence, satisfies the requirement that the sentencer not be precluded from considering relevant mitigating circumstances. *See Eddings v. Oklahoma*, 455 U.S. 104, 113-14 (1982). Virginia’s procedure thus comports with the individualized sentencing mandated by *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978) (plurality opinion).

**II.** **The Jury’s Verdict Meets the Standards of Reliability Required by the Eighth Amendment.**

The Eighth Amendment requires heightened reliability in capital sentencing, but that requirement is satisfied where, as here, the jury considered and rejected the defendant’s claim of mental retardation. *See Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 197-98 (1976) (plurality opinion). The jury heard conflicting expert testimony and made a credibility determination that Atkins’ mental deficits did not preclude the death penalty. This Court has long recognized that the “punishment of death is not cruel and unusual” if imposed after a fair trial. *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890). Atkins’ trial was fair; he was convicted and sentenced under procedures that provided “that degree of certainty which is the equivalent of proof beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 702 (1975) (quotation omitted).

The Court has consistently upheld capital punishment for defendants with diminished capacity where the jury could rationally conclude that the defendant deserved execution. *See, e.g., Roberts v. Louisiana*, 428 U.S. 325, 334 n.8 (1976) (plurality opinion) (upholding mandatory death penalty for certain crimes, but noting individualized consideration). Here, Atkins’ own forensic psychologist conceded that Atkins understood the wrongfulness of his actions, distinguishing him from those who are wholly incompetent. *See Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam) (test for competency to stand trial). The jury’s verdict thus reflects a rational judgment that Atkins’ culpability warranted the ultimate sanction.

**III.** **Historical Practice Confirms That Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders Is Not Cruel and Unusual.**

The common law long permitted execution of mentally retarded offenders. *See, e.g., 4 William Blackstone, Commentaries *24* (stating that “a gross and notorious defect of understanding” could exempt a person from punishment, but requiring that the defect be “total” or “absolute”); *Freeman v. People*, 4 Denio 9, 28 (N.Y. Sup. Ct. 1847) (holding that idiocy must be “complete” to bar execution). This historical understanding supports the conclusion that only those wholly lacking the capacity to form criminal intent are exempt from capital punishment. *See 1 Matthew Hale, Pleas of the Crown *31 (1736) (distinguishing between “total” and “partial” idiocy). Atkins’ mild mental disability, as found by the jury, does not meet that historical standard. *See Jordan v. State*, 124 Ga. 417, 52 S.E. 776, 777 (1905) (holding that “feeble-mindedness” short of idiocy does not bar execution).

**IV.** **Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
