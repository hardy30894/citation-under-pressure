## Argument

The Ninth Circuit erred by expanding FOIA Exemption 2 beyond its statutory text and Supreme Court precedent. Exemption 2 protects only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The court below adopted a “predominantly internal” standard that strips the word “solely” of its limiting force and allows agencies to withhold any internal document whose disclosure might risk circumvention of regulation. This contravenes FOIA’s core purpose of “full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976).

### I. The “Predominantly Internal” Standard Violates the Plain Text of Exemption 2

Congress chose the word “solely” to cabin Exemption 2 to matters that are exclusively internal personnel rules and practices. The D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test, which the Ninth Circuit adopted, effectively reads “solely” out of the statute. *See Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051, 1056-57 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc). This Court has repeatedly instructed that FOIA exemptions “must be narrowly construed.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361; *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 152 (1989). The “predominantly internal” standard is not narrow; it is a license to withhold any document that an agency deems primarily for internal use, regardless of whether it qualifies as a “personnel rule or practice.”

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Rose* is misplaced. *Rose* recognized that Exemption 2 could cover materials whose disclosure “may risk circumvention of agency regulation,” but only if those materials are first “internal personnel rules and practices.” 425 U.S. at 369. The Court gave examples: rules governing parking, lunch hours, and sick leave—mundane employment matters. *Id.* at 363. It did not authorize withholding technical safety data about explosives storage. The ESQD arc maps are not rules governing how agency personnel behave; they are engineering calculations about blast effects. They bear no resemblance to the personnel matters Congress intended to exempt.

### II. The ESQD Arc Maps Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices”

The Navy’s own description confirms that the ESQD data is a “long term planning tool” used to design storage facilities and “organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation.” It is not a rule or practice governing personnel conduct. This Court has consistently limited Exemption 2 to materials that directly regulate employee behavior. In *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980), the court applied Exemption 2 to a raid manual that instructed agents on how to execute search warrants—a classic personnel practice. In *Maricopa Audubon Society v. U.S. Forest Service*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085 (9th Cir. 1997), this Court rejected Exemption 2 for goshawk nesting data because it did not “relate ‘solely,’ or even predominantly, ‘to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.’” The ESQD maps are even further removed: they are technical specifications, not instructions to employees.

The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to characterize the maps as “law enforcement materials” fails. The maps are not compiled for any adjudicative or enforcement purpose. *See Church of Scientology v. Dep’t of Defense*, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (9th Cir. 1979). They are safety planning documents. The Navy could have sought protection under Exemption 1 (classified information) or Exemption 7(F) (law enforcement records endangering safety), but it chose not to classify the maps and could not satisfy Exemption 7(F)’s requirements. FOIA’s exemptions are “explicitly exclusive.” *Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989). The Navy cannot use Exemption 2 as a catch-all for documents that do not fit other exemptions.

### III. Risk of Circumvention Does Not Transform Non-Personnel Documents into Exempt Material

The Ninth Circuit erred by focusing on the risk that disclosure would allow circumvention of safety regulations. Even if that risk exists, it is irrelevant unless the documents first qualify as “internal personnel rules and practices.” *Rose* made clear that the circumvention analysis applies only after the threshold classification is satisfied. 425 U.S. at 369. The court below inverted this inquiry, treating the risk of circumvention as a substitute for the statutory text. This approach would allow agencies to withhold any internal document that could be misused—a result Congress did not intend.

### IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Undermines FOIA’s Purpose

FOIA reflects “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 360. The public has a legitimate interest in knowing the blast zones near Puget Sound communities. The Navy’s own conduct—releasing identical ESQD maps for another base without incident—demonstrates that disclosure poses no genuine security risk. The dissent below correctly observed that the Navy’s reluctance stems from “political difficulties,” not national security. By expanding Exemption 2, the Ninth Circuit has created a loophole that allows agencies to shield embarrassing or inconvenient information from public scrutiny.

The judgment should be reversed.
