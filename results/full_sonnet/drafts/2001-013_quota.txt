**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 4(b)(1) Exempts a Workplace From OSHA Coverage Whenever Another Federal Agency Possesses Statutory Authority to Regulate the Working Conditions at Issue.**

Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA jurisdiction over "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This Court has long recognized that when Congress commits regulation of an industry to an expert agency possessing comprehensive statutory authority, courts should not construe a later, general statute to create overlapping jurisdiction absent a clear mandate. Cf. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984) (deferring to an agency's reasonable construction of a statute committing a subject to its expertise). The Fifth Circuit, applying this principle to the maritime context for two decades, has consistently held that the touchstone of § 4(b)(1) is the *existence* of another agency's statutory authority over the working conditions of seamen—not whether that agency has promulgated a regulation addressing the precise hazard in a citation. In Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), the court held that "OSHA regulations . . . do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation," id. at 1122, because the Coast Guard is the agency vested with authority over that subject matter. The court reaffirmed and elaborated that holding in Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), explaining that "[i]t is the law of this circuit that OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation," id. at 826, and that the Coast Guard's "comprehensive regulation and supervision of seamen's working conditions" creates "an industry-wide exemption [from OSHA] for seamen serving on vessels operating on navigable waters," id. at 826-27. That rule does not turn on whether the Coast Guard has issued a rule specifically addressing the hazard that injured the employee; it turns on the Coast Guard's status as the agency Congress has charged with the field.

**II. The Coast Guard Possesses—and Has Exercised—Comprehensive Statutory Authority Over the Working Conditions of Seamen Aboard Vessels Such as Rig 52, Regardless of Its Inspection Status.**

Petitioner errs in suggesting that Coast Guard authority, and hence the § 4(b)(1) exemption, exists only for "inspected" vessels subject to the most exacting regime of Coast Guard oversight. Congress vested the Coast Guard with authority to "administer laws and promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States," 14 U.S.C. § 2, and that grant of authority draws no distinction between inspected and uninspected vessels. Congress separately authorized the Coast Guard to regulate uninspected vessels' fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and emergency equipment, 46 U.S.C. § 4102, and the Coast Guard has done so extensively, see 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq.; 46 C.F.R. §§ 26.01 et seq. The employees aboard Rig 52—a drilling barge—are seamen within the settled meaning of that term. See Colomb v. Texaco, Inc., 736 F.2d 218 (5th Cir. 1984); Producers Drilling Co. v. Gray, 361 F.2d 432 (5th Cir. 1966). Clary itself involved a drilling barge with no indication that the vessel was an inspected one, yet the court announced no exception for uninspected craft; its holding rested on the "one significant decision" that OSHA does not apply to "the working conditions of seamen on vessels operating on the high seas." 609 F.2d at 1121-22. Because the type of vessel here is materially indistinguishable from the vessel in Clary, and because the Coast Guard's authority over uninspected vessels is real and actively exercised, petitioner's proposed inspected/uninspected line finds no footing in either the statute or the case law construing it.

**III. The Historic Federal Interest in Uniform Regulation of Maritime Working Conditions Confirms That Jurisdiction Over Seamen's Safety Must Rest Exclusively With the Coast Guard.**

This Court has long insisted that regulation of maritime activity be governed by uniform federal rules rather than a patchwork of overlapping or conflicting authorities. See Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205, 216 (1917) (recognizing the need for the "harmony and uniformity" of maritime law); American Dredging Co. v. Miller, 510 U.S. 443, 450-52 (1994) (reaffirming that admiralty's uniformity principle bars the displacement of established maritime rules by piecemeal local or agency regulation absent clear congressional intent). Permitting OSHA to regulate the same vessels the Coast Guard already oversees would produce precisely the "confusion, governmental proliferation, and stultification of enterprise" that Congress sought to avoid through § 4(b)(1). Donovan, 720 F.2d at 827. It would also create the "anomaly" the Fifth Circuit identified "of steaming in and out of OSHA coverage" as a vessel crosses jurisdictional lines or changes inspection status. Id. at 829. Because "a single, uniform set of rules should govern the maritime workplace," and because OSHA's "geographic limitations" make it impossible for OSHA's rules to serve that role, the governing rules "must be those of the Coast Guard." Id. Any residual doubt about the scope of the Coast Guard's authority should be resolved by deferring to the Coast Guard's own longstanding practice of regulating uninspected vessels, a practice entitled to respect under the standards of Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944).

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
