**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the lodestar, calculated as hours reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate, is presumed to reflect a “reasonable” fee under statutes like 42 U.S.C. § 1988. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897, 104 S.Ct. 1541, 1549 (1984). Any enhancement beyond that figure must be justified by more than mere commendation of counsel’s skill or the success of the litigation. The Court has made clear that such enhancements are permissible only in the “rare” and “exceptional” case, and even then, only when supported by “specific evidence” in the record demonstrating that the quality of representation was superior to what one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the results obtained were truly exceptional. *Id.* at 898–901, 104 S.Ct. at 1549–50; *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 565, 106 S.Ct. 3088, 3098 (1986).

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision to affirm a $4.5 million enhancement—nearly 43% of the total fee award—based solely on the district judge’s personal observations of counsel’s “stellar performance” and the “comprehensiveness” of the injunction, violates this settled principle. The district court did not rely on specific, record-based evidence of exceptional performance or results. Instead, it offered no comparative analysis, no citation to other cases with similar outcomes, and no quantification of the additional burden or risk borne by counsel. Rather, the court relied on its own “substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta,” and its “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge,” to conclude that the lodestar “did not fully reflect the quality of representation.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1290 (N.D.Ga. 2006). This is not a reviewable finding; it is judicial self-testimony, uncorroborated and unchallengeable.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected such subjective, unverifiable assessments. In *Blum*, the Court emphasized that “the quality of representation” and “the results obtained” cannot serve as independent bases for increasing the fee unless the applicant offers “specific evidence” to support an enhancement. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549. The Court did not permit the district court to “testify” on behalf of the plaintiff’s counsel. Nor did it allow the judge to substitute personal admiration for evidentiary support. The Court made clear that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 566, 106 S.Ct. at 3099 (emphasis added), and that enhancements are only appropriate when the record demonstrates “exceptional” success and “superior” performance that go beyond the norm for a case of this type.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *NAACP v. Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332 (11th Cir. 1987), and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292 (11th Cir. 1988), to uphold the enhancement is misplaced. Those cases did not hold that enhancements based on quality and results are automatically permissible. *NAACP* vacated and remanded because the district court failed to address the plaintiffs’ argument for an enhancement based on “exceptional success,” not because it approved such enhancements. 812 F.2d at 1336–37. *Norman* reversed because the district court applied the wrong standard in evaluating results, not because it endorsed enhancements on quality grounds. 836 F.2d at 1306. Neither case held that a district court may enhance a fee based on the judge’s personal evaluation of counsel’s performance. To the contrary, *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* make clear that such enhancements are not a matter of judicial discretion but of evidentiary necessity.

The dissent’s assertion that the district court’s findings were “explanations” rather than “testimony” is legally irrelevant. The distinction between explanation and testimony collapses when the judge, in the same order, uses his own subjective impressions—unreviewable, unchallenged, and unrecorded—to justify a $4.5 million increase in a fee award. This is not deference to the district court’s expertise; it is abdication of appellate review. The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned against allowing judges to “testify” in fee determinations. *See, e.g., Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549 (“The district court must make findings of fact to support any enhancement.”).

Moreover, the enhancement here was not justified by any exceptional circumstance. The case involved a class of foster children, a matter of significant public importance, but not one involving unpopular clients, extreme risk, or professional sacrifice. *Carnes, J., concurring in *Kenny A.*, 532 F.3d at 1234–35. The plaintiffs obtained a comprehensive injunction, but the district court offered no evidence that the outcome was “unprecedented” or that the litigation required “extraordinary effort” beyond what is reasonably expected in a complex civil rights case. *See id.* at 1233–34 (citing *Guam Soc’y of Obstetricians & Gynecologists v. Ada*, 100 F.3d 691, 698–99 (9th Cir. 1996), where enhancement was justified due to death threats and public hostility).
