**Argument**

The court of appeals correctly framed the governing principle, but it erred in applying it. Rule 23(b)(3) demands more than a damages theory in the abstract; the district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” and find, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the Rule 23 requirements are actually satisfied. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350-51 (2011); *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 320 (3d Cir. 2008). That inquiry may require the court to look beyond the pleadings and resolve disputes that bear on certification. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 351; *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 316-17. But a court may not certify a class on the basis of an untested, inadmissible, or non-classwide damages model that does not measure damages attributable to the sole theory of liability authorized for the class. *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 35-36 (2013); *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 323-24.

That is what happened here. The district court recertified the class after narrowing liability to a single theory: that Comcast’s clustering conduct deterred overbuilder entry in the Philadelphia DMA. Yet the damages model accepted for certification did not isolate damages caused by that theory alone. Under *Comcast*, “[a]ny model supporting a class action must be consistent with its liability case.” 569 U.S. at 35. A model that “does not isolate damages resulting from any one theory of antitrust impact” cannot establish predominance. *Id.* at 36. The point is not that damages must be calculated classwide at the certification stage; it is that the plaintiff must show a classwide method capable of measuring damages caused by the common questions that will actually be tried. *Id.*; *Tyson Foods, Inc. v. Bouaphakeo*, 577 U.S. 442, 453-54 (2016).

The Third Circuit’s contrary approach conflicts with this Court’s precedents. In *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 623-24 (1997), the Court held that Rule 23(b)(3) predominance is not satisfied by general common issues alone; the court must examine whether the common questions are truly central to the claims and defenses. Likewise, *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974), forbids a merits trial in the guise of certification, while *Dukes* confirms that overlap between certification and merits issues does not eliminate the court’s duty to resolve disputed certification facts. 564 U.S. at 351. Those decisions together require a class-specific evidentiary showing—not speculation, and not a damages model that fits some theories but not the one actually certified. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35-36.

The same principle follows from the Court’s antitrust cases. In *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 294, 324-25 (1962), the Court explained that relevant market definition is tied to competitive realities, not abstractions. In *Continental Ore Co. v. Union Carbide & Carbon Corp.*, 370 U.S. 690, 699 (1962), the Court warned against “dismember[ing]” antitrust proof by isolating fragments while ignoring the total competitive context. But here the opposite error occurred: the lower courts accepted a damages model untethered from the particular anticompetitive conduct the class was permitted to prove. Rule 23 does not allow certification on the assumption that some damages could be shown from some wrong. The question is whether the proffered proof can measure damages from the actual theory of liability common to the class. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35-36.

Nor can the certification be defended on the ground that damages questions may be handled later. Predominance under Rule 23(b)(3) requires the district court to determine now whether common issues predominate over individual ones. *Amchem*, 521 U.S. at 623-24; *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 319-20. The court cannot postpone that showing and hope that damages will somehow become manageable at trial. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 34-36. And because class certification can exert substantial settlement pressure, this Court has insisted on exacting scrutiny before a class is certified. *Gen. Tel. Co. of Sw. v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982); *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350.

The better rule is straightforward: where a plaintiff offers no admissible evidence that damages can be measured on a classwide basis consistent with the sole liability theory, certification under Rule 23(b)(3) must be denied. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35-36; *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 323-24. Because the courts below certified despite a damages model that did not match the liability theory, they abused their discretion. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 312; *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 351. The judgment should be reversed.
