**ARGUMENT**

I. The Eleventh Circuit Correctly Applied the Plain-Error Standard and Did Not Exceed Its Jurisdiction by Considering the Entire Record

A. Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), plain error review is limited to errors that are (1) clear or obvious, (2) affect substantial rights, and (3) seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The Eleventh Circuit properly applied this framework by examining the entire trial record—not outside evidence—to determine whether the errors in this case met the third prong. *See id.* at 736 (“[T]he court of appeals’ inquiry into prejudice must focus on the error’s effect on the trial process.”).

B. The Eleventh Circuit did not err by considering the full record, including Greer’s prior convictions, to assess prejudice. *United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258, 265 (2010) (explaining that appellate courts may review the entire record to determine whether an error undermined the fairness of the trial). The court did not rely on extraneous facts but instead evaluated the record as a whole to determine whether the government’s failure to prove *Rehaif*’s knowledge requirement prejudiced Greer. *See id.* at 264–65.

C. The Eleventh Circuit’s approach aligns with Supreme Court precedent holding that appellate courts may consider the entire record to assess prejudice under plain-error review. *See Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 469–70 (1997) (permitting review of the full record to determine whether an error affected substantial rights). The court did not exceed its jurisdiction by examining the record holistically rather than in a piecemeal fashion.

II. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Concluding That Greer Failed to Demonstrate Prejudice

A. To establish prejudice under plain-error review, a defendant must show a reasonable probability that the error affected the outcome of the trial. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. The Eleventh Circuit correctly determined that Greer could not meet this burden because the record demonstrated that he was a felon in possession of a firearm, regardless of whether he knew his status. *See id.* at 738 (holding that prejudice requires a showing that the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings”).

B. The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion is supported by *United States v. Resendiz-Patino*, 425 F.3d 765, 770 (9th Cir. 2005), which held that a defendant’s knowledge of his felon status is irrelevant to a § 922(g) conviction. The court properly relied on this precedent to conclude that the government’s failure to prove knowledge did not prejudice Greer’s substantial rights. *See id.* at 769–70.

C. The Eleventh Circuit also correctly considered Greer’s prior convictions to assess prejudice. *See United States v. Vazquez*, 949 F.3d 126, 133 (2d Cir. 2020) (permitting review of a defendant’s criminal history to determine whether an error affected the fairness of the trial). The court did not err by examining the record as a whole to determine whether the error undermined the integrity of the proceedings. *See id.* at 132–33.

III. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Err in Declining to Vacate Greer’s Conviction or Order a New Trial

A. The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with Supreme Court precedent holding that plain-error review is discretionary and that courts are not required to vacate a conviction or order a new trial even if error is found. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 735–36 (“[T]he court of appeals should correct a plain forfeited error affecting substantial rights if the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.”) (emphasis added).

B. The Eleventh Circuit’s refusal to vacate Greer’s conviction is further supported by *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 632 (2002), which held that a constitutional error does not require reversal if it is harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The court properly applied this standard to conclude that the error did not seriously affect the fairness of the trial. *See id.* at 633.

C. Finally, the Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004), which held that appellate courts have broad discretion in determining whether to correct plain error. The court did not abuse its discretion by declining to vacate Greer’s conviction or order a new trial. *See id.* at 82–83.

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the plain-error standard and did not exceed its jurisdiction by considering the entire trial record. The court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice or that the error did not seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The judgment below should be affirmed.
