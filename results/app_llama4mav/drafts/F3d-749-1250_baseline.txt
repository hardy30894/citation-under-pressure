## Argument

The district court erred in admitting the expert testimony of FBI Special Agent Charles Jones regarding the veracity of Stanley Hill's statements during an interrogation. As Agent Jones's testimony was not objected to at trial, this Court reviews for plain error. United States v. Ruiz, 462 F.3d 1082, 1088 (9th Cir. 2006). However, the admission of this testimony constitutes plain error because it invades the province of the jury to determine the credibility of witnesses. United States v. Toledo, 985 F.2d 1462, 1470 (10th Cir. 1993).

It is well-established that expert testimony regarding the credibility of a witness is generally inadmissible. United States v. Charley, 189 F.3d 1251, 1265-66 (10th Cir. 1999) (citing United States v. Binder, 769 F.2d 595, 602 (9th Cir. 1985)). In Charley, this Court held that expert testimony regarding the truthfulness of a child victim of sexual abuse was improper because it "usurps the jury's role as the sole arbiter of witness credibility." Id. at 1266. Similarly, in United States v. Adams, 271 F.3d 1236, 1245-46 (10th Cir. 2001), this Court held that expert testimony regarding the credibility of a cooperating witness was inadmissible.

Agent Jones's testimony regarding Stanley's veracity during the interrogation directly invaded the province of the jury. Jones stated that Stanley's answers "did not make sense" and displayed "evasive behaviors common among the criminal element." He also testified that Stanley's statement that he would "rather die than face these charges" was unprecedented in his experience with innocent persons. Furthermore, Jones opined that Stanley's repeated invocations of God to validate his truthfulness were indicative of deception. This testimony was tantamount to an expert opinion on Stanley's guilt or innocence, which is impermissible under the Federal Rules of Evidence. See United States v. Hill, 749 F.3d 1250, 1258 (10th Cir. 2014) ("An expert witness may not opine on whether a defendant is guilty or innocent.").

The admission of Agent Jones's testimony was not harmless error. The government's case against Stanley relied heavily on circumstantial evidence, and Jones's testimony may have tipped the scales in the government's favor. The jury's deadlock in the first trial and the government's change in theory between the two trials underscore the closeness of the case. Therefore, the plain error in admitting Agent Jones's testimony warrants reversal of Stanley's conviction.

Reversal is also supported by the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1 (1985), which emphasized the importance of preserving the jury's role as the sole arbiter of witness credibility. The admission of Agent Jones's testimony undermined this fundamental principle, and this Court should reverse Stanley's conviction accordingly.
