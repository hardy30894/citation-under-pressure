**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Code exempts only funds that function as retirement savings for the debtor at the time the petition is filed.**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt "retirement funds" held in tax-favored accounts. Whether an asset qualifies for that exemption is measured by its character at the commencement of the case, not by what it once was in someone else's hands. This Court has long held that the status of exempt property is fixed as of the filing date. In *White v. Stump*, 266 U.S. 310 (1924), the Court explained that the bankrupt's exemptions "are to be determined as of the date of the filing of the petition." The account's character on that date controls, not its history in a decedent's hands.

*Lockwood v. Exchange Bank*, 190 U.S. 294 (1903), confirms that the scope and content of an exemption are fixed by the legal rules that create it, not by the debtor's own characterization of the asset. The Court there explained that "the exemption is created by the state," and that once the governing law has defined the exemption, "it is not for the bankrupt court to inquire" behind that definition. *Id.* at 299. Applying that principle here, the governing law—the Internal Revenue Code's own rules for inherited IRAs—defines this account as a mandatory, time-limited distribution vehicle, not a retirement fund. It is not, and cannot become, a retirement account "of" the debtor merely because it once belonged to her mother.

**II. The functional character of the asset, not its label, controls.**

This Court has repeatedly instructed that exemption and tax provisions turn on the substance and function of an asset, not its formal name. In *Gregory v. Helvering*, 293 U.S. 465 (1935), the Court refused to accept a transaction's self-description, asking instead "whether what was done, apart from the tax motive, was the thing which the statute intended." *Id.* at 469. The word "IRA" in "inherited IRA" is a similar formal label; it does not change the fact that, functionally, the account no longer serves a retirement purpose for its current holder. The same functional approach governs bankruptcy's own treatment of exempt property. In *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375 (1966), this Court examined the practical operation of the asset at issue—a tax loss-carryback refund—rather than resting on a mechanical label, in deciding whether it belonged to the estate. The inherited IRA should receive the same functional scrutiny: it is, in economic reality, a time-limited, freely spendable fund that must be distributed on an accelerated schedule wholly divorced from Heidi Heffron-Clark's own retirement date.

This Court's older insurance-exemption cases confirm that the "retirement" or "protective" character of an exempt fund depends on whether it continues to serve its protective purpose in the debtor's hands, not on its origin. In *Holden v. Stratton*, 198 U.S. 202 (1905), the Court tied the exemption of a life-insurance policy to its ongoing function of providing for dependents, and in *Burlingham v. Crouse*, 228 U.S. 459 (1913), the Court likewise reasoned from the continuing protective purpose the policy served for the insured and his family. Here, by contrast, the inherited IRA serves no analogous ongoing retirement-protective function for the Clarks; the very tax rules governing such accounts compel current, not future, consumption.

**III. Exempting inherited IRAs would contradict, not serve, the purpose of the bankruptcy exemption scheme.**

Exemptions exist to secure a fresh start for debtors, not to shelter assets that were never set aside by the debtor for retirement in the first place. In *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934), this Court explained that one of the central purposes of the Bankruptcy Act is to give the honest but unfortunate debtor a renewed opportunity to build a future unburdened by old debts. That purpose is advanced by protecting funds the debtor has personally saved for retirement. It is not advanced by shielding an inheritance that the debtor never contributed to, has no obligation to preserve, and must currently spend down by statute. Treating such funds as exempt "retirement funds" would extend the exemption beyond its purpose and impair the legitimate expectations of ordinary creditors, a result this Court has cautioned against when a literal or formalistic reading would produce consequences at odds with the statute's aim. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (courts must look to "the purpose of the act" when a literal reading would yield results the legislature could not have intended).

For these reasons, the inherited IRA held by the Clarks is not "retirement funds" within the meaning of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), and the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
