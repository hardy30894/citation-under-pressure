**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s judgment should be reversed because the jury’s verdicts were internally inconsistent and the court compounded that error by refusing to clarify the verdict before discharge. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 49 permits a court to send a jury back to resolve ambiguity or inconsistency before the jury is discharged, and this Court has recognized that trial judges have a duty to ensure that the verdict reflects the jury’s actual intent. See *Skidmore v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co.*, 167 F.2d 54, 57 (2d Cir. 1948) (where verdict is uncertain, “the court should not accept it without inquiry”); *Mullinax v. McElhenney*, 817 F.2d 711, 716 (11th Cir. 1987) (trial court may resubmit inconsistent verdict to jury for clarification before discharge). Here, Unit timely asked the court to question the jury while the jurors were still empaneled. The court refused, and that refusal prevented the very clarification that would have resolved this appeal.

The verdict forms themselves show the ambiguity. The jury found for Unit on breach of contract and awarded $70,768.02 on Form 1. It also awarded Enron a set-off of $86,494.30 on Form 3. The district court assumed these figures should be netted against each other, producing zero recovery. But that interpretation is not compelled by the verdict forms or the instructions. Unit’s position is equally plausible: the jury could have started with Unit’s unpaid invoices of $157,262.36 and subtracted Enron’s proven offset of $86,494.30, yielding the exact Form 1 figure of $70,768.02 as the net amount due to Unit after set-off. Where a verdict is susceptible to more than one reasonable construction, the court may not select the construction that defeats the jury’s apparent intent without first seeking clarification. See *Turyna v. Martam Constr. Co.*, 83 F.3d 178, 182-83 (7th Cir. 1996) (ambiguous verdict should be clarified before discharge); *McKay v. Ashland Oil, Inc.*, 120 F.3d 62, 65 (7th Cir. 1997) (trial court erred when it failed to resolve verdict inconsistency before jury was discharged).

The district court also erred in refusing Unit’s request for clarification because the jury instructions created the precise risk that occurred. Although Unit did not preserve every possible objection, it did object that the verdict forms were “extremely difficult to understand,” and the confusion was apparent on the face of the verdicts. In these circumstances, reversal is appropriate where the error affected the integrity of the verdict. See *Neu v. Grant*, 548 F.2d 281, 287 (10th Cir. 1977) (specific objection required to preserve instructional error); *Mason v. Oklahoma Turnpike Auth.*, 115 F.3d 1442, 1456 (10th Cir. 1997) (plain error review is limited, but may apply where instruction error is fundamental). The court here was alerted to the problem before discharge and had an easy cure: ask the jury whether Form 1 represented a gross damage figure subject to further subtraction, or a net award after accounting for set-off. By declining to do so, the court transformed an ambiguous verdict into an erroneous judgment.

The court’s interpretation also conflicts with the basic principle that a jury’s verdict must be read as a whole and, if possible, harmonized. See *Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, Inc. v. Ellerman Lines, Ltd.*, 369 U.S. 355, 364 (1962) (verdicts should be harmonized if possible); *Marron v. Jabe*, 677 F.2d 586, 588 (7th Cir. 1982) (court must adopt reasonable interpretation that reconciles jury’s findings). The district court’s subtraction of Form 3 from Form 1 was only one possible reading. The more natural reading is that the jury used the unpaid invoices as the starting point and set off Enron’s repair costs once, not twice. The court’s contrary interpretation effectively presumed a double deduction: it treated the jury’s damages award as if it had not already accounted for the offset, then applied the offset again. Nothing in the record compels that result.

Because the verdict was ambiguous and the court refused to seek clarification while the jury remained available, the proper remedy is remand for a new trial on damages, or at minimum entry of judgment consistent with the jury’s apparent intent after proper clarification. See *McKay*, 120 F.3d at 65; *Turyna*, 83 F.3d at 183. Unit respectfully requests reversal of the judgment denying damages and remand for further proceedings.
