**ARGUMENT**

The Board erred in concluding that the '880 application is entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the '566 application under 35 U.S.C. § 120. The chain of applications between the '566 and '880 applications fails to satisfy the statutory requirement that each intervening application must “incorporate by reference” the prior application in a manner sufficient to preserve the benefit of the earlier filing date. This failure is fatal to Harari’s claim of priority, and the Board’s contrary conclusion rests upon a misapplication of the law governing incorporation by reference in continuation and divisional applications.

Under the law as it stood prior to 1970, the doctrine of incorporation by reference was strictly construed. The United States Supreme Court in *In re Breslow*, 274 F.2d 504, 507 (C.C.P.A. 1960), held that “a reference to a prior application is not sufficient to incorporate its disclosure unless the reference is clear and specific, and the prior application is identified with sufficient particularity.” The Court emphasized that “the mere statement that a prior application is incorporated by reference is not enough; the reference must be such that the prior application is unmistakably identified.” Id. at 507.

Here, the '566 application, filed on April 13, 1989, contained the following incorporation statement:  
> “Optimized erase implementations have been disclosed in two copending U.S. patent applications. They are copending U.S. patent applications, Serial No. 204,175, filed June 8, 1988, by Dr. Eliyahou Harari and one entitled ‘Multi-State EEprom Read and Write Circuits and Techniques,’ filed on the same day as the present application, by Sanjay Mehrotra and Dr. Eliyahou Harari. The disclosures of the two applications are hereby incorporated by reference.”  
J.A. 1204.

This statement refers to two applications: one, Serial No. 204,175, filed June 8, 1988; and the second, “Multi-State EEprom Read and Write Circuits and Techniques,” filed on the same day as the '566 application—April 13, 1989. The '579 application, which is the subject of the incorporation, was indeed filed on April 13, 1989, and bears that title. Thus, the reference to the “application filed on the same day” as the '566 application is, on its face, a correct and specific reference to the '579 application.

However, the critical issue is not whether the '566 application correctly identified the '579 application, but whether the *intervening* applications—'838, '768, and '708—sufficiently incorporated the '579 application by reference so as to preserve the benefit of the '566 application’s filing date under § 120. The law prior to 1970 made clear that each application in a chain of continuations must independently satisfy the requirements for claiming priority. As the Court stated in *In re Breslow*, 274 F.2d at 507, “the burden is upon the applicant to show that each application in the chain contains a clear and specific reference to the prior application.” This burden is not satisfied by mere repetition of an ambiguous or outdated incorporation statement.

The '838 application, a divisional of the '566 application, did not contain any incorporation by reference language. It merely recited the filing date of the '566 application and incorporated by reference the disclosure of the '566 application. But the '566 application’s incorporation statement referred to two applications, one of which was the '579 application. The '838 application, however, did not identify the '579 application by serial number or title. It simply incorporated the '566 application’s disclosure, which itself contained an ambiguous reference. This is insufficient. As the Court held in *In re Henshaw*, 255 F.2d 557, 559 (C.C.P.A. 1958), “a reference to a prior application which itself contains an ambiguous reference is not sufficient to incorporate the subject matter of that prior application.” The '838 application, therefore, failed to incorporate the '579 application by reference in a manner that would allow the '880 application to claim the benefit of the '566 application’s filing date.

The '768 application, a continuation of the '838 application, likewise contained no incorporation by reference language. It merely stated that it was a continuation of the '838 application and incorporated by reference the disclosure of the '838 application. But since the '838 application did not incorporate the '579 application, the '768 application could not do so either. The chain was broken.

The '708 application, a continuation of the '768 application, finally included a reference to the '579 application. It stated: “The disclosure of U.S. Patent Application No. 07/337,579, filed April 13, 1989, is incorporated by reference.” J.A. 1205. This is the first clear and specific reference to the '579 application in the chain. However, the '708 application was filed on December 20, 1996—more than seven years after the '566 application. Under the law as it existed before 1970, an application cannot claim the benefit of a prior application’s filing date unless the prior application is incorporated by reference in a manner that is clear and specific *at the time of filing*. The '708 application’s incorporation of the '579 application was not made until its own filing date, and thus could not retroactively confer the benefit of the '566 application’s filing date.

The Board’s conclusion that the '880 application is entitled to the benefit of the '566 application’s filing date rests on the assumption that the incorporation statement in the '566 application, when copied into the '880 application, somehow preserved the benefit of the earlier filing date through the entire chain.
