## ARGUMENT

Milford Central School’s Community Use Policy creates a limited public forum open only for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community.” The policy expressly excludes “religious purposes.” The Good News Club’s proposed activities—prayer, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as a personal savior—constitute religious instruction and worship, not a viewpoint on a secular subject. Milford’s exclusion of the Club is therefore a permissible subject-matter restriction on its limited forum, and any incidental burden on speech is justified by the school’s compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation.

**I. The School’s Policy Is a Reasonable Regulation of Its Property**

The government has broad authority to control the use of its property. *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (streets and parks are public forums but subject to regulation); *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 575–76 (1941) (upholding permit requirement for parades as reasonable time, place, and manner restriction). A state may reserve its property for specified purposes and exclude uses inconsistent with those purposes. *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47–48 (1966) (no constitutional right to demonstrate on jail grounds). Milford has not opened its facilities for religious worship or instruction. The Club’s meetings—featuring prayer, Bible stories, and invitations to “believe on the Lord Jesus”—fall squarely within the excluded category. The school’s decision to deny access is a reasonable exercise of its proprietary authority.

**II. The Exclusion Is Viewpoint-Neutral Subject-Matter Discrimination**

The Club argues that its activities teach morals from a religious viewpoint, and that Milford permits other groups to teach morals from secular viewpoints. This argument conflates subject matter with viewpoint. The school’s policy excludes “religious purposes” as a subject, not religious viewpoints on secular subjects. The Club’s meetings are not merely discussions of morality from a Christian perspective; they are religious instruction that includes prayer, proselytizing, and worship. As this Court recognized in *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–72 (1942), certain categories of speech are not protected because their “utterance” is “no essential part of any exposition of ideas.” Religious worship, like obscenity or fighting words, may be excluded from a limited forum without offending the First Amendment. The school’s distinction between secular moral development and religious instruction is a permissible subject-matter line, not viewpoint discrimination.

**III. The Establishment Clause Provides an Independent Justification**

Even if the exclusion were deemed to burden speech, Milford’s compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation justifies the restriction. The First Amendment prohibits public schools from endorsing or promoting religion. *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 15–16 (1947) (the state must be neutral in matters of religion). Allowing a religious club to conduct worship and instruction on school premises—even after hours—would create a substantial risk that the school appears to endorse religion. *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 209–12 (1948) (striking down religious instruction in public schools during the school day); *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 430–31 (1962) (school prayer violates Establishment Clause); *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 222–23 (1963) (Bible reading in public schools unconstitutional). The school’s policy of excluding religious worship is a reasonable measure to fulfill its constitutional duty to remain neutral. Permitting the Club’s meetings would place the school in the position of sponsoring religious activity, a result the Establishment Clause forbids.

**Conclusion**

Milford’s Community Use Policy is a lawful, viewpoint-neutral restriction on the use of its limited public forum. The Good News Club’s activities are religious worship and instruction, not a permissible viewpoint on a secular subject. The school’s exclusion is justified both by its proprietary authority and by its compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
