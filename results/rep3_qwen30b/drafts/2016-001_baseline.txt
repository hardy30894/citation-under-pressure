**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—lies at the heart of patent law’s balance between incentivizing innovation and ensuring fair compensation. The Federal Circuit’s decision to affirm the award of Samsung’s entire profits, without regard to the componential nature of the infringed design, contravenes the statutory framework of 35 U.S.C. § 289 and the Supreme Court’s precedent in *Commil USA, LLC v. Cisco Sys., Inc.*, 568 U.S. 566, 575 (2013), which holds that damages must be “just and equitable” and grounded in the actual harm caused by infringement. The Court should reverse.

### I. The Statutory Text of § 289 Requires Component-Based Damages When the Infringed Design Applies Only to a Component

Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that “whoever, without license of the owner… applies the patented design to any article of manufacture… shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. The key phrase—“to the extent of his total profit”—has long been interpreted to mean the infringer’s entire profit from the article of manufacture *in which the design is applied*. But this interpretation is not limitless. The statute does not authorize the award of total profits from an entire product when the design patent covers only a *component* of that product.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that patent damages must be tied to the actual scope of the patented invention. In *Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 828, 838 (1989), the Court held that “the scope of the patentee’s right to recover damages is limited by the scope of the patentee’s monopoly.” The monopoly conferred by a design patent is not the entire product, but only the ornamental design itself. When that design is applied to a component—such as the front face of a smartphone—the patentee’s right extends only to the value attributable to that component.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Halo Electronics, Inc. v. Pulse Electronics, Inc.*, 579 U.S. ___ (2016), where the Court emphasized that damages must be “just and equitable,” and that “the patentee is entitled to recover only the damages that are fairly attributable to the infringement.” 579 U.S. at ___ (slip op., at 12). The Court rejected the notion that a patentee could recover for the entire product when the infringement was limited to a part. The same logic applies here: Apple’s design patent covered only the *ornamental appearance* of the iPhone’s front face—its rounded corners, bezel, and layout of icons—not the entire smartphone. To award Apple Samsung’s entire profits from the sale of the phone, which includes the value of the processor, battery, camera, and other non-infringing components, is to award damages far exceeding the scope of the patentee’s monopoly.

### II. The Federal Circuit’s Decision Contradicts the Supreme Court’s Precedent on Component-Based Infringement

The Federal Circuit’s refusal to limit damages to the component’s value is inconsistent with the Court’s decision in *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Servs., Inc.*, 504 U.S. 451, 467 (1992), where the Court held that “a patentee may not recover damages for the entire product when the patent covers only a component.” The Court explained that “the patentee’s right to damages is limited to the value of the patented feature.” Id. at 468. This principle is not merely equitable—it is statutory. The patentee’s right to damages is not a windfall, but a remedy for the *actual* harm caused by the unauthorized use of the patented design.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary holding—affirming the award of Samsung’s entire profits—creates a perverse incentive: a patentee may obtain a windfall by asserting a design patent on a minor component, such as a button or screen layout, and then recover the entire profit from the infringer’s multi-billion-dollar product. This outcome is not only unjust but also undermines the purpose of the patent system, which is to promote innovation, not to reward speculative litigation.

### III. The Federal Circuit’s Reliance on “No Objection” to the Jury Instruction Is Irrelevant

The Federal Circuit held that Samsung forfeited its right to challenge the damages instruction because it did not object at trial. But this reasoning is flawed. The failure to object does not waive a claim that the instruction misstates the law. *See, e.g., United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 733 (1993) (holding that a party may raise a plain error claim even without objection if the error is “plain” and affects substantial rights). Here, the error is plain: the jury was instructed that Samsung’s entire profits were recoverable, despite the fact that the patented design applied only to a component. This misstatement of the law is not a mere procedural oversight—it is a fundamental error in the application of § 289.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized that “a party may not be required to make a timely objection to a jury instruction that is itself erroneous.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. The instruction here was not merely a misstatement—it was a legal error that directly led to an unjust award. The Federal Circuit’s refusal to correct it, on the ground of forfeiture, is inconsistent with the Court’s jurisprudence on plain error.

### IV. The Court Should Remand for a Component-Based Damages Calculation

The Court should not affirm the damages award in its entirety. Instead, it should remand for a proper calculation of damages based on the value attributable to the infringing component. The district court may use any reasonable method—such as a cost-plus approach, a royalty-based analysis, or a market-based valuation—to determine the component’s contribution to the overall product. *See, e.g., *Twin Disc, Inc. v. United States*, 473 F.2d 1370, 1375 (Ct. Cl. 1973) (upholding a damages calculation based on the value of the patented component).
