## Argument

The judgment should be reversed because the record does not support a finding that Sieben discriminated against Feltmann because of sex or retaliated against her for protected activity. Title VII and the MHRA require proof that the challenged action was taken “because of” sex or retaliation, not merely that the employee belonged to a protected class and later received criticism or discipline. See *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424 (1971). Even before *Griggs*, the governing discrimination cases recognized that the law reaches purposeful exclusion, not ordinary managerial judgment. See *Sacher v. United States*, 343 U.S. 1 (1952); *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954).

Here, the undisputed facts show legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for Rich’s actions. Feltmann’s sales manager observed declining productivity after the incentive program ended, low gross profit on each car, and concerns about her selling methods, including excessive “guest drives” and poor teamwork. Employers remain free to set performance standards and enforce them so long as they do not discriminate. *NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co.*, 304 U.S. 333 (1938); *Virginian Ry. Co. v. System Federation No. 40*, 300 U.S. 515 (1937). A court may not convert a disagreement over sales technique or output into sex discrimination absent proof that comparable male employees were treated differently for like conduct. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886); *McCabe v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co.*, 235 U.S. 151 (1914).

The proof here instead shows evenhanded response to business problems. Rich reprimanded and placed Feltmann on probation only after her January and February sales fell. He also placed a male consultant, Rick Beutel, on probation at the same time for the same reason. Such parity defeats an inference of sex bias. See *Gibson v. Mississippi*, 162 U.S. 565 (1896); *Fay v. New York*, 332 U.S. 261 (1947). Likewise, the Chicago sales competition was offered to the top consultants; when the top two declined, Rich did not offer it to multiple male consultants either. That is not discrimination; it is a neutral management decision. See *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944); *Hirabayashi v. United States*, 320 U.S. 81 (1943).

Feltmann’s reliance on the single vulgar remark by a different employee also fails. Sieben promptly investigated, reported the matter to ownership, met with Feltmann, reprimanded the offender, and warned him of termination for any repeat. Title VII does not make an employer strictly liable for a coworker’s isolated insult where the employer acts to correct it. *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945); *United States v. Wood*, 299 U.S. 123 (1936). Nothing in the record shows further vulgar comments, retaliation by the offender, or a hostile pattern attributable to Sieben.

The demo-car restriction likewise reflects a business rule applied after dealership costs became a concern. Rich restricted all consultants’ use of demo cars to the St. Louis area. Feltmann alone continued weekly 300-mile trips to Marion after receiving the memo. The fact that no other employee received a second memo does not show sex discrimination; it shows only that the manager addressed the known violation by the employee who kept violating the rule. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950); *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283 (1944). Courts do not sit as super-personnel departments second-guessing ordinary workplace discipline. *Sacher*, 343 U.S. 1.

The retaliation claim also fails for lack of causation. The protected conduct identified by Feltmann was her complaint about the vulgar remark in November 1992. But the record shows that Sieben responded immediately and favorably to that complaint. A later criticism memo in January, and probation in March, were tied to sales performance and selling practices, not to the complaint. A retaliation claim cannot rest on temporal sequence alone where legitimate reasons are documented. *Snowden*, 321 U.S. 1; *Fay*, 332 U.S. 261. Moreover, the fact that Feltmann continued to work, received a personal memo, and was then managed more closely after declining sales is consistent with ordinary supervision, not retaliatory discharge. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1 (1937).

Finally, any Missouri common-law theory rises and falls with the same proof. Because the evidence shows legitimate business reasons and prompt remedial action, the verdict cannot stand. *Brown*, 347 U.S. 483; *McCabe*, 235 U.S. 151.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
