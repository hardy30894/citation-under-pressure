**Argument**

This case belongs in district court. The Civil Service Reform Act channels review of most MSPB decisions to the Federal Circuit, but expressly carves out “cases of discrimination,” which are reviewed in district court under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). In *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596 (2012), the Supreme Court held that when an employee brings a mixed case and the MSPB dismisses it before reaching the merits, “judicial review lies in federal district court, not in the Federal Circuit.” *Id.* at 56. That rule controls here.

Perry’s case was a mixed case from the outset. He alleged that the agency’s suspension, forced retirement, and alleged misconduct were motivated by disability, age, and race discrimination, and he challenged the agency action as involuntary. A mixed case is one in which an employee “has been affected by an action which is appealable to the MSPB” and also alleges prohibited discrimination. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1); *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 56-57. The MSPB’s dismissal on jurisdictional grounds did not change the nature of Perry’s claim. As the Supreme Court explained, Congress keyed review to the kind of case the employee brought, not to the label later assigned by the Board. *Id.* at 55-56.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule cannot survive *Kloeckner*. Before *Kloeckner*, some courts, including the D.C. Circuit, drew a distinction between jurisdictional dismissals and procedural dismissals. See *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597, 599-600 (D.C. Cir. 1998). But *Kloeckner* rejected forum rules that turn on how the MSPB disposes of the case. The Court held that “mixed cases” go to district court whether the MSPB decides them on the merits or dismisses them before reaching the merits. 568 U.S. at 50-51, 56. That reasoning forecloses a special Federal Circuit rule for jurisdictional dismissals. See *Mele v. Department of Justice*, 359 F.3d 525, 528-29 (D.C. Cir. 2004) (recognizing the statutory structure), abrogated in part by *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. 41.

The Federal Circuit’s leading cases likewise rest on a pre-*Kloeckner* understanding that this Court has already displaced. In *Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board*, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013), the Federal Circuit held that jurisdictional dismissals still belong in that court. But *Conforto* predates the Court’s later clarification in *Boechler, P.C. v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue*, 596 U.S. 199 (2022), that Congress’s jurisdictional instructions must be read according to their text, not perceived administrative convenience. More importantly, *Kloeckner* itself rejected the idea that a case falls out of district court merely because the MSPB never reached the merits. 568 U.S. at 54-56.

The statutory text confirms this result. Section 7703(b)(2) directs that cases of discrimination shall be filed “under any such law, rule, regulation, or policy” and reviewed “in accordance with the provisions of section 7702.” 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). Section 7702, in turn, addresses the employee’s mixed case allegation and the agency’s adverse action, not the MSPB’s eventual jurisdictional ruling. See *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 49-50. That is why the Court in *Kloeckner* held that even an MSPB dismissal “on procedural grounds” does not send the case to the Federal Circuit. *Id.* at 54. A jurisdictional dismissal is no different for purposes of the review statute. See *Harms v. Internal Revenue Service*, 321 F.3d 1001, 1007-08 (10th Cir. 2003); *Downey v. Runyon*, 160 F.3d 139, 145-46 (2d Cir. 1998).

Review in district court also accords with the broader discrimination-review scheme. In *Perry v. Merit Systems Protection Board*, 137 S. Ct. 1975 (2017), this Court granted certiorari to resolve the precise issue presented here and vacated the jurisdictional transfer rule that would have confined Perry to the Federal Circuit. The Court later confirmed in *Perry v. Merit Systems Protection Board*, 582 U.S. 420 (2017), that a mixed case dismissed by the MSPB for lack of jurisdiction belongs in district court. That disposition leaves no room for the Federal Circuit’s contrary approach. See also *Elgin v. Department of Treasury*, 567 U.S. 1, 10-13 (2012) (explaining the CSRA’s integrated review scheme), and *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 47-48.

Finally, district court review is the only reading that avoids arbitrary forum shopping and preserves the employee’s discrimination claims. If a mixed case is sent to different courts depending on whether the MSPB labels its dismissal “procedural” or “jurisdictional,” identical discrimination claims will be split based on a threshold characterization by the Board. That is precisely the sort of “jurisdictional” trap this Court rejected in *Kloeckner*. 568 U.S. at 55-56. Because Perry’s case is a mixed case, and because the MSPB dismissed it without resolving the merits, judicial review lies in district court.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that Perry’s petition was properly reviewable in district court, not the Federal Circuit.
