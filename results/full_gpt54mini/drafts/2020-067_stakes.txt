**Argument**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly rejected Greer’s invitation to confine plain-error review to the trial record alone. This Court’s Rule 52(b) cases require a reviewing court to consider the *entire* appellate record when deciding whether an error affected substantial rights and whether relief is necessary to protect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Nothing in the plain-error doctrine limits that inquiry to the evidence presented to the jury.

Under *United States v. Olano*, a defendant seeking relief for forfeited error must show that the error “affect[s] substantial rights,” and even then the court of appeals has discretion to correct it only if the error “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” 507 U.S. 725, 732, 736 (1993). *Olano* made clear that the appellate court’s remedial inquiry is not mechanical; rather, it asks whether the error in fact caused prejudice and whether correction is warranted in light of the whole case. Id. at 734-37.

This Court has repeatedly confirmed that appellate review under Rule 52(b) is not blind to the rest of the record. In *United States v. Vonn*, the Court held that, in assessing a Rule 11 error under plain-error review, a court of appeals “may consult the whole record” rather than “the plea colloquy alone.” 535 U.S. 55, 59, 74-75 (2002). The Court explained that limiting review to the isolated proceeding below would be inconsistent with the basic structure of plain-error review, which asks whether the error affected substantial rights. Id. at 62-63, 74. That same principle applies here: a court deciding whether a Rehaif error prejudiced a defendant may consider the full record, not just the evidence formally recited to the jury.

Likewise, in *Johnson v. United States*, the Court held that an unpreserved instructional error did not require reversal where the evidence of guilt was “overwhelming” and “essentially uncontroverted,” even though the jury had not been instructed on an element later recognized by the Court. 520 U.S. 461, 470, 470-71 (1997). The Court did not confine its assessment to the jury charge in isolation; it examined the trial evidence to determine whether the error affected substantial rights and whether correction was necessary to preserve the fairness and integrity of the proceedings. Id. at 466-70. *Johnson* thus confirms that plain-error review is record-based, not instruction-bound.

*United States v. Dominguez Benitez* reinforces the point. There, the Court held that a defendant seeking plain-error relief must show “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, he would not have entered the plea.” 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004). That standard requires a reviewing court to consider “the whole record” because prejudice cannot be assessed in a vacuum. Id. at 80-83. If the court may consult the entire record to determine whether a plea error mattered, it may do so here to determine whether a trial error mattered.

Most importantly, *Greer v. United States* itself answers the question presented. The Court held that, in Rehaif-error cases, “the court of appeals can consider the entire record” in assessing whether the error affected substantial rights under Rule 52(b). 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2098 (2021). The Court reasoned that, because felon-status errors often concern facts obvious from the defendant’s own criminal history, an appellate court may look beyond the trial evidence to determine whether there is a reasonable probability of a different outcome. Id. at 2097-99. The Court further held that, in deciding whether to exercise discretion under the fourth prong of plain-error review, the court may similarly consider the whole record. Id. at 2100. That is exactly what the Eleventh Circuit did here.

Greer’s contrary position would improperly transform plain-error review into a rule of artificial blindness. Rule 52(b) is meant to correct only prejudicial errors affecting the fairness and integrity of proceedings. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736-37. A court cannot responsibly make that judgment if it must ignore the defendant’s undisputed prior convictions and other record evidence demonstrating that the Rehaif omission made no practical difference. The Eleventh Circuit therefore acted consistently with this Court’s precedents in considering the entire record on remand.

Because the court of appeals may review matters outside the trial record when applying both the substantial-rights prong and the fairness/integrity prong of plain-error review, the judgment below should be affirmed.
