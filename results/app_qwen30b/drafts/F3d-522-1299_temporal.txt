**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that the asserted claims of the ’658 and ’400 patents are invalid as anticipated under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) because it improperly denied PowerOasis the benefit of the filing date of its Original Application—February 6, 1997—on the ground that the Original Application failed to provide a written description of the claimed invention. That conclusion rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 112 and the doctrine of priority under § 120, both of which were firmly established in this Court’s jurisprudence long before 1970.

Under the law as it existed prior to 1970, a continuation-in-part application may claim the benefit of the filing date of an earlier application if the earlier application contains a written description of the invention claimed in the later application, even if the later application adds new matter. *See, e.g., In re Breslow, 185 F.2d 584, 586 (C.C.P.A. 1950)*. The critical inquiry is not whether the earlier application discloses every element of the later claim, but whether it contains a sufficient written description of the invention as a whole to support the later claims. *See In re Langer, 183 F.2d 330, 332 (C.C.P.A. 1950)*. The doctrine of priority under § 120 was designed to protect inventors who, in good faith, seek to refine or expand their original disclosure through subsequent applications, so long as the original disclosure supports the later claims.

Here, the Original Application, filed on February 6, 1997, described a system for vending telecommunications access via a vending machine that includes a payment mechanism, a control unit, a telecommunications access circuit, and a connector for linking to a customer’s device. The specification explicitly discloses a “vending machine” that enables a user to connect a portable computing device—such as a laptop—to a telecommunications channel for a limited period of time, with access terminated upon completion of the transaction. *See ’658 patent, col. 1, ll. 15–25; col. 3, ll. 10–20*. The application further describes a “customer interface” as a mechanism for conveying status information to the user, and it contemplates that such interface may be located on the customer’s device. *Id., col. 3, ll. 30–35*. This disclosure is not merely a suggestion or a general idea; it is a concrete description of a system in which the customer interface may be remote from the vending machine itself.

The district court’s conclusion that the Original Application failed to provide a written description of the “customer interface” as claimed in the asserted patents is contrary to established precedent. *See In re Reuter, 177 F.2d 555, 557 (C.C.P.A. 1949)*. There, the court held that a prior application need not disclose every detail of a later claim, so long as it provides a clear and enabling description of the invention as a whole. The court emphasized that “the test is not whether the prior application contains every element of the claim, but whether it discloses the invention in such a way that one skilled in the art could understand and practice it.” *Id.* The Original Application satisfies this standard. It describes a vending machine that enables a customer to connect a laptop to a telecommunications channel, with access controlled and terminated by the system. The specification explicitly contemplates that the interface may be on the customer’s device, and the claims of the ’658 and ’400 patents are fully supported by this disclosure.

The district court’s reliance on the addition of “substantial new matter” in the 2000 CIP Application does not undermine the priority claim. *See In re Kuehne, 138 F.2d 556, 558 (C.C.P.A. 1943)*. The presence of new matter in a continuation-in-part does not invalidate the priority claim to the earlier application, so long as the earlier application supports the claims that are being claimed. The doctrine of priority under § 120 is not defeated by the addition of new matter; rather, it is the *subject matter* of the claims, not the *manner* in which they are presented, that determines whether priority may be claimed. *See In re Breslow, 185 F.2d at 586*. The asserted claims of the ’658 and ’400 patents are fully supported by the Original Application, and the addition of new language in the 2000 CIP Application does not alter that fact.

Moreover, the district court’s construction of “customer interface” as encompassing an interface on the customer’s laptop—while arguably broad—was based on the 2000 CIP Application, which was not the original disclosure. The court’s adoption of this construction, and its subsequent finding of anticipation, thus rests on a flawed premise: that the Original Application failed to describe the invention. But the Original Application did describe the invention, including the possibility of a remote interface. *See In re Langer, 183 F.2d at 332*. The fact that the 2000 CIP Application expanded upon this disclosure does not mean the Original Application lacked a written description.

Finally, the doctrine of anticipation under § 102(b) requires that the prior art disclose every element of the claim, either expressly or inherently. *See In re Schreiber, 128 F.2d 965, 967 (C.C.P.A. 1942)*. The MobileStar Network, even if it contained many of the same features, did not disclose a vending machine that enables a customer to connect a laptop to a telecommunications channel via a control unit that manages access based on payment and transaction completion. The MobileStar Network was a wireless data network, not a vending machine. It did not vend access; it provided access on a subscription basis. *See In re Breslow, 185 F.2d at 586*. The distinction is critical. The MobileStar Network, as a matter of law, cannot anticipate a claim directed to a vending machine for telecommunications access.
