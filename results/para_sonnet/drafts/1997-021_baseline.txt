# ARGUMENT

## I. THE BOARD'S "GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT" STANDARD FOR POLLING IS ARBITRARY AND CANNOT BE RECONCILED WITH THE BOARD'S OWN ADMINISTRATIVE SCHEME

This Court reviews Board rules to ensure they are "rational and consistent with the Act." *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 787 (1990). While the Board possesses "primary responsibility for developing and applying national labor policy," *id.* at 786, that deference is not unbounded. An agency rule fails this standard when the agency "entirely failed to consider an important aspect of the problem" or offers an explanation that "runs counter to the evidence." *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983).

The Board's polling standard fails precisely this test. The Board requires an employer to possess evidence sufficient to justify *withdrawing recognition entirely*—the most drastic option available—before it may even conduct a secret-ballot poll, the most cautious and protective option. As the Sixth, Ninth, and Fifth Circuits have recognized, this renders polling "useless" as an independent tool. *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984). If an employer already has evidence sufficient to withdraw recognition, it has no need to poll; if it lacks such evidence, the Board forbids polling altogether. There is no daylight in which the tool can operate.

The Board's own precedent confirms this incoherence. In *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-62 (1989), the Board acknowledged that polling could serve employers who, though possessing "reasonable doubt," seek "more certain, precise information . . . than is provided by its own reasonable doubt," so that the employer "can then act with confidence and certainty in light of the results of the poll." *Id.* at 1063. But if reasonable doubt is the *threshold* for polling, the poll adds nothing—the employer could simply withdraw recognition on that same doubt. The Board's asserted rationale for polling is irreconcilable with the identical evidentiary bar it imposes on withdrawal.

Compounding this defect, the Board maintains that RM elections—which are Board-supervised and carry procedural protections wholly absent from employer polls—are the "preferred" mechanism for testing union support. *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061. Logic dictates that the *less* protected mechanism (unsupervised polling) should require *more*, not equal, evidentiary justification. The Board's insistence on identical standards for polling, RM elections, and outright withdrawal treats three procedurally distinct actions—each carrying materially different risks to employees and to labor peace—as fungible. That approach cannot be squared with reasoned decisionmaking. *See Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n*, 463 U.S. at 43.

This Court has recognized that Board rules must rest on "a reasoned analysis" reflecting the "practical realities" facing employers who must comply with the Act's competing commands under §§ 8(a)(2) and 8(a)(5). *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 38 (1987) (recognizing employer's need for a workable standard to test "continuing majority status"). An employer's polling in strict conformity with the procedural safeguards of *Struksnes Construction Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)—secret ballot, advance notice, no threats or promises—poses none of the coercive risks that justify the Board's broader concern with disruption. The Board erred by conflating the evidentiary threshold for a minimally invasive, procedurally protected poll with that for a unilateral, unreviewable withdrawal of recognition.

## II. EVEN UNDER THE BOARD'S OWN STANDARD, ITS FACTUAL FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN LACKED REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

This Court reviews Board factual findings for "substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole." *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Substantial evidence review requires that "the whole record" be examined, not merely evidence supporting the Board's conclusion; a court "must also take into account whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." *Id.* at 488.

The Board's evaluation of the evidence here fails that standard by applying inconsistent, results-driven rules for crediting employee statements. The Board discounted statements from employees such as Ridgick and Marsh, made during job interviews, on the theory that expressions of anti-union sentiment in that context are inherently suspect. But the Board applied no comparable skepticism to the pro-union inferences it drew elsewhere, revealing a one-sided methodology rather than neutral fact-finding. Similarly, the Board discounted secondhand reports from employees such as Bloch and Mohr regarding coworkers' sentiments as unreliable hearsay, yet the Board's own "good-faith doubt" standard has never required that an employer's doubt rest exclusively on formal testimony—it requires only that the employer's belief be reasonable given the totality of information reasonably available to it at the time.

As Judge Sentelle recognized in dissent below, "there is no substantial evidence to support the Board's conclusion that the Company did not have a good faith doubt as to the Union's majority status." *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 868 (6th Cir. 1982) (so holding on materially similar facts). The Sixth Circuit's reasoning in *Thomas* is directly applicable: where approximately one-fourth of a bargaining unit voices dissatisfaction with union representation, and where—as here—the bargaining unit itself was newly constituted from a larger predecessor unit never independently tested for majority support, the presumption of continuing majority status rests on considerably weaker footing than in the ordinary successor case addressed in *Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 36-41.

The Board's selective crediting of evidence, combined with its refusal to weigh the structural realities of a downsized successor unit, means its ultimate finding does not survive scrutiny under "the record considered as a whole." *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 488. The poll—conducted with exemplary procedural safeguards, including independent supervision by a neutral religious official—confirmed what the objective indicia already suggested: the union lacked majority support. The Board's contrary finding cannot stand.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
