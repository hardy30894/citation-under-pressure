ARGUMENT

A federal court loses jurisdiction only when no live case or controversy remains. Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth, 300 U.S. 227, 240-41 (1937). An offer that satisfies one plaintiff’s individual demand does not extinguish the controversy when the complaint seeks relief for a class. Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 366 (1921). The district court therefore erred in dismissing the action.

Mootness doctrine requires that “an actual controversy must be extant at all stages.” Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895). When the defendant’s tender arrives before class certification, the named plaintiff retains a personal stake in obtaining a class-wide determination. Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32, 41-42 (1940). That stake is not destroyed by an unaccepted Rule 68 offer, because the complaint continues to present “a real, substantial controversy between parties having adverse legal interests.” Aetna, 300 U.S. at 241.

The capable-of-repetition exception further preserves jurisdiction. Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. ICC, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911). A defendant’s ability to repeat the same offer against successive named plaintiffs creates a controversy that “evades review” if each individual claim is held moot. Moore v. Ogilvie, 394 U.S. 814, 816 (1969). Courts therefore retain power to adjudicate the class issues presented in the original pleading. St. Pierre v. United States, 319 U.S. 41, 42 (1943).

An unaccepted offer leaves the plaintiff “free to proceed with the action.” California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R. Co., 149 U.S. 308, 314 (1893). Jurisdiction is measured by the facts existing when the complaint was filed and the motion for certification is made. United States v. Alaska S.S. Co., 253 U.S. 113, 116 (1920). Because Lucero’s motion for class certification was pending, the case remained justiciable.

Dismissal on the basis of an unaccepted offer would permit defendants to nullify class actions by successive tenders, contrary to the rule that a controversy does not become moot merely because one party has changed its conduct. United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). The district court’s order therefore contravened established principles of justiciability. Sibron v. New York, 392 U.S. 40, 57 (1968).

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for consideration of the pending certification motion.
