**ARGUMENT**

**I. OSHA had jurisdiction because the OSH Act applies unless another federal agency has actually exercised statutory authority over the specific working conditions at issue.**  

Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA coverage only when “another Federal agency” has **exercised** statutory authority “to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health” as to the relevant working conditions. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the preemption inquiry is condition-specific, not industry-wide. In *Chao v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n (Mellon Services Co.)*, 540 F.3d 519, 525 (5th Cir. 2008), the court explained that § 4(b)(1) requires actual exercise of authority over the precise hazard or condition, not merely possession of general regulatory power. Likewise, in *Southern Pac. Transp. Co. v. Usery*, 539 F.2d 386, 389 (5th Cir. 1976), the court held that general jurisdiction does not displace OSHA absent specific standards addressing the working conditions at issue.

That principle controls here. The Coast Guard’s authority over vessels is not synonymous with regulation of all occupational safety and health conditions aboard all vessels. As this Court recognized in *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235, 242–43 (2002), the Coast Guard’s marine-casualty investigation and its limited rules for uninspected vessels did not amount to comprehensive regulation of the drilling-rig conditions implicated by the explosion. The Court held that “the existence of Coast Guard authority does not pre-empt OSHA’s jurisdiction unless the Coast Guard has actually exercised that authority.” *Id.* at 243. That is precisely the situation here.

**II. The Coast Guard’s limited regulation of uninspected vessels does not preempt OSHA because it does not comprehensively govern the relevant workplace hazards.**  

The vessel at issue was uninspected. Uninspected-vessel regulation is necessarily limited, and the Supreme Court in *Mallard Bay* rejected the very argument the Fifth Circuit accepted: that the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is “exclusive” for purposes of § 4(b)(1). 534 U.S. at 248–49. The Court explained that the Coast Guard had promulgated only a “limited number of regulations” for uninspected vessels and that those regulations did not cover the working conditions associated with drilling operations aboard the rig. *Id.* at 249–50. Because the Coast Guard had not regulated the “specific working conditions” that caused the explosion, OSHA retained jurisdiction. *Id.* at 250.

The Fifth Circuit erred by treating the Coast Guard’s authority over maritime safety as an across-the-board displacement of OSHA. That approach conflicts with the statutory text, which requires exercise of authority over “working conditions,” not mere authority over a vessel class. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). It also conflicts with *Mallard Bay*’s holding that “limited regulation” is not enough. 534 U.S. at 250. OSHA therefore properly issued citations after the accident revealed hazards outside the Coast Guard’s regulatory scheme.

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on seamen cases such as Clary and Donovan was misplaced.**  

The Fifth Circuit relied on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983). Those cases do not control here. In *Clary*, the court addressed a seaman’s attempt to use OSHA standards as evidence in a personal-injury action; it did not hold that every vessel-related condition is outside OSHA’s reach. 609 F.2d at 1121–22. In *Donovan*, the court described Coast Guard regulation as “comprehensive” in the context before it. 720 F.2d at 826–29. But *Mallard Bay* clarified that any broad reading of those decisions “was mistaken” to the extent it suggested OSHA is categorically excluded from uninspected vessels. 534 U.S. at 248.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235 (2002), is controlling. It holds that OSHA retains jurisdiction unless another agency has affirmatively regulated the specific hazards at issue. Because the Coast Guard had not done so here, the citations were valid.  

**CONCLUSION**  

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and OSHA’s citations should be upheld.
