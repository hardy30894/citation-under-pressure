**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NLRB’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS IRRATIONAL AND CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.

The National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) does not prohibit employers from testing employee sentiment regarding union representation. Indeed, Congress recognized that employers may have a legitimate interest in determining whether an incumbent union continues to enjoy majority support—particularly where the union’s status is not self-evident. Yet the NLRB’s current “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard renders this interest illusory. By requiring an employer to possess *objective evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition* before conducting a poll, the Board effectively eliminates the utility of polling altogether. As the Board itself acknowledges, polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling,” Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), and thus should be permitted only when it serves a genuine purpose. But under the Board’s rule, an employer may poll *only* when it already has enough evidence to justify abandoning the union—making the poll redundant. This circular logic undermines the very purpose of polling: to *resolve uncertainty*.

The Board’s standard is not only irrational but also inconsistent with the NLRA’s statutory framework. Section 9(c)(1)(B) authorizes the Board to conduct an election—known as an “RM election”—to determine employee sentiment. The Act does not distinguish between an RM election and a private poll in terms of their function: both are mechanisms for testing union support. Yet the Board applies a higher evidentiary threshold to the employer’s poll than to the Board’s own election. This is anomalous. If an employer cannot meet the standard to conduct a poll, it cannot meet it to request an RM election. See *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41, 107 S.Ct. 2225, 2232–35, 96 L.Ed.2d 22 (1987). The Board’s own logic thus leads to the absurd result that an employer may not conduct a poll *unless* it already has sufficient grounds to withdraw recognition—yet it may not withdraw recognition without first conducting a poll or an election. The Board’s standard, therefore, creates a self-defeating trap.

The courts of appeals have correctly recognized this flaw. The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits rejected the Board’s standard, holding instead that an employer may conduct a poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence falls short of justifying withdrawal of recognition. *A.W. Thompson, Inc.*, 651 F.2d 1139, 1145 (5th Cir.1981); *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir.1982); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir.1984). These decisions reflect a sound understanding of the NLRA’s purpose: to balance employer interests in avoiding bargaining with a minority union against the statutory protection of stable collective bargaining relationships. The Board’s rigid standard fails this balance. It forces employers to choose between risking an unfair labor practice by bargaining with a union they believe lacks majority support, or doing nothing—despite having credible indications of declining support.

The Board’s attempt to justify its standard by asserting that RM elections are “preferred” over polling is unavailing. *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061. The NLRA does not designate one method as superior. Congress left the choice to the employer, subject only to procedural safeguards. The Board’s preference for elections cannot override the employer’s right to use a less formal, more efficient method when appropriate. Moreover, the Board’s own precedent confirms that polling is a legitimate tool. *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967). The Board cannot simultaneously claim that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” and then render it functionally useless by requiring a standard that only the most extreme evidence can satisfy.

II. THE NLRB’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked “objective reasonable doubt” rests on a selective and erroneous interpretation of the record. The ALJ and the Board discounted the statements of seven employees who expressed opposition to the union, relying on technicalities and speculative inferences. But the record contains overwhelming objective evidence that the union had lost majority support.

First, seven employees—Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, Randy Zoltack, Mike Ridgick, Dennis Marsh, Kermit Bloch, and Ron Mohr—made clear statements indicating that they or others in the unit opposed the union. McArthur, Wehr, and Zoltack were former employees who had worked for Mack Trucks and were present during the transition. Their statements, made before January 25, 1991—the date of Allentown Mack’s letter—were contemporaneous and relevant. The Board’s refusal to count them because they were not on the payroll on that date is arbitrary. The question is not whether they were employed on January 25, but whether their statements, made before the poll, provided a reasonable basis for doubt. *See* *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1298 (statements made before the poll are relevant if they reflect a genuine shift in sentiment).

Second, Ridgick and Marsh’s statements, though made during job interviews, were not “equivocal” or “insincere.” Ridgick stated that “as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union”—a clear expression of anti-union sentiment. The ALJ’s reliance on the 1986 decertification inquiry is misplaced; the 1991 statement was recent, direct, and made in a context where the employer had signaled a non-union stance.
