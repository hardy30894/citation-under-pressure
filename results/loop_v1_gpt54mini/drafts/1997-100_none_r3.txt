**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars the State from retrying a prior conviction allegation after it has already failed to prove that allegation in a bifurcated sentencing proceeding. U.S. Const. amend. V; *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 794-96 (1969); *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The Clause protects against successive attempts by the sovereign to secure a harsher punishment after an adverse final determination. *Ball v. United States*, 163 U.S. 662, 671-72 (1896); *United States v. Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. 85, 87-88 (1916); *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957). That is exactly what the State seeks here: a second chance to prove the same prior conviction allegation after the Court of Appeal held the evidence insufficient.

The controlling principle is not the formal label “sentencing,” but whether the State has already put the defendant in jeopardy on the factual issue it seeks to relitigate. This Court has long refused to permit the government to do indirectly what it cannot do directly. In *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 128-29 (1904), the Court held that the sovereign may not pursue a second conviction after the first adjudication has gone against it. In *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 173-74 (1873), the Court explained that once the court has imposed a lawful punishment, it may not increase it in a way that subjects the defendant to multiple jeopardies. And in *Sanges v. United States*, 144 U.S. 310, 312-13 (1892), the Court recognized that the Constitution restrains repeated prosecutorial efforts to secure a conviction. Those cases reflect the same constitutional concern present here: the State has one opportunity to prove the allegation that triggers enhanced punishment.

The State’s contrary position depends on the proposition that double jeopardy protection applies only to guilt-phase prosecutions or, at most, to capital sentencing. Nothing in the Constitution supports that limitation. The Fifth Amendment says no person shall be “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court’s cases have applied that principle by examining whether the sovereign is making a second attempt to establish the same factual basis for punishment, not by asking whether the proceeding is denominated a trial or a sentencing hearing. *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717; *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88; *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671-72. If the State could relitigate a failed prior conviction allegation whenever it arose in sentencing, the Double Jeopardy Clause would have no practical force in the very setting where the government most often seeks to enlarge punishment based on past conduct.

That result would also undermine the finality the Clause is meant to secure. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Constitution protects not only against multiple punishments, but also against the “embarrassment, expense and ordeal” of successive proceedings and the anxiety of repeated exposure to prosecutorial power. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88; *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717. Those harms are present whether the second proceeding is called a retrial of guilt or a retrial of a sentencing enhancement. The defendant faces the same sovereign, the same allegation, and the same risk of an increased deprivation of liberty.

The California Supreme Court’s rule creates an impermissible end run around these constitutional protections. Once the prosecution’s proof was found insufficient, the allegation should have ended. To permit retrial after a failed showing would allow the State to make “repeated attempts to convict an individual” until it obtains the result it wants. *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671; *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88. The Constitution does not tolerate that strategy. *Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. at 87-88.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed. The Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial on petitioner’s prior conviction allegation in this noncapital sentencing proceeding. *Benton*, 395 U.S. at 794-96; *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717; *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 128-29.
