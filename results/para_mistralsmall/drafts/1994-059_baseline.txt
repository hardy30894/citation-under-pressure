**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT VIOLATES THE UNITED STATES CONSTITUTION BECAUSE IT IMPOSES ADDITIONAL QUALIFICATIONS FOR CONGRESS THAT ARE NOT SPECIFIED IN THE CONSTITUTION.**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment impermissibly alters the qualifications for service in the United States Congress by adding new eligibility requirements not found in the federal Constitution. This Court has long recognized that the qualifications for federal office are exclusively set forth in the Constitution and may not be supplemented by state law. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) ("[T]he Constitution leaves the House [and Senate] without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution."); *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 804 (1995) ("[T]he qualifications for service in Congress established in the text of the Constitution are fixed and may not be altered by the States.").

The text of the Constitution is unambiguous. Article I, Section 2 provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, have been a citizen for seven years, and be an inhabitant of the state from which elected. Article I, Section 3 similarly enumerates the qualifications for Senators. These are the *only* qualifications permitted. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 804 ("The qualifications for membership in Congress set forth in the text of the Constitution are part of the structure of government adopted when the Constitution was ratified, and they remain the same today."). Any attempt by a state to impose additional qualifications—whether by outright exclusion or by ballot-access restrictions—is unconstitutional. *Id.* at 805 ("[A]llowing the several States to craft their own qualifications for Congress would . . . erode the structure designed by the Framers to form a ‘more perfect Union.’").

Arkansas’s Amendment 73 violates this principle by imposing term limits on its federal delegation. Section 3(a) of the Amendment provides that no person who has served three or more terms in the U.S. House may appear on the ballot for re-election, and Section 3(b) imposes a similar restriction on Senators who have served two or more terms. These provisions function as additional qualifications for federal office, as they effectively preclude otherwise qualified candidates from serving beyond the specified term limits. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 805 ("[T]he Arkansas Amendment is an indirect attempt to accomplish what the Constitution forbids states from doing directly.").

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly concluded that these provisions violate the federal Constitution. *See* Ark. Sup. Ct. Op. at 12 ("Section 3 pertaining to U.S. senators and representatives violated the Qualifications clauses of the U.S. Constitution."). This Court should affirm that holding.

**II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT PROPERLY DECLINED TO SEVER THE UNCONSTITUTIONAL PORTIONS OF AMENDMENT 73 BECAUSE THE TERM LIMITS FOR STATE AND FEDERAL OFFICIALS WERE INEXTRICABLY LINKED.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court held that the unconstitutional provisions of Amendment 73 could not be severed from the remainder of the Amendment because the term limits for state and federal officials were presented to the voters as a single, indivisible package. *See* Ark. Sup. Ct. Op. at 13-14 (dissenting opinion) ("Sections 1, 2, and 3 of Amendment 73 were presented to the voters as an ‘all or nothing’ package."). This conclusion is consistent with this Court’s severability jurisprudence, which requires that the unconstitutional portion of a law be "severable without doing violence to the legislative scheme." *Regan v. Time, Inc.*, 468 U.S. 641, 652 (1984).

Here, the Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment was structured as a single initiative, with state and federal term limits presented together in the Preamble and text. The voters were not given the opportunity to approve term limits for state officials alone; they were required to vote on the entire package. *See* Ark. Sup. Ct. Op. at 13 ("The offices specified are state and federal officeholders."). Under these circumstances, the Arkansas Supreme Court properly declined to sever the unconstitutional provisions, as doing so would have rewritten the Amendment in a manner contrary to the voters’ intent. *See generally Ayotte v. Planned Parenthood of N. New Eng.*, 546 U.S. 320, 331 (2006) ("[W]e will not rewrite a state law to conform it to constitutional requirements.").

**III. THE ABSENCE OF AN ENACTING CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE INVALIDATION OF AMENDMENT 73.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court also invalidated Amendment 73 on the ground that it lacked an Enacting Clause, as required by Article 19, Section 22 of the Arkansas Constitution. *See* Ark. Sup. Ct. Op. at 9. However, this Court need not address that issue, as the unconstitutionality of Section 3 alone is sufficient to uphold the judgment below. *See* Ark. Sup. Ct. Op. at 12-14. Even if the Enacting Clause issue were properly before this Court, it would not alter the conclusion that Amendment 73 violates the federal Constitution.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment impermissibly imposes additional qualifications for federal office and cannot be severed from its unconstitutional provisions.
