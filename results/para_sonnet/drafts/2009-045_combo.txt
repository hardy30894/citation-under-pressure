**ARGUMENT**

The question is not whether a "reasonable fee" may *ever* rise above a mechanical hours-times-rate calculation to reflect exceptional performance and results, but whether the century-old equitable understanding of what makes a fee "reasonable" survives the enactment of modern fee-shifting statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988. It does. Long before Congress ever spoke of "reasonable attorney's fees" in a civil-rights statute, this Court had already established that the quality of an attorney's work and the results he achieves are proper, sometimes decisive, components of a reasonable fee award. Congress legislated against that background, and nothing in the text of § 1988 disturbs it.

**I. The traditional equity practice of this Court has always permitted, and at times required, fee awards to reflect the quality of representation and the results obtained.**

In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1881), this Court approved compensation to an attorney out of a fund he had created for others, recognizing that the value of counsel's contribution to the litigation—not merely the hours consumed—determines fair compensation. The Court did not treat time records as the ceiling on a reasonable fee; it looked to what the attorney's efforts actually accomplished for those who benefited.

That principle was elaborated four years later in *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), where the Court identified the character and importance of the litigation, the professional skill required, and the results secured for the parties as legitimate measures of a reasonable fee, alongside the time expended. *Pettus* thus confirms that "reasonable" compensation for legal services has never been synonymous with a bare hourly multiplication; skill and outcome have always mattered.

This Court reaffirmed that approach in the corporate reorganization context in *Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co. of New York*, 259 U.S. 101 (1922), and in *Kansas City Southern Railway Co. v. Guardian Trust Co.*, 281 U.S. 1 (1930), both of which examined the results achieved for the estate or fund in setting compensation for counsel, and in *United States v. Equitable Trust Co.*, 283 U.S. 738 (1931), which likewise tied the size of a reasonable award to the benefit counsel's work conferred. See also *American United Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. City of Avon Park*, 311 U.S. 138 (1940) (fee awards in reorganization proceedings must be tethered to the actual value of services to those benefited, not merely hours logged).

*Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), placed this practice squarely within "the historic equity jurisdiction of the federal courts," id. at 166, confirming that federal courts possess inherent authority to shape fee awards according to the substance of what was accomplished, not merely the arithmetic of time records. And in *Woods v. City National Bank & Trust Co.*, 312 U.S. 262 (1941), this Court again evaluated a fee award by reference to the difficulty of the litigation and the benefit secured, not simply the hours claimed.

These are not fringe or outlier decisions. They constitute an unbroken line of authority, spanning sixty years before the enactment of modern fee-shifting statutes, establishing that a "reasonable fee" in federal practice has always been understood to encompass quality of performance and the results obtained. Congress does not write on a blank slate; it is presumed to legislate with knowledge of this settled background principle.

**II. Congress enacted fee-shifting statutes against, and consistent with, this settled equitable backdrop.**

When Congress directs that a prevailing party "may recover a reasonable attorney's fee," it invokes a term of art with a well-established equitable pedigree—precisely the pedigree reflected in *Greenough*, *Pettus*, and their progeny. This Court has recognized that fee-shifting provisions serve the purpose of ensuring adequate compensation for counsel who take on difficult and important litigation, a purpose that would be frustrated by a rule mechanically excluding consideration of exceptional skill or exceptional results. *See Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 718 (1967) (fee-shifting statutes reflect a considered legislative judgment that certain successful litigants should recover the costs of vindicating rights Congress deemed important). Nothing in § 1988 signals any intent to sever the word "reasonable" from the equitable content this Court had already given it for nearly a century.

**III. District courts remain best positioned to determine, in the rare case, when performance and results justify departure from the ordinary lodestar figure.**

The district court here did precisely what *Pettus* and *Greenough* contemplated: it examined the character of the litigation, the skill required, and the results obtained, and made detailed, record-based findings before adjusting the fee. Consistent with this Court's traditional equity practice, that determination is entitled to substantial deference, for it is the trial court, not a reviewing court, that observes counsel's performance and the fruits of that performance firsthand.

For these reasons, the reasonable-fee inquiry under a federal fee-shifting statute may, in the rare and exceptional case supported by specific evidence, be enhanced based on the quality of performance and the results obtained.
