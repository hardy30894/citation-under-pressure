# ARGUMENT

## I. A Violation of Rule 32(a)(2) Is Not Itself Grounds for Collateral Relief Absent a Showing That the Error Worked a Fundamental Miscarriage of Justice

This Court has long recognized that not every departure from the formal requirements of the criminal rules opens the door to collateral attack under 28 U.S.C. § 2255. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), the Court confronted a materially indistinguishable claim: the sentencing court had failed to comply with the then-existing allocution requirement of Rule 32(a), which, like Rule 32(a)(2), was designed to protect a defendant's procedural interests at sentencing. The Court held that such an omission does not warrant relief unless it is "a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice" or "an omission inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure." *Id.* at 428. Because the petitioner in *Hill* suffered no prejudice from the omission, the Court refused to convert a technical rule violation into a basis for vacating the sentence. The same result follows here.

This limitation on collateral relief reflects the settled understanding, expressed in *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174 (1947), that habeas corpus and its statutory analogues are not "the equivalent of an appeal," and that claims of trial-level error—particularly those that could have been, but were not, pressed on direct review—are ordinarily unavailable on collateral attack absent exceptional circumstances. *Id.* at 178-79. Peguero's situation is precisely the case *Sunal* anticipated: a procedural claim, correctable on direct appeal, resurrected years later on collateral review, with no showing that the underlying purpose of the rule was ever actually frustrated.

*United States v. Hayman*, 342 U.S. 205 (1952), confirms that Congress intended § 2255 to serve as a practical substitute for habeas corpus, not as an independent vehicle for policing every deviation from the rules of procedure. *Id.* at 219. And where, as here, the District Court held an evidentiary hearing consistent with *Machibroda v. United States*, 368 U.S. 487, 494-95 (1962), and made a factual finding—undisturbed on appeal—that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing, the predicate for relief under *Hill* is entirely absent.

## II. Because Peguero Was Actually Aware of His Right to Appeal, the Purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) Was Fully Served and No Substantial Right Was Affected

Rule 32(a)(2) exists to ensure that a defendant is not deprived, through ignorance, of the opportunity to seek appellate review. Where a defendant already possesses that knowledge, the omission is harmless in the fullest sense recognized by this Court's harmless-error jurisprudence. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court articulated the governing standard: reversal is required only "if one cannot say, with fair assurance ... that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error." *Id.* at 765. Here, the District Court's finding that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal establishes precisely the "fair assurance" that *Kotteakos* requires. The court's oversight did not deprive Peguero of any right he did not already know he possessed; it therefore could not have "substantially swayed" his decision not to appeal.

This is not a case, moreover, of a court withholding information a defendant would otherwise never have obtained. It is a case in which the defendant, fully informed, made a considered choice. That distinction is decisive. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court defined waiver as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Id.* at 464. Peguero's failure to note an appeal, made with full knowledge of the right, fits comfortably within that definition. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), reaffirmed that the *Zerbst* standard governs whether a defendant's forgone opportunity for review should be excused on collateral attack, and recognized that federal courts retain discretion to deny relief where a defendant has, with knowledge of his rights, chosen not to exercise them. *Id.* at 438-40. Peguero's knowing inaction is fundamentally different from the ignorance Rule 32(a)(2) was designed to prevent, and it forecloses any claim that the District Court's oversight caused him cognizable harm.

## III. Extending Habeas Relief to Purely Technical, Non-Prejudicial Violations Would Exceed the Proper Scope of Collateral Review

This Court has been careful to confine collateral remedies to errors of constitutional magnitude or those working manifest injustice, reserving relief for genuinely serious deprivations. *Kaufman v. United States*, 394 U.S. 217 (1969), reaffirmed that § 2255 relief tracks the historic office of the writ—correcting fundamental unfairness, not policing every procedural imperfection. A rule requiring automatic vacatur whenever a sentencing court omits the Rule 32(a)(2) advisement, regardless of the defendant's actual knowledge, would transform a technical procedural safeguard into a windfall unmoored from any showing of prejudice, contrary to the teachings of *Hill*, *Sunal*, and *Kotteakos* alike.

## CONCLUSION

Because Peguero was actually aware of his right to appeal, the District Court's failure to advise him under Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless and does not present the kind of fundamental defect that justifies relief under § 2255. The judgment below should be affirmed.
