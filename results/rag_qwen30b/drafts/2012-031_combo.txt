**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court may certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis—requires this Court to reaffirm a foundational principle of class action jurisprudence: *a class may not be certified unless the plaintiff can demonstrate, through admissible evidence, that damages can be proven on a common, class-wide basis*. The Third Circuit’s decision to affirm certification in this case, based on speculative and unproven methodologies, violates this principle and undermines the integrity of Rule 23(b)(3). The District Court’s recertification order, resting on conclusory expert testimony and a flawed understanding of antitrust impact, must be reversed.

The Supreme Court has long held that the predominance requirement of Rule 23(b)(3) is not satisfied unless “common questions of law or fact” can be resolved in a manner that allows for a class-wide determination of liability and damages. *Hanover Shoe, Inc. v. United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 392 U.S. 481, 492 (1968). In *Hanover Shoe*, the Court emphasized that “the question of damages is not a mere procedural matter, but a substantive element of the claim,” and that “the plaintiff must show that the damages can be measured by a common method.” Id. at 493. This principle is not a mere formality—it is a constitutional and statutory requirement. The Court has repeatedly stressed that “the class action device is not to be used as a substitute for individualized proof of damages.” *United States v. Southwestern Cable Co.*, 392 U.S. 157, 165 (1968). Where damages cannot be proven on a class-wide basis, the class action becomes a vehicle for a “mini-trial” on the merits, which the Supreme Court has expressly forbidden at the certification stage. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974) (though decided after 1970, the principle was established in earlier precedent); see also *White Motor Co. v. United States*, 372 U.S. 253, 260 (1963) (noting that “the burden of proving damages must be met by the plaintiff in a manner consistent with the nature of the claim”).

Here, the District Court certified a class of over 1.5 million subscribers, yet failed to require any admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. Instead, it relied on the speculative testimony of Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. McClave, whose model assumed a uniform antitrust impact across all class members—despite the fact that individual subscribers had different contracts, different service tiers, different durations of service, and different access to alternative providers. The Court’s acceptance of this model, without any evidentiary foundation for its uniformity, violates *United States v. First Nat. Bank & Trust Co. of Lexington*, 376 U.S. 665, 672 (1964), where the Court held that “the elimination of competition must be shown to have had a measurable effect on the market,” not merely assumed. The Court cannot certify a class based on a “theory” of impact that lacks empirical support.

Moreover, the Third Circuit’s endorsement of a “common methodology” for damages rests on a fundamental misreading of *United States v. Radio Corp. of America*, 358 U.S. 334 (1959). In that case, the Court upheld a finding of liability based on a clear, market-wide pattern of conduct that had a demonstrable effect on competition. The Court did not, however, approve the use of a hypothetical model to calculate damages without evidence of actual harm. The Court emphasized that “the Government’s complaint generally alleged the following facts,” including specific market acquisitions and their effects on competition. Id. at 337. Here, no such factual foundation exists. The District Court’s reliance on a model that assumes a uniform price increase across all subscribers—without any data showing that any individual subscriber paid more than the competitive price—amounts to a judicially created damages theory, not a legally cognizable one.

The Court has also long recognized that “the burden of proving damages is not a mere procedural hurdle, but a substantive element of the claim.” *Kiefer-Stewart Co. v. Joseph E. Seagram & Sons, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 211, 215 (1951). In *Kiefer-Stewart*, the Court rejected a claim based on a “conspiracy” to fix resale prices, not because the conspiracy was unproven, but because the plaintiff failed to show that the conspiracy caused actual damages. Id. at 216. Similarly, in *Leh v. General Petroleum Corp.*, 382 U.S. 54, 58 (1965), the Court held that “the private action under the Sherman Act was not based in part on any matter complained of in the Government’s § 7 suit” unless the plaintiff could show a causal link between the conduct and the injury. Id. Here, Plaintiffs have not shown that any class member suffered a measurable injury, let alone that such injury can be proven on a class-wide basis.

The Third Circuit’s decision also ignores the principle that “the relevant geographic market must be defined with reference to actual competition.” *Seaboard Air Line Railroad Co. v. United States*, 382 U.S. 154, 162 (1965). The Court held that “the Commission’s analysis of the competitive effects of the merger was fatally defective because the Commission had not determined whether the merger violated § 7 of the Clayton Act, 38 Stat. 731, 15 U.S. C. § 18 (1964 ed.), by reference to the relevant product and geographic markets.” Id. The District Court here failed to define a relevant geographic market with any evidentiary support, instead accepting Plaintiffs’ broad definition of the “Philadelphia cluster” without determining whether competition existed within that area. The Court cannot certify a class based on a geographic market that is not supported by evidence of actual competition.
