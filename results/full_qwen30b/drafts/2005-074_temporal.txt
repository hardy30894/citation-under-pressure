**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, 8 U.S.C. § 1252(f)(1), DOES NOT APPLY TO DEPORTATION ORDERS ISSUED BEFORE ITS ENACTMENT, AND TO APPLY IT RETROACTIVELY WOULD VIOLATE THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE OF THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.**

The question presented is whether Congress, in enacting the reinstatement statute of the Immigration and Nationality Act—Section 241(a)(5), now codified as 8 U.S.C. § 1252(f)(1)—intended to apply that provision to aliens who re-entered the United States before the statute’s effective date in 1997. The answer is no. The statute, by its terms and in light of the settled principles of statutory construction and constitutional law, cannot be applied retroactively to reinstate a deportation order issued in 1981, when the law did not exist.

The fundamental rule of statutory construction is that “a statute is not to be given a retrospective operation unless such intention is clearly expressed.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). This principle is rooted in the due process guarantee that no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without fair notice and a reasonable opportunity to respond. *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). Where a statute alters the legal consequences of past conduct, it must be clearly stated to do so. *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 442 (1965) (citing *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. at 543). The absence of such clarity renders the application of the statute impermissible.

Here, the reinstatement statute was enacted in 1996 and became effective in 1997. It provides that “if an alien who has been ordered removed from the United States reenters the country without the permission of the Attorney General, the prior order of removal shall be reinstated.” 8 U.S.C. § 1252(f)(1). The statute contains no express language indicating that it applies to orders issued prior to its enactment. Indeed, the very concept of “reinstatement” presupposes a prior order that was not yet final or effective at the time of the re-entry. *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939) (“The word ‘reinstate’ implies a restoration to a former condition, not a creation of a new one.”). To apply the statute to a deportation order issued in 1981—before the statute existed—is to create a new legal consequence for conduct that occurred under a different legal regime. That is precisely the kind of retroactive application the Constitution forbids.

The Supreme Court has long held that “a statute which imposes a new disability or imposes a new obligation, or which creates a new offense or increases the punishment for an old one, is not to be applied to acts committed before its passage.” *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 26 (1953). The reinstatement statute, by denying an alien the right to apply for any form of relief—including adjustment of status—after re-entry, imposes a new disability on conduct that was not subject to such a penalty when it occurred. In 1982, when Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States, no law existed that would have barred him from seeking relief under the INA. The law did not prohibit him from applying for permanent residence, nor did it provide that a prior deportation order could be automatically reinstated upon re-entry. To apply the statute now is to impose a penalty for conduct that was lawful at the time.

Moreover, the principle that “a man’s conduct may be judged only by the law in force at the time it was committed” is a cornerstone of American jurisprudence. *United States v. Hudson*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 32, 34 (1812). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “no one can be punished for an act which was not a crime when committed.” *Ex parte Garland*, 4 Wall. 333, 380 (1866). While the reinstatement statute does not criminalize re-entry, it does impose a severe and permanent consequence—deportation without any opportunity for relief—on conduct that was not subject to such a penalty at the time. This is not a mere procedural change; it is a substantive alteration of rights.

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that Congress intended the statute to apply retroactively rests on a flawed assumption: that the absence of a saving clause implies intent to apply the law to prior orders. But the absence of a saving clause does not imply retroactive application. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. at 543. The Court has consistently held that “the mere fact that a statute is silent as to its retroactive effect does not justify its application to past conduct.” *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. at 26. The burden is on the government to show clear intent to apply the statute retroactively, and no such intent is evident here.

Finally, the due process clause of the Fifth Amendment protects individuals from being deprived of liberty without fair notice and a reasonable opportunity to be heard. *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. at 325. Fernandez-Vargas had no notice that his re-entry in 1982 would result in automatic deportation without any form of relief. He acted in reliance on the legal regime that existed at the time. To now deprive him of the opportunity to adjust his status—based on a marriage to a U.S. citizen and years of lawful residence—is to violate the fundamental fairness required by due process.
