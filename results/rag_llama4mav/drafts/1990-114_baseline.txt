The petitioners, Missouri state court judges, challenge the constitutionality of Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges under the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) and the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause. The Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit correctly held that the petitioners are exempt from the ADEA's coverage and that the mandatory retirement provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.

I. The Petitioners Are Exempt from the ADEA's Coverage

The ADEA exempts from its coverage "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The petitioners, appointed state court judges, fall within this exemption. As judges, they exercise significant policymaking authority, particularly in their supervisory roles. The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, is responsible for adopting rules governing the professional conduct of the Missouri judiciary and the Missouri Bar. Mo. Const. art. V, Sec. 4. The Court's decisions also establish common law for the entire state, Gustafson v. Benda, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo.1983) (en banc), and sometimes involve close and difficult questions of law with far-reaching ethical, moral, and public policy implications, Cruzan v. Harmon, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo.1988) (en banc).

The petitioners argue that judges are not "appointees on the policymaking level" because their primary function is to decide cases, not make policy. However, this view is too narrow. Judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents. EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir.1988). The policymaking level exception is not limited to appointees within the executive and legislative branches; it applies to appointed judges as well.

The legislative history of the ADEA exemptions supports this interpretation. The conference committee report indicates that the exemption is intended to apply to "persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments." Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin.News 2179-2180. The state judiciary can be considered one of the more powerful "agencies" of state government, EEOC v. Massachusetts, 680 F.Supp. at 460-61, and appointed judges are therefore exempt from the ADEA's coverage.

II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause

The petitioners also argue that Missouri's mandatory retirement provision violates the Equal Protection Clause. However, the rational basis test applies to age-based classifications, Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979), and Missouri's provision easily passes this test.

The state has several rational bases for the mandatory retirement provision. Mandatory retirement eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged judges, Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.1980), and reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges, Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir.1979). The provision also tends to assure that important decisions are made by judges who command respect because they share experiences and understandings similar to those of the majority of adult citizens, Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988).

The Court has upheld similar age-based classifications in the past, see, e.g., Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93 (1979); Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307 (1976) (per curiam), and should do so here. The mandatory retirement provision is a rational means of achieving the state's legitimate goals, and the petitioners have not shown that it is arbitrary or unreasonable.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
