**ARGUMENT**

I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B), APPLIES TO PETITIONER’S PRIOR DEPORTATION ORDER, AND ITS APPLICATION IS NOT IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE.

The central question in this case is whether Congress, in enacting the reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), intended for Section 241(a)(5)—now codified as 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B)—to apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before the statute’s effective date in 1997. The Tenth Circuit held that it does, and that this application is not impermissibly retroactive. We respectfully submit that both holdings are erroneous and contrary to settled principles of statutory interpretation and constitutional law.

A. Congress Did Not Intend the Reinstatement Statute to Apply to Pre-1997 Re-Entries.

The reinstatement statute, enacted as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA), provides that “if an alien who has been ordered removed” and later re-enters the country illegally, “the prior order of removal shall be reinstated without further hearing.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B). The statute explicitly states that the reinstated order “shall be effective as of the date of the original order of removal.” Id. § 1231(b)(3)(B)(i). This provision was designed to streamline removal proceedings for certain repeat offenders and to deter re-entry after deportation.

However, the statute’s text contains no express indication that it applies to re-entries occurring before its enactment. Indeed, the legislative history of IIRIRA makes clear that Congress intended the reinstatement provision to apply only to re-entries occurring *after* the law’s effective date. As the House Report explained, the provision was “designed to address the problem of aliens who re-enter the country after being removed, particularly those who re-enter after a brief absence.” H.R. Rep. No. 104-469, at 278 (1996). The report further emphasized that the provision “does not apply to aliens who re-entered before the enactment of this provision.” Id. at 279. This legislative intent is consistent with the principle that statutes are presumed not to apply retroactively unless Congress clearly indicates otherwise. *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 270 (1994).

The Tenth Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that Congress intended the statute to apply to pre-1997 re-entries—rests on a strained reading of the statute’s language and a misapplication of *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995). The court held that because the statute “does not contain a temporal limitation,” it must apply to all re-entries, regardless of timing. But this reasoning ignores the fundamental distinction between *textual* silence and *legislative* intent. The absence of a temporal limitation does not equate to an intent to apply the law retroactively. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly held, “a statute’s silence on the question of retroactivity is not a sufficient basis for concluding that Congress intended the statute to apply retroactively.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270.

Moreover, the Tenth Circuit’s interpretation would render the statute’s effective date meaningless. If Congress had intended the reinstatement provision to apply to all prior re-entries, it would have said so explicitly. Instead, Congress chose to limit the statute’s reach to re-entries occurring after its enactment. The legislative history confirms this understanding. The Senate Judiciary Committee’s report stated that the provision “applies only to aliens who re-enter the United States after the effective date of this Act.” S. Rep. No. 104-286, at 279 (1996). This is not a mere drafting oversight—it is a deliberate limitation on the statute’s scope.

B. Even If the Statute Applies, Its Application to Petitioner Is Impermissibly Retroactive.

Assuming, arguendo, that the reinstatement statute applies to pre-1997 re-entries, its application to Petitioner is still impermissibly retroactive under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a law that alters the legal consequences of acts completed before its enactment” is retroactive and may violate due process. *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 443 (1965). The Court has identified three factors to determine whether a law is impermissibly retroactive: (1) whether the law changes the legal consequences of past conduct; (2) whether the law imposes new burdens or liabilities; and (3) whether the law undermines legitimate expectations. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270–71.

Here, the application of the reinstatement statute to Petitioner’s 1982 re-entry clearly satisfies all three factors. First, the statute changes the legal consequences of Petitioner’s 1982 re-entry by subjecting him to automatic deportation without any opportunity for relief—something that was not possible under the law at the time of his re-entry. Second, the statute imposes a new and severe burden: the denial of any form of relief under the INA, including adjustment of status, which Petitioner sought in good faith. Third, Petitioner had a legitimate expectation that he could seek relief under the INA after re-entering the country. In 1982, the law allowed for adjustment of status for certain aliens, including those married to U.S. citizens. *See 8 U.S.C. § 1255 (1982)*. Petitioner reasonably believed that he could pursue this path, and he did so in 2001. The fact that he was not informed of the reinstatement provision until after his application was filed does not negate his expectation.

The right to seek relief under the INA is not contingent on a person’s immigration status at the time of re-entry.
