**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING THE DEFENDANT’S EXPERT’S OPINION AS TO THE CAUSE OF THE LINE’S PARTING BECAUSE THE TESTIMONY WAS NOT PROPERLY QUALIFIED EXPERT TESTIMONY UNDER FEDERAL RULE OF EVIDENCE 702.

The district court erred in admitting the testimony of American Steamship’s expert, Mr. James H. Smith, who opined that the manilla rope parted due to “thermal degradation” caused by Cook’s use of the propane torch near the deck-level bearing. This conclusion was not based on scientific methodology, nor did it reflect the application of specialized knowledge within the realm of engineering or materials science. Instead, Mr. Smith’s opinion was speculative and grounded in common observation, rendering it inadmissible under Federal Rule of Evidence 702.

Rule 702 requires that expert testimony be “based upon sufficient facts or data,” “the product of reliable principles and methods,” and that the expert “has reliably applied the principles and methods to the facts of the case.” *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 592 (1993). The Supreme Court has made clear that “the trial judge must ensure that any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable.” *Id.* at 592–93. In *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 150 (1999), the Court extended *Daubert*’s reliability standard to all expert testimony, including that of non-scientific experts.

Here, Mr. Smith offered no testing, no laboratory analysis, no examination of the rope’s composition, and no data on the thermal properties of manilla rope under prolonged exposure to flame. His opinion was based solely on the fact that the rope was near a torch and that it failed. This is not expert testimony—it is speculation. As the Sixth Circuit held in *United States v. Hines*, 977 F.2d 1007, 1012 (6th Cir. 1992), “an expert may not testify to conclusions that are merely the product of common sense or personal belief.” Mr. Smith’s opinion was precisely that: a conclusion drawn from common observation, not scientific or technical analysis.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has emphasized that “the trial judge must ensure that the expert’s testimony rests on a reliable foundation and is relevant to the task at hand.” *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 592. The district court failed in this duty. The admission of Mr. Smith’s testimony prejudiced Cook by allowing the jury to infer that Cook’s actions caused the rope’s failure, despite the absence of any scientific basis for that conclusion. The error was not harmless. *See* *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764–65 (1946) (error is not harmless if it “had substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the jury’s verdict”).

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ISSUE OF UNSEAWORTHINESS.

The district court erred in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. Under the Jones Act, a vessel is unseaworthy when it is “not reasonably fit for its intended purpose.” *Tompkins v. United States*, 356 U.S. 363, 365 (1958). The unseaworthiness doctrine applies when a vessel’s condition or equipment creates a hazard that a reasonably prudent mariner would recognize as dangerous. *See* *Barnes v. United States*, 362 U.S. 199, 202 (1960).

Here, the evidence established that the ship’s boom bearings had not been greased with winter grease, despite the sub-zero temperatures and icy conditions. The failure to change the grease was a known and foreseeable risk. The crew had to use propane torches to deice the bearings, a dangerous and unconventional method. The fact that the boom would not pivot without this intervention demonstrates a failure in maintenance and preparation. *See* *Barnes*, 362 U.S. at 202 (a vessel is unseaworthy when it lacks proper equipment or maintenance for the conditions).

Furthermore, the use of a propane torch near a manilla rope—especially one that was already in use and stored in a heated room—created a foreseeable risk of fire or thermal degradation. The rope was not inspected for heat damage before use, and no safety protocol existed to prevent such exposure. *See* *Harris v. United States*, 457 F.2d 1057, 1060 (5th Cir. 1972) (a vessel is unseaworthy when equipment is used in a manner that creates an unreasonable risk of harm).

The district court’s denial of Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness was error. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Cook, established that the vessel was not reasonably fit for its intended purpose. *See* *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 251 (1986) (summary judgment is appropriate when no genuine issue of material fact exists). The jury should have been instructed on unseaworthiness as a matter of law.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK’S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS.

The district court did not err in admitting evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments.
