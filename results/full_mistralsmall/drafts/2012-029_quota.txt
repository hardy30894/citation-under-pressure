**ARGUMENT**

**I. FEDERAL COURTS POSSESS EXCLUSIVE JURISDICTION OVER STATE MALPRACTICE CLAIMS THAT REQUIRE RESOLUTION OF A SUBSTANTIAL AND NECESSARY PATENT ISSUE.**

The Supreme Court has long held that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases “arising under” federal patent law. *See Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988) (construing 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a)). This jurisdiction extends to state-law claims where “a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” *Id.* at 808–09. The Court has further refined this test, requiring that the federal issue be (1) necessary to resolution of the state-law claim, (2) actually disputed, (3) substantial, and (4) capable of resolution without disturbing the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).

Here, Minton’s legal malpractice claim necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial and disputed patent issue—the applicability of the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (explaining the experimental use exception). Without resolving this patent issue, Minton cannot establish causation in his malpractice claim, as he must prove that his attorneys’ failure to raise the exception proximately caused his injury. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004) (legal malpractice requires proof of proximate cause). The patent issue is not merely tangential; it is the linchpin of Minton’s claim. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808–09.

The Federal Circuit has consistently applied this framework to legal malpractice claims arising from patent litigation. *See Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (holding that federal jurisdiction exists where patent law is a necessary element of the malpractice claim); *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281, 1288 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (same). This Court’s reliance on *Grable* is misplaced, as the Supreme Court itself has recognized that patent cases are uniquely suited to federal jurisdiction due to Congress’s intent to ensure uniformity in patent law. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 818–19 (noting the federal interest in patent law uniformity).

**II. THE PATENT ISSUE IN THIS CASE IS SUBSTANTIAL AND NECESSARY, AND FEDERAL JURISDICTION WILL NOT DISTURB THE BALANCE OF FEDERAL AND STATE RESPONSIBILITIES.**

The experimental use exception is a well-established doctrine in patent law, and its application here is neither frivolous nor incidental. *See Electromotive*, 417 F.3d at 1210. The Federal Circuit has repeatedly held that such issues are substantial enough to trigger federal jurisdiction. *See Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1273; *Immunocept*, 504 F.3d at 1288. Moreover, resolving this issue will not create a jurisdictional imbalance, as the federal courts are best equipped to interpret patent law consistently. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 818–19.

The dissent’s reliance on *Grable* is misguided, as that case involved a state tax dispute with only a tangential federal issue. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 315. Here, by contrast, the patent issue is central to Minton’s malpractice claim. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808–09. The Federal Circuit’s decisions in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept* correctly apply the *Grable* framework to patent cases, and this Court should defer to their reasoning. *See id.*

**III. THE TEXAS SUPREME COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING MINTON’S CLAIM FOR LACK OF JURISDICTION.**

The Texas Supreme Court’s decision conflicts with controlling Supreme Court precedent and the Federal Circuit’s well-reasoned decisions. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808–09; *Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1273. The experimental use exception is a substantial and necessary patent issue, and its resolution is essential to Minton’s malpractice claim. The Texas courts lacked jurisdiction to decide this case, and this Court should reverse the judgment below.
