ARGUMENT

I.   THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO WORKERS INJURED AS A DIRECT RESULT OF OPERATIONS CONDUCTED ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain language of the statute requires a direct causal link between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf. The Fifth Circuit has correctly interpreted this provision to require a "situs-of-injury" test, holding that an injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it to be eligible for OCSLA benefits. Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc).

The Supreme Court's dictum in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), supports this interpretation. Although the Court did not directly address the issue, it noted that § 1333(b) "superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement." Id. at 219 n.2. This statement suggests that the Court views the OCSLA as having a situs requirement, which is consistent with the Fifth Circuit's interpretation.

Furthermore, the Third Circuit's "but for" test, adopted by the Ninth Circuit below, is overly broad and would lead to an expansive and unintended reading of the statute. Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Serv., Inc., 849 F.2d 805, 809-11 (3d Cir. 1988). Under this test, an injury would be covered as long as it was somehow related to operations on the outer Continental Shelf, regardless of where the injury occurred. This interpretation ignores the plain language of the statute and would lead to an unworkable and overly broad application of the OCSLA.

II.  THE BRB CORRECTLY DENIED BENEFITS UNDER THE LHWCA BECAUSE LA CONCHITA IS NOT A MARITIME SITES

The LHWCA requires that an injury occur on a maritime situs, which includes "any adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel." 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Ninth Circuit has applied a "functional relationship" test to determine whether a facility is a maritime situs. Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978).

La Conchita, the onshore oil-processing facility where Valladolid was killed, does not meet this test. Although it is located near the coast, it is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks and has no direct access to any pier, dock, or other loading facility. The facility's primary purpose is to process crude oil slurry extracted by the offshore platforms, which is a non-maritime activity. Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray (Herb's Welding I), 470 U.S. 414, 422-24 (1985).

Petitioner's argument that La Conchita is a "transshipment" facility because it handles scrap metal from the offshore platforms is unpersuasive. The handling of scrap metal at La Conchita is not an integral part of maritime commerce, and the facility is not "adjoining" to any maritime facilities. P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford, 444 U.S. 69 (1979), is distinguishable because the injuries in that case occurred on a dock and a pier, which are indisputably maritime situses.

CONCLUSION

The OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to workers injured as a direct result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf. The BRB correctly denied benefits under the LHWCA because La Conchita is not a maritime situs. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and remand for further proceedings consistent with the correct interpretation of the OCSLA.
