**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sixth Amendment’s Right to Counsel Applies to Tribal Court Convictions Used as Predicate Offenses in Federal Prosecutions Under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to uncounseled tribal court convictions used as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). *United States v. Bryant*, 769 F.3d 671, 677 (9th Cir. 2014). This conclusion is compelled by the Supreme Court’s decision in *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), which recognized that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel attaches to prior uncounseled convictions used in sentencing. *Id.* at 746-47. The Court in *Nichols* explicitly relied on *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367 (1979), which held that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies to any criminal proceeding where imprisonment is imposed. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746.

The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is not limited to federal or state courts; it applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts. *See Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 345 (1963) (holding that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies to the states via the Fourteenth Amendment). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are fundamental to ensuring the reliability of criminal convictions. *See Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 685 (1984) (noting that the right to counsel is "the bedrock of our adversary system"). Thus, the use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense in a federal prosecution under § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment.

**II. The Use of Uncounseled Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause requires that prior convictions used to enhance punishment in federal prosecutions be reliable. *See United States v. Tucker*, 404 U.S. 443, 446-47 (1972) (holding that a sentencing court may not rely on a prior conviction obtained in violation of the defendant’s constitutional rights). The Supreme Court has recognized that uncounseled convictions are inherently unreliable because they lack the procedural safeguards necessary to ensure a fair trial. *See Argersinger v. Hamlin*, 407 U.S. 25, 40 (1972) (holding that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies to misdemeanor cases where imprisonment is imposed).

The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) violates due process because these convictions are unreliable. *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 677. The Supreme Court has made clear that the reliability of prior convictions is a critical concern in recidivist sentencing schemes. *See Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747 (noting that the Court’s decision in *Nichols* was based on the reliability concerns inherent in the Sixth Amendment right to counsel). Thus, the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) violates the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Does Not Undermine Tribal Sovereignty or Federal Efforts to Combat Domestic Violence.**

The dissent argues that the Ninth Circuit’s decision undermines tribal sovereignty and federal efforts to combat domestic violence. *See* Dissenting Opinion at 1-2. This argument is misguided. The Ninth Circuit’s decision does not invalidate tribal court convictions or undermine tribal sovereignty. Instead, it ensures that prior convictions used in federal prosecutions comply with constitutional requirements. *See Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 677.

Congress has recognized the importance of ensuring that tribal court convictions are reliable by enacting the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303, which requires tribal courts to provide certain procedural safeguards, including the right to counsel in some cases. *See* 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)-(c). The Ninth Circuit’s decision does not conflict with ICRA; it merely ensures that tribal court convictions used as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions comply with constitutional requirements. *See Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 677.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Is Consistent with Supreme Court Precedent.**

The dissent argues that the Ninth Circuit’s decision conflicts with Supreme Court precedent. *See* Dissenting Opinion at 1-2. This argument is incorrect. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is fundamental to ensuring the reliability of criminal convictions. *See Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 345; *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 685. The Ninth Circuit’s decision is consistent with this precedent and ensures that prior convictions used in federal prosecutions comply with constitutional requirements.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Ninth Circuit correctly held that the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel and the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause. The petition for certiorari should be granted.
