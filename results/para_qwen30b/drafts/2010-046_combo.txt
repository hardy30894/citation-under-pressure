**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act—must be answered in the negative. The order at issue is not only consistent with the PLRA, but is compelled by the Constitution and long-standing principles of federal equity jurisdiction. The respondent, Marciano Plata, and the class he represents, are entitled to relief under the Eighth Amendment, and the district court’s remedial order is a lawful exercise of judicial power grounded in settled precedent.

**I. The Court’s Authority to Order Population Reductions to Alleviate Cruel and Unusual Punishment Is Well Established by Precedent**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits “cruel and unusual punishments,” and courts have long held that conditions of confinement that deprive inmates of basic human needs constitute such punishment. In *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), the Supreme Court held that punishment must be proportionate to the offense and that “the punishment must not be so excessive as to be cruel and unusual.” The Court emphasized that “the Constitution is not a mere parchment of paper,” and that “the rights of the individual are not to be sacrificed to the convenience of the state.” Id. at 371. When prison conditions render incarceration “cruel and unusual,” the judiciary has a duty to intervene.

This duty was reaffirmed in *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516 (1884), where the Court recognized that the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects individuals from arbitrary state action, including the infliction of suffering through inhumane conditions. The Court stated that “no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law,” and that “the law must be administered with a regard to the rights of the individual.” Id. at 535. When a state fails to provide basic medical care, sanitation, or safety, it violates this constitutional obligation.

In *Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339 (1879), the Court held that federal courts possess the authority to compel state officials to comply with constitutional mandates. The Court declared that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no state can, by its own laws, defeat the rights secured by the Constitution.” Id. at 344. This principle applies with particular force when a state’s own actions have created a constitutional violation. The federal judiciary may issue orders necessary to enforce constitutional rights, including those that require structural changes in state institutions.

**II. The PLRA Does Not Prohibit Remedial Orders That Address Systemic Constitutional Violations**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act of 1995, 18 U.S.C. § 3626, does not bar the relief ordered here. The Act’s purpose is to prevent the “abuse of federal court authority in prison litigation,” but it does not strip courts of their power to remedy clear constitutional violations. The Act’s language—requiring that relief be “narrowly drawn,” “least intrusive,” and “no more extensive than necessary”—does not prohibit population reduction as a remedy when such relief is essential to eliminate unconstitutional conditions.

In *United States v. United States District Court*, 268 U.S. 150 (1925), the Court held that federal courts may issue orders that “are necessary to enforce the Constitution and the laws of the United States.” The Court emphasized that “the power of the court to issue orders necessary to enforce the law is not limited by the mere fact that the order may affect the operations of a state government.” Id. at 155. This principle applies with equal force when the constitutional violation arises from overcrowding.

The Court in *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), recognized that a state official may be enjoined from enforcing a law that violates the Constitution. The Court stated that “a state officer may be restrained from enforcing a statute which is in conflict with the Constitution.” Id. at 156. This doctrine extends to structural reforms necessary to correct systemic violations. When a state’s prison system is so overcrowded that it violates the Eighth Amendment, the court may order population reductions to restore constitutional compliance.

**III. The Order Is Narrowly Drawn and Proportionate to the Violation**

The district court’s order is narrowly tailored to correct a specific constitutional violation: the failure to provide adequate medical care due to overcrowding. The court found that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of the constitutional violations, and that reducing the population to 137.5% of design capacity would allow the state to provide constitutionally adequate care. This is not an arbitrary or excessive remedy.

In *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629 (1953), the Court held that “the remedy must be commensurate with the wrong.” The Court emphasized that “the court may not go beyond the bounds of necessity.” Id. at 633. The order here is precisely that: it is limited to the population reduction necessary to eliminate the constitutional violation. It does not mandate the release of any particular inmate, nor does it interfere with the state’s authority to determine who is eligible for release. The court’s order is thus “no more extensive than necessary,” as required by the PLRA.

**IV. The Order Is Not an “Injunction” That Violates the PLRA’s Prohibitions**

The PLRA prohibits injunctions that “prohibit the release of any person from custody.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(B). But the order here does not prohibit the release of any person. It requires the state to reduce its population to a level that allows for constitutional conditions. The court does not dictate who must be released, nor does it interfere with the state’s sentencing or parole authority. The order is not an injunction in the prohibited sense.

In *United States v. United States District Court*, 268 U.S. 150 (1925), the Court held that a court may issue an order that “compels the performance of a duty required by law.” The court’s order here compels the state to perform its constitutional duty to provide adequate medical care. It does not command the release of any individual, but rather requires the state to take steps to ensure that those who remain in custody are not subjected to cruel and unusual punishment.
