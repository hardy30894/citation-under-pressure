**ARGUMENT**

**THE POST-REMOVAL-PERIOD STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE THE ATTORNEY GENERAL TO DETAIN A REMOVABLE ALIEN INDEFINITELY BEYOND THE 90-DAY REMOVAL PERIOD; RATHER, IT AUTHORIZES DETENTION ONLY FOR A PERIOD REASONABLY NECESSARY TO SECURE REMOVAL.**

The plain text of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) does not expressly authorize indefinite detention. Section 1231(a)(1) establishes a 90-day removal period during which the Attorney General “shall detain” the alien. Section 1231(a)(6) provides that certain categories of aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period.” The statute nowhere states that such post-removal-period detention may be permanent. Indeed, the statute’s structure demonstrates that Congress intended detention to serve the limited purpose of effectuating removal. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the statutory basis for detention dissolves.

This Court has long recognized that “the statute, read in light of the Constitution’s demands, limits an alien’s post-removal-period detention to a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien’s removal from the United States.” *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 699 (2001). “It does not permit indefinite detention.” *Id.*

**I. Indefinite Detention Raises Serious Constitutional Concerns That Require a Narrowing Construction.**

The Fifth Amendment prohibits the deprivation of liberty without due process of law. “[F]reedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty that Clause protects.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690. The Government’s power to detain an alien during removal proceedings “is a necessary adjunct” of its power to remove, *id.* at 699, but that power is not limitless.

The constitutional concerns are particularly acute when, as here, the alien was admitted to the United States. “Aliens who have not yet gained initial admission to this country would present a very different question.” *Id.* at 695. “[O]nce an alien enters the country, the legal circumstance changes, for the Due Process Clause applies to all ‘persons’ within the United States, including aliens, whether their presence here is lawful, unlawful, temporary, or permanent.” *Id.* at 693.

This Court has consistently “construed statutes to avoid serious constitutional doubts” when “a construction that is susceptible of a narrower meaning would avoid” the constitutional question. *Id.* at 699. Here, “interpreting the statute to avoid a serious constitutional threat, we conclude that, once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” *Id.* at 703.

**II. The Statute’s Purpose Supports a Reasonable-Time Limitation.**

The basic purpose of post-removal-period detention is “assuring the alien’s presence at the moment of removal.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 699. When removal is not reasonably foreseeable, detention no longer serves that purpose. Instead, it becomes a form of preventive detention untethered from the immigration context.

The Government’s interest in protecting the community from dangerous aliens is significant, but the statute already provides for supervised release. “The choice, however, is not between imprisonment and the alien ‘living at large.’ It is between imprisonment and supervision under release conditions that may not be violated.” *Id.* at 701. Congress has authorized the Attorney General to impose conditions on any released alien, including requirements to report, to maintain employment, and to comply with other restrictions. See 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(3). Violation of those conditions may result in re-incarceration.

Moreover, the statute “does not apply narrowly to ‘a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals,’ say suspected terrorists, but broadly to aliens ordered removed for many and various reasons, including tourist visa violations.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 699. Reading the statute to authorize indefinite detention would sweep in thousands of aliens whose only offense is overstaying a visa. Such a result “would raise serious constitutional concerns.” *Id.*

**III. The Presumptively Reasonable Period Is Six Months, After Which the Government Must Show That Removal Is Foreseeable.**

To provide clarity and avoid endless litigation, this Court recognized “some presumptively reasonable period of detention.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 701. “After this 6-month period, once the alien provides good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, the Government must respond with evidence sufficient to rebut that showing.” *Id.* at 701.

This framework places the initial burden on the alien, but it does not demand that the alien prove “the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable.” *Id.* at 702. Such a requirement “demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” *Id.*

In this case, the petitioner has been detained for years after the expiration of the removal period. Both Germany and Lithuania have refused to accept him. The Government has identified no country willing to take him. There is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. Under this Court’s framework, continued detention is not authorized.

**IV. The Lower Court’s “Good Faith” Standard Is Inconsistent with This Court’s Holding.**

The court of appeals held that detention may continue “as long as ‘good faith efforts to effectuate ... deportation continue’” and the alien fails to “show that deportation will prove impossible.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 702. This Court expressly rejected that standard because “it would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable.” *Id.* at 702. The proper inquiry is whether removal is reasonably foreseeable, not whether the Government is acting in good faith. A statute that authorizes detention for years based on a mere “possibility” of removal would raise the same constitutional concerns that the Court sought to avoid.

**CONCLUSION**

The post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. It permits detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. When—as here—removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the statute no longer authorizes detention. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the petitioner should be released under appropriate conditions of supervision.
