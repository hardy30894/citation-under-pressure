# ARGUMENT

## THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT RECOGNIZE A "CLASS OF ONE" CAUSE OF ACTION ABSENT ALLEGATIONS OF MEMBERSHIP IN A CLASS OR GROUP

The Seventh Circuit's holding that a single property owner may state an equal protection claim by alleging nothing more than a "spiteful effort to 'get' him" cannot be squared with this Court's equal protection jurisprudence, which has always required a plaintiff to identify some class or group to which she belongs and against which the state has discriminated. The Equal Protection Clause guarantees that "[n]o State shall . . . deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. That guarantee, by its very structure, is a guarantee of equal treatment as between persons similarly situated by virtue of some shared characteristic—not a general prohibition on arbitrary government action directed at any single individual.

### A. This Court's Precedents Confirm That Equal Protection Claims Require Class-Based Discrimination

This Court has consistently analyzed equal protection claims through the lens of classification. In *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432 (1985), the Court explained that the Equal Protection Clause "is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike." *Id.* at 439. Critically, the Court's analysis proceeded by identifying the class at issue—individuals with mental disabilities—and asking whether the challenged ordinance's differential treatment of that class survived the appropriate level of scrutiny. *Id.* at 442-47. Nothing in *Cleburne* suggests that a single individual, disconnected from any class, could invoke the Clause merely by alleging that government treated him differently than someone else.

Similarly, in *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), this Court held that a plaintiff bringing an equal protection claim premised on facially neutral state action must demonstrate that the action "had a discriminatory purpose" directed at an identifiable class. *Id.* at 240. The entire discriminatory-purpose framework presupposes that some class exists that the government intended to burden because of its class characteristics. A "class of one" theory eliminates this requirement altogether, permitting courts to find an equal protection violation whenever a plaintiff subjectively believes she was treated worse than some other similarly situated individual—without any showing that this differential treatment stemmed from membership in a disfavored group.

This Court's decision in *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256 (1979), reinforces that equal protection analysis is fundamentally about classifications. The Court there stated that "[d]iscriminatory purpose . . . implies more than intent as volition or intent as awareness of consequences. It implies that the decisionmaker selected or reaffirmed a particular course of action at least in part because of its adverse effects upon an identifiable group." *Id.* at 279. The touchstone is the "identifiable group"—a concept wholly absent from a "class of one" claim.

### B. The Seventh Circuit's "Class of One" Theory Conflicts With The Rational Basis Standard's Purpose

The rational basis standard, articulated in cases such as *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), was developed specifically to govern review of classifications—distinctions drawn between groups of persons. In *Railway Express*, this Court upheld a New York traffic regulation that distinguished between vehicles advertising their own business and those carrying advertisements for other businesses, reasoning that "the local authorities may well have concluded that those who advertise their own wares on their trucks do not present the same traffic problem." *Id.* at 110. The analysis necessarily involved comparing the treatment of one class of advertisers against another.

Extending this framework to a lone individual's grievance about a single discretionary decision—here, the size of an easement demanded of one property owner—would transform every act of governmental discretion into a potential equal protection lawsuit. Zoning decisions, permit conditions, and countless other administrative judgments routinely produce different outcomes for different individuals based on case-specific facts. Under the Seventh Circuit's rule, any such difference, however explicable by legitimate case-specific considerations, becomes actionable so long as a plaintiff alleges the decisionmaker acted with subjective ill will. This approach threatens to constitutionalize garden-variety administrative and tort disputes, contrary to this Court's repeated admonition that "the Fourteenth Amendment does not deprive local governments of the general power to enact reasonable regulatory measures" simply because such measures produce disparate results in application. *See Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas*, 416 U.S. 1, 8 (1974) (upholding a zoning ordinance because "[t]he police power is not confined to elimination of filth, stench, and unhealthy places").

### C. Respondent's Allegations, At Most, State A Substantive Due Process Or State-Law Claim, Not An Equal Protection Violation

Respondent's grievance—that the Village demanded a 33-foot easement from her when it purportedly required only a 15-foot easement from others—is, at bottom, an allegation of arbitrary or irrational government action directed at a single property owner. Such claims sound in substantive due process, not equal protection. This Court has cautioned against conflating the two doctrines. In *Collins v. City of Harker Heights*, 503 U.S. 115 (1992), the Court emphasized that "the Due Process Clause was intended to prevent government from abusing its power, or employing it as an instrument of oppression," *id.* at 126, and analyzed the plaintiff's claim of arbitrary governmental action through that lens rather than equal protection.

Respondent's failure to allege membership in any class—whether defined by race, religion, or any other characteristic—means her claim does not implicate the concerns that animate equal protection doctrine: namely, the risk that government has systematically disadvantaged a discrete group. Absent such an allegation, respondent's remedy, if any, lies in state tort or contract law, or in a properly pleaded substantive due process claim demonstrating that the Village's action was wholly arbitrary and irrational in a constitutional sense—a far more demanding showing than the "spiteful effort to 'get' him" standard adopted below.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Seventh Circuit and hold that the Equal Protection Clause does not furnish a cause of action to a plaintiff who fails to allege membership in any class or group.
