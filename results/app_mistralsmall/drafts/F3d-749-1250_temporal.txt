**ARGUMENT**

**I. The admission of Agent Jones’s expert testimony regarding Stanley’s credibility violated Federal Rule of Evidence 702 and this Court’s precedents.**

Agent Jones’s testimony that Stanley’s statements were “not worthy of credence” and that his invocation of faith was indicative of deception was inadmissible under Rule 702 and *United States v. Amaral*, 488 F.2d 1148 (9th Cir. 1969). In *Amaral*, the Ninth Circuit held that expert testimony on a witness’s credibility is impermissible because it usurps the jury’s role in assessing credibility. *Id.* at 1152 (“[T]he jury is fully capable of assessing credibility without the aid of an expert.”). Agent Jones’s opinion that Stanley’s behavior was “common among the criminal element” similarly invaded the jury’s province. *See United States v. Fosher*, 354 F.2d 749, 751 (1st Cir. 1965) (excluding expert testimony that a defendant’s behavior was “typical of a guilty person”).

Further, Agent Jones’s assertion that he had “never in [his] career” seen an innocent person make a particular statement improperly bolstered the government’s case. *See United States v. Linehan*, 171 F.2d 367, 369 (7th Cir. 1948) (excluding testimony that a defendant’s conduct was “inconsistent with innocence”). Such testimony impermissibly suggested that Agent Jones’s subjective experience could determine Stanley’s guilt or innocence, a role reserved for the jury. *See id.*

Because Stanley did not contemporaneously object, plain error review applies. *See United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152 (1982) (applying plain error where no objection was made). The admission of this testimony was plain error under Rule 702 and *Amaral*, as it misled the jury and deprived Stanley of a fair trial.

**II. The district court erred in denying Stanley’s motion for a judgment of acquittal because the evidence was insufficient to prove he was a principal in the robbery.**

The government’s evidence established only that Stanley was present at the East Pine residence and shared familial ties with the actual robbers. *See United States v. Stromberg*, 224 F.2d 589, 591 (2d Cir. 1955) (requiring more than mere presence to sustain a conviction). The tracking device’s location and the officers’ observations did not link Stanley to the robbery itself. *See Stromberg*, 224 F.2d at 591 (“Mere association with criminals is not enough.”).

The government’s theory that Stanley acted as a getaway driver rested on speculation. *See United States v. Becker*, 160 F.2d 739, 741 (7th Cir. 1947) (insufficient evidence where the government’s case was “based on conjecture”). Without direct or circumstantial evidence connecting Stanley to the robbery, the jury’s verdict was unsupported by the record. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Stanley’s conviction should be reversed. The admission of Agent Jones’s impermissible credibility testimony and the insufficiency of the evidence warrant vacatur of the judgment.
