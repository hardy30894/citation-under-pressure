The district court erred in holding that the releases bar appellants’ WARN Act claims. The Act mandates that covered employers provide sixty days’ written notice before a plant closing or mass layoff. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). An employer may instead provide pay in lieu of notice, but only if the payment equals the full amount the employee would have received during the notice period. 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(1)–(2); see also North Star Steel Co. v. Thomas, 515 U.S. 29, 32–33 (1995). DHL’s agreements supplied at most ten weeks of pay—far short of the statutory minimum for employees whose employment ended before the sixty-day period expired—and simultaneously extracted a release of the very claim the statute creates.

Releases of federal statutory rights are enforceable only when they are knowing and voluntary. See, e.g., Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Co., 415 U.S. 36, 52 n.15 (1974) (Title VII); Pierce v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Ry. Co., 65 F.3d 562, 570–71 (7th Cir. 1995) (ADEA). The same standard governs WARN claims. See, e.g., Local 1239, Int’l Bhd. of Teamsters v. Allstates Transp. LLC, 2006 WL 695454, at *4 (N.D. Ill. Mar. 15, 2006). Here, the releases were executed under severe time pressure: drivers had two days to elect the ten-week package and simultaneously submit rebid forms, while other employees received at most thirteen days. Such abbreviated consideration periods preclude a finding of voluntariness. See, e.g., Wagner v. NutraSweet Co., 95 F.3d 527, 532 (7th Cir. 1996).

Moreover, a union may not waive individual employees’ statutory rights under the WARN Act through collective bargaining. The Act confers a private right of action on “aggrieved employee[s],” 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(5), and its legislative history confirms that the right is individual, not collective. See United Steelworkers of Am. v. North Star Steel Co., 5 F.3d 39, 43 (3d Cir. 1993). The Teamsters therefore lacked authority to bargain away appellants’ claims.

Because the releases are invalid and DHL concededly failed to provide timely notice or equivalent pay, summary judgment was improper. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for calculation of damages under 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a).
