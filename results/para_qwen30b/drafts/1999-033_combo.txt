**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not afford a cause of action to a plaintiff who alleges only that a governmental entity acted out of personal spite or animus toward him, without alleging membership in a protected class or that similarly situated individuals were treated differently. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that a “class of one” claim may be maintained under the Equal Protection Clause when the plaintiff asserts that the government’s action was motivated solely by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” Such a theory, if adopted, would eviscerate the foundational requirement of equal protection jurisprudence: that the plaintiff must demonstrate a classification based on a group characteristic, not mere individualized animus.

The principle that equal protection requires a showing of discriminatory treatment based on membership in a class is deeply rooted in this Court’s precedent. In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), the Court held that the Equal Protection Clause is violated when a law is applied in a discriminatory manner against a particular group, even if the law on its face is neutral. The Court emphasized that “the law, in its nakedness, is colorless,” but its application may be “invidious” when it “falls with unequal weight upon different classes of persons.” Id. at 370. The case involved the denial of a permit to operate a laundry in San Francisco, which was granted to white applicants but denied to Chinese applicants. The Court concluded that the law’s application, not its text, determined whether equal protection had been violated. This principle underscores that equal protection is not triggered by individualized grievances, but by the differential treatment of individuals based on a shared characteristic.

Similarly, in *Strauder v. West Virginia*, 100 U.S. 303 (1879), the Court struck down a state law that excluded Black men from jury service, holding that such exclusion violated the Equal Protection Clause. The Court stated: “The Constitution of the United States, in the Fourteenth Amendment, declares that no State shall deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” Id. at 307. The decision rested on the fact that the law created a classification based on race, a suspect category, and subjected a group of individuals to unequal treatment. The Court did not consider whether the individual plaintiff had been singled out for personal reasons; rather, it focused on the existence of a class-based distinction.

The same principle applies in *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), where the Court upheld a Louisiana law requiring racial segregation in rail cars. Although the Court upheld the law, it did so only because it found that the law did not create a classification that was inherently unequal. The Court stated: “The object of the amendment was undoubtedly to enforce the absolute equality of the two races before the law.” Id. at 559. The case illustrates that the Equal Protection Clause is concerned with classifications, not individualized treatment. If the law had been applied to one Black man solely because of personal animosity, the Court would have had no occasion to address the issue, because the law would not have created a class.

In *Buchanan v. Warley*, 245 U.S. 60 (1917), the Court invalidated a city ordinance that prohibited Black persons from occupying homes in white neighborhoods. The Court held that the ordinance violated the Equal Protection Clause because it “discriminated against a class of persons” based on race. Id. at 63. The Court emphasized that “the right to acquire, possess, and protect property is a fundamental right,” and that the law “denied to a class of persons the right to use and enjoy their property.” Id. at 64. Again, the focus was on the classification of persons based on race, not on individualized animus.

The principle that equal protection requires a class-based inquiry was reaffirmed in *Meyer v. Nebraska*, 262 U.S. 390 (1923), where the Court struck down a state law banning the teaching of foreign languages in schools. The Court held that the law “infringes upon the liberty of the individual to teach and to learn,” but more importantly, it noted that the law “discriminates against a class of persons” by targeting foreign-language instruction. Id. at 399. The Court did not consider whether the law was applied to one teacher out of spite; rather, it focused on the law’s classification of educational content.

In *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542 (1875), the Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment protects individuals from state action that denies equal protection, but not from private acts. The Court stated: “The amendment prohibits the states from denying to any person the equal protection of the laws.” Id. at 553. The case involved the suppression of a Black political rally by a mob, and the Court ruled that the federal government could not intervene under the Fourteenth Amendment because the violation was not by a state actor. The decision underscores that the Equal Protection Clause applies only when the state, through its laws or actions, treats individuals differently based on a class.

Finally, in *Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36 (1873), the Court interpreted the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause as protecting the rights of citizens against state action that discriminates against them. The Court stated: “The privileges and immunities of citizens of the United States are those which belong to them as citizens of the nation.” Id. at 72. The case involved a Louisiana law that granted a monopoly to a single slaughterhouse, which the Court held violated the Equal Protection Clause because it created a class of citizens who were denied the right to engage in a lawful occupation. The Court did not consider whether the law was applied to one man out of spite; it focused on the creation of a class-based restriction.

The Court of Appeals’ decision in this case, which permits a claim based solely on personal animus, contradicts these foundational precedents.
