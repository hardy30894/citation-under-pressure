**ARGUMENT**

### I. The district court erroneously truncated Moll’s hostile-work-environment claim by isolating only the most explicitly sexual allegations.

A plaintiff opposing summary judgment on a hostile-work-environment claim is entitled to have the court consider the “totality of the circumstances,” not only incidents that are themselves overtly sexual. *Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 23 (1993); *Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986). In this Circuit, sex-based harassment may be shown through facially neutral conduct when the record permits an inference that the conduct was motivated by sex. *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 374–75 (2d Cir. 2002). As *Alfano* explains, “facially neutral incidents may be included, of course, among the ‘totality of the circumstances’ that courts consider in any hostile work environment claim.” *Id.* at 375.

That rule controls here. Moll did not allege only a handful of sexually explicit incidents; she alleged a sustained pattern of sex-based exclusion, differential treatment, and humiliation spanning years. The record includes Irving’s inappropriate notes, repeated nighttime calls to her hotel room, the note stating he thought about her while showering, restrictions on communicating with him by email or telephone, denial of promotion consideration while male colleagues were promoted, denial of work-from-home flexibility while male counterparts continued to receive it, denial of vacation when less-tenured men were granted it, and exclusion of women from work-related social events. Taken together, these facts permit a reasonable inference that Moll was “treated less favorably than male employees because of her sex.” *Back v. Hastings on Hudson Union Free School District*, 365 F.3d 107, 119 (2d Cir. 2004).

The district court instead limited its analysis to whether Moll alleged “sexually offensive acts” within the limitations period. That was legal error. A hostile-work-environment claim is not parsed act-by-act; the question is whether the workplace was “permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult” sufficient to alter the terms and conditions of employment. *Harris*, 510 U.S. at 21. Conduct outside the limitations period may also be considered so long as at least one component act occurred within the filing period. *National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Morgan*, 536 U.S. 101, 117 (2002). And facially neutral acts can support the claim where, as here, they are part of the same hostile pattern. *Alfano*, 294 F.3d at 375.

Because the district court failed to assess Moll’s allegations in their totality, the grant of summary judgment on the hostile-environment claim cannot stand. *See* *Patane v. Clark*, 508 F.3d 106, 113–14 (2d Cir. 2007) (reversing dismissal where plaintiff alleged a pattern of sex-based mistreatment supporting hostile-environment claim).

### II. The district court also erred in granting summary judgment on Moll’s disparate-treatment and retaliation claims.

Summary judgment is inappropriate where the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmovant, permits a reasonable inference of discriminatory or retaliatory motive. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986); *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150–51 (2000). Under *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802–04 (1973), a plaintiff need only raise an inference of discrimination at the prima facie stage; the burden then shifts to the employer to articulate a legitimate reason, and the plaintiff may prevail by showing pretext. *Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981).

Moll adduced ample evidence of disparate treatment: she was the lowest-paid Sales Engineer in the Buffalo office; male comparators were promoted while she was not; men were permitted to work from home and receive vacation she was denied; and women were excluded from social opportunities tied to advancement. Such evidence is more than sufficient to create a triable issue of discriminatory intent. *Bickerstaff v. Vassar College*, 196 F.3d 435, 450–51 (2d Cir. 1999); *Tomka v. Seiler Corp.*, 66 F.3d 1295, 1317–18 (2d Cir. 1995), abrogated on other grounds by *Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742 (1998).

Moll also presented a classic retaliation case. She complained internally and to the EEOC about sex discrimination and harassment; thereafter Verizon transferred the Sales Engineers, imposed mandatory reporting restrictions, and forced her into an untenable choice between relocation, resignation, or severance. Title VII retaliation is established where the plaintiff shows protected activity, employer knowledge, a materially adverse action, and causation. *Crawford v. Metropolitan Government of Nashville & Davidson County*, 555 U.S. 271, 276 (2009); *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006); *Jute v. Hamilton Sundstrand Corp.*, 420 F.3d 166, 173 (2d Cir. 2005). The transfer and its conditions would deter a reasonable employee from complaining further. *Burlington Northern*, 548 U.S. at 68. At minimum, the temporal and circumstantial evidence here creates a jury question. *Jute*, 420 F.3d at 173.

### III. The district court should not have disregarded the later testimony of a non-party witness under the “sham issue of fact” doctrine.

A district court may disregard a party’s affidavit that merely contradicts prior sworn testimony to create a feigned factual dispute. *Perma Research & Development Co. v. Singer Co.*, 410 F.2d 572, 578 (2d Cir. 1969); *Hayes v. New York City Department of Corrections*, 84 F.3d 614, 619 (2d Cir. 1996). But that doctrine is narrow. It is designed to prevent a litigant from manufacturing a dispute through self-contradiction; it does not compel the court to disregard a non-party witness’s later testimony simply because it conflicts with an earlier statement. *See* *Jeffreys v. City of New York*, 426 F.3d 549, 554 (2d Cir. 2005); *Hayes*, 84 F.3d at 619.

If the district court excluded or discounted non-party testimony on that basis, it erred. Credibility conflicts are for the jury, not the court on summary judgment. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255; *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 150.

For these reasons, this Court should vacate the judgment insofar as it granted summary judgment to Verizon and remand for trial.
