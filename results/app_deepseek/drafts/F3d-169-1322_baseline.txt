ARGUMENT

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to Little Rock and Hall on the “Utilization” Claim Under 29 U.S.C. § 1842.**

The AWPA requires any person who “utilizes” the services of a farm labor contractor (FLC) to verify that the contractor is registered and insured. 29 U.S.C. § 1842(b). The term “utilize” must be construed broadly to effectuate the statute’s remedial purpose of protecting migrant workers. *See Caro-Galvan v. Curtis Richardson, Inc.*, 993 F.2d 1500, 1505 (11th Cir. 1993) (AWPA should be interpreted broadly to achieve its protective goals). Here, Little Rock and Hall did not merely sell seeds or buy produce; they actively subsidized the crop, provided boxes and transport trailers, and directed the Burtons to obtain labor for the harvest. These actions demonstrate that Little Rock and Hall “utilized” the FLC’s services within the meaning of § 1842(b).

This Court has not defined “utilize” in a vacuum. Other circuits hold that a party “utilizes” an FLC when it obtains the benefit of the FLC’s labor, even if it does not directly hire or supervise the workers. *See, e.g., Solis v. Laurelbrook Sanitarium & Sch., Inc.*, 642 F.3d 518, 528 (6th Cir. 2011) (defining “utilize” as “to make use of” or “to employ for a purpose”). Little Rock required the Burtons to fertilize the snap bean crop and obtain labor; the FLC’s workers picked beans that Little Rock bought and sold. Little Rock provided the boxes the workers used and the trailer that hauled the harvest. Hall, as president, orchestrated this arrangement. By structuring the contract to require the Burtons to hire an FLC—and then taking the harvest as profit—Little Rock and Hall made “use of” the FLC’s services. The district court erred by imposing a narrow, direct-control requirement that § 1842 does not contain.

**II. The District Court Properly Found the Burtons Were Not Joint Employers, But It Erred in Holding That the Workers Cannot Recover Actual Damages for the Burtons’ Independent Violations of the AWPA’s Registration and Insurance Provisions.**

The district court correctly concluded that the Burtons did not “employ” the workers under the AWPA or the FLSA. *See Charles v. Burton*, 857 F. Supp. 1574, 1583 (M.D. Ga. 1994). Applying the “economic reality” test from *Caro-Galvan*, 993 F.2d at 1505, factors such as the Burtons’ limited supervision, lack of control over wages, and the FLC’s independent operation support this finding. That portion of the judgment should be affirmed.

However, the district court then erroneously held that, because the Burtons were not employers, the workers could not recover actual damages for the Burtons’ failure to verify Luxama’s registration and insurance under §§ 1841(b) and 1842. The AWPA imposes duties on *any person* who utilizes an FLC, not solely employers. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 1842(b) (“[a] person utilizing the services of a farm labor contractor” must verify registration). The Burtons conceded they utilized Luxama’s services. The district court expressly found that the Burtons “violated the registration verification provisions under 29 U.S.C. § 1842.” This independent duty does not depend on an employer-employee relationship. *See, e.g., Reyes v. Quality Sols.*, 838 F.3d 1152, 1157 (11th Cir. 2016) (recognizing that AWPA creates duties separate from the employment relationship). When a person utilizes an unregistered, uninsured FLC, and workers are injured as a result, the AWPA permits recovery of actual damages proximately caused by that violation. 29 U.S.C. § 1854(a), (c)(1). Here, the Burtons’ failure to verify Luxama’s lapsed registration and insurance directly contributed to the fatal truck accident. The district court therefore erred in precluding the workers from seeking actual damages for these proven statutory violations.

**III. The District Court Erred in Limiting the Workers to Statutory Damages Only.**

Even if the Burtons were not joint employers, the district court’s holding that the workers may recover only statutory damages under § 1854(c)(1) ignores the statute’s plain language. Section 1854(c)(1) provides for statutory damages when a violation occurs, but subsection (a) allows for actual damages. The two remedies are not mutually exclusive; a plaintiff may recover both. *See, e.g., Calderon v. Presidio Valley Farmers Ass’n*, 863 F.2d 384, 390 (5th Cir. 1989) (holding that AWPA provides for actual and statutory damages). Where, as here, the Burtons’ failure to verify registration led directly to the use of an uninsured vehicle causing death and serious injury, actual damages are manifestly appropriate. The district court’s contrary ruling misapplies the statute and should be reversed.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, this Court should affirm the district court’s holding that the Burtons were not joint employers, but reverse the summary judgment in favor of Little Rock and Hall on the utilization claim and the limitation of damages against the Burtons.
