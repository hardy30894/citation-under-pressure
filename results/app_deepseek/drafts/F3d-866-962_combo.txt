**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine disputes of material fact preclude a finding that BNSF did not retaliate against Appellants for engaging in protected activity under the Federal Railroad Safety Act. The record reveals that Appellants reported unsafe conditions at the bridge—specifically, the lack of side rails and lighting—to BNSF officials immediately after the accident. Such reports constitute protected activity under 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). BNSF then subjected Appellants to increased operations testing, formal discipline, and, in Foster’s case, termination. These adverse actions followed closely on the heels of Appellants’ safety complaints, and the proffered reasons for the discipline are contradicted by evidence of pretext.

**I. Appellants Engaged in Protected Activity**

The FRSA protects employees who report hazardous safety conditions. Appellants’ written and recorded statements to Trainmaster Dixon and Claims Representative Murphy explicitly noted the absence of handrails and lighting on the bridge—conditions that created a risk of serious injury. Such reports are precisely the kind of “good faith” safety complaints Congress intended to shield from employer retaliation. *See NLRB v. Washington Aluminum Co.*, 370 U.S. 9, 14 (1962) (holding that employees’ concerted complaints about unsafe working conditions are protected). The fact that Appellants also described the accident does not negate the protected nature of their safety concerns. *Cf. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 49 (1937) (concerted activities for mutual aid or protection are protected).

**II. BNSF Took Adverse Actions Against Appellants**

BNSF’s response to Appellants’ safety reports was swift and punitive. Within days, BNSF initiated an investigation and subjected Appellants to increased operations testing—unannounced observations that created a hostile work environment. Kline and Snyder testified that they felt compelled to avoid inexperienced coworkers and accepted less lucrative assignments out of fear. These actions constitute adverse employment actions. *See Mitchell v. DeMario Jewelry*, 361 U.S. 288, 292 (1960) (employer’s retaliatory conduct need not be discharge; any discrimination that discourages protected activity is unlawful). Moreover, BNSF disciplined all three Appellants: Snyder received a formal reprimand and a one-year review period; Kline received a thirty-day record suspension and a one-year review period; Foster was fired. The Public Law Board later reversed or modified these penalties, but the fact remains that BNSF imposed them in the first instance.

**III. A Causal Connection Exists Between the Protected Activity and the Adverse Actions**

Temporal proximity alone can establish causation. Appellants reported unsafe conditions on April 2 and 5, 2012. BNSF began its investigation on April 6 and imposed discipline within months. This close timing supports an inference of retaliation. *See NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 347 (1938) (discriminatory motive may be inferred from timing and surrounding circumstances). Additionally, BNSF’s stated reasons for the discipline—alleged rule infractions—are undermined by evidence that the crew-change location was disputed. BNSF officials Knutstrom and Dixon testified that crews were orally instructed to change east of the bridge, but Appellants and other crewmembers testified that they were told to stop west of the bridge. This factual dispute goes directly to whether the infractions actually occurred or were pretextual. *See NLRB v. Burnup & Sims, Inc.*, 379 U.S. 21, 23–24 (1964) (employer’s honest belief in misconduct is no defense if the belief is based on false accusations and the employee was actually engaged in protected activity).

**IV. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because Motive and Credibility Are at Issue**

Where, as here, the employer’s motive is central to the claim, summary judgment is rarely appropriate. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (“Summary judgment should be used sparingly in complex antitrust litigation where motive and intent play leading roles.”). The same principle applies in retaliation cases. Credibility determinations and the weighing of conflicting testimony are for the jury, not the court. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 628 (1944) (credibility of witnesses is a question for the trier of fact). Here, the district court improperly resolved disputed facts—such as whether BNSF’s increased testing was routine or retaliatory, and whether the discipline was based on actual rule violations or on Appellants’ safety complaints. *See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951) (the substantial evidence standard requires consideration of all evidence, including that which detracts from the agency’s finding).

**V. BNSF’s Proffered Reasons Are Pretextual**

The record contains ample evidence that BNSF’s stated reasons for the discipline were pretextual. First, the investigating officer relied on testimony from Dixon and Knutstrom, who were sequestered but whose accounts conflicted with every crewmember’s testimony. Second, BNSF subjected Appellants to increased testing only after they reported unsafe conditions, yet offered no explanation for this change in practice. Third, the Public Law Board’s reversal of the discipline—reinstating Foster, removing Snyder’s reprimand, and removing Kline’s suspension—further suggests that BNSF’s penalties were unwarranted. *See Steele v. Louisville & N.R. Co.*, 323 U.S. 192, 204 (1944) (an employer may not use its disciplinary authority to retaliate against employees who assert their rights). Finally, BNSF’s failure to discipline the outbound crewmember who actually fell off the bridge—while punishing those who reported the unsafe conditions—is itself indicative of retaliatory intent. *Cf. Texas & N.O.R. Co. v. Brotherhood of Ry. & S.S. Clerks*, 281 U.S. 548, 569 (1930) (employer’s discriminatory treatment of employees who engage in protected activity violates the law).

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the district court’s grant of summary judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for trial.
