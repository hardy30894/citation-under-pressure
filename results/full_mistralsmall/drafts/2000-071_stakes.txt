**ARGUMENT**

I. MILFORD’S DENIAL OF THE GOOD NEWS CLUB’S REQUEST WAS CONSTITUTIONAL SUBJECT DISCRIMINATION, NOT UNCONSTITUTIONAL VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION.

The Second Circuit correctly held that Milford permissibly excluded the Good News Club’s proposed activities because they were religious in subject matter, not because of any disfavored viewpoint. *See Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) (a public forum’s “policy of excluding certain speech based on the content of that speech is not unconstitutional if the regulation is reasonable and viewpoint neutral”); *Rosenberger v. University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (“Viewpoint discrimination is thus an egregious form of content discrimination.”). Milford’s policy expressly barred “religious purposes,” and the Club’s proposed activities—singing hymns, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying—plainly fell within that prohibition. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993) (distinguishing between speech that is “religious in subject matter” and speech that is “religious in viewpoint”).

The Club’s reliance on *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), is misplaced. In *Widmar*, the Court held that a university’s open forum could not exclude a student religious group because the exclusion was based on the group’s religious *viewpoint*, not its religious *subject matter*. *Id.* at 276. Here, by contrast, Milford’s policy excluded the Club’s activities because they were religious in *subject matter*—i.e., worship and religious instruction—not because Milford disfavored the Club’s religious *viewpoint*. *See Good News/Good Sports Club v. School Dist. of the City of Ladue*, 28 F.3d 1501, 1506 (8th Cir. 1994) (“The distinction between subject matter and viewpoint discrimination is critical.”).

II. MILFORD’S POLICY WAS NEUTRAL AND REASONABLE, AND IT DID NOT TARGET RELIGION.

Milford’s policy was viewpoint neutral because it excluded *all* religious activities, not just those with which the school disagreed. *See Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829 (“A regulation that serves purposes unrelated to the content of expression is deemed neutral, even if it has an incidental effect on some speakers or messages.”). The policy applied equally to all groups, religious and secular alike, and Milford permitted numerous secular groups—including the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club—to use its facilities. *See Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806 (a policy is viewpoint neutral if it “applies to all speech irrespective of content”).

Milford’s policy was also reasonable. The school sought to avoid violating the Establishment Clause by allowing religious worship on its premises. *See Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271 n.11 (“[T]he University could constitutionally exclude every form of religious worship.”). The Club’s proposed activities—prayer, scripture memorization, and evangelical instruction—were indistinguishable from religious worship. *See id.* at 268 (describing similar activities as “religious worship and discussion”); *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395 (noting that prayer and religious teaching are “quintessentially religious” activities). Milford’s policy was thus a permissible effort to avoid state endorsement of religion. *See Agostini v. Felton*, 521 U.S. 203, 234 (1997) (“[T]he Court has been particularly vigilant in monitoring compliance with the Establishment Clause in elementary and secondary schools.”).

III. THE SECOND CIRCUIT CORRECTLY REJECTED THE CLUB’S ATTEMPT TO RECAST ITS ACTIVITIES AS SECULAR MORAL INSTRUCTION.

The Club’s attempt to recharacterize its activities as “moral and character development” is unavailing. *See Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 512 (2d Cir. 2000) (“The Club’s activities are quintessentially religious, not merely discussions of morals from a religious viewpoint.”). The Club’s own materials and lesson plans demonstrate that its primary purpose was religious instruction and evangelism, not secular moral development. *See id.* at 507-08 (describing the Club’s activities, including prayers, Bible lessons, and an invitation to accept Jesus as Savior). The Club cannot evade Milford’s policy by relabeling its activities. *See Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831 (“The dissent’s premise that all speech is subject to characterization into secular and religious categories is unsupported and contrary to our precedents.”).

The Club’s reliance on *Board of Educ. of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), is misplaced. In *Mergens*, the Court held that a student-initiated religious club could meet on the same terms as other noncurriculum student groups because the club’s activities were “religious in *viewpoint*, not in *subject matter*.” *Id.* at 250 (plurality opinion). Here, by contrast, the Club’s activities were religious in *subject matter*—i.e., worship and religious instruction—and Milford permissibly excluded them under its neutral policy. *See Good News/Good Sports Club*, 28 F.3d at 1506-07 (distinguishing *Mergens* on this basis).

IV. MILFORD’S CONCERN ABOUT THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE WAS A LAWFUL BASIS FOR ITS POLICY.

Milford permissibly relied on the Establishment Clause to justify its policy. *See Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271 n.11 (“The University could constitutionally exclude every form of religious worship.”); *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395 (“[T]he school district could have reasonably concluded that the proposed use—prayer services and religious worship—would be inconsistent with the school’s obligation to maintain neutrality toward religion.”). The Club’s proposed activities—prayer, scripture memorization, and evangelical instruction—would have sent a message of state endorsement of religion, in violation of the Establishment Clause. *See Agostini*, 521 U.S. at 234 (“[T]he Court has been particularly vigilant in monitoring compliance with the Establishment Clause in elementary and secondary schools.”).

The Club’s argument that Milford’s policy was an unconstitutional attempt to suppress religion is meritless. *See Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 828 (“The government must abstain from regulating speech when the specific motivating ideology or the opinion or perspective of the speaker is the rationale for the restriction.”). Milford’s policy was neutral and applied equally to all religious activities, not just those with which the school disagreed. *See Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Milford’s denial of the Good News Club’s request was constitutional subject discrimination, not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. Milford’s policy was neutral and reasonable, and its concern about violating the Establishment Clause was a lawful basis for its decision. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.
