**ARGUMENT**

I. The Case Is Not Moot Because A Claim For Nominal Damages Preserves A Live Article III Controversy.

A case becomes moot only when it is “impossible for a court to grant any effectual relief whatever to the prevailing party.” *Chafin v. Chafin*, 568 U.S. 165, 172 (2013). That standard is not met here. The Court has long recognized that “a request for nominal damages satisfies the redressability element of standing where a plaintiff’s claim is based on a completed violation of a legal right.” *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978). Nominal damages are not a technical afterthought; they provide a concrete remedy for the past infringement of constitutional rights. Id. at 266–67.

That principle controls even when the challenged policy has been changed. In *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 190 (2000), the Court held that voluntary cessation does not moot a case unless it is “absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” A defendant’s unilateral policy revision during litigation does not eliminate the judicial power to adjudicate whether the prior policy was unconstitutional and to award nominal damages for that violation. *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 n.10 (1982).

Respondents rely on mootness because the university revised its speech policy after suit was filed. But that change does not erase the alleged past constitutional injury. The Court has repeatedly treated completed First Amendment violations as justiciable notwithstanding the absence of ongoing prospective relief. *Buckhannon Board & Care Home, Inc. v. West Virginia Department of Health & Human Resources*, 532 U.S. 598, 608–09 (2001), distinguished between prevailing on the merits and obtaining fees, but it did not suggest that nominal damages disappear when prospective relief becomes unavailable. To the contrary, *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*, 592 U.S. 279, 285–86 (2021), makes clear that “a request for nominal damages satisfies the redressability element necessary for Article III standing where a plaintiff’s claim is based on a completed violation of a legal right.”

II. The Lower Court Erred In Treating The Policy Revision As Erasing The Past Injury.

The Eleventh Circuit’s view that nominal damages cannot save an otherwise moot case conflicts with basic mootness doctrine. A defendant bears a “heavy burden” to show mootness through voluntary cessation. *Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Slater*, 528 U.S. 216, 222 (2000) (per curiam). That burden is especially difficult where, as here, the challenged policy change came only after suit was filed and after the defendant had every incentive to avoid an adverse judgment. *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013).

More fundamentally, a nominal-damages claim itself supplies the requisite redress. The Court in *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266, explained that nominal damages are appropriate “to vindicate” rights whose violation caused no provable compensatory loss. That is precisely this case: respondents allegedly infringed petitioners’ First Amendment rights by enforcing the former speech-zone policy. Once that completed injury occurred, later policy revision could not retroactively eliminate it. See *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 308 n.11 (1986) (“Nominal damages, and not damages based on some undefinable ‘value’ of infringed rights, are the appropriate means of vindicating rights whose deprivation has not caused actual, provable injury.”).

III. Respondents’ Remaining Mootness Arguments Fail.

Respondents may argue that declaratory and injunctive relief are moot. Even if that were so, the presence of a viable nominal-damages claim preserves the case. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969), held that a claim for damages can keep a controversy alive after prospective claims have become moot. Likewise, *Super Tire Engineering Co. v. McCorkle*, 416 U.S. 115, 122–23 (1974), recognized that a dispute is not moot where “the parties retain a concrete interest in the outcome” of the damages claim.

Nor does the fact that petitioners seek only nominal damages defeat jurisdiction. The Court has never required a plaintiff to show compensatory injury to obtain nominal relief for a constitutional violation. *Uzuegbunam*, 592 U.S. at 286–87. To the contrary, nominal damages are “the damages award appropriate for an injury that is suffered but not quantified.” Id. at 288. That is enough.

IV. Because The Case Is Not Moot, The Judgment Should Be Reversed.

The Constitution does not allow a defendant to immunize a completed constitutional violation by changing policy midstream. The proper course is to adjudicate the merits of the past injury and, if petitioners prevail, award nominal damages. *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266; *Uzuegbunam*, 592 U.S. at 285–86. The judgment dismissing this case as moot should therefore be reversed.
