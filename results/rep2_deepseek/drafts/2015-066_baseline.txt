**ARGUMENT**

**I. THIS COURT’S DECISION IN *NICHOLS V. UNITED STATES* AUTHORIZES THE USE OF UNCounseled Tribal Court Convictions As Predicate Offenses Under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).**

In *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 746–47 (1994), this Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid under the Sixth Amendment because no term of imprisonment was imposed, may be used to enhance a sentence for a subsequent offense. The logic of *Nichols* compels the same result here: the tribal court convictions at issue are constitutionally valid because the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts. *See United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323–24 (1978) (affirming “the sovereign power to punish tribal offenders” as retained tribal sovereignty). Since these convictions are valid, they may serve as predicate offenses under § 117(a).

The Ninth Circuit held otherwise, reasoning that *Nichols* concerned sentencing, not the establishment of an element of an offense. That distinction is irrelevant. Under *Nichols*, the crucial inquiry is whether the prior conviction is “reliable.” *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747. The Court in *Nichols* approved the use of uncounseled convictions at sentencing because the sentencing court could consider their reliability. *Id.* at 747–48. Similarly, a jury in a § 117(a) prosecution may weigh the reliability of tribal court convictions, which are valid as a matter of law. This Court has consistently held that recidivist statutes “penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” *Id.* at 747. Thus, the predicate convictions serve only to establish the defendant’s status, not to prove guilt for the current offense.

**II. THE SIXTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT APPLY TO TRIBAL COURT PROCEEDINGS.**

The Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”), 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1303, not the Sixth Amendment, governs tribal court proceedings. This Court has made clear that the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes. *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). The Ninth Circuit erred by imposing a Sixth Amendment coextensive right to counsel on tribal courts, thereby ignoring Congress’s deliberate choice in ICRA to provide a right to counsel that is not identical to the Sixth Amendment.

Applying the Sixth Amendment to tribal courts would undermine tribal sovereignty and comity. *See Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) (cautioning against “intrud[ing] needlessly on tribal self-government”). The Ninth Circuit’s holding that tribal court convictions are “constitutionally infirm” unless they guarantee a Sixth Amendment-compliant right to counsel is an impermissible extension of federal judicial power into tribal affairs. *United States v. Bryant*, 769 F.3d 671, 677 (9th Cir. 2014).

**III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION CONFLICTS WITH THE EIGHTH AND TENTH CIRCUITS AND FRUSTRATES CONGRESSIONAL INTENT.**

The Eighth and Tenth Circuits have held that valid tribal court convictions may be used as predicates under § 117(a). *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997–98 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary ruling creates an unwarranted split, leaving victims of domestic violence on tribal lands—who suffer rape and assault at rates 2.5 times the national average—without the protection Congress intended.

Congress enacted § 117(a) to address the crisis of domestic violence in Indian country by providing felony-level punishment for repeat offenders. The Ninth Circuit’s decision frustrates this purpose by exempting a class of prior convictions from the statute, even when those convictions were obtained through valid tribal court proceedings.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Ninth Circuit and hold that uncounseled tribal court convictions may serve as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).
