**ARGUMENT**

The National Labor Relations Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employer polling is irrational, inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act, and unsupported by substantial evidence. The Board’s rigid requirement that an employer must possess “objective indications” sufficient to raise a “reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status before conducting a poll renders the practice functionally obsolete and contradicts long-standing precedent recognizing polling as a legitimate, non-coercive tool for employers to test employee sentiment. The Court should reject the Board’s standard and enforce the employer’s right to conduct a poll when it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support—evidence that, in this case, was overwhelming and unrefuted.

The Board’s standard is irrational because it creates a paradox: an employer may not poll employees to test the union’s majority status unless it already has sufficient evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition. *See* Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41 (1987) (recognizing the rebuttable presumption of continuing majority status). But if the employer already has such evidence, the poll serves no purpose. The Board’s own rationale admits this: polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling,” *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), yet it permits polling only when the employer already has a basis to withdraw recognition. This circular logic renders polling a legal fiction. As the Fifth Circuit observed, “If the employer already has sufficient evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition, why conduct a poll?” *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1139, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981) (quoting *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967)). The Board’s standard thus undermines the very purpose of polling: to obtain more precise information before acting.

The Board’s standard is also inconsistent with the Act’s purpose. Section 8(a)(1) prohibits coercive conduct, but does not prohibit employers from seeking information about employee sentiment. *See* NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575, 614 (1969). The Act protects the right to self-organization, but not the right to perpetual, unchallenged majority status. *See* NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 37 (1937). The presumption of continuing majority support is rebuttable, *see* *Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 37–41, and the employer has a right to test that presumption. The Board’s standard, however, effectively denies that right unless the employer already has conclusive evidence—evidence that, by definition, would make the poll unnecessary.

The Board’s standard is further undermined by its own precedent. In *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967), the Board held that an employer may conduct a poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. *Id.* at 1063. The Board has consistently applied this standard in subsequent decisions. *See* *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978) (holding that an employer may rely on employee statements made during interviews, even if the employer has indicated a non-union stance, so long as the statements are substantial and objective). The Board’s current standard, which requires a higher threshold than that for withdrawal of recognition, is a departure from its own settled doctrine and lacks any statutory or constitutional basis.

The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. The ALJ and the Board rejected the statements of seven employees who expressed opposition to the union. But the Board’s rejection of these statements was arbitrary. *See* *NLRB v. American National Ins. Co.*, 343 U.S. 395, 400 (1952) (holding that the Board’s findings must be supported by substantial evidence). Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack were employees in the bargaining unit on January 25, 1991—the date of the company’s letter refusing recognition. Their statements were contemporaneous and directly relevant. The Board’s refusal to count them, without explanation, violates *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 131 (1944), which requires the Board to consider all relevant evidence.

The Board also rejected the statements of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh. Ridgick stated that “as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union.” This is not equivocal; it is a clear expression of opposition to union representation. *See* *NLRB v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 370 (1948) (holding that statements indicating a desire to be free of union representation are relevant). Marsh stated he was paying $35 a month in dues and “not getting his money’s worth.” This is not mere dissatisfaction with union services; it is a direct repudiation of the union’s value. *See* *NLRB v. W. J. L. Corp.*, 333 U.S. 364, 370 (1948) (holding that expressions of dissatisfaction with union performance are relevant to majority status). The Board’s refusal to credit these statements is arbitrary and capricious.
