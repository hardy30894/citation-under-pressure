## ARGUMENT

### A. The District Court's Supplemental Jury Instruction on "Shielding" was Erroneous

The district court's definition of "shielding" was too vague and too broad, and thus, erroneous. The term "shielding" is not defined in 8 U.S.C. § 1324. However, several other circuits have adopted a definition that requires "substantial" or "material" efforts to prevent detection by the government. See United States v. DeJesus-Batres, 410 F.3d 915, 919 (7th Cir. 2005) (citing with approval the Ninth Circuit's definition of "shielding" in United States v. You, 382 F.3d 958, 966 (9th Cir. 2004), which requires "substantial" facilitation of the aliens' remaining in the United States). This Court should follow the lead of those circuits and hold that the district court's definition was too broad.

The district court's instruction defined "shielding" as "to protect or to keep safe from detection." (R. at 123). This definition does not require any level of substantiality or materiality, and thus, could encompass trivial or de minimis acts. For example, under this definition, providing a glass of water to an illegal alien could be considered "shielding" if it made the alien more comfortable and thus less likely to be detected. This is not a reasonable interpretation of the statute.

Other circuits have rejected broad definitions of "shielding" in favor of more nuanced ones. For instance, the Ninth Circuit has held that "shielding" requires "substantial" facilitation of the aliens' remaining in the United States. You, 382 F.3d at 966. Similarly, the Fifth Circuit has held that "shielding" involves "actively assisting an alien to evade detection." United States v. Rubio-Gonzalez, 674 F.2d 1067, 1072 (5th Cir. 1982). These definitions provide a more accurate understanding of the term "shielding" and ensure that only significant acts are considered.

The district court's instruction was not only too broad but also failed to provide the jury with a clear understanding of the term "shielding." As a result, the jury may have convicted Ye based on trivial or insignificant acts, rather than substantial or material ones. This error was not harmless, as it may have affected the outcome of the trial.

In sum, the district court's definition of "shielding" was erroneous, and this Court should reverse Ye's conviction under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) and remand for a new trial with a proper instruction on the meaning of "shielding."
