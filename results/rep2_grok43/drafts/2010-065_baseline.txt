ARGUMENT

A criminal defendant lacks standing to challenge her conviction on the ground that the statute of conviction exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers and violates the Tenth Amendment. In Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies contended that the Tennessee Valley Authority’s sale of electrical power constituted an impermissible federal regulation of a local matter in violation of the Tenth Amendment. The Court rejected the claim, holding that “absent the states or their officers, [private parties] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144.

That holding controls here. Bond, a private defendant, seeks to invalidate 18 U.S.C. § 229 on the ground that it intrudes upon powers reserved to the States. Because neither Pennsylvania nor its officers have objected, Bond “absent the states or their officers, ha[s] no standing” to press the Tenth Amendment claim. Tennessee Electric, 306 U.S. at 144.

The requirement that a litigant demonstrate an injury “particularized” to herself, rather than a generalized grievance shared with the public, reinforces this conclusion. See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560 n.1 (1992). A Tenth Amendment violation, if any, would be suffered by the State itself; an individual defendant cannot convert that structural injury into a personal stake sufficient for Article III standing. Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 523 U.S. 83, 101-02 (1998) (jurisdictional questions must precede the merits).

Although two circuits have permitted private parties to assert Tenth Amendment challenges, see Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis, 185 F.3d 693, 703-04 (7th Cir. 1999); Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. U.S. Department of Energy, 666 F.2d 1359, 1368 n.16 (11th Cir. 1982), the majority of circuits have followed Tennessee Electric and denied standing. See, e.g., United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004). The latter view is consistent with the principle that “the States possess primary authority for defining and enforcing the criminal law.” Brecht v. Abrahamson, 507 U.S. 619, 635 (1993).

Bond’s conviction therefore rests on a statute whose validity she may not contest through the Tenth Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
