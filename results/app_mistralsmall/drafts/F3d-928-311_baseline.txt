**ARGUMENT**

**I. The district court erred in classifying Furlow’s prior Georgia arson conviction as a “violent felony” under the ACCA and a “crime of violence” under the Guidelines.**

The ACCA defines a “violent felony” to include any offense that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another,” or “is burglary, arson, or extortion.” *18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii)*. Similarly, the Guidelines define a “crime of violence” to include “arson.” *USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2)*. However, the Supreme Court has made clear that these terms must be interpreted in light of the categorical approach, which requires courts to look only to the statutory elements of the prior offense, not the facts underlying the conviction. *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 602 (1990); *Mathis v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 2243, 2248 (2016).

Under South Carolina law, first-degree arson is defined as “burning or causing to be burned any dwelling house or other building,” with the intent to destroy or damage it. *S.C. Code Ann. § 16-11-110(A)*. The statute does not require proof of any risk to human life or the use of force against another person. *See State v. Burdette*, 335 S.C. 34, 43 (1999) (noting that arson under South Carolina law does not require endangerment to human life). Because the statute sweeps more broadly than the generic definition of arson under the ACCA and Guidelines, the district court erred in treating Furlow’s Georgia arson conviction as a predicate offense. *See United States v. McNeal*, 884 F.3d 484, 490 (4th Cir. 2018) (holding that a prior conviction does not qualify as a predicate under the ACCA or Guidelines if the statute of conviction criminalizes conduct broader than the generic offense).

**II. The district court erred in classifying Furlow’s prior South Carolina drug conviction as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA and a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines.**

The ACCA defines a “serious drug offense” as “an offense under State law ... for which a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more is prescribed by law.” *18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii)*. The Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” as one “punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year.” *USSG § 4B1.2(b)*. Furlow’s South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine, however, carried a maximum term of imprisonment of five years. *S.C. Code Ann. § 44-53-370(e)(1)*. Because the maximum term of imprisonment for this offense is less than ten years, it does not qualify as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA. *See United States v. Harcum*, 898 F.3d 227, 232 (4th Cir. 2018) (holding that a prior conviction does not qualify as a predicate under the ACCA if the maximum term of imprisonment is less than ten years).

Similarly, because the maximum term of imprisonment for Furlow’s South Carolina drug conviction is five years, it does not qualify as a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines. *See United States v. McNeal*, 884 F.3d at 491 (holding that a prior conviction does not qualify as a predicate under the Guidelines if the maximum term of imprisonment is five years or less).

**III. The district court erred in classifying Furlow’s prior Georgia drug convictions as “serious drug offenses” under the ACCA and “controlled substance offenses” under the Guidelines.**

The government alleged that Furlow had been convicted in Georgia for possession with intent to distribute cocaine and marijuana in 2003. However, the record does not establish that these convictions qualify as predicates under the ACCA or the Guidelines. The ACCA requires that a predicate drug offense carry a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more. *18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii)*. Under Georgia law, possession with intent to distribute cocaine is punishable by a maximum term of imprisonment of fifteen years. *O.C.G.A. § 16-13-30(d)*. However, possession with intent to distribute marijuana is punishable by a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years. *O.C.G.A. § 16-13-30(j)*. Because the maximum term of imprisonment for the marijuana conviction is exactly ten years, it does not qualify as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA. *See United States v. Harcum*, 898 F.3d at 232 (holding that a prior conviction does not qualify as a predicate under the ACCA if the maximum term of imprisonment is exactly ten years).

Similarly, because the maximum term of imprisonment for the marijuana conviction is ten years, it does not qualify as a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines. *See United States v. McNeal*, 884 F.3d at 491 (holding that a prior conviction does not qualify as a predicate under the Guidelines if the maximum term of imprisonment is ten years or less).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, Furlow respectfully requests that this Court vacate his sentence and remand for resentencing without the ACCA and career offender enhancements.
