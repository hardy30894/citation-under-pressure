**Argument**

Bond lacks standing to press a Tenth Amendment challenge to her conviction. The Constitution’s federal courts do not entertain abstract objections to the allocation of legislative power at the behest of a private defendant when the asserted structural injury is one the States themselves may choose to vindicate. The Supreme Court said as much in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118, 143-144 (1939), where it held that, absent the States or their officers, private utility companies had “no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144. That rule controls here.

1. **Private defendants may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to defeat federal criminal convictions.**  
The Tenth Amendment “states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered.” United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). More importantly, the Supreme Court has repeatedly treated federalism objections as matters for the sovereign States, not private parties, unless the private litigant asserts some personal constitutional injury independent of the States’ prerogative. In Tennessee Electric, the Court rejected a private challenge to federal action allegedly invading state authority and held that the utility companies, “absent the states or their officers,” had no standing to raise the Tenth Amendment. 306 U.S. at 144.

The courts of appeals have divided on whether Tennessee Electric forecloses private Tenth Amendment standing, but several have followed the Supreme Court’s instruction that a private party may not litigate generalized federalism objections divorced from a personal right. See United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-527 (8th Cir. 2009); Oregon v. Legal Services Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); Brooklyn Legal Services Corp. v. Legal Services Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004). Those decisions correctly recognize that the Tenth Amendment protects the structural balance between sovereigns, and that injury to that balance is not, without more, a concrete personal injury for a criminal defendant to assert. See Dillard v. Chilton County Commission, 495 F.3d 1324, 1335 (11th Cir. 2007).

2. **Bond’s claim is a generalized federalism objection, not a personal constitutional injury.**  
Bond does not contend that the statute singled her out on any impermissible basis, that federal prosecution denied her a personal right, or that she suffered any injury distinct from ordinary criminal enforcement. Her theory is only that Congress exceeded its enumerated powers and invaded an area of state criminal jurisdiction. But a claim that the federal government regulated too broadly is precisely the kind of structural objection Tennessee Electric deemed unavailable to private parties. 306 U.S. at 144. The Supreme Court has long distinguished between personal constitutional rights and structural limits designed to allocate power between sovereigns. See Bond v. United States, 564 U.S. 211, 222 (2011) (recognizing that federalism “secures to citizens the liberties that derive from the diffusion of sovereign power”); New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144, 181 (1992) (noting that the federalism principle “protects the liberty of the individual from arbitrary power”). But where, as here, the defendant seeks only to invalidate a federal criminal statute by invoking the States’ reserved powers, the injury is not hers to assert.

3. **Steel Co. confirms that standing must be resolved before the merits, and Bond cannot manufacture standing by pleading constitutional invalidity.**  
Standing is a threshold jurisdictional requirement. Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 523 U.S. 83, 101-02 (1998). A court may not leap to the merits of an enumerated-powers challenge before determining whether the litigant has a justiciable stake in that claim. Id. at 94-95, 101-02. Here, Bond’s Tenth Amendment theory is indistinguishable from a merits attack on Congress’s authority to enact 18 U.S.C. § 229. But the Supreme Court’s standing jurisprudence requires more than disagreement with the validity of a statute; it requires an injury the plaintiff may personally redress through suit. Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560-61 (1992). Bond has not shown one.

4. **The appropriate remedy for any true Tenth Amendment concern lies with the States, not a private criminal defendant.**  
As the First Circuit explained, “the State represents the interests of its citizens in general,” and citizens remain free to pursue political remedies if state officials decline to contest federal encroachment. Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 35 (1st Cir. 2005). That is especially apt here. Pennsylvania has not objected to the federal prosecution, and Bond offers no basis for treating her as the sovereign’s proxy. If the States wish to defend their reserved powers against federal overreach, they may do so in their own name. See Tennessee Electric, 306 U.S. at 144.

Bond therefore lacks standing to press her Tenth Amendment challenge, and the Court should affirm her conviction without reaching the merits of her federalism arguments.
