ARGUMENT

The Act is a content-neutral time, place, and manner restriction that satisfies intermediate scrutiny. A regulation of speech in a traditional public forum is valid if it is “justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech,” “narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest,” and “leave[s] open ample alternative channels for communication of the information.” Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (quoting Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984)).

The Act is content neutral. Its restrictions apply equally to all speakers and do not “draw content-based distinctions on [their] face.” Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 719 (2000). The exemptions for clinic employees and certain passersby are likewise content neutral; they “make[] no reference to the content of the speech” and serve the “legitimate interest in protecting” patients and staff. Id. at 719-20.

The Act is narrowly tailored. It “promotes a substantial government interest that would be achieved less effectively absent the regulation” and does not “burden substantially more speech than is necessary.” Ward, 491 U.S. at 799 (quoting United States v. Albertini, 472 U.S. 675, 689 (1985)). Massachusetts has a significant interest in “ensuring public safety and order” and “protecting a woman’s freedom to seek pregnancy-related services.” Hill, 530 U.S. at 715. The fixed 35-foot zones directly advance that interest by preventing physical obstruction and harassment at clinic entrances while leaving open “a substantial portion of the sidewalk” for speech. Id. at 726.

The Act leaves ample alternative channels. Petitioners may “communicate at a ‘normal conversational distance’” outside the zones, display signs, distribute literature, and use amplification. Id. at 729-30. These opportunities are “more than adequate.” Id. at 729.

Hill v. Colorado controls and should not be overruled. The Colorado statute upheld there imposed an eight-foot floating buffer within 100 feet of clinic entrances; the Massachusetts fixed zones are less restrictive in critical respects and rest on the same governmental interests. Stare decisis requires adherence absent a “special justification” for departure. See Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pa. v. Casey, 505 U.S. 833, 854-55 (1992). No such justification exists here.

The as-applied challenge likewise fails. Petitioners have not shown that the Act “has been applied in a discriminatory manner” or that its “justifications ‘are not compelling.’” Hill, 530 U.S. at 719 (quoting Carey v. Brown, 447 U.S. 455, 464-65 (1980)). The record demonstrates that the zones are enforced evenhandedly and that petitioners continue to reach patients through permitted means.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
