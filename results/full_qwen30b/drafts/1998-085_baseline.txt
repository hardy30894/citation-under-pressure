**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous” *independent of its state of mind* to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—requires this Court to interpret the plain language of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which amended Title VII to authorize punitive damages for intentional discrimination. The answer is no. Congress did not require conduct to be “egregious” or “outrageous” in a vacuum; rather, it tied the availability of punitive damages to the defendant’s *mental state*: malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals, which correctly held that punitive damages are available under § 1981a(b)(1) when a plaintiff proves intentional discrimination and demonstrates that the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference—standards that are satisfied here.

**I. Congress Rejected the “Egregious Conduct” Standard in Favor of a Mental-State Requirement**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 amended Title VII to provide for compensatory and punitive damages in cases of intentional discrimination. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(a). The statute explicitly limits punitive damages to cases where the plaintiff “demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” § 1981a(b)(1). This language is unambiguous: the focus is not on the *nature* of the conduct, but on the *defendant’s state of mind*. The statute does not require that the conduct be “egregious,” “outrageous,” or “extraordinarily egregious”—terms that would impose a separate, objective standard of conduct independent of intent.

The legislative history confirms this interpretation. While Senator Dole’s statement that punitive damages should be reserved for “extraordinarily egregious cases” has been cited by some courts, it was not adopted by Congress. The final version of the Act, as passed, contains no such limitation. Indeed, the House Report, which the majority in the Court of Appeals correctly dismissed as reflecting an unadopted version of the bill, did include the word “callous” in the alternative to “reckless indifference,” but that version was rejected in favor of the current statutory language. See 137 Cong. Rec. H 9527 (Nov. 7, 1991) (Rep. Edwards: punitive damages under § 1981a “to the same extent and under the same standards that they are available under 42 U.S.C. § 1981”). That statement, which aligns with the common law principle that punitive damages require more than mere liability, reflects Congress’s intent to bring Title VII into line with § 1981—where punitive damages have long been available upon proof of malice or reckless indifference.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that when Congress uses clear statutory language, courts must give effect to that language without importing additional requirements. See *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989) (“Where the statute’s language is unambiguous, we need not look beyond it”). Here, the language of § 1981a(b)(1) is clear: punitive damages are available when the employer acts with malice or reckless indifference. The Court should not read into this provision a requirement that the conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” in the absence of any such language.

**II. The “Egregious Conduct” Standard Conflicts with the Purpose of the 1991 Act**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 was enacted to restore and strengthen the remedies available to victims of employment discrimination. Congress found that “the availability of compensatory and punitive damages under § 1981 but not under Title VII created a disparity in protection” and that “additional remedies under Federal law are needed to deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination in the workplace.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981 (note). The Act’s purpose was to close the gap between § 1981 and Title VII by making punitive damages available under Title VII—*but only when the employer’s conduct reflects a particularly blameworthy state of mind*.

Requiring “egregious” conduct would undermine this purpose. It would allow employers to avoid punitive damages simply by engaging in intentional discrimination that is not “outrageous” in form—such as a pattern of gender-based decisions that are not overtly violent or shocking—so long as the conduct does not meet some undefined threshold of “egregiousness.” That would defeat Congress’s intent to deter *intentional* discrimination, regardless of whether it is “outrageous.” As the Court has held, “the purpose of punitive damages is to punish and deter,” not merely to compensate. *BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 575 (1996). The mental-state standard of malice or reckless indifference is precisely the mechanism Congress chose to achieve that goal.

**III. The Evidence Here Supports a Finding of Reckless Indifference**

The jury found that ADA intentionally discriminated against Kolstad on the basis of sex. The evidence supports a finding that ADA acted with reckless indifference to her federally protected rights. The employer’s stated reasons—lack of experience in health care reform and weak writing skills—were contradicted by evidence that Spangler had no such experience and that Kolstad had consistently received “distinguished” evaluations.
