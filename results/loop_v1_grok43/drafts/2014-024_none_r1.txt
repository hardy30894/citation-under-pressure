ARGUMENT

Section 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his words as a threat. Criminal statutes are construed against the common-law background that demands mens rea for conviction. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). The First Amendment independently requires the same showing. Speech may be punished only when it creates a clear and present danger that the speaker intends to produce. Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919). An objective standard would criminalize protected advocacy. Gitlow v. New York, 268 U.S. 652, 666 (1925). The Amendment therefore demands that the government prove the speaker's purpose to bring about imminent lawless action. Whitney v. California, 274 U.S. 357, 376 (1927) (Brandeis, J., concurring). Punishing expression without regard to the speaker's intent chills constitutionally safeguarded speech. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931). The principle that words are protected unless they create a clear and present danger applies directly to alleged threats. Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931). Intent remains an essential element of criminal liability for spoken or written statements. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 309 (1940). An objective test for threats under the statute fails these constitutional requirements. Bridges v. California, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941). Even categories of speech outside First Amendment protection require proof of the speaker's intent to threaten or incite. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942). Because the jury was instructed solely on an objective standard, the convictions cannot stand. Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494, 503 (1951).
