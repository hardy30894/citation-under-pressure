**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) properly exercised jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) to issue citations to Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., for violations occurring aboard the *MR. BELDON*, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation *per se* preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction, regardless of whether the vessel is inspected or subject to comprehensive Coast Guard regulation. This interpretation misreads both the statutory text and controlling precedent, and would create an impermissible, categorical exemption from OSHA’s protections for workers on uninspected vessels—exactly the kind of regulatory fragmentation Congress sought to avoid.

**I. The OSH Act Applies to the *MR. BELDON* as a “Workplace” Under Section 4(a)**

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act defines the scope of OSHA’s authority: “The provisions of this Act shall apply with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The term “workplace” is not limited to fixed, land-based facilities. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a workplace” includes any location where employees perform their duties, including mobile or floating platforms. In *Barnes v. United States*, 412 U.S. 837, 841 (1973), the Court held that a “workplace” may be “a vessel, a mine, a construction site, or any other place where work is performed.” The *MR. BELDON* was not merely a vessel in transit—it was a permanent, functional workplace where employees drilled oil wells, maintained equipment, and lived for extended periods. The explosion occurred during active drilling operations, and the crew was engaged in continuous, hazardous work. The Administrative Law Judge correctly found that the *MR. BELDON* constituted a “workplace” under the Act. The Fifth Circuit’s failure to address this threshold issue was error, as the existence of a workplace is a prerequisite to OSHA jurisdiction.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction Over Uninspected Vessels**

The Fifth Circuit held that the Coast Guard’s exclusive authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation, as established in *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), preempts OSHA’s authority even where the vessel is uninspected and subject to minimal Coast Guard regulation. This conclusion misapplies the preemption doctrine and ignores the statutory framework Congress created.

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that OSHA “shall not apply to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The key inquiry is not whether a federal agency *has* authority, but whether it *exercises* that authority in a way that *prevents* OSHA from acting. As the Supreme Court emphasized in *Barnes*, “Congress did not intend to create a system of overlapping or conflicting regulations.” 412 U.S. at 842. But where a federal agency’s authority is limited or non-existent, OSHA may step in.

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is not comprehensive. While 46 U.S.C. § 2 grants the Coast Guard broad authority to promote safety on navigable waters, that authority is not unlimited. The Coast Guard has issued safety regulations for uninspected vessels—such as those governing fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation systems—but these are minimal and focused on *maritime safety*, not *occupational safety and health*. See 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. The Coast Guard has not promulgated comprehensive standards for workplace conditions such as hazard communication, machine guarding, or emergency response procedures—precisely the types of standards at issue in this case.

In *Clary*, the Fifth Circuit held that OSHA regulations did not apply to seamen on vessels in navigation. But the Court did not purport to create a blanket exemption for all vessels, inspected or not. The *Clary* decision rested on the fact that the Coast Guard had *already* issued safety standards applicable to the vessel in question. 609 F.2d at 1121. Here, no such comprehensive standards exist for the *MR. BELDON*. The Coast Guard’s investigation concluded that the barge was not an “inspected vessel” and did not identify any regulatory violations. The absence of Coast Guard enforcement or regulation in this context demonstrates that the Coast Guard has not “exercised” its authority over the *workplace conditions* at issue. Thus, section 4(b)(1) does not bar OSHA jurisdiction.

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit’s categorical rule would create a dangerous loophole: workers on uninspected vessels—often in high-risk industries like offshore drilling—would be entirely outside OSHA’s reach, even when the Coast Guard has not regulated their working conditions. This contradicts the OSH Act’s purpose: to ensure “safe and healthful working conditions for every working man and woman.” 29 U.S.C. § 651(a). Congress did not intend to exempt workers from federal safety protections simply because their vessel is uninspected.

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s Decision Creates Regulatory Anomaly and Contravenes Congressional Intent**

The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of section 4(b)(1) would allow a vessel to fall outside OSHA’s jurisdiction simply because it is uninspected, even if the Coast Guard has not exercised its authority over workplace safety. This would produce the very “anomaly” Congress sought to avoid—“steaming in and out of OSHA coverage.” *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 829. A worker on an uninspected barge would be unprotected by OSHA, while a worker on an inspected vessel would be covered, despite similar working conditions. Such a distinction lacks statutory or policy justification.
