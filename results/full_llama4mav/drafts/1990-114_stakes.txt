The Missouri Constitution's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges at age seventy does not violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) or the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause. 

The ADEA excludes from its definition of "employee" individuals who are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). As the Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit correctly held, Missouri's appointed state court judges fall within this exception. The role of a judge involves significant policymaking, as judges must often fill gaps in the law and exercise discretion in their decision-making. EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988) ("'Policymaking' is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents."). The supervisory powers exercised by Missouri's judges, particularly those on the Supreme Court, further demonstrate their policymaking responsibilities. Mo. Const. art. V, Sec. 4.

The legislative history of the ADEA's exemptions supports the conclusion that appointed judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." Although the conference report accompanying the 1972 amendments to the ADEA states that the exemption should be "construed narrowly," it also indicates that the exemption applies to "persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments." Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180. The state judiciary can be considered one of the more significant "agencies" of state government, and appointed judges can be seen as holding policymaking positions.

Furthermore, Missouri's mandatory retirement provision for state judges is rationally related to legitimate state interests and therefore does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. The state's interest in ensuring a judiciary that is vigorous and capable of performing its duties is a legitimate concern. Mandatory retirement at age seventy eliminates the need for individualized assessments of a judge's fitness to serve, reducing the potential for controversy and delay. Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979) (holding that mandatory retirement for judges is rationally related to the state's interest in maintaining a capable judiciary). The state's decision to draw a bright line at age seventy is a rational means of achieving this goal. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 314-316 (1976) (per curiam) (upholding a mandatory retirement age for state police officers).

In conclusion, Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges is constitutional and does not conflict with the ADEA. The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit should be affirmed.
