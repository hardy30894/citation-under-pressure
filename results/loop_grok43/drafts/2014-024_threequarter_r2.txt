ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in affirming petitioner's convictions under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) because that statute requires proof of subjective intent to threaten. Criminal statutes are construed to incorporate the common-law requirement of mens rea. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250-251 (1952). "A statute which . . . forbids the doing of an act" without regard to intent "is inconsistent with our philosophy of criminal law." Id. at 250.

The First Amendment likewise demands subjective intent before speech may be punished as a threat. The clear-and-present-danger test protects expression unless the speaker intends to produce imminent lawless action. Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919); Abrams v. United States, 250 U.S. 616, 627-628 (1919) (Holmes, J., dissenting). The question in every case is whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger that the speaker intends to bring about. Schenck, 249 U.S. at 52. An objective standard would criminalize protected advocacy. United States v. Schwimmer, 279 U.S. 644, 649 (1929); Whitney v. California, 274 U.S. 357, 372 (1927) (Brandeis, J., concurring).

Punishment of speech without regard to the speaker's purpose chills expression the Amendment safeguards. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931); Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697, 713-714 (1931). The safeguarding of free speech demands that the state show a clear and present danger arising from the speaker's intended meaning. Fiske v. Kansas, 274 U.S. 380, 387 (1927). An objective test fails this requirement. Bridges v. California, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941).

Even "fighting words" require that the speaker intend to provoke violence. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942). The same principle governs threats under § 875(c). Debs v. United States, 249 U.S. 211, 216 (1919). Because the jury was instructed only on an objective standard, the convictions cannot stand.
