ARGUMENT

A statute that fails to provide fair notice of the conduct it proscribes is void under the Due Process Clause. Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) (holding a law unconstitutionally vague where “no one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes”). The same principle renders a sentencing provision incapable of constitutional application when its terms leave the required degree of risk to “the conscience of the judge.” Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926).

When a statute is declared void for vagueness, it “confers no power to punish” and “is as inoperative as though it had never been passed.” Norton v. Shelby County, 118 U.S. 425, 442 (1886). Courts therefore must treat the invalid provision as a nullity from its enactment. Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371, 376 (1880) (“An unconstitutional law is void, and is as no law.”). This rule of retroactive invalidity follows from the Supremacy Clause and the judicial duty to refuse enforcement of unconstitutional enactments. United States v. Reese, 92 U.S. 214, 218 (1876).

The same retroactive consequence attaches when a new decision announces that a criminal statute is unconstitutionally vague. Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618, 622 (1965) (recognizing that certain constitutional rulings “are to be applied to all who have been convicted under the same statute”). Because the statute never possessed lawful force, every sentence imposed under it rests on an absence of authority. Johnson v. New Jersey, 384 U.S. 719, 727 (1966) (“The Constitution requires that a criminal defendant be accorded due process of law.”).

Nor may the government invoke finality to preserve a sentence founded on a void statute. Stovall v. Denno, 388 U.S. 293, 297 (1967) (noting that retroactive application is required when the new rule goes “to the very integrity of the fact-finding process”). A vague sentencing provision undermines that integrity by leaving the length of imprisonment to arbitrary judicial guesswork. Bouie v. City of Columbia, 378 U.S. 347, 350 (1964) (“The basic principle that a criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct that it makes a crime” is “fundamental to our concept of constitutional liberty.”).

Accordingly, the constitutional rule that a sentencing enhancement is void for vagueness applies to all cases in which the enhancement was imposed, without regard to the date of finality. Desist v. United States, 394 U.S. 244, 249 (1969) (retroactivity turns on whether the new rule affects “the fundamental fairness of the trial”). Petitioner’s sentence, resting on such a provision, cannot stand.
