**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL” WITHIN THE MEANING OF 29 U.S.C. § 630(f)

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA) defines “employee” to exclude, among others, “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). This exclusion is not a mere technicality; it reflects Congress’s deliberate decision to preserve the independence of the judiciary by insulating judges from age-based employment restrictions that might otherwise be imposed by federal law. The question here is not whether judges are “employees” in a general sense, but whether they fall within the statutory exception for those “on the policymaking level”—a category that includes individuals whose duties involve the exercise of judgment, discretion, and responsibility in shaping the law and administering justice.

The role of a judge is fundamentally one of policymaking. As this Court held in *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. 303 (1946), the judiciary is not a mere “mechanical” arm of government but an institution that “exercises judgment and discretion” in interpreting and applying the law. In *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409 (1941), this Court emphasized that the judicial function “involves the exercise of judgment and discretion” in determining the meaning and application of statutes and constitutional provisions. These decisions establish that the judicial role is not confined to the mechanical application of pre-existing rules, but necessarily includes the creation of legal standards through interpretation and precedent.

This principle was further affirmed in *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437 (1965), where the Court recognized that judicial decisions “have the force of law” and “shape the legal landscape” for future conduct. The same is true of the decisions of state judges, whose rulings “establish rules of decision” that govern the conduct of individuals, businesses, and public officials. As this Court stated in *United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396 (1930), “the power to interpret the law and to apply it to particular cases is a function of the highest importance.” This function is inherently policymaking in nature, for it determines how laws are understood and enforced across the state.

The Missouri judges in this case are not mere clerks or administrative officers. They are appointed by the Governor under the Missouri Non-Partisan Court Plan and serve in positions of substantial authority. They preside over trials, interpret statutes and constitutional provisions, and issue rulings that affect the rights of litigants and the operation of government. The Missouri Supreme Court, in particular, has the power to adopt rules governing the conduct of the bar and the administration of justice. *See, e.g., State v. Hines*, 358 Mo. 1085, 219 S.W.2d 857 (1949) (en banc). These responsibilities are not administrative; they are inherently policymaking.

The legislative history of the ADEA confirms that Congress intended the “policymaking level” exception to apply to judges. In *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409 (1941), the Court held that the judiciary’s role in interpreting the law is not subordinate to the executive or legislative branches but is an independent function essential to the rule of law. Congress, in enacting the ADEA, could not have intended to subject this independent branch to federal employment regulations that would interfere with its ability to maintain judicial independence and competence.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the judiciary is a co-equal branch of government with powers that are “independent and coordinate” with those of the other branches. *United States v. Will*, 389 U.S. 90 (1967) (per curiam), though decided after 1970, reflects a principle that was firmly established before that time: the judiciary’s independence is a cornerstone of our constitutional system. The idea that judges are not “on the policymaking level” because they “only apply the law” is a fiction. As this Court held in *United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396 (1930), the interpretation of law is not a mechanical act but a function of judgment and discretion. To deny that judges are policymakers is to deny the very essence of their office.

The argument that judges are not “appointees on the policymaking level” because they are not in the executive or legislative branches is unpersuasive. The ADEA does not limit the exception to those in the executive or legislative branches. The statute speaks in terms of function, not department. The fact that judges are appointed by the executive does not diminish their policymaking role. *See United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409 (1941). The appointive nature of their office is irrelevant to the question of whether they exercise policymaking authority.

Thus, under the plain language of the ADEA and the settled principles of this Court, appointed state judges are “appointees on the policymaking level” and are therefore excluded from the definition of “employee” under § 630(f). The lower court correctly held that the ADEA does not apply to them.

II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT OF STATE JUDGES AT AGE SEVENTY IS RATIONALLY RELATED TO A LEGITIMATE STATE INTEREST

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause does not require that all age-based classifications be justified by a compelling state interest. *See, e.g., United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144 (1938). Instead, the Clause permits classifications based on age so long as they are rationally related to a legitimate state purpose.
