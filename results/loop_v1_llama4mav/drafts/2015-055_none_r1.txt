The issue before this Court is whether the rule announced in Johnson v. United States regarding what constitutes a violent offense applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. The principle that the Supreme Court's decisions construing federal statutes apply retroactively supports the retroactive application of Johnson. See United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103, 110 (1801) (establishing that a court must apply the law as it exists at the time of its decision).

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) imposes a minimum sentence of 15 years for defendants with three prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). The ACCA defines a "violent felony" as a crime that has as an element the "use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another," or that is "burglary, arson, or extortion, involves use of explosives, or otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another." 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B). The Supreme Court has long held that a statute is void for vagueness if it fails to provide adequate notice of the conduct it prohibits. Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939); Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926).

In Johnson, the Court held that the residual clause of the ACCA was unconstitutionally vague because it failed to provide clear guidance on what constitutes a violent felony. This decision was based on the principle that a statute must provide fair notice of the conduct it prohibits. The Court has also held that a statute is unconstitutional if it is so vague that it allows for arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement. Cline v. Frink Dairy Co., 274 U.S. 445, 465 (1927); United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921).

The rule announced in Johnson is substantive because it changes the definition of a violent felony under the ACCA. As such, it applies retroactively to Welch's case. See Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1974) (holding that a new rule that narrows the scope of a criminal statute applies retroactively); Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643, 657-58 (1961) (applying the exclusionary rule retroactively). The retroactive application of Johnson is consistent with the principle that a court must apply the law as it exists at the time of its decision. United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103, 110 (1801).

Furthermore, the vagueness doctrine, as established in cases such as Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 101-02 (1945), requires that a penal statute be sufficiently explicit to inform those subject to it of the conduct that will render them liable to its penalties. The residual clause of the ACCA fails to meet this standard, and its unconstitutionality is a substantive rule that applies retroactively to Welch's case.

In light of these authorities, the rule announced in Johnson v. United States applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. The residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague, and Welch's prior conviction for strong arm robbery does not qualify as a predicate offense under the ACCA. Therefore, Welch's sentence should be vacated, and his case should be remanded for resentencing.
