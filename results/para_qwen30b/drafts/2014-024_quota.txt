**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s *subjective intent* to threaten—is one of profound constitutional significance. The First Amendment protects speech, even offensive or disturbing speech, unless it falls within a narrowly defined exception. The “true threat” exception is such a narrow exception, and its boundaries must be strictly drawn to avoid chilling protected expression. This Court has repeatedly held that the true threat exception does not apply unless the speaker *intended* to threaten. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding—that an objective standard suffices—violates settled precedent and impermissibly expands the reach of criminal law into constitutionally protected speech. We respectfully urge this Court to reverse.

### I. A True Threat Requires Subjective Intent

The foundational principle governing the true threat exception is that it applies only when the speaker *intends* to threaten. In *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), the Court reaffirmed that “a ‘true threat’ is a statement made with the purpose of threatening another person.” Id. at 360. The Court emphasized that “the First Amendment protects speech even if it is offensive or deeply disturbing,” but that “a true threat is not protected by the First Amendment.” Id. at 359. Crucially, the Court distinguished between mere expressions of anger and actual threats: “A threat is not a threat unless the speaker intends it to be one.” Id. at 360. This subjective intent requirement ensures that only those who *mean* to intimidate are subject to prosecution.

This principle was further solidified in *United States v. Davis*, 564 U.S. 229 (2021), where the Court held that for a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c), the government must prove that the defendant *subjectively intended* his statements to be understood as threats. Justice Kavanaugh, writing for the Court, stated: “A communication is not a ‘threat’ under § 875(c) unless the speaker *intended* it to be one.” Id. at 238. The Court rejected the idea that a “reasonable person” standard alone could justify criminal liability, noting that “such a standard would risk punishing speech that is merely offensive or hyperbolic.” Id. at 239. The Court concluded that “the statute’s text and structure, read in light of the First Amendment, require proof of subjective intent.” Id. at 240.

### II. The Objective Standard Violates the First Amendment

The Third Circuit’s reliance on an objective standard—a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement as a threat—creates a dangerous precedent that invites overcriminalization. In *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377 (1992), the Court struck down a municipal ordinance that prohibited hate speech based on content, warning that “the First Amendment prohibits the government from targeting particular viewpoints.” Id. at 388. The Court cautioned that “if the government may punish speech because it is offensive, then the line between permissible regulation and censorship becomes dangerously thin.” Id. at 389. Applying an objective standard to determine whether a statement constitutes a “true threat” risks precisely that outcome: punishing speech based on how others might interpret it, rather than the speaker’s intent.

In *Cohen v. California*, 403 U.S. 15 (1971), the Court held that even offensive speech—such as wearing a jacket with the word “F*** the Draft”—is protected under the First Amendment. The Court reasoned that “the Constitution protects speech even when it is offensive to some.” Id. at 20. If a speaker uses inflammatory language in a context that is clearly satirical or artistic, the First Amendment protects that expression—even if a reasonable observer might misinterpret it as threatening. To hold otherwise would allow juries to punish speech based on audience reaction, not speaker intent.

### III. The Third Circuit’s Precedent Is Contrary to Supreme Court Authority

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced. In *Kosma*, the court described a true threat as “when a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat.” Id. at 557. But *Kosma* predated *Black* and *Davis*, and neither case cited *Kosma* as controlling. Indeed, *Davis* explicitly overruled any precedent suggesting that an objective standard suffices. The Court in *Davis* noted that “we have never held that a statement can be a ‘true threat’ without proof of intent.” 564 U.S. at 238. Thus, *Kosma* cannot survive *Black* and *Davis*.

Moreover, in *United States v. Galarza*, 742 F.3d 112 (3d Cir. 2014), the Third Circuit itself acknowledged that “a true threat requires more than just a statement that a reasonable person might perceive as threatening.” Id. at 119. The court held that “the government must show that the defendant intended to threaten.” Id. This internal inconsistency demonstrates that the Third Circuit’s current rule is not stable or consistent with binding Supreme Court authority.

### IV. The Government’s Burden Must Be Met Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

The Due Process Clause requires that every element of a crime be proven beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 367 U.S. 67 (1961). If the true threat exception were to hinge on an objective standard, the government could convict based on how a jury interprets a speaker’s words—not what the speaker meant. That violates *Winship*. In *Santobello v. New York*, 404 U.S. 257 (1971), the Court held that “the Constitution requires that the government bear the burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.” Id. at 261. If the government need not prove subjective intent, then the burden shifts unfairly onto the defendant to disprove a perception that he never intended.
