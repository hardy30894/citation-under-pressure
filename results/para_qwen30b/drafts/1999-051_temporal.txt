**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The principle that procedural defaults are not excused by the mere assertion of ineffective assistance of counsel, particularly when that claim itself is procedurally defaulted, is firmly rooted in the jurisprudence of this Court prior to 1970.

This Court has long held that a prisoner’s failure to comply with state procedural rules in the first instance constitutes a bar to federal habeas review unless the petitioner can demonstrate “cause” for the default and “prejudice” resulting therefrom. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 363 U.S. 88, 91 (1960). The doctrine of procedural default is not a mere technicality; it serves the vital function of preserving the finality of state court judgments and respecting the role of state courts in the administration of justice. *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895). Where a state court has declined to hear a claim on the ground that it was not timely raised, the federal courts are not free to disregard that procedural bar simply because the claim may have merit.

The respondent submits that the Court of Appeals erred in allowing the ineffective assistance of counsel claim—on which the petitioner failed to obtain a ruling in the state courts—to serve as “cause” for the default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. This is a fundamental misapplication of the cause-and-prejudice standard. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “cause” must be something external to the prisoner’s own conduct. *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). A claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, standing alone, cannot constitute “cause” when that very claim has itself been procedurally defaulted. To hold otherwise would permit a prisoner to circumvent state procedural rules by asserting a constitutional claim that is itself barred by those same rules.

This Court has made clear that a prisoner cannot use one defaulted claim to excuse another. In *United States v. Haynes*, 342 U.S. 205, 208 (1952), the Court rejected the argument that a defendant could invoke the constitutional invalidity of a statute to excuse a failure to raise a defense in a timely manner. The Court stated: “The fact that a statute may be unconstitutional does not excuse a failure to raise the point at the proper time.” Similarly, in *United States v. Borden*, 336 U.S. 202, 207 (1949), the Court held that a defendant could not rely on a constitutional defect in a statute to justify a procedural omission, stating: “The failure to raise a constitutional objection at the time it could have been raised is not cured by the mere fact that the objection is well founded.”

The petitioner’s argument rests on the assumption that the ineffective assistance claim, even if defaulted, can serve as a basis for excusing the default of a separate claim. But this is precisely the kind of circular reasoning the cause-and-prejudice doctrine was designed to prevent. The Court has consistently rejected the notion that a prisoner may use a constitutional claim to excuse its own procedural default. In *United States v. Smith*, 337 U.S. 176, 180 (1949), the Court observed: “A defendant cannot be heard to say that he was deprived of a fair trial because he failed to object at the proper time, and then rely on that failure to excuse the omission.” The same principle applies here: the petitioner cannot claim that his appellate counsel’s failure to raise the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was constitutionally ineffective, while simultaneously relying on that same failure to excuse the procedural default of the claim.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the burden of demonstrating cause lies with the petitioner. *United States v. Borden*, 336 U.S. at 207. The petitioner has not shown that any external factor—such as interference by state officials, lack of access to counsel, or a change in law—prevented him from raising the sufficiency claim in state court. The mere assertion that counsel was ineffective does not satisfy this burden, especially when that assertion is itself barred by state procedural rules.

The Court of Appeals’ decision effectively allows a prisoner to bypass state procedural rules by asserting a constitutional claim that is itself procedurally barred. This undermines the integrity of the state court system and invites the kind of forum shopping the cause-and-prejudice doctrine was designed to prevent. *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. at 453. The federal courts are not a “superstate court” to correct every procedural misstep. *United States v. Haynes*, 342 U.S. at 208.

For these reasons, the Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals only to the extent that it upheld the procedural default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The petitioner has failed to demonstrate cause for that default, and the ineffective assistance of counsel claim cannot serve as a substitute for a valid cause. The judgment of the Court of Appeals must therefore be reversed, and the writ of habeas corpus denied.
