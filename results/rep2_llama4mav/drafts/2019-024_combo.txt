The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case misinterprets the "actual knowledge" requirement under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The court held that a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of both the transaction and its imprudence to trigger the statute of limitations. However, this interpretation is not supported by the case law.

The "actual knowledge" requirement has its roots in trust law, which ERISA was designed to codify. See Scott v. McGrath, 104 F.2d 606, 612 (D.C. Cir. 1939) ("The trustee is under a duty to the beneficiary to give him upon his request at reasonable times complete and accurate information as to the nature and amount of the trust property."). In trust law, a beneficiary's knowledge of a transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations, regardless of whether they know it is imprudent. See In re Estate of McGuffie, 123 Cal. App. 2d 501, 507 (Cal. Ct. App. 1954) ("A beneficiary who has knowledge of a breach of trust cannot sit idly by and permit the trustee to continue to breach the trust without taking any action to enforce his rights.").

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a plaintiff's knowledge of a transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations. See Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. 342, 349 (1874) ("[W]here the party injured by the fraud remains in ignorance of it without any fault or want of diligence or care on his part, the bar of the statute does not begin to run until the fraud is discovered."). Similarly, in Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135, 139 (1879), the Court held that a plaintiff's knowledge of a transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations, even if they do not know it is unlawful.

Other pre-ERISA cases also support the proposition that knowledge of a transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations. See, e.g., United States v. Diamond Coal & Coke Co., 255 F. 633, 636 (8th Cir. 1919) ("The statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff has knowledge of the facts which would put a reasonable man on inquiry."); Lippitt v. Ashley, 89 Conn. 451, 474 (Conn. 1915) ("The statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff has knowledge of the facts which constitute the cause of action."); Shelby County v. Bragg, 135 F. 257, 262 (8th Cir. 1905) ("The statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the facts which constitute the cause of action.").

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's own pre-ERISA cases support this interpretation. See, e.g., Curtis v. Connolly, 146 F. 824, 828 (9th Cir. 1906) ("The statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff has knowledge of the facts which would put a reasonable man on inquiry."); Oregon Home Builders v. Crowley, 87 F. 543, 546 (9th Cir. 1898) ("The statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the facts which constitute the cause of action.").

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the Ninth Circuit's decision in this case was incorrect. The "actual knowledge" requirement under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) is triggered when a plaintiff has knowledge of the transaction, not when they have knowledge of both the transaction and its imprudence. Therefore, the Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that Sulyma's claims are barred by the statute of limitations.
