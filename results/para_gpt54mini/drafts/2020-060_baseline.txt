### Argument

Guam’s claims are not time-barred under CERCLA section 113(f)(3)(B) because the 2004 consent decree did not “resolve[] [its] liability” for a CERCLA response action. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Section 113(f)(3)(B) authorizes contribution only where a party has resolved CERCLA liability “for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action” in a “settlement.” Id. This Court has held that a contribution claim accrues only when a settlement actually resolves liability for a CERCLA response action; absent that resolution, section 113(f)(3)(B) is not triggered, and section 107 remains available. Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 166–67 (2004); United States v. Atlantic Research Corp., 551 U.S. 128, 139 (2007).

The 2004 consent decree did not do that. It arose from a Clean Water Act suit, not a CERCLA enforcement action, and it required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and install a cover system to prevent discharges of untreated leachate. The decree expressly stated that it was entered “without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” That language matters. A settlement resolves liability only when its terms show that the parties intended to settle a claim or response action, not merely when the agreement contains obligations that happen to resemble cleanup measures. See Niagara Mohawk Power Corp. v. Chevron U.S.A., Inc., 596 F.3d 112, 127–28 (2d Cir. 2010); Agere Systems, Inc. v. Advanced Environmental Technology Corp., 602 F.3d 204, 228–29 (3d Cir. 2010); Bernstein v. Bankert, 733 F.3d 190, 203–07 (7th Cir. 2013). Here, the decree never identified CERCLA, never allocated CERCLA liability, and never released Guam from a CERCLA claim by the United States. Under those circumstances, Guam’s compliance with a Clean Water Act injunction cannot be transformed into a CERCLA settlement. See Fla. Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002–03 (6th Cir. 2015).

That conclusion follows from the structure of CERCLA itself. Congress created section 113(f) to provide contribution “during or following” specified CERCLA proceedings and settlements, while section 107(a) supplies the cost-recovery remedy for a party that has incurred cleanup costs. 42 U.S.C. §§ 9607(a), 9613(f)(1), (3)(B); Atlantic Research, 551 U.S. at 138–39. The remedies are complementary, but section 113(f) is triggered only by an actual resolution of CERCLA liability. Cooper Industries, 543 U.S. at 168–69. Because Guam’s decree did not resolve CERCLA liability, Guam did not forfeit its section 107 remedy.

The court of appeals’ contrary rule improperly converts any government cleanup order into a contribution trigger. That approach conflicts with this Court’s recognition that CERCLA liability turns on statutory text, not broad notions of fairness. Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States, 556 U.S. 599, 610–11 (2009). It also disregards the ordinary meaning of “resolve.” A party does not resolve liability to the United States for a CERCLA response action unless the agreement settles that liability. See AmeriPride Services Inc. v. Texas Eastern Overseas Inc., 782 F.3d 474, 483–84 (9th Cir. 2015) (en banc); PCS Nitrogen Inc. v. Ashley II of Charleston LLC, 714 F.3d 161, 177–78 (4th Cir. 2013). Guam’s Clean Water Act decree did not do so.

Even if there were ambiguity, CERCLA’s remedial structure confirms that the six-year section 107 limitations period should govern here. This Court has recognized that section 107 affords recovery for costs “incurred” by a private party, while section 113 supplies contribution for a party that has paid more than its share in an underlying CERCLA process. Atlantic Research, 551 U.S. at 139–40. Guam incurred the costs of closing and remediating the dump because the government compelled that work through a non-CERCLA consent decree. Nothing in the decree extinguished Guam’s separate right to seek cost recovery from the Navy for the contamination it allegedly caused. See H. F. Allen Orchards v. United States, 749 F.3d 893, 899–900 (9th Cir. 2014).

The judgment below should be reversed. CERCLA section 113(f)(3)(B) was not triggered by Guam’s Clean Water Act consent decree, so Guam’s section 107 action is not barred. See Cooper Industries, 543 U.S. at 168; Atlantic Research, 551 U.S. at 139.
