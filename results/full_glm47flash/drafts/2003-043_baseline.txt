**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE GOVERNMENT MUST ASSESS EACH INDIVIDUAL PARTNER TO EXTEND THE TEN-YEAR STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS.**

The government must assess each individual partner to extend the ten-year collection period. The district court correctly held that the assessment against the partnership did not extend the limitations period to the partners. Under 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a), the ten-year period for collection begins "within 10 years after the assessment of the tax." To trigger this period, the tax must be assessed against the specific "taxpayer" subject to the liability. 26 U.S.C. § 6203 requires an assessment to "record the liability of the taxpayer." 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1 further mandates that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer." The Internal Revenue Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The partners are distinct "individuals" and separate "taxpayers" from the partnership. Consequently, an assessment against the partnership cannot extend the ten-year limitations period to the partners. This principle is established in *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), where the court held that a tax lien on an individual's property under § 6321 requires an assessment against that individual, not merely the partnership.

**II. CALIFORNIA LAW DOES NOT ALLOW THE GOVERNMENT TO BYPASS THE INDIVIDUAL ASSESSMENT REQUIREMENT.**

The government also argues that California law, which makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, allows the IRS to bypass the individual assessment requirement. However, California law does not permit a creditor to collect a partnership debt from an individual partner without first obtaining a judgment against the partner. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c) provides that "a judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner." A creditor may only reach a partner's personal assets through a judgment against the partner. 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V. Because the IRS has not obtained a judgment against the partners, and the time to do so has expired under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), the IRS's claim is unenforceable. Under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1), a bankruptcy court may disallow a claim that is "unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor." The bankruptcy court therefore properly disallowed the IRS's claims.
