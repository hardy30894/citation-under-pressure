**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE BANKRUPTCY CODE’S RETIREMENT-FUND EXEMPTION PROTECTS INHERITED IRAS BECAUSE THEY ARE “RETIREMENT FUNDS” UNDER SECTION 522(b)(3)(C) AND (d)(12)**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” the Internal Revenue Code’s retirement-account provisions. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). An inherited IRA is such an account. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 408(e)(2)(A) (defining an IRA as a trust created or organized in the United States for the exclusive benefit of an individual or his beneficiaries); *see also* 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C) (providing that inherited IRAs remain tax-exempt). The Seventh Circuit erred by reading an unstated “retirement-use” requirement into the statute. *Cf. United States v. Nordic Village, Inc.*, 503 U.S. 30, 37 (1992) (“Statutory exemptions are to be narrowly construed in favor of the government.”).

The Bankruptcy Code does not define “retirement funds,” but it does not limit the exemption to funds held for the debtor’s own retirement. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The Fifth Circuit correctly held in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), that the exemption applies to funds that were “retirement funds” in the hands of the decedent, even if they are not used for the debtor’s retirement. *Id.* at 491 (“The text of § 522(b)(3)(C) does not require that the funds be the debtor’s retirement funds.”). The Eighth Circuit reached the same conclusion in *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), aff’d, 653 F.3d 771 (8th Cir. 2011). *See also* *In re Sill*, 470 B.R. 738, 742 (Bankr. D. Colo. 2012) (following *Chilton* and *Nessa*).

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary rule ignores the plain text of the statute. *See Connecticut Nat’l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253–54 (1992) (“Courts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there.”). The exemption applies to “retirement funds” held in accounts that are tax-exempt under the Internal Revenue Code. Inherited IRAs are tax-exempt accounts under 26 U.S.C. § 408, and the funds within them are “retirement funds” in the hands of the beneficiary. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C) (providing that inherited IRAs retain their tax-exempt status).

II. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S “RETIREMENT-USE” REQUIREMENT IS CONTRARY TO THE BANKRUPTCY CODE’S PURPOSE OF PROTECTING DEBTORS’ ESSENTIAL ASSETS**

The Seventh Circuit’s rule undermines the Bankruptcy Code’s core purpose: to provide debtors with a fresh start by protecting essential assets from creditors. *See* *Owen v. Owen*, 500 U.S. 305, 308 (1991) (“The purpose of the bankruptcy laws is to give the debtor a ‘fresh start’ in life.”). The exemption for retirement funds furthers this purpose by ensuring that debtors do not lose their retirement savings in bankruptcy. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 95-595, at 362 (1977), *reprinted in* 1978 U.S.C.C.A.N. 5963, 6318 (“The purpose of this provision is to encourage savings for retirement by exempting retirement funds from the bankruptcy estate.”).

The Seventh Circuit’s rule, by contrast, penalizes debtors who inherit retirement funds. *See* *Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320, 325 (2005) (noting that retirement funds are “critical to the financial health of millions of Americans”). If inherited IRAs are not exempt, debtors who inherit retirement funds may lose those funds to creditors, even though the funds were intended to provide for retirement. This result is inconsistent with the Bankruptcy Code’s purpose. *See* *Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz*, 503 U.S. 638, 644 (1992) (“The Bankruptcy Code is designed to give debtors a fresh start, and the exemptions play a key role in effectuating that purpose.”).

III. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S RULE IS INCONSISTENT WITH THE TREATMENT OF OTHER EXEMPTIONS UNDER THE BANKRUPTCY CODE**

The Seventh Circuit’s rule is inconsistent with the treatment of other exemptions under the Bankruptcy Code. For example, the homestead exemption under 11 U.S.C. § 522(d)(1) protects the debtor’s residence, regardless of whether the residence was acquired through inheritance. *See* *Owen*, 500 U.S. at 308. Similarly, the exemption for household goods and furnishings under 11 U.S.C. § 522(d)(3) protects items that may have been inherited by the debtor. *See id.* The Seventh Circuit’s rule would create an arbitrary distinction between inherited retirement funds and other inherited assets, even though all are protected by the Bankruptcy Code’s exemptions. *See* *Taylor*, 503 U.S. at 644 (“The exemptions are designed to ensure that debtors retain a minimum amount of property necessary for their survival.”).

IV. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S RULE WILL DISCOURAGE SAVINGS AND HARM DEBTORS**

The Seventh Circuit’s rule will discourage savings and harm debtors. *See* *Rousey*, 544 U.S. at 325. If inherited IRAs are not exempt, debtors who inherit retirement funds may be reluctant to save for retirement, knowing that their savings could be lost to creditors if they inherit additional funds. This result is inconsistent with the Bankruptcy Code’s purpose of encouraging savings. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 95-595, at 362. The Fifth and Eighth Circuits’ rule, by contrast, encourages savings by protecting inherited retirement funds from creditors. *See Chilton*, 674 F.3d at 491; *Nessa*, 426 B.R. at 318.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be reversed. The Bankruptcy Code’s retirement-fund exemption protects inherited IRAs because they are “retirement funds” held in tax-exempt accounts under the Internal Revenue Code. The Seventh Circuit’s contrary rule is inconsistent with the text of the statute, the Bankruptcy Code’s purpose, and the treatment of other exemptions under the Code.
