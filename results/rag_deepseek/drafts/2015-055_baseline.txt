**ARGUMENT**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. ___ (2015)—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is unconstitutionally vague—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. Under the framework of *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure generally does not apply retroactively to final convictions. But *Teague* recognized two exceptions: (1) rules that place “certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe,” *id.* at 311 (plurality opinion); and (2) “watershed rules of criminal procedure” that are “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty,” *id.* (internal quotation marks omitted). *Johnson* falls squarely within the first exception because it altered the substantive reach of the ACCA, rendering a category of prior convictions no longer punishable under the statute.

**I. *Johnson* Announced a Substantive Rule**

A rule is substantive when it “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). *Johnson* did exactly that. The residual clause defined a “violent felony” to include any felony that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). The Court held that this clause “violates the Constitution’s prohibition of vague criminal laws.” *Johnson*, slip op. at 4. Because the residual clause is void, a defendant whose sentence was enhanced under that clause was punished for conduct that Congress could not constitutionally define as a violent felony. *Johnson* thus “narrow[ed] the scope of a criminal statute” and “alter[ed] the range of conduct” subject to the ACCA’s mandatory minimum. *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620 (1998) (holding that a decision interpreting a statute to exclude certain conduct is substantive).

The Court in *Johnson* emphasized that the residual clause “denies fair notice to defendants and invites arbitrary enforcement by judges.” *Johnson*, slip op. at 4. This due-process defect is not a procedural innovation; it is a declaration that the clause itself is a legal nullity. As this Court has recognized, “a decision that a criminal statute is unconstitutionally vague is a substantive rule that applies retroactively.” *United States v. Davis*, 588 U.S. ___ (2019) (citing *Johnson*). Indeed, *Johnson* “did not announce a new rule of procedure; it held that a particular statutory provision is invalid.” *Welch v. United States*, 578 U.S. ___, slip op. at 6 (2016).

**II. Substantive Rules Apply Retroactively Under *Teague***

*Teague*’s first exception is “meant to apply to rules forbidding punishment of certain conduct.” *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 330 (1989). *Johnson* forbids punishment under the residual clause. A defendant sentenced under that clause—like Welch—was subjected to a 15-year mandatory minimum that Congress had no power to impose. The rule is therefore “substantive” and “should be applied retroactively because it ‘necessarily carries a significant risk that a defendant stands convicted of an act that the law does not make criminal.’” *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 620 (quoting *Davis v. United States*, 417 U.S. 333, 346 (1974)).

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary view—that *Johnson* is procedural because it changes how courts assess predicate offenses—misapprehends the nature of the holding. A rule is not procedural simply because it “alter[s] the method of determining whether a defendant’s prior conviction qualifies as a violent felony.” *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353. The key is whether the rule “place[s] a class of conduct beyond the reach of the law.” *Id.* at 352. *Johnson* does so by invalidating the very standard by which prior convictions were judged. Without the residual clause, many prior convictions—including Welch’s Florida strong-arm robbery—cannot support an ACCA enhancement. That is a substantive change.

**III. *Johnson* Is Not a Watershed Procedural Rule, But It Need Not Be**

Because *Johnson* is a substantive rule, it automatically satisfies *Teague*’s first exception. There is no need to analyze whether it also qualifies as a watershed procedural rule. *See Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 352 (substantive rules “apply retroactively because they necessarily carry a significant risk that a defendant stands convicted of an act that the law does not make criminal”). The Court has consistently applied substantive rules retroactively without requiring a showing of fundamental fairness. *See, e.g., Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 620; *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 522-23 (1995).

**IV. Retroactive Application Serves the Core Purpose of Collateral Review**

The “paramount purpose” of habeas corpus is “to free individuals from wrongful confinement.” *Kuhlmann v. Wilson*, 477 U.S. 436, 447 (1986). Welch is serving a 15-year sentence that Congress could not constitutionally authorize. To deny him retroactive relief would leave him incarcerated for a punishment that the Constitution forbids. This Court has never countenanced such a result. *See, e.g., Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. ___ (2016) (holding that substantive rules apply retroactively because they “prohibit a certain category of punishment for a class of defendants”).

**Conclusion**

*Johnson* announced a substantive rule that decriminalized a category of conduct under the ACCA. That rule applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed, and Welch’s sentence should be vacated.
