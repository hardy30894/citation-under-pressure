**ARGUMENT**

I. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the longstanding constitutional test for navigability for title, which requires inquiry into whether the relevant stretch of river was navigable at the time Montana entered the Union.

The equal footing doctrine vests states admitted to the Union with title to lands beneath waters that were navigable at the time of their admission. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the navigability inquiry for title purposes is historical, focusing on the condition of the river at statehood. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 57 (1894) ("The question is one of fact to be determined by the condition of the river at the time the State was admitted into the Union."); *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 229 (1845) ("The shores of navigable rivers, therefore, are protected by the political society, against invasion; and the riparian proprietor, when the river is a boundary, holds his title under the State, subject to the public right of navigation.").

The Montana Supreme Court faithfully applied this test. It considered historical evidence—including government surveys, territorial records, and contemporaneous accounts—to determine whether the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable in fact at the time of Montana’s admission in 1889. *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 2010 MT 293, ¶¶ 60–80 (citing *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 57). This approach comports with this Court’s instruction that "[t]he title thus vested in the State is subject to the paramount right of navigation, and the power of Congress to control navigation, but is otherwise absolute." *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (citing *Pollard’s Lessee*, 44 U.S. at 223).

II. The trial court did not err in admitting historical evidence to prove navigability at statehood.

Federal Rule of Evidence 803(8) (and its Montana counterpart, Rule 803(8)) permits the admission of public records and reports, including historical surveys and governmental compilations, to prove navigability. *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 403–04 (1940) (admitting government reports and maps as evidence of navigability). The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that such evidence is admissible under Rule 803(8) and Rule 902, as the documents were self-authenticating and fell within the ancient documents exception. *PPL Montana*, 2010 MT 293, ¶¶ 90–94 (citing *Smith v. Royce Hosiery Mills*, 162 Mont. 141, 150–51 (1973)).

This Court has long recognized that historical evidence—including early explorers’ accounts, territorial surveys, and legislative records—may be used to establish navigability at statehood. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14–15 (relying on historical evidence to determine navigability); *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75 (same). The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on such evidence was consistent with this precedent.

III. The federal navigational servitude does not preempt Montana’s claim to riverbeds underlying PPL’s dams.

The federal navigational servitude is a power reserved to Congress to ensure that navigable waters remain open to interstate and foreign commerce. *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596–97 (1941). However, this servitude does not divest states of title to riverbeds acquired under the equal footing doctrine. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14 ("The title thus vested in the State is subject to the paramount right of navigation, and the power of Congress to control navigation, but is otherwise absolute.").

PPL’s reliance on *Federal Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239 (1954), is misplaced. In *Niagara Mohawk*, the Court held that the federal navigational servitude did not authorize the Federal Power Commission to condemn private lands without compensation. *Id.* at 252. The Court did not disturb the principle that states retain title to riverbeds under the equal footing doctrine. *Id.* at 249–51. Thus, Montana’s claim to riverbeds beneath PPL’s dams is not preempted by the federal navigational servitude.

IV. The Federal Power Act does not preempt Montana’s Hydroelectric Resources Act.

The Federal Power Act establishes a "dual system" of regulation, preserving state authority over proprietary rights while vesting FERC with authority over licensing. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 167–68 (1946). This Court has recognized that state law claims for compensation for the use of state-owned lands are not preempted by the FPA. *Id.* at 175–76 ("[T]he Federal Power Act... does not withdraw from the States the power to regulate the *proprietary* rights... which may be affected by a federal license.").

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the HRA operates in harmony with the FPA. *PPL Montana*, 2010 MT 293, ¶¶ 14–16. The FPA requires licensees to comply with state law regarding the use of state-owned lands, and it does not preempt state claims for compensation. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 161 n.6, 175–76.

**CONCLUSION**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the historical navigability test, admitted proper evidence, and held that Montana’s title to riverbeds beneath PPL’s dams is not preempted by federal law. The judgment should be affirmed.
