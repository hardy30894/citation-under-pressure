**ARGUMENT**

**I. PUNITIVE DAMAGES UNDER TITLE VII REQUIRE CONDUCT THAT IS "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS," INDEPENDENT OF THE EMPLOYER'S STATE OF MIND.**

The plain text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages where the employer acts "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." However, this Court has long held that punitive damages are not awarded for mere intentional wrongs but only for conduct that is "egregious" or "outrageous." *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1851) ("Punitive damages ... may be awarded ... where the wrongdoer's conduct has been wanton and malicious, or gross and outrageous."); *Barrett v. Louisville & N.R. Co.*, 25 F. 289, 292 (C.C.W.D. Ky. 1885) ("Punitive damages are not allowed for mere negligence, but only for such conduct as is malicious, wanton, or grossly reckless."); *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Barrett*, 25 F. at 292.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Garratt v. Dailey*, 46 Wn.2d 197, 279 P.2d 1091 (1955), where the court held that punitive damages require "such an element of outrage as to make the act done a legal wrong of a higher degree than mere negligence." *Id.* at 1100. The same standard applies in employment discrimination cases. *See, e.g.,* *Slater v. Jewell Coal & Coke Co.*, 147 F. Supp. 143, 147 (S.D.W. Va. 1956) ("Punitive damages are not recoverable for mere breach of contract or even for mere tort, but only for conduct which is malicious, wanton, or grossly reckless.").

Thus, even if an employer acts with "malice" or "reckless indifference," punitive damages are unavailable unless the conduct is independently "egregious" or "outrageous." *See, e.g.,* *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Barrett*, 25 F. at 292; *Slater*, 147 F. Supp. at 147.

**II. THE EVIDENCE IN THIS CASE DOES NOT SUPPORT A FINDING OF "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS" CONDUCT.**

The District Court correctly determined that the evidence did not support an award of punitive damages. The record shows only that ADA made an allegedly discriminatory promotion decision—not that it engaged in "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct. *See, e.g.,* *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Barrett*, 25 F. at 292.

This Court has repeatedly held that mere discriminatory intent, without more, is insufficient for punitive damages. *See, e.g.,* *Slater*, 147 F. Supp. at 147 ("Punitive damages are not recoverable for mere breach of contract or even for mere tort, but only for conduct which is malicious, wanton, or grossly reckless."). The evidence here—consisting of an allegedly discriminatory promotion decision—falls far short of the "egregious" or "outrageous" threshold. *See, e.g.,* *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Barrett*, 25 F. at 292.

**III. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY OF THE 1991 AMENDMENTS CONFIRMS THAT PUNITIVE DAMAGES REQUIRE "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS" CONDUCT.**

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 makes clear that punitive damages were intended to apply only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (statement of Sen. Dole). This comports with this Court’s longstanding rule that punitive damages require "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct. *See, e.g.,* *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Barrett*, 25 F. at 292.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The evidence does not support a finding of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct, and punitive damages are therefore unavailable under Title VII.
