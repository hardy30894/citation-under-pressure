**ARGUMENT**

I. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT PROPERLY EXERCISED EQUITABLE JURISDICTION TO ISSUE THE POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER.

The District Court’s remedial order rests on this Court’s longstanding recognition that federal courts possess broad equitable powers to remedy constitutional violations in prisons. *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 155–56 (1908) (federal courts may enjoin state officials to prevent ongoing constitutional violations). This authority is not displaced by the PLRA. *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 n.9 (1978) (approving injunctive relief to remedy Eighth Amendment violations); *Procunier v. Martinez*, 416 U.S. 396, 414–15 (1974) (federal courts may fashion remedies to protect constitutional rights of prisoners). The PLRA’s savings clause expressly preserves this equitable authority. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (permitting “prospective relief” to remedy constitutional violations).

The District Court’s order is consistent with this Court’s instruction that remedial decrees must be narrowly tailored to address the constitutional injury. *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ.*, 402 U.S. 1, 15–16 (1971) (remedial orders must be “specifically addressed to the elimination of the [constitutional] violations”). Here, the court found that severe overcrowding was the “primary cause” of unconstitutional conditions, *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 607 F.3d 953, 960 (9th Cir. 2010), and the population cap was the least intrusive means to remedy the harm. *Milliken v. Bradley*, 433 U.S. 267, 280–81 (1977) (remedial orders must not exceed the scope of the constitutional violation).

II. THE POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PLRA’S “NECESSARY MEANS” STANDARD.

The PLRA’s “necessary means” provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), requires that prospective relief be “narrowly drawn, extend[] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” The District Court’s order satisfies this standard. *Brown v. Bd. of Educ.*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955) (remedial orders must be “limited to the scope of the constitutional violation”); *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 17 (1958) (remedies must be “necessary to eliminate the [constitutional] wrong”).

The District Court’s factual findings support its conclusion that a population cap is the least intrusive means to remedy the Eighth Amendment violations caused by overcrowding. *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. at 687 n.9 (upholding injunctive relief to remedy Eighth Amendment violations caused by overcrowding). The PLRA does not bar such relief. *Procunier v. Martinez*, 416 U.S. at 414–15 (federal courts may fashion remedies to protect constitutional rights of prisoners).

III. THE POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PLRA’S “TIME LIMITS” PROVISION.

The PLRA’s “time limits” provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(e)(1), requires that prospective relief “shall not remain in effect for more than five years.” The District Court’s order does not violate this provision because it is not a “term” of relief but a remedial measure to address ongoing constitutional violations. *Flast v. Cohen*, 392 U.S. 83, 100 (1968) (remedial orders may continue until the constitutional violation is corrected).
