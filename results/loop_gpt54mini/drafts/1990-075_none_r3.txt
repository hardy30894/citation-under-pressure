The Controlled Substances Act is constitutional. Congress supplied an intelligible principle to govern temporary scheduling, and it expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate departmental functions to the DEA. Under Supreme Court precedent predating 1970, both propositions are settled.

I. Congress Validly Delegated Temporary Scheduling Authority

The nondelegation doctrine asks whether Congress has supplied an “intelligible principle” to guide the executive’s exercise of delegated power. J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress states the policy, identifies the standards, or confines the circumstances in which the delegate may act. Cargo of the Brig Aurora v. United States, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 382, 388 (1813); Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692-94 (1892); Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904); United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 517-19 (1911); Opp Cotton Mills, Inc. v. Administrator of Wage and Hour Division of Department of Labor, 312 U.S. 126, 145-46 (1941); Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 424-27 (1944); American Power & Light Co. v. Securities and Exchange Commission, 329 U.S. 90, 104-05 (1946); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 249-50 (1947).

The CSA meets that test. Congress made the basic policy choice that dangerous drugs should be controlled to protect the public. It then directed the Attorney General, before scheduling a substance, to consider specific factors, including abuse potential, scientific evidence, current scientific knowledge, history and pattern of abuse, public-health risk, dependence liability, and precursor status. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). It required a scientific and medical evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services, which is binding if the Secretary recommends against scheduling. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b). And for temporary scheduling, Congress limited action to an “imminent hazard to the public safety,” prescribed the factors to be considered, required notice, and imposed a short duration. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h). Those are legislative standards, not a blank check. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 426; Fahey, 332 U.S. at 249-50.

Petitioners’ reliance on Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those statutes failed because Congress left the executive with essentially standardless policymaking power. Panama Refining, 293 U.S. at 430; Schechter Poultry, 295 U.S. at 541-42. Here, by contrast, Congress itself selected the policy, identified the emergency trigger, prescribed the criteria, and imposed temporal limits. The CSA therefore resembles the delegations sustained in Field, 143 U.S. at 693-94, Buttfield, 192 U.S. at 496, and Grimaud, 220 U.S. at 517-19, where the Court recognized that Congress may authorize the Executive to execute a legislative program by filling in details.

The criminal consequences do not change the result. Congress may attach criminal liability to violations of valid administrative regulations. Grimaud, 220 U.S. at 518-19; United States v. Eaton, 144 U.S. 677, 688 (1892). The constitutional question is whether Congress supplied a lawful standard. It did. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 424-27.

II. Congress Also Authorized the Attorney General to Delegate to the DEA

Petitioners’ subdelegation challenge fails because Congress expressly authorized it. Section 871(a) provides that the Attorney General “may delegate any of his functions under” the drug laws. That is direct congressional permission for internal departmental delegation. This Court has long recognized that executive functions may be performed through subordinates when Congress has vested authority in a department head rather than requiring personal action. Shurtleff v. United States, 189 U.S. 311, 318-19 (1903); United States v. George, 228 U.S. 14, 24-25 (1913).

The Attorney General’s regulation assigning temporary scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator therefore falls squarely within the authority Congress granted. The statute did not require the Attorney General to act personally, and Congress affirmatively authorized delegation of departmental functions. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a); Shurtleff, 189 U.S. at 318-19; George, 228 U.S. at 24-25.

III. The CSA Is Especially Appropriate in This Technical, Fast-Moving Field

This Court has repeatedly recognized that Congress may delegate more broadly in complex and rapidly changing areas requiring expertise and speed. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 425-27; American Power & Light Co., 329 U.S. at 105; Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250. Temporary scheduling addresses precisely that problem. Designer drugs can emerge faster than ordinary legislative processes can respond, and Congress responded by creating a narrow emergency mechanism with defined standards and temporal limits. That choice is consistent with the Court’s delegation cases, not in tension with them. J.W. Hampton, 276 U.S. at 409; Field, 143 U.S. at 692-94.

For these reasons, the Court should affirm.
