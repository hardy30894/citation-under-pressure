The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution, Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress. The Constitution sets forth specific requirements for age, citizenship, and residency, and the States are not permitted to add to or alter these qualifications. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969); U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill, 316 Ark. 251, 872 S.W.2d 349 (1994) (dissenting opinion).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are a limitation on the power of the States to regulate the qualifications of congressional candidates. In Powell v. McCormack, the Court held that the House of Representatives could not exclude a duly elected member based on a qualification not specified in the Constitution. The Court reasoned that the Constitution's enumeration of qualifications is exclusive, and that the Framers intended to ensure that the qualifications for Congress would be uniform across the States.

The attempt by Arkansas to impose term limits on its congressional delegation through Amendment 73 is a clear infringement on the exclusive qualifications established by the Constitution. By preventing certain individuals from appearing on the ballot based on their past service in Congress, Amendment 73 effectively adds a new qualification for congressional office. This is impermissible under the Qualifications Clauses, as it alters the constitutional qualifications for membership in Congress. See Cook v. Gralike, 531 U.S. 510 (2001).

The fact that Amendment 73 is couched in terms of "ballot access" rather than an outright bar to candidacy does not save it from constitutional infirmity. The Supreme Court has recognized that the distinction between a qualification and a ballot access restriction is one of form rather than substance. Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983). In this case, the effect of Amendment 73 is to exclude certain individuals from the ballot based on a qualification not specified in the Constitution.

The severability clause in Amendment 73 is insufficient to save the provisions related to state officeholders. The test for severability is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion. Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). In this case, it is impossible to determine whether the voters would have approved term limits for state officeholders without the provision related to congressional officeholders. The sponsors of Amendment 73 created this uncertainty by packaging the two provisions together, and they must bear the burden of that uncertainty.

The joinder of the state and federal term limits provisions in a single amendment is not merely a matter of convenience; it is a systemic fusion that renders the entire amendment suspect. The voters were not given the opportunity to approve or reject the provisions separately, and it is therefore impossible to determine whether they would have approved the state term limits provision standing alone. See Hoban v. Hall, 229 Ark. 416, 316 S.W.2d 185 (1958).

Furthermore, the circuit court correctly determined that the matter was justiciable. The appellees have standing to challenge Amendment 73, as they have a legitimate interest in participating in the political process and supporting candidates of their choice. Anderson v. Celebrezze, supra; Thorsted v. Gregoire, C93-770WD (W.D.D.C. Wash. Feb. 10, 1994). The uncertainty created by Amendment 73 is sufficient to establish a case or controversy, and the circuit court was correct to hear the matter.

The absence of an Enacting Clause in Amendment 73 is also a fatal flaw. The requirement of an Enacting Clause is a fundamental aspect of the initiative process, and its omission renders the amendment void. Amendment 7 to the Arkansas Constitution; Plugge v. McCuen, 310 Ark. 654, 841 S.W.2d 139 (1992). The appellants' argument that the Enacting Clause is not required for constitutional amendments is unpersuasive, as the language of Amendment 7 clearly requires it.

In conclusion, Amendment 73 is unconstitutional because it imposes term limits on congressional candidates, thereby altering the exclusive qualifications established by the Constitution. The severability clause is insufficient to save the provisions related to state officeholders, and the absence of an Enacting Clause is a fatal flaw. The judgment of the circuit court should be affirmed.
