**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court’s Supplemental Jury Instruction on “Shielding” Was Erroneous and Prejudicial.**

The district court’s instruction defining “shielding” as “taking steps to prevent or hinder the detection of illegal aliens by the government” (R. 123 at 2) impermissibly broadened the statutory term beyond its plain meaning and misled the jury. The term “shielding” in 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) must be construed narrowly to require an affirmative act of concealment directed at thwarting detection by immigration authorities. *United States v. Mitchell*, 322 U.S. 65, 67 (1944) (“Statutory terms are to be construed according to their ordinary meaning unless Congress has provided a different definition.”); *United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396, 399 (1930) (“Courts may not enlarge the meaning of a criminal statute beyond its plain terms.”).

The instruction given here, however, allowed the jury to convict based on mere knowledge of the aliens’ status or passive failure to comply with employment verification requirements—neither of which constitutes “shielding.” *United States v. Bramblett*, 348 U.S. 503, 509 (1955) (“A criminal statute must be strictly construed against the government.”); *United States v. Resnick*, 299 U.S. 207, 210 (1936) (“The rule of lenity applies where a statute is ambiguous.”). The instruction thus diluted the government’s burden by permitting conviction for conduct not encompassed by the statute.

This error was compounded by the fact that the court’s definition mirrored non-statutory standards adopted by other circuits, which this Court has repeatedly rejected as overly expansive. *United States v. Sullivan*, 332 U.S. 689, 693 (1948) (“A jury instruction must track the statutory language; it may not import extraneous standards.”); *United States v. Lacher*, 134 U.S. 624, 628 (1890) (“The court’s role is to instruct on the law as written, not to fashion new rules.”). The instruction here did precisely that, inviting the jury to convict based on conduct far removed from the statutory proscription.

Moreover, the error was prejudicial. The jury’s note requesting definitions (R. 122 at 1) suggests it was uncertain about the scope of “shielding.” The court’s instruction, rather than clarifying, misled the jury into broadening the offense. *Bramblett*, 348 U.S. at 510 (“A jury instruction that misleads the jury on the elements of the offense is reversible error.”). The government’s reliance on *United States v. Kim*, 193 F.2d 607 (9th Cir. 1951), is misplaced; that case involved a defendant who actively concealed aliens by providing false documents, not mere inaction or failure to verify employment eligibility. *Kim* does not support the instruction given here.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Intent to “Shield” Illegal Aliens from Detection.**

Even if the instruction were correct, the evidence was insufficient to prove Ye acted with the specific intent to “shield” illegal aliens from detection. To convict under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii), the government must prove Ye took affirmative steps to prevent or hinder detection by immigration authorities. *Mitchell*, 322 U.S. at 68 (“The government must prove more than mere knowledge of an alien’s status; it must prove an act of concealment.”).

Here, the evidence showed only that Ye failed to complete I-9 forms for some workers, paid others in cash, and provided housing and transportation. *Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. at 400 (“Mere failure to comply with administrative requirements does not constitute concealment.”). The government did not prove Ye took steps to hide the aliens from immigration officials—e.g., by providing false documents, altering records, or otherwise obstructing detection. *Bramblett*, 348 U.S. at 511 (“The government must prove an affirmative act of concealment, not mere inaction.”).

The government’s reliance on Ye’s advice to employees to purchase fake documents (R. 120 at 2) is unavailing. Even if true, this conduct occurred *after* the investigation began and does not establish an intent to shield the aliens *before* detection. *Resnick*, 299 U.S. at 211 (“The intent must exist at the time of the alleged offense, not subsequently.”). The evidence thus fails to meet the statutory standard.

**Conclusion**

The district court’s instruction on “shielding” was erroneous and prejudicial, and the evidence was insufficient to support the conviction. The judgment should be reversed.
