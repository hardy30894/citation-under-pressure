**ARGUMENT**

**A single competency standard, not a heightened "reasoned choice" test, governs a defendant's capacity to waive counsel or plead guilty.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require courts to apply a separate, more demanding competency standard before accepting a waiver of counsel or a guilty plea. This Court has articulated one constitutional floor for competency in criminal proceedings, and nothing in that standard, or in this Court's waiver jurisprudence, calls for a second, higher threshold keyed to the particular decision a defendant makes.

In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), this Court held that the test for competency is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. That formulation was not limited to the defendant's capacity to sit through trial; it defines the mental competence required to participate meaningfully in the proceeding against him, whatever form that participation takes. This Court reaffirmed that a defendant may not be tried, convicted, or sentenced while he "lacks capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense." *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 378 (1966). Nothing in *Dusky* or *Pate* suggests that a defendant who is competent to stand trial under this standard is nonetheless incompetent to decide, with the same cognitive faculties, to waive counsel or to plead guilty. The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" test manufactures a distinction the competency standard itself does not draw.

The separate protections that guard against an improvident waiver come not from a heightened competency rule but from this Court's longstanding requirement that any waiver of a constitutional right be knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court explained that "the determination of whether there has been an intelligent waiver of the right to counsel must depend, in each case, upon the particular facts and circumstances surrounding that case, including the background, experience, and conduct of the accused." *Id.* at 464. That inquiry protects the defendant fully: it requires the trial court to satisfy itself that the defendant understands what he is giving up, not that he meets some elevated psychiatric threshold beyond ordinary competency. Likewise, in *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), the Court recognized that "[t]he Constitution does not force a lawyer upon a defendant. He may waive his Constitutional right to assistance of counsel if he knows what he is doing and his choice is made with eyes open." *Id.* at 279. The touchstone is knowledge and voluntariness, assessed under the totality of the circumstances, not a freestanding mental-capacity standard divorced from competency doctrine. *See Culombe v. Connecticut*, 367 U.S. 568, 602 (1961) (voluntariness determined "in the light of the totality of the circumstances").

This Court's guilty-plea decisions confirm the point. In *Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), the Court upheld an uncounseled guilty plea because the record showed the plea was entered with understanding of the charge and the consequences—again, a voluntariness and comprehension inquiry, not a separate competency hurdle. And in *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), the plurality required that a waiver of counsel be made "with an apprehension of the nature of the charges, the statutory offenses included within them, the range of allowable punishments thereunder, possible defenses to the charges and circumstances in mitigation thereof, and all other facts essential to a broad understanding of the whole matter." *Id.* at 724. That searching inquiry into the *voluntariness and comprehension* of the waiver—not the defendant's baseline mental competence—is what due process demands. So too in *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), the Court insisted that "the record must affirmatively disclose that a defendant who pleaded guilty entered his plea understandingly and voluntarily," *id.* at 242, again framing the safeguard as one of voluntariness and understanding, not heightened competency.

To be sure, this Court has recognized that a defendant's mental condition may bear differently on different decisions in a criminal case; in *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), the Court observed that a defendant found fit to stand trial might still require counsel because of his mental disturbance. *Id.* at 108. But *Massey* addressed whether counsel should be furnished as a procedural safeguard for a mentally disturbed defendant—it did not hold, and this Court has never held, that due process imposes a distinct, higher competency standard before a waiver or plea may be accepted. The safeguard *Massey* endorsed is precisely the safeguard already built into the waiver inquiry under *Johnson v. Zerbst* and *Von Moltke*: rigorous scrutiny of the voluntariness and comprehension of the defendant's choice.

Because the *Dusky* competency standard already requires that a defendant possess a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and the ability to consult with counsel, and because the waiver inquiry under *Johnson v. Zerbst*, *Adams*, *Von Moltke*, and *Boykin* independently ensures that any relinquishment of rights is knowing and voluntary, no additional "reasoned choice" competency standard is required by the Due Process Clause. The court below erred in imposing one.
