ARGUMENT

The district court’s failure to advise petitioner of his right to appeal did not entitle him to habeas relief because he undisputedly knew of that right. The error was harmless, and the court of appeals correctly affirmed.

**I. The Right to Appeal Is Statutory, Not Constitutional, and Rule 32 Violations Are Nonjurisdictional**

The right to appeal in federal criminal cases is a creature of statute, not the Constitution. *Rodriquez v. United States*, 395 U.S. 327, 329 (1969). A district court’s failure to comply with the advice requirement of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not implicate any fundamental constitutional right. *See Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (holding that a Rule 32 violation is “not a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice” and is not jurisdictional). Because the error is nonjurisdictional, it is subject to harmless-error review.

**II. Harmless-Error Analysis Applies to Rule 32 Violations**

The harmless-error standard for nonconstitutional errors is well established. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) (error is harmless unless it “affect[s] the substantial rights of the parties”). Here, the error did not affect petitioner’s substantial rights. The district court found, and petitioner does not contest, that he knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. The purpose of Rule 32’s advice requirement—to ensure that defendants are informed of their appeal rights—was fully served. *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428 (explaining that Rule 32 “is intended to insure that a defendant is aware of his right to appeal”).

Even constitutional errors may be harmless when the defendant has actual knowledge of the right at stake. *See Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967) (recognizing that some constitutional errors may be harmless). If that is true for constitutional rights, it is certainly true for a purely procedural rule like Rule 32.

**III. Actual Knowledge of the Appeal Right Renders the Omission Harmless**

This Court and the courts of appeals have consistently held that a defendant’s actual knowledge of his appeal right cures a district court’s failure to advise him. *United States v. Harr*, 271 F.2d 943, 945 (3d Cir. 1959) (per curiam) (“Since the defendant knew of his right to appeal, the failure of the court to advise him was not prejudicial.”); *United States v. Bays*, 266 F.2d 793, 794 (6th Cir. 1959) (per curiam) (same); *United States v. Sassano*, 226 F.2d 567, 568 (7th Cir. 1955) (per curiam) (same); *see also United States v. Muehlbauer*, 242 F.2d 847, 848 (2d Cir. 1957) (per curiam) (holding that failure to advise was harmless where defendant “had actual knowledge of his right to appeal”). These decisions reflect the commonsense principle that the rule’s purpose is satisfied when the defendant already knows what the court would have told him.

**IV. Petitioner’s Failure to Appeal After Knowing of His Right Constitutes a Waiver**

A defendant who knows of his right to appeal and chooses not to exercise it waives that right. *Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189, 200-01 (1943) (holding that a defendant may waive procedural rights by conduct); *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 439 (1963) (holding that a defendant’s “deliberate bypass” of appeal procedures bars habeas relief). Petitioner had actual knowledge and elected not to appeal. He cannot later use habeas corpus to circumvent that deliberate choice.

**V. Habeas Corpus Is Not a Substitute for a Direct Appeal**

A defendant who fails to appeal when he is aware of the right cannot obtain habeas relief for a nonjurisdictional procedural error. *Price v. Johnston*, 334 U.S. 266, 284 (1948) (habeas corpus “does not open the door to review of every question of law” that could have been raised on appeal); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947) (holding that habeas relief is not available to review errors that could have been raised on direct appeal, absent a showing of cause). Petitioner has shown no cause for his failure to appeal. He knew of his right; he simply chose not to act.

**Conclusion**

The district court’s failure to advise petitioner of his right to appeal was harmless because petitioner knew of that right. The purpose of Rule 32 was fulfilled, and the error did not affect petitioner’s substantial rights. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
