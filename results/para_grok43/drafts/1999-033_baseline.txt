The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment secures to every person “the right to the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. That guarantee “is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr., Inc., 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985). It therefore presupposes differential treatment “between classes of individuals.” Nordlinger v. Hahn, 505 U.S. 1, 10 (1992).

A plaintiff who alleges neither membership in a protected class nor disparate treatment of a defined group fails to state a claim under this provision. The Clause does not “assure uniformity of judicial decisions” or “immunity from personal spite or inconsistent judgment of the courts of a state.” American Railway Express Co. v. Kentucky, 273 U.S. 269, 273 (1927). Nor does it constitutionalize every instance in which a state actor treats one individual differently from another. See Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) (“[A]n erroneous or mistaken performance of [a state] duty may constitute a breach of [state] law but it does not constitute a denial of due process of law.”).

Recognizing a “class of one” action would convert the Clause into “a font of tort law.” Paul v. Davis, 424 U.S. 693, 701 (1976). Every discretionary decision—whether to grant a permit, connect a water line, or enforce an ordinance—could be recast as an equal-protection violation simply by alleging that the decisionmaker acted out of “spite” or without a “rational basis.” Such a rule would “make of the Fourteenth Amendment a font of federal tort law” and “trivialize” the guarantee. Id. at 701.

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary holding—that a plaintiff may prevail by showing state action “motivated solely by a spiteful effort to ‘get’ him”—finds no support in this Court’s precedents. It rests instead on an unwarranted extension of cases addressing class-based discrimination. See, e.g., Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Comm’n, 488 U.S. 336, 345 (1989) (invalidating systematic undervaluation of comparable properties). Those decisions presuppose a cognizable class; they do not authorize federal courts to superintend every individualized exercise of state authority.

Because respondent pleaded neither class membership nor group-based animus, the complaint fails to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
