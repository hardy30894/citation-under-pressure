**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that Harold Wasek failed to state a claim for hostile work environment harassment under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), and Michigan’s Elliot-Larsen Civil Rights Act (“EL-CRA”), MCL 37.2101 et seq., because the record contains sufficient evidence of severe and pervasive conduct that a reasonable person would find objectively hostile or abusive, and that the conduct was motivated by Wasek’s gender. The district court’s dismissal of Wasek’s claim based on the absence of proof that the harassment was *because of* his gender misapplies the law and ignores binding precedent establishing that gender-based conduct—particularly sexualized, gendered, and degrading behavior—can constitute actionable harassment even when the harasser is not motivated by animus toward the victim’s gender per se, but rather by the victim’s perceived gender nonconformity or gender identity.

**I. The District Court Misapplied the Standard for Hostile Work Environment Claims by Requiring Proof of Discriminatory Intent Based on Gender, When the Law Requires Only That the Conduct Be Gender-Based.**

Title VII prohibits employment discrimination “because of . . . sex,” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), and the Supreme Court has long recognized that this includes harassment that is “so severe or pervasive as to create a work environment that a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive.” Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993). The critical inquiry is not whether the harasser harbored animus toward the victim’s gender, but whether the conduct was “because of” sex—i.e., whether the harassment would not have occurred but for the victim’s sex. See Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc., 523 U.S. 75, 80 (1998) (“[T]he statutory prohibition against discrimination ‘because of . . . sex’ applies to harassment that is based on sex, regardless of the harasser’s intent.”).

Here, the record demonstrates that Ottobre’s conduct was explicitly sexualized and gendered. He repeatedly touched Wasek in a sexual manner—grabbing his buttocks, poking him in the rear with a hammer handle and a sucker rod—while making sexually suggestive remarks such as “you’ve got a pretty mouth,” “boy you have pretty lips,” and “you know you like it sweetheart.” These comments were not neutral or casual; they were directed at Wasek’s physical appearance in a manner that objectified him and implied a sexual interest. The conduct was not merely “boorish” or “offensive” in a general sense, but was specifically gendered and sexual in nature. As the Supreme Court held in *Oncale*, “the same conduct may be evidence of sex discrimination even if the harasser is not motivated by sexual desire.” 523 U.S. at 81. The conduct here was not about Wasek’s job performance or personality; it was about his body, his appearance, and his perceived gender expression—conduct that would not have occurred but for Wasek’s sex.

Moreover, the harassment was not isolated or trivial. It was repeated over time, escalated in severity, and included physical contact and threats of violence. The conduct culminated in Wasek’s forced resignation after he was repeatedly ignored by supervisors and told to “kick [Ottobre’s] ass” or “duke it out”—responses that demonstrate a complete failure to address the harassment and instead encourage violence. The Supreme Court has made clear that “a hostile work environment claim may be established even if the harassment is not motivated by sexual desire, so long as it is based on sex.” *Oncale*, 523 U.S. at 81. The conduct here was based on Wasek’s sex—specifically, his male sex—and was carried out in a manner that targeted him as a man through sexualized, gendered, and degrading behavior.

**II. The Employer’s Failure to Respond to Complaints Constitutes a Failure to Exercise Reasonable Care, Satisfying the Employer Liability Element Under Title VII.**

Under Title VII, an employer may be liable for harassment by a coworker if it knew or should have known of the harassment and failed to take prompt and effective remedial action. See Faragher v. City of Boca Raton, 524 U.S. 775, 780 (1998). Here, Wasek reported the harassment to his supervisor, Pat Tripp, who observed the conduct firsthand and laughed. Tripp explicitly warned Wasek not to “make waves” or “whine,” and threatened to retaliate by placing him on a “starvation schedule” if he reported to higher management. When Wasek finally reported to Dick Smillie and Bill Strong, both supervisors responded with derision and hostility—Smillie told Wasek to “kick [Ottobre’s] ass,” and Strong suggested they “duke it out.” These responses were not merely dismissive; they were affirmatively hostile and encouraged violence, demonstrating a complete failure to take the complaint seriously.

The employer’s inaction was not merely passive—it was active and affirmatively obstructive. By discouraging reporting and ridiculing Wasek’s complaints, the employer created a climate in which harassment was tolerated and retaliation was threatened. This conduct satisfies the *Faragher* standard for employer liability: the employer had actual or constructive knowledge of the harassment and failed to take prompt and effective action. See *Faragher*, 524 U.S. at 780–81.

**Conclusion**

The district court erred in dismissing Wasek’s claim on the ground that he could not prove the harassment was “because of” his gender. The law does not require proof of animus toward the victim’s gender; it requires only that the conduct be based on sex. The record here shows a pattern of severe, pervasive, and gendered harassment that a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive. The employer’s failure to respond to repeated complaints, and its encouragement of violence, constitutes a failure to exercise reasonable care. For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
