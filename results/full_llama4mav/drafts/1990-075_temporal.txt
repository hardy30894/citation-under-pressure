The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The Constitution grants Congress the power to make laws, but it does not prohibit Congress from obtaining assistance from other branches of government. As the Supreme Court held in J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394 (1928), Congress may delegate authority to another branch when it lays down "an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform." Id. at 409.

The CSA provides an intelligible principle guiding the Attorney General's authority to temporarily schedule substances. The statute requires the Attorney General to consider specific factors, such as the history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health, before temporarily scheduling a substance. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4), (5), and (6). This guidance is sufficient to satisfy the non-delegation doctrine, as the Supreme Court has upheld delegations with similar guidance. See Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414 (1944) (upholding delegation to the Price Administrator to regulate commodity prices with guidance to "generally fair and equitable" and "effectuate the purposes of the Emergency Price Control Act").

The Attorney General's authority to temporarily schedule substances is also limited by the requirement that the substance pose "an imminent hazard to the public safety." 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This limitation provides a clear standard for the Attorney General to follow and ensures that the delegation is not overly broad.

Furthermore, the CSA allows for judicial review of the Attorney General's actions, although it is limited by 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6). However, as the Supreme Court held in Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245 (1947), the existence of "well-known and generally acceptable standards" in the field being regulated can mitigate the need for strict judicial review. The regulation of controlled substances is a complex field with established standards, and the CSA provides sufficient guidance to the Attorney General.

The Attorney General's sub-delegation of authority to the DEA is also authorized. The CSA grants the Attorney General the power to delegate his functions to other officials, 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), and the Attorney General has done so through regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). This sub-delegation is consistent with the Supreme Court's holding in United States v. Chemical Foundation, 272 U.S. 1 (1926), that an executive officer may delegate his authority to a subordinate when authorized by statute or regulation.

In conclusion, the CSA's delegation of authority to the Attorney General to temporarily schedule substances is constitutional. The statute provides an intelligible principle guiding the Attorney General's actions, and the delegation is limited by clear standards. The sub-delegation of authority to the DEA is also authorized. Therefore, the Toubys' convictions should be upheld.
