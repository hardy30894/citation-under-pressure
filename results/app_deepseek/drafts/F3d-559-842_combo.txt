**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Genuine Disputes of Material Fact Exist on Every Element of the Mouas’ Rescission Claim.**

**A. The Three-Year Rescission Period Applies Whenever a Lender Fails to Make Material Disclosures or Provide Proper Notice.**

Congress made the right to rescind automatic and self-executing: the obligor “may rescind the transaction … until midnight of the third business day following the consummation of the transaction or the delivery of the information and rescission forms required under this section … whichever is later.” 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a). If the required notice or material disclosures are not delivered, the right “expires three years after the date of consummation.” *Id.* The disclosure duties are not empty formalities. The Supreme Court has long read remedial statutes broadly to effectuate their protective purposes. *J.I. Case Co. v. Borak*, 377 U.S. 426, 432 (1964) (private remedies are a necessary supplement to administrative enforcement); *SEC v. Capital Gains Research Bureau, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 180, 186 (1963) (disclosure requirements embody the “highest standard of business ethics”). The duty to deal candidly with a party whose financial livelihood is at stake is an old one. *Strong v. Repide*, 213 U.S. 419, 431 (1909) (special circumstances may impose a duty to disclose material facts). The Mouas, facing imminent foreclosure and a redemption deadline, were precisely the vulnerable parties the disclosure laws protect.

**B. Rand Failed to Make the Required HOEPA Advance Disclosures, and the Record Presents Triable Issues.**

The Mouas testified they received no advance HOEPA disclosures before the April 22, 2005 closing. Rand’s only response is its own allegation of a last-minute waiver. But waiver is an affirmative defense, and the “waiver” here raises issues for a jury. The statement was typed by Rand, handed to Manisy Moua, who testified she copied it without understanding it. Rand’s own evidence shows the waiver was sought only because Rand had failed to obtain the sheriff’s redemption payoff—a consequence of Rand’s own scheduling choices, not of any bona fide emergency independent of Rand. A party may not create an exigency and then invoke it to strip another of statutory rights. *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. 342, 349 (1874) (a party may not benefit from delay or concealment it caused); *Glus v. Brooklyn Eastern District Terminal*, 359 U.S. 231, 232 (1959) (same principle); *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 396 (1946) (equity will not permit a statute to be used as an instrument of injustice).

The two versions of the Notice of Right to Cancel deepen the dispute. One version contains only a “Receipt”; the other adds a “Confirmation” falsely stating that “[m]ore than 3 business days have elapsed” since the new transaction. All signatures are dated April 22, 2005—the day of closing. A borrower cannot knowingly confirm that a three-day period has elapsed when it has not. The Confirmation was at best a product of lender error and at worst an attempt to extinguish a right the borrower had not yet enjoyed. Either way, summary judgment was improper.

**C. The Prepayment Penalty Violates HOEPA and Independently Extends the Rescission Period.**

The Note contains a prepayment penalty that HOEPA expressly prohibits for this type of loan. A contract provision that conflicts with a statute is unenforceable. *Henningsen v. Bloomfield Motors, Inc.*, 32 N.J. 358, 161 A.2d 69, 84–85 (1960) (courts refuse to enforce terms that violate public policy). The inclusion of the penalty is itself a material non-disclosure, because borrowers deciding whether to refinance would plainly care about a financial penalty hidden in their paperwork. *List v. Fashion Park, Inc.*, 340 F.2d 457, 462 (2d Cir. 1965) (materiality is whether a “reasonable man would attach importance” to the fact in determining his course of action).

**D. This Contract Is One of Adhesion, and Equity Must Intervene.**

The Mouas had no meaningful choice: they were in foreclosure, their redemption period was expiring, and they had been told a loan would rescue them. The loan’s rate step-up, payment shock, and prohibited prepayment penalty were dictated entirely by Rand. As the court recognized, unconscionability exists where there is “an absence of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party.” *Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co.*, 350 F.2d 445, 449 (D.C. Cir. 1965). That description fits this record. Lenders who deal with unsophisticated borrowers must be held to strict standards of candor. *FTC v. Standard Education Soc’y*, 302 U.S. 112, 116 (1937).

**II. CONCLUSION**

The district court’s judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial on the Mouas’ rescission claim, or for entry of judgment in their favor, because Rand failed to make required disclosures, obtained an ineffective waiver, and included a prohibited prepayment penalty—all of which extended the three-day rescission period to three years.
