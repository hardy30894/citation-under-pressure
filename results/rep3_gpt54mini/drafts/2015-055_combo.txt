**Argument**

Johnson announced a substantive rule that must apply retroactively on collateral review. A new rule is retroactive when it alters the range of conduct or the class of persons the law punishes, rather than merely regulating procedure. *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), established that a conviction obtained under an unconstitutional statute is void; a court “cannot give it validity.” Id. at 376. Likewise, *Hawker v. New York*, 170 U.S. 189 (1898), recognized that a criminal law may not constitutionally enlarge punishment beyond the legislature’s lawful power. Johnson did exactly that: it narrowed ACCA by invalidating one basis for the fifteen-year mandatory minimum.

This Court has long treated decisions that withdraw criminal punishment from a category of conduct as fully applicable to cases still open on direct or collateral review. In *United States v. Chambers*, 291 U.S. 217 (1934), the Court held that a conviction under an invalid statute could not stand. In *Norton v. Shelby County*, 118 U.S. 425 (1886), the Court explained that an unconstitutional act “is, in legal contemplation, as inoperative as though it had never been passed.” Id. at 442. And in *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court emphasized the habeas court’s duty to correct unconstitutional custody. Id. at 430-31. Johnson fits that line: it does not merely alter a sentencing procedure; it declares that the residual clause cannot constitutionally be used to impose ACCA’s enhanced punishment.

The government’s contrary position misunderstands the nature of vagueness invalidation. A vague criminal law violates due process because it fails to give fair notice and invites arbitrary enforcement. *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385 (1926), held that a penal statute must be sufficiently definite so ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited. Id. at 391. *United States v. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81 (1921), invalidated a criminal prohibition for lack of ascertainable standards. Id. at 89. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451 (1939), likewise struck a statute that left citizens “without a reasonably ascertainable standard of guilt.” Id. at 453. Johnson’s holding that ACCA’s residual clause is void for vagueness therefore means the clause was never a valid basis for punishment. Under *Norton* and *Siebold*, that substantive invalidity applies retroactively.

The traditional distinction between direct and collateral review does not save the sentence. Retroactivity is compelled when the new rule “takes away no vested right” of the sovereign to imprison under an unconstitutional law. *Boyd v. United States*, 116 U.S. 616 (1886), explained that penal enforcement cannot rest on unconstitutional means. Id. at 635. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), likewise held that punishment exceeding constitutional limits must fall. Id. at 367-68. Johnson is of the same kind: it removes a constitutionally defective sentencing basis and thereby forbids the enhanced punishment Welch received.

The Eleventh Circuit’s denial of retroactivity also conflicts with foundational habeas principles. The writ exists to test the legality of detention. *Ex parte Yerger*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 85 (1869), described habeas as the “great writ” to secure liberty from unlawful restraint. Id. at 95. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874), held that once a court imposes punishment beyond lawful authority, continued custody is unlawful. Id. at 176. Welch is serving a sentence enhanced by a clause the Supreme Court has now declared unconstitutional; that custody is no more lawful than the sentence in *Lange*.

Johnson should therefore be applied retroactively to cases on collateral review. Because Welch’s ACCA sentence depended on the residual clause, his enhanced mandatory minimum cannot stand. The judgment should be reversed.
