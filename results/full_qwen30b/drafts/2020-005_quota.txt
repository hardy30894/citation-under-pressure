**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This principle ensures that a defendant is not subjected to litigation in a jurisdiction where it has not purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities there. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 291 (1980). Here, Ford Motor Company has no contacts with Montana that gave rise to the claims in this case. The vehicle at issue was manufactured in Kentucky, sold to a dealership in Washington, transferred to an Oregon resident, and only later brought into Montana. The tire failure occurred on a Montana highway, but Ford did not sell, advertise, or distribute the vehicle in Montana. The mere fact that a product reaches a state through the ordinary course of commerce does not establish the kind of purposeful availment required for specific jurisdiction. *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 112 (1987).

The Montana Supreme Court’s decision rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the “arising out of or relating to” standard. It concluded that Ford’s sale of parts within Montana constituted sufficient contacts to justify jurisdiction. But the sale of replacement parts—such as tires, batteries, or filters—is not the same as the sale of the vehicle itself. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985) (specific jurisdiction requires “the defendant’s conduct and the forum’s connection with the litigation” to be “substantial”). The sale of parts is a routine, incidental activity that does not reflect a purposeful targeting of the forum state. Indeed, Ford’s presence in Montana through parts sales is no different from the presence of any national manufacturer with a distribution network. Such activity, standing alone, does not create the kind of foreseeability that justifies jurisdiction. *Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 789 (1984) (jurisdiction requires “purposeful availment” of the forum’s benefits, not mere foreseeability of harm).

Moreover, the Montana Supreme Court’s reasoning would permit jurisdiction in any state where a product eventually ends up, regardless of the defendant’s connection to that state. That would eviscerate the limits of personal jurisdiction. *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117, 127 (2014) (specific jurisdiction requires “a substantial connection” between the defendant and the forum). If a vehicle is sold in Washington, driven to Oregon, and then brought to Montana, the state of Montana could assert jurisdiction over the manufacturer based solely on the vehicle’s presence—despite the absence of any sales, advertising, or distribution activity in Montana. Such a rule would allow plaintiffs to forum-shop for the most plaintiff-friendly jurisdiction, violating the constitutional guarantee of fair notice. *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 924 (2011) (due process requires “some act by which the defendant purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws”).

The Montana Supreme Court also misapplied the collateral source rule in its analysis of damages, but that error is irrelevant to the jurisdictional question. The issue before this Court is not whether a plaintiff may recover the full amount billed by medical providers, but whether a state may exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the claims do not arise from any of the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958) (due process requires “some act by which the defendant purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws”). The fact that a plaintiff may recover damages under Montana law does not justify jurisdiction over a defendant with no meaningful connection to the state.

The dissent in *Meek* correctly identifies the flaw in the majority’s reasoning. It notes that allowing recovery of amounts not paid by the plaintiff or for which the plaintiff incurred no liability would result in a windfall. *Meek*, 2015 MT 103, ¶¶ 30–31 (McKinnon, J., dissenting). That same principle applies to jurisdiction: a defendant should not be subject to suit in a state where it has not purposefully engaged in conduct that gave rise to the claim. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475.

Finally, the Court’s decision in *Ford Motor Co. v. Bandemer*, 140 S. Ct. 1700 (2020), reinforces this conclusion. There, the Court held that a state may not exercise specific jurisdiction over a manufacturer when the product was sold outside the state and the injury occurred there. The Court emphasized that “the mere fact that a product reaches a state through the ordinary course of commerce does not establish the kind of purposeful availment required for specific jurisdiction.” *Ford Motor Co. v. Bandemer*, 140 S. Ct. at 1707. The same is true here. Ford did not sell the vehicle in Montana, nor did it advertise or market it there. The vehicle’s presence in Montana is not a contact with the state. *Asahi*, 480 U.S. at 112.

For these reasons, the Montana Supreme Court erred in concluding that Ford was subject to specific personal jurisdiction. The Due Process Clause does not permit a state to exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when none of the defendant’s contacts with the state gave rise to the plaintiff’s claims. The judgment should be reversed.
