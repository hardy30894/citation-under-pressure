**ARGUMENT**

**I. The reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date.**

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), permits the Attorney General to reinstate a prior deportation order against an alien who "has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed." The statute does not expressly address whether it applies to re-entries that occurred before its enactment. However, this Court has long held that statutes affecting substantive rights are presumed not to apply retroactively unless Congress clearly expresses such an intent. *United States v. Magness, 356 U.S. 525, 529 (1958)* ("[A] statute will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless such construction is required by explicit language or by necessary implication."); *Shaw v. United States, 332 U.S. 24, 31 (1947)* ("[A] statute ought not to be construed to operate retroactively unless the language is so clear that it would be impossible to misunderstand it.").

Here, the reinstatement statute does not explicitly state that it applies to pre-enactment re-entries. The absence of such language compels the conclusion that Congress did not intend retroactive application. *See Magness, 356 U.S. at 529*. Moreover, the statute’s structure—conditioning reinstatement on a post-enactment re-entry—supports this interpretation. *See id.* ("[W]here rights have been once vested, they are not to be divested by subsequent legislation unless the intention is clear.").

The government’s contrary position—that the statute applies to all illegal re-entries, regardless of when they occurred—would impermissibly divest aliens of substantive rights without clear congressional direction. *See Magness, 356 U.S. at 529*. This Court should reject such an expansive reading. *See id.*

**II. Even if the statute applies to pre-enactment re-entries, its application is impermissibly retroactive.**

A statute operates retroactively if it "takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability in respect to transactions or considerations already past." *Shaw, 332 U.S. at 31*. The reinstatement statute does precisely that by reviving a prior deportation order and stripping the alien of eligibility for discretionary relief based on pre-enactment conduct. *See Magness, 356 U.S. at 529* ("[A] statute which imposes a new duty or attaches a new disability to a past transaction is retroactive.").

This Court has repeatedly invalidated retroactive statutes that impair vested rights. *See, e.g., United States v. Hudson, 299 U.S. 498, 503 (1937)* ("[A] statute cannot be applied retroactively so as to impair a vested right."); *United States v. Chicago, M., St. P. & P.R. Co., 282 U.S. 311, 338 (1931)* ("[A] statute will not be construed to have retroactive effect in the absence of clear language manifesting such purpose."). Here, the reinstatement statute attaches a new disability—deportation without a hearing—based on pre-enactment conduct, thereby impairing any vested right to challenge removal. *See Hudson, 299 U.S. at 503*.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103 (1801)*, is misplaced. *Peggy* held that a statute applies to pending cases unless it expressly states otherwise, but it does not permit retroactive application to past transactions. *Id.* at 110 ("[A]n act of the legislature may be considered as affecting cases pending, unless it is expressly made retrospective."). The reinstatement statute here does not merely affect pending cases—it revives a prior deportation order based on past conduct, which *Peggy* does not sanction. *See id.*

**III. The Tenth Circuit’s "no protectable expectation" rationale is legally unsound.**

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that petitioner had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status." *See Canales-Vargas v. Ashcroft, 340 F.3d 1213, 1218 (10th Cir. 2003)* (citing *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales, 421 F.3d 1295 (10th Cir. 2005)*). This reasoning conflates retroactivity with prospective application. *See Magness, 356 U.S. at 529* ("[A] statute which attaches a new disability to a past transaction is retroactive, regardless of whether the party had a 'protectable expectation.'"). The reinstatement statute’s retroactive effect is determined by its impact on past conduct, not by the alien’s subjective expectations. *See id.*

Moreover, the Tenth Circuit’s approach ignores this Court’s precedent that retroactivity analysis focuses on whether the statute "takes away or impairs vested rights." *Shaw, 332 U.S. at 31*. Petitioner’s right to challenge removal based on pre-enactment conduct is a vested right that cannot be impaired retroactively. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment below and hold that the reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date. Even if the statute applies, its retroactive application violates due process. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
