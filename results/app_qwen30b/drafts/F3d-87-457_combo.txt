**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS OF CHAPTER 93-121 VIOLATE THE FIRST AMENDMENT BY CHILLING PROTECTED SPEECH IN A MANNER THAT IS NOT JUSTIFIED BY A SUBSTANTIAL GOVERNMENTAL INTEREST.

The First Amendment protects the right to engage in political expression, including the communication of ideas to government officials in an effort to influence legislation. *Bates v. State Bar of Arizona*, 361 U.S. 516, 524 (1960). This protection extends to the activities of professional lobbyists, who serve as intermediaries between private interests and the legislative process. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941). The disclosure requirements imposed by Chapter 93-121, however, go far beyond what is necessary to serve any legitimate governmental interest and instead create a substantial chilling effect on protected speech.

The statute mandates that lobbyists disclose all expenditures—regardless of whether they are made by the lobbyist or the principal—along with the source of funds, and requires categorization of expenditures into specific types, including “food and beverages, entertainment, research, communication, media advertising, publications, travel, and lodging.” Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a). This sweeping mandate compels the disclosure of information that is not inherently related to the content of the speech but instead exposes the financial relationships and operational details of lobbying efforts. Such disclosure is not merely a neutral administrative requirement; it is a form of compelled speech that forces individuals to reveal private information about their associations and funding sources.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that compelled disclosure of information about one’s associations may violate the First Amendment. *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 460 (1958). In that case, the Court held that requiring the NAACP to disclose its membership list violated the right to freedom of association, stating that “the right to associate is an aspect of the liberty protected by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.” Id. at 460. The Court emphasized that “the freedom to engage in association for the advancement of beliefs and ideas is an inseparable aspect of the ‘liberty’ assured by the Due Process Clause.” Id. at 461. The disclosure of lobbying expenditures and funding sources under Chapter 93-121 similarly implicates the right to associate and to communicate without fear of public exposure or retaliation.

Moreover, the statute’s requirement that expenditures be disclosed by category—especially those involving “entertainment,” “food and beverages,” and “travel”—creates a risk that legitimate, constitutionally protected activities will be mischaracterized as improper or corrupt. *Bates v. State Bar of Arizona*, 361 U.S. at 524. The mere act of meeting with a legislator over dinner, for example, may be subject to scrutiny and public interpretation under the statute, even when such meetings are routine and non-coercive. This risk of misinterpretation and public shaming constitutes a significant deterrent to political expression. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 574.

The government’s interest in preventing corruption or the appearance of corruption is not sufficient to justify such broad disclosure requirements. *United States v. Robel*, 389 U.S. 258, 264 (1967). The Court has repeatedly held that “the mere possibility of abuse does not justify the suppression of constitutionally protected expression.” *Bates v. State Bar of Arizona*, 361 U.S. at 524. The state has not demonstrated that the disclosure of expenditures by category is necessary to prevent corruption, nor has it shown that less intrusive alternatives—such as targeted investigations or audits—would be ineffective. *NAACP v. Alabama*, 357 U.S. at 460. The statute’s breadth renders it facially invalid under the First Amendment.

II. THE BAN ON CONTINGENCY-FEE LOBBYING IS UNCONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE IT DIRECTLY RESTRICTS THE CONTENT OF PROTECTED SPEECH.

The prohibition on contingency-fee lobbying—whereby a lobbyist may not receive compensation contingent upon legislative success—constitutes a direct restriction on the content of political speech. *Bates v. State Bar of Arizona*, 361 U.S. at 524. The Supreme Court has consistently held that content-based restrictions on speech are subject to strict scrutiny and are “presumptively invalid.” *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 574. The state has not demonstrated that this restriction is necessary to serve a compelling governmental interest.

The statute’s ban on contingency fees is not merely a regulation of conduct; it is a regulation of the manner in which political speech is funded. By prohibiting compensation tied to legislative outcomes, the statute effectively penalizes those who are most confident in their ability to influence legislation. This is not a neutral regulation of economic activity but a content-based restriction on the form of political expression. *United States v. Robel*, 389 U.S. at 264. The Court has held that “the government may not regulate speech based on its message, its ideas, its subject matter, or its content.” *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 574.

The state’s argument that the ban prevents corruption is insufficient. *NAACP v. Alabama*, 357 U.S. at 460. The mere possibility of corruption does not justify the suppression of protected speech. *Bates v. State Bar of Arizona*, 361 U.S. at 524. Moreover, the ban is not narrowly tailored. It applies equally to lobbyists who are engaged in legitimate, non-coercive advocacy and those who may be attempting to influence legislation through improper means. *United States v. Robel*, 389 U.S. at 264. The statute fails to distinguish between different types of lobbying activities or levels of risk, rendering it overbroad.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects the right to engage in political advocacy, even when such advocacy is funded by contingent compensation. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 574. The state’s interest in regulating lobbying does not justify a blanket prohibition on contingency fees. *NAACP v. Alabama*, 357 U.S.
