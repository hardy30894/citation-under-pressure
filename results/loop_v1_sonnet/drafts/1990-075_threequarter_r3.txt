# ARGUMENT SECTION — BRIEF FOR RESPONDENT

## I. Section 811(h) Satisfies the Intelligible-Principle Standard That Governs Congressional Delegations.

Article I vests “all legislative powers” in Congress, but that provision has never been read to forbid Congress from enlisting the Executive's assistance in administering a complex regulatory scheme. This Court settled the controlling test more than sixty years ago in the rate-making context: “If Congress shall lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to fix such rates is directed to conform, such legislative action is not a forbidden delegation of legislative power.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). That same principle governs delegations generally, and Section 811(h) satisfies it many times over.

Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. It specified the factors he must weigh — the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)) — required a finding that scheduling is necessary “to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” § 811(h)(1), mandated notice to and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, § 811(h)(4), and capped the duration of any temporary schedule at one year, extendable by only six months, § 811(h)(2). That is precisely the kind of governing standard whose *absence* doomed the statute in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935) (delegation invalid where statute failed to establish any “criterion to govern the President's course”). The companion case, *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 521-22 (1935), likewise invalidated a delegation containing no comparable limiting standards. Those decisions turned on the total absence of standards, not on the mere fact that criminal consequences might follow from the delegatee's action; Section 811(h) supplies exactly the standards those statutes lacked.

The Court has consistently sustained delegations far less confined than this one when Congress faced a fast-moving regulatory problem. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 423-27, 432 (1944), the Court upheld the Price Administrator's authority to fix “fair and equitable” wartime prices, recognizing that the rapidity of change in the regulated subject justified broad executive discretion. The same is true here: Congress found that clandestine chemists could synthesize new “designer drugs” faster than the ordinary scheduling process could respond, which is precisely why it authorized *temporary* action pending completion of full notice-and-comment proceedings. Likewise, in *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 216, 225-26 (1943), the Court held that the statutory standard governing broadcast licensing was an adequate guide for the Federal Communications Commission's exercise of delegated authority, and in *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946), it approved the similarly open-ended “fair and equitable” standard for utility reorganizations. Section 811(h)'s multi-factor, imminent-hazard standard is considerably more specific than either.

Nor is the absence of exhaustive technical detail fatal. In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court upheld a conservatorship statute containing scant express standards because the field possessed “well established and generally accepted standards” on which the delegatee could draw; the same is true of the pharmacological and law-enforcement criteria Congress specified in § 811(c). And in *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-86 (1948), the Court sustained the Renegotiation Act's authorization to recapture “excessive profits” — a standard with no fixed numerical content — because Congress reasonably relied on the delegatee's expertise to fill in details Congress could not practically anticipate. The same practical necessity justifies Congress's choice here.

Criminal consequences do not demand a different result. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521-22 (1911), this Court upheld Congress's delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture of authority to promulgate forest-use regulations whose violation carried criminal penalties, holding that such delegation was permissible where Congress had defined the scope of the regulatory power and left only its detailed execution to the Executive. Section 811(h) follows that same structure: Congress defined the scope and criteria for temporary scheduling and left only the case-by-case application of those criteria to the Attorney General.

Finally, judicial review of the *constitutionality* of the delegation remains available, satisfying the concern this Court identified in *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952). Although § 811(h)(6) bars review of the substantive correctness of a particular scheduling order, it does not — and could not — foreclose a defendant's right to challenge the delegation itself, as the court below correctly recognized. This Court has long treated the non-delegation principle as “vital to the integrity and maintenance of the system of government ordained by the constitution,” *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), and that structural safeguard remains fully available to petitioners regardless of § 811(h)(6)'s limitation on review of the merits of a particular scheduling decision.

## II. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Redelegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

Petitioners' subdelegation argument fails for a simpler reason: Congress expressly authorized it. Section 871(a) of Title 21 empowers the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Controlled Substances Act to officers and employees of the Department of Justice. That express statutory authorization distinguishes this case from *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 363-64 (1942), where the Court invalidated a subdelegation because the enabling statute there contained no comparable authorization permitting the Administrator to delegate his personal duties to regional officers. Where Congress has supplied that authorization, as it did in § 871(a), subdelegation to an agency built to administer the very statute at issue is unremarkable. Cf. *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911) (sustaining executive rulemaking carrying criminal consequences where Congress defined the delegated authority's scope). The Attorney General's 1987 regulation assigning temporary-scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), falls squarely within the authority Congress granted, and the delegation to the DEA was therefore proper.
