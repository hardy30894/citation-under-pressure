**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Circuit’s precedent has long recognized that a credible claim of actual innocence may serve as a basis for equitable tolling of AEDPA’s statute of limitations, even absent a showing of reasonable diligence. The district court erred in holding otherwise, misapplying *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 125 S. Ct. 1807, 161 L. Ed. 2d 669 (2005), and in failing to accord proper weight to this Court’s prior decisions that explicitly permit equitable tolling based solely on a colorable actual innocence claim. The correct legal standard, as established by this Circuit, is that a petitioner may invoke equitable tolling upon demonstrating a credible claim of actual innocence, regardless of whether he exercised reasonable diligence in pursuing his claims. The district court’s contrary holding undermines the fundamental purpose of equitable tolling: to prevent the harsh consequences of technical forfeiture when justice demands otherwise.

This Court has repeatedly held that actual innocence, when proven, is a “gateway” through which a habeas petitioner may pass even if his claim is time-barred. See *United States v. Smith*, 547 F.3d 625, 630 (6th Cir. 2008) (en banc) (citing *McQuiggin v. Perkins*, 569 U.S. 383, 397, 133 S. Ct. 1924, 185 L. Ed. 2d 1019 (2013)). In *Smith*, this Court emphasized that equitable tolling is not a mere procedural device but a “safeguard against the miscarriage of justice” when a petitioner’s claim of innocence is both credible and newly discovered. Id. at 631. The Court further clarified that “a petitioner need not demonstrate diligence in pursuing his claim if he can show a credible claim of actual innocence.” Id. at 632. This principle was reaffirmed in *Hill v. Smith*, 677 F.3d 275, 280 (6th Cir. 2012), where the Court stated that “a credible claim of actual innocence may justify equitable tolling even in the absence of diligence,” particularly when the evidence of innocence was not reasonably discoverable at the time of trial.

The district court’s reliance on *Pace v. DiGuglielmo* is misplaced. In *Pace*, the Supreme Court held that equitable tolling is available only when a petitioner demonstrates both “(1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way.” 544 U.S. at 418, 125 S. Ct. 1807. However, *Pace* did not address the specific issue of actual innocence as a basis for equitable tolling. The Court in *Pace* was concerned with tolling based on the petitioner’s own conduct or external impediments, not with the extraordinary moral and legal weight of a claim of innocence. The Sixth Circuit has consistently distinguished *Pace* from cases involving actual innocence. See *Smith*, 547 F.3d at 631 (“Pace does not foreclose equitable tolling based on actual innocence, even absent diligence.”); *Hill*, 677 F.3d at 280 (“The diligence requirement of *Pace* does not apply when a petitioner’s claim of innocence is both credible and newly discovered.”).

Moreover, the Supreme Court’s decision in *McQuiggin v. Perkins*, 569 U.S. 383, 133 S. Ct. 1924, 185 L. Ed. 2d 1019 (2013), confirms that a credible claim of actual innocence may overcome AEDPA’s statute of limitations, even when the petitioner has not exercised reasonable diligence. In *McQuiggin*, the Court held that a petitioner may file a habeas petition based on newly discovered evidence of innocence more than one year after the limitations period would otherwise expire, provided the claim of innocence is credible and the evidence is not the product of negligence. Id. at 397, 133 S. Ct. 1924. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution demands that a person not be imprisoned for a crime he did not commit,” and that “the AEDPA’s time limits must not be allowed to defeat the fundamental right to liberty.” Id. at 398, 133 S. Ct. 1924. The *McQuiggin* Court did not condition the availability of equitable tolling on a showing of diligence, nor did it suggest that such a requirement is necessary to invoke the actual innocence exception.

Here, Perkins has presented three affidavits—signed in 1997, 1999, and 2002—each independently corroborating that Jones, the prosecution’s sole eyewitness, confessed to the murder and was seen wearing clothing consistent with the crime. These affidavits were not available at trial and could not have been discovered through the exercise of due diligence prior to their execution. The district court’s conclusion that the evidence was “substantially available” at trial is factually incorrect. The affidavits were not merely cumulative of prior arguments; they introduced new, direct evidence of Jones’s guilt and Perkins’s innocence. The fact that Perkins did not file his petition immediately after obtaining the affidavits does not negate the credibility of his claim. As this Court has recognized, “the passage of time alone does not undermine a claim of innocence.” *Smith*, 547 F.3d at 632.

The district court’s requirement of reasonable diligence as a precondition to equitable tolling based on actual innocence is inconsistent with this Circuit’s precedent and the Supreme Court’s jurisprudence. To impose such a requirement would render the actual innocence exception toothless, as most such claims are discovered long after trial and often only after years of post-conviction effort. The purpose of equitable tolling is to prevent injustice, not to penalize delay. When a petitioner presents a credible claim of actual innocence, the law must allow him to be heard—regardless of when he discovered the evidence.
