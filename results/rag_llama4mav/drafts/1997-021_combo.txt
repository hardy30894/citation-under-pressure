The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). The NLRB's standard is too strict, rendering employer polls useless, and is inconsistent with the NLRA's goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships.

The NLRA imposes a duty on employers to bargain with unions that represent a majority of their employees. Vaca v. Sipes, 386 U.S. 171 (1967). When a union is certified or voluntarily recognized, it enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority status. United Mine Workers of America v. Arkansas Oak Flooring Co., 351 U.S. 62 (1956). An employer may rebut this presumption by showing that the union no longer represents a majority of employees. La Crosse Telephone Corp. v. Wisconsin Employment Relations Board, 336 U.S. 18 (1949).

The NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard requires an employer to have objective evidence sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status before conducting a poll. However, this standard is too stringent, as it is the same standard applied to withdrawing recognition. Allowing an employer to conduct a poll only when it has sufficient evidence to withdraw recognition renders polling "marginally useful" to employers. See Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057 (1989).

The courts have recognized that polling can be a "useful and legitimate tool" for employers to test union support. Marine Engineers Beneficial Ass'n v. Interlake Steamship Co., 370 U.S. 173 (1962). In Brotherhood of Railway & Steamship Clerks, Freight Handlers, Express & Station Employees v. Association for the Benefit of Non-Contract Employees, 380 U.S. 650 (1965), the Court emphasized the importance of allowing employers to test union support. A more lenient standard for polling would be consistent with the NLRA's goal of promoting stable collective-bargaining relationships. Retail Clerks International Ass'n, Local Unions Nos. 128 & 633 v. Lion Dry Goods, Inc., 369 U.S. 17 (1962); Smith v. Evening News Assn., 371 U.S. 195 (1962).

Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence. The Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) and the NLRB erred in discounting the statements of several employees who expressed anti-union sentiments. McLeod v. General Electric Co., 385 U.S. 533 (1967); Boire v. Greyhound Corp., 376 U.S. 473 (1964). The record shows that a significant number of employees expressed dissatisfaction with the union, which should have been considered by the NLRB.

In Federal Communications Commission v. Allentown Broadcasting Corp., 349 U.S. 358 (1955), the Court held that an agency's decision must be supported by substantial evidence. Here, the NLRB's decision is not supported by substantial evidence, and its standard for polling is inconsistent with the NLRA. Therefore, the Court should reverse the NLRB's order and hold that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. did not commit an unfair labor practice by conducting a poll and refusing to bargain with the union based on the poll's results.
