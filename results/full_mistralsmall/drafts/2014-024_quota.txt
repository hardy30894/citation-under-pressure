**ARGUMENT**

I. The First Amendment’s “true threat” exception requires proof of the defendant’s *subjective* intent to threaten.

The Third Circuit erred in affirming Elonis’s conviction under an objective standard that dispenses with any showing of the defendant’s intent. This Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects even repugnant speech unless it falls within a narrow exception. *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (“[T]he constitutional guarantees of free speech and free press do not permit a State to forbid or proscribe advocacy of the use of force or of law violation except where such advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.”). The “true threat” exception is no broader. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003) (“[T]rue threats” encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.”) (emphasis added).

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—that a reasonable person would foresee the statement as threatening—finds no support in this Court’s precedents. In *Black*, the Court explicitly rejected an objective standard, holding that the First Amendment requires proof of the speaker’s *subjective* intent to threaten. *Id.* at 360 (“The speaker need not actually intend to carry out the threat.”). The Court cautioned that an objective standard would chill protected speech by allowing juries to convict speakers whose statements were not intended as threats but were merely reckless or careless. *Id.* at 365 (“[A]lthough it is certainly reasonable to consider the surrounding circumstances in determining whether a statement is a true threat, the circumstances should not be the only factor considered.”).

This Court’s decision in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), further underscores the necessity of a subjective intent requirement. In *Watts*, the Court reversed a conviction under a statute criminalizing threats against the President, emphasizing that the defendant’s hyperbolic political statement—“If they ever make me carry a rifle, the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J.”—was not a true threat because it was not intended as a serious expression of intent to harm. *Id.* at 708. The Court’s focus on the defendant’s subjective intent comports with the First Amendment’s protections for even controversial speech. *See also NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886, 928 (1982) (recognizing that speech may be punished only when it is “intended and likely to incite imminent lawless action”).

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced. *Kosma* predates *Black* and did not address the subjective intent requirement. The Third Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Black* by suggesting that *Black* involved cross-burning rather than threats is unavailing. *Black* squarely addressed the “true threat” exception and rejected an objective standard. 538 U.S. at 359-60. The Court’s reasoning applies with equal force to threats communicated via Facebook or other means.

II. The government’s failure to prove Elonis’s subjective intent to threaten violates the First Amendment.

Elonis’s posts were not true threats because he did not subjectively intend them as such. His statements were rap lyrics, hyperbole, and dark humor—genres that often employ violent imagery without intending to communicate genuine threats. *See, e.g., United States v. Jeffries*, 692 F.3d 473, 483 (6th Cir. 2012) (recognizing that rap lyrics may constitute protected speech). The government presented no evidence that Elonis intended his posts to be taken seriously. To the contrary, Elonis testified that his posts were artistic expression and not intended as threats. *See also Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 452 (2011) (recognizing that speech’s context and content are critical to determining its constitutional protection).

The Third Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that Elonis’s intent was irrelevant so long as a reasonable person would perceive the statements as threats—impermissibly chills protected speech. *See Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (“[T]he right to speak freely and to promote diversity of ideas and programs is therefore one of the chief distinctions that sets us apart from totalitarian regimes.”). The First Amendment does not permit the government to punish speech based on how others might interpret it, particularly when the speaker’s intent was not to threaten. *See Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 720 (2000) (“[T]he First Amendment does not permit the government to impose an undifferentiated, content-neutral burden on speakers wishing to disseminate their message.”).

III. The Third Circuit’s rule conflicts with this Court’s precedents and risks criminalizing protected speech.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard conflicts with this Court’s decisions requiring proof of *mens rea* in criminal statutes. *See, e.g., Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 274 (1952) (“The contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion. It is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will and a consequent ability and duty of the normal individual to choose between good and evil.”). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that criminal liability generally requires proof of a culpable mental state. *See Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001, 2009 (2015) (Alito, J., dissenting) (“[T]he Court has long held that ‘wrongdoing must be conscious to be criminal.’” (quoting *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 252)).

The Third Circuit’s rule also conflicts with this Court’s decision in *NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886 (1982). In *Claiborne*, the Court held that speech advocating boycotts and economic pressure—even when intended to coerce—was protected by the First Amendment because it was not intended to incite imminent lawless action. *Id.* at 928. The same principle applies here: Elonis’s posts, even if perceived as threatening by some, were not intended as true threats and thus are protected speech.

**Conclusion**

The Third Circuit’s decision upholding Elonis’s conviction under an objective standard violates the First Amendment. This Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings consistent with the subjective intent requirement.
