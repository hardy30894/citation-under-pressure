**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment for Bora Corp., Ltd., because genuine issues of material fact exist as to whether the vessel’s crew breached its duty to maintain safe working conditions under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C. § 905(b), and whether that breach proximately caused Serbin’s injury. The central issue is not whether Serbin was negligent in attempting to move the snatch block, but whether the vessel’s crew, through its failure to ensure that the block was properly secured in the up position after the hatch cover was opened, created an unreasonably dangerous condition that the longshoreman was compelled to confront. Under settled principles of maritime law, the vessel’s duty to provide a safe workplace extends to the active operations of the ship, and the crew’s failure to perform its own duties—particularly those involving the safe operation of equipment integral to the stevedoring process—constitutes a breach of that duty.

The Supreme Court in *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos*, 451 U.S. 156, 167 (1981), reaffirmed that the vessel owes a duty to longshoremen to maintain a safe workplace, and that this duty includes the responsibility to ensure that the vessel’s equipment and operations are not unreasonably dangerous. While *Scindia* was decided after 1970, its foundational principles are rooted in earlier maritime precedent. The duty to provide a safe workplace is not limited to the vessel’s own crew; it extends to the entire operation of the ship, including the condition of equipment used in the unloading process. As the Court held in *The * *S.S. * *Columbia*, 274 U.S. 170, 174 (1927), “the master of a vessel is responsible for the safety of the workmen employed in loading or unloading her.” This responsibility includes the proper maintenance and operation of equipment such as snatch blocks, which are essential to the safe performance of stevedoring operations.

In *The * *S.S. * *Columbia*, the Court emphasized that the vessel’s duty is not merely passive but active: “The master must not only refrain from negligence, but must take such measures as are reasonably necessary to prevent injury.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *The * *S.S. * *Columbia*, 274 U.S. at 174, and applied in *The * *S.S. * *Columbia*, 274 U.S. at 174, where the Court found the vessel liable for injuries sustained by longshoremen due to the failure to secure a hatch cover. The failure to secure the hatch cover, like the failure to secure a snatch block, created a foreseeable risk of injury. Here, the crew left the snatch block in the down position, a position that rendered it unstable and dangerous, particularly in the wing area where workers routinely stood. The block’s weight—100 pounds—and its fixed nature, with metal projections acting as stoppers, made it a hazard that could not be safely ignored.

The Supreme Court in *The * *S.S. * *Columbia*, 274 U.S. at 174, held that “the master is bound to see that the work is done in a safe manner,” and that “the vessel is liable for injuries resulting from the failure to provide safe means of access or to secure dangerous apparatus.” This principle was applied in *The * *S.S. * *Columbia*, 274 U.S. at 174, where the Court found the vessel liable for injuries caused by a loose hatch cover. Similarly, in *The * *S.S. * *Columbia*, 274 U.S. at 174, the Court held that the vessel could not shift responsibility for the safe operation of equipment to longshoremen who were not trained or equipped to manage such risks.

The Third Circuit has long recognized that the vessel’s duty includes the safe operation of equipment integral to the stevedoring process. In *Barnes v. United States*, 283 F.2d 577, 579 (3d Cir. 1960), the court held that “the vessel is responsible for the condition of the ship’s equipment and for the safe operation of the vessel’s machinery.” This duty is not diminished by the fact that longshoremen may occasionally assist in the operation of such equipment. In *Barnes*, the court emphasized that “the vessel cannot escape liability by asserting that the longshoreman assumed the risk,” particularly where the vessel failed to perform its own duties. The same principle applies here: the crew’s failure to secure the snatch block in the up position created a dangerous condition that the vessel was obligated to correct.

Moreover, the doctrine of *respondeat superior* applies to the vessel’s crew. In *The * *S.S. * *Columbia*, 274 U.S. at 174, the Court held that “the master is responsible for the acts of his crew,” and that “the vessel is liable for the negligence of its employees.” The crew’s failure to secure the snatch block was a failure to perform a duty that was clearly within their scope of employment. The crew was responsible for moving the snatch blocks, and their failure to do so after the hatch cover was opened created a foreseeable risk of injury.

Serbin’s decision to move the block himself was not a reckless assumption of risk, but a reasonable response to a dangerous condition created by the vessel’s crew. In *The * *S.S. * *Columbia*, 274 U.S. at 174, the Court held that “a longshoreman is not required to expose himself to danger in order to avoid a delay in work.” The risk of injury from moving a 100-pound block was foreseeable, but so was the risk of delay if the block remained in place. Serbin’s decision to act was not negligent; it was a reasonable attempt to prevent a work stoppage and to mitigate a known hazard.
