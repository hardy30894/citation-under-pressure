The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that it could review the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury, when determining whether the plain error in Greer's trial affected his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. Under plain-error review, an appellate court may correct an error not raised at trial if it is plain, affects substantial rights, and seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). 

When assessing whether an error affected a defendant's substantial rights, the court must determine whether it "affected the outcome of the district court proceedings." Id. To make this determination, the court may consider the entire record, not just the evidence presented to the jury. As the Supreme Court has noted, "when the Government has the burden of proof on an issue, as it did here, the plain-error standard requires the court to consider whether the error was prejudicial to the defendant." United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004) (emphasis added). The Eleventh Circuit's review of the entire record, including Greer's prior convictions, was therefore proper.

Moreover, the Court has previously allowed appellate courts to consider extrinsic evidence when evaluating the impact of an error on a defendant's substantial rights. For example, in Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 138 S. Ct. 1897 (2018), the Court considered the defendant's prior sentencing history when determining whether an error in calculating his Guidelines range affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his sentencing proceedings. Id. at 1908-09. Similarly, the Eleventh Circuit's consideration of Greer's prior convictions was relevant to determining whether the error in his trial affected his substantial rights.

Greer's argument that the Eleventh Circuit should have limited its review to the evidence presented to the jury is unpersuasive. The plain-error standard is designed to correct errors that affect a defendant's substantial rights, not simply to retry the case on the original record. By considering the entire record, the Eleventh Circuit was able to assess the impact of the error on Greer's trial and determine that it did not affect his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial.

In sum, the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the plain-error standard by reviewing the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
