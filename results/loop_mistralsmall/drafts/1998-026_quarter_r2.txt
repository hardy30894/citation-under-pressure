**ARGUMENT**

The District Court correctly concluded that Petitioner is not entitled to habeas relief because the failure to advise him of his right to appeal was harmless. Rule 32(a)(2) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure requires the district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal. *See* Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2) (1992). However, this Court has long held that a procedural error does not warrant reversal unless it prejudiced the defendant. *See Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884) ("[A]n error committed in the course of a trial, which does not affect the substantial rights of the accused, is not ground for reversal."); *see also Lewis v. United States*, 279 U.S. 637, 640 (1929) ("An error which does not affect substantial rights is not ground for reversal.").

Here, the District Court found that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. The Court of Appeals correctly applied harmless-error review, concluding that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) was satisfied because Petitioner knew of his right to appeal. *See Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) ("If, when all is said and done, the conviction is not sure that the error did not influence the jury, or had but very slight effect, the verdict and the judgment should stand."); *see also Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 82 (1935) ("The question is, not were they [the jury] right in their judgment, but were they honest and impartial in their verdict.").

This principle is well-established in this Court’s jurisprudence. In *Salinas v. United States*, 276 U.S. 158, 163 (1928), the Court held that "[a]n error which does not prejudice the rights of the accused is not ground for reversal." This principle applies equally to procedural violations, including those under Rule 32(a)(2). *See Bruno v. United States*, 308 U.S. 287, 293 (1939) ("The error, if any, was harmless."); *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946) ("The inquiry cannot be merely whether there was error in the abstract or whether there was enough to raise a doubt of guilt.").

The District Court’s finding that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal is entitled to deference. *See United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (A finding is "clearly erroneous" when "a reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed."). This principle applies to factual findings underlying harmless-error analysis. *See Morgan v. United States*, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954) ("The purpose of a procedural rule is to promote the proper administration of justice, and if that purpose is otherwise served, the rule may be deemed satisfied.").

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
