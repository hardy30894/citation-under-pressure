## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Bryant's Claim to Enforce the Arbitration Award

The district court erred in dismissing Bryant's claim seeking enforcement of the arbitration award. As an employee covered by a collective bargaining agreement, Bryant has standing to seek enforcement of the arbitration award won against his employer, Bell Atlantic. See United Steelworkers of Am. v. Enter. Wheel & Car Corp., 363 U.S. 593, 596 (1960) (holding that an arbitration award is final and binding on the parties). The Supreme Court has long recognized that an employee has a federal right to enforce an arbitration award won pursuant to a collective bargaining agreement. See Vaca v. Sipes, 386 U.S. 171, 196-197 (1967) (holding that an employee may bring a claim to enforce an arbitration award). Therefore, the district court should have enforced the arbitration award in favor of Bryant.

### II. The District Court Erred in Failing to Recognize the Recent Supreme Court Clarification to the Proof Requirements Under the Burden-Shifting Standard in Title VII and Section 1981 Cases

The district court erred in failing to apply the recent Supreme Court clarification to the proof requirements under the burden-shifting standard in Title VII and Section 1981 cases. In Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc., 530 U.S. 133 (2000), the Supreme Court held that a plaintiff need not always introduce additional, independent evidence of discrimination beyond establishing a prima facie case and showing that the employer's proffered reason is pretextual. Id. at 148. The Court emphasized that "a plaintiff's prima facie case, combined with sufficient evidence to find that the employer's asserted justification is false, may permit the trier of fact to conclude that the employer unlawfully discriminated." Id. at 148. Here, Bryant established a prima facie case of discrimination and presented evidence that Bell Atlantic's proffered reason for his termination was pretextual. Therefore, the district court should have applied the Reeves standard and found that Bryant presented sufficient evidence to survive summary judgment.

### III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because There Are Genuine Issues of Material Fact Still in Dispute

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because there are genuine issues of material fact still in dispute. Bryant presented evidence that Bell Atlantic's Red Letter Day policy disproportionately affected him due to his child care responsibilities, and that Bell Atlantic refused to accommodate him despite his efforts to comply with the policy. See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) (holding that the evidence of the non-movant is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in his favor). Moreover, the record shows that Bell Atlantic's proffered reason for terminating Bryant was pretextual, as evidenced by the company's refusal to accept the CWA's offer to have co-workers perform Bryant's Red Letter Day assignments. See Reeves, 530 U.S. at 147 (holding that a plaintiff may establish pretext by showing that the employer's proffered explanation is unworthy of credence). Therefore, the district court should have denied summary judgment and allowed the case to proceed to trial.

In conclusion, the district court erred in dismissing Bryant's claim to enforce the arbitration award, failed to apply the correct standard under the burden-shifting framework, and granted summary judgment despite the presence of genuine issues of material fact. This Court should reverse the district court's decision and remand the case for further proceedings.
