The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act (LHWCA) to employees injured "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources" of the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). A plain reading of this provision indicates that the injury must be connected to operations on the outer Continental Shelf.

The Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of the locality of the injury in determining the applicability of the LHWCA. In Nogueira v. New York, New Haven & Hartford Railroad Co., 281 U.S. 128 (1930), the Court held that the LHWCA applies to injuries occurring on navigable waters, highlighting the significance of the location of the injury. Similarly, in Swanson v. Marra Bros., 328 U.S. 1 (1946), the Court reaffirmed that the LHWCA is concerned with injuries occurring on navigable waters.

The OCSLA's extension of LHWCA coverage to outer Continental Shelf operations suggests that the location of the injury is crucial. The statute's language, "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf," implies a causal connection between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf. This causal connection is more likely to exist when the injury occurs on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

In Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, 314 U.S. 244 (1941), the Court emphasized the importance of the locality test in determining the applicability of the LHWCA. The Court held that the LHWCA applies to injuries occurring on navigable waters, even if the employment is not traditionally considered maritime. This locality test is relevant to the OCSLA's extension of LHWCA coverage, suggesting that the location of the injury is a critical factor.

The Fifth Circuit's decision in Mills v. Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), is consistent with this interpretation. Although not binding, the Mills court's reasoning is persuasive. The court held that the OCSLA requires a situs-of-injury test, limiting coverage to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it.

In conclusion, the OCSLA's extension of LHWCA coverage is limited to injuries occurring as a result of operations on the outer Continental Shelf. A plain reading of the statute, combined with the Supreme Court's emphasis on the locality of the injury in LHWCA cases, supports the conclusion that the OCSLA requires a situs-of-injury test. Therefore, the respondent's argument that the OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to workers injured on the outer Continental Shelf itself is supported by the statutory language and relevant case law.
