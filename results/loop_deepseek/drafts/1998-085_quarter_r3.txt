**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 permits punitive damages under Title VII only where the employer’s discriminatory practice is “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). The question presented is whether this standard requires conduct that is independently “egregious” or “outrageous,” beyond the state of mind necessary for liability. It does. Congress incorporated the common-law punitive-damages tradition, under which the character of the defendant’s conduct—not merely its intent—must be sufficiently blameworthy to warrant punishment and deterrence. This Court’s pre-1970 decisions uniformly confirm that punitive damages require conduct that is malicious, wanton, or oppressive; mere intentional discrimination, without more, is insufficient.

**I. The Common Law Requires Conduct That Is “Egregious” or “Outrageous”**

From the earliest days of this Court, punitive damages have been reserved for conduct marked by malice, wantonness, or oppression. In *The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. 546, 558 (1818), the Court held that exemplary damages may be awarded only where the tort is committed with “a wanton or malicious” intent. *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1851), likewise recognized that juries may give damages “for the sake of example” when the act is done oppressively or maliciously. The Court reaffirmed this limitation in *Philadelphia & Reading R.R. v. Derby*, 55 U.S. 468, 474 (1852), stating that vindictive damages depend on proof of wanton or gross misconduct.

The principle continued throughout the nineteenth century. In *Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore R.R. v. Quigley*, 62 U.S. 202, 214 (1858), the Court emphasized that exemplary damages are not available for every intentional wrong; they require conduct evincing a criminally reckless disregard for civil obligations. *Derby*, 55 U.S. at 474, held that punitive damages are proper only where the defendant has been guilty of gross negligence, malice, or wantonness. And *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Ry. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), confirmed that to warrant exemplary damages, the act must be done with actual malice, willfulness, or wantonness. The Court applied the same standard in admiralty, requiring “wanton or malicious” conduct before punitive liability attaches. *The Malek Adhel*, 43 U.S. 210, 233 (1844); *The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. at 558.

Thus, the common law does not permit punitive damages simply because the defendant acted intentionally. The conduct itself must reflect a culpable state of mind beyond the mere intent to do the act—a state of mind that, when combined with the conduct, merits punishment. The statute’s use of “malice” and “reckless indifference” echoes this tradition. Those terms require proof that the employer’s actions were not merely intentional but were taken with a conscious disregard for the plaintiff’s rights that is so severe as to be akin to malice.

**II. The District Court Correctly Applied This Standard**

The district court denied Kolstad’s punitive damages claim because the evidence did not show that the American Dental Association’s conduct was “egregious” or “outrageous.” The court found that the case was a routine discrimination dispute involving a preselection decision and after-the-fact rationalizations. Such evidence, while sufficient to support a finding of intentional discrimination, falls far short of the common-law standard. No evidence showed that the ADA acted with “malice” or “reckless indifference” to Kolstad’s rights. The preselection, the failure to interview, and the shifting explanations are examples of classic pretext—not the kind of wanton or reckless misconduct that the common law demands. *Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 107. The record contains no evidence of hatred, spite, or a deliberate plan to violate the law. The isolated testimony about offensive jokes, while troubling, does not rise to the level of egregious conduct that the common law requires for punitive damages.

**III. A Contrary Interpretation Would Evade the Common-Law Tradition**

Petitioner asks this Court to hold that any intentional discrimination, without more, satisfies the “malice or reckless indifference” standard. That would effectively read the common-law conduct requirement out of the statute. But Congress expressly invoked the common-law tradition when it enacted § 1981a. *See* 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (statement of Sen. Dole) (punitive damages only in “extraordinarily egregious cases”). This Court’s decisions confirm that punitive damages are not a routine remedy for every intentional tort. *See, e.g., Derby*, 55 U.S. at 474. The standard is a high one: the conduct must be wanton, malicious, or grossly negligent. *Quigley*, 62 U.S. at 214. The district court properly concluded that the evidence here does not meet that threshold.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed insofar as it remanded for a trial on punitive damages, and the district court’s denial of punitive damages should be affirmed.
