**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN LIFE AND LIBERTY THAT TRIGGERS DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that petitioner Woodard lacks a constitutionally protected liberty interest in clemency proceedings. This Court has long recognized that clemency is not merely a "matter of grace" but an integral part of the state’s system for determining the deprivation of life and liberty. *See Ohio Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 281 (1998) (plurality opinion) (acknowledging that clemency may implicate due process where it is "an integral part of the ... system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant").

In *Connecticut Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981), the Court declined to find a protected liberty interest in clemency because Connecticut law did not create one. However, where a state establishes a formal clemency procedure—particularly one that is mandatory and structured—due process protections attach. *See, e.g., Greenholtz v. Inmates of Neb. Penal & Corr. Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 12 (1979) (finding a liberty interest in parole release where state law created "a legitimate claim of entitlement"). Ohio’s clemency process, as outlined in its regulations, is not a mere "matter of grace" but a structured proceeding that directly affects the deprivation of life and liberty. *See Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07* (mandating clemency review for death-sentenced inmates). Thus, Woodard has a protected liberty interest in the clemency process itself.

**II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS OHIO FROM IMPOSING A "HOBSON’S CHOICE" BETWEEN PARTICIPATION IN CLEMENCY AND ASSERTING THE RIGHT AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION.**

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that Ohio’s clemency process does not violate the Fifth Amendment is erroneous. The Fifth Amendment provides that no person "shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." U.S. Const. amend. V. The Court has held that the government may not condition a benefit on the waiver of constitutional rights unless the waiver is "voluntary, knowing, and intelligent." *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972).

Here, Ohio’s clemency process presents Woodard with an unconstitutional dilemma: either participate in an interview and risk self-incrimination, or refuse and face adverse inferences in clemency proceedings. *See Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 318 (1976) (holding that a prisoner’s silence in disciplinary proceedings may be used against him). The Court has repeatedly condemned such "Hobson’s choice" scenarios. *See Lefkowitz v. Cunningham*, 431 U.S. 801, 808 (1977) (finding an unconstitutional condition where a state conditioned a benefit on waiver of Fifth Amendment rights).

Ohio’s regulation does not explicitly compel testimony, but its structure pressures inmates to waive their rights. The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that Woodard can simply refuse to answer questions ignores the coercive effect of clemency proceedings. *See Woodard*, 523 U.S. at 285 (plurality opinion) (acknowledging that clemency interviews may create "a risk of self-incrimination"). The Fifth Amendment does not permit Ohio to force inmates into this position.

**III. THE COURT SHOULD REJECT OHIO’S CLAIM THAT CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS ARE BEYOND JUDICIAL REVIEW.**

Ohio argues that clemency is a purely executive function and thus unreviewable. *See Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. However, where clemency procedures implicate constitutional rights—such as due process or the Fifth Amendment—they are subject to judicial scrutiny. *See, e.g., United States v. Klein*, 80 U.S. 128, 147 (1871) (holding that executive actions violating constitutional rights are reviewable).

The Sixth Circuit’s remand for further proceedings was correct. The district court should determine whether Ohio’s clemency process, as applied, violates Woodard’s constitutional rights. *See Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985) (holding that due process protections apply to state-created procedures).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s judgment and hold that Ohio’s clemency process violates Woodard’s due process and Fifth Amendment rights.
