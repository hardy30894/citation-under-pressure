**ARGUMENT**

Section 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten. The court of appeals’ contrary rule—allowing conviction based only on how a reasonable listener might understand the communication—cannot be squared with the statute, this Court’s “true threat” cases, or the background principle that criminal liability ordinarily demands a culpable mental state. See Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250–52 (1952); Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600, 605–06 (1994).

This Court has long held that speech may be punished only if it falls within the “true threat” exception, not merely because it is offensive or alarming. Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 707–08 (1969) (per curiam), treated context, not literal words alone, as essential to identifying a true threat. In Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343, 359–60 (2003), the Court again explained that “true threats” are statements where the speaker means to communicate “a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” That formulation focuses on the speaker’s intent to threaten, not simply the effect on a hypothetical reasonable recipient. Black further held that intimidation is a true threat because the speaker intends to place the victim “in fear of bodily harm or death.” Id. at 360. A statute punishing threats therefore requires more than proof that a reasonable person would perceive the communication as threatening.

The Third Circuit relied on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), which adopted a purely objective standard for § 871. But Kosma predated Black and should not control after this Court clarified that the true-threat inquiry turns on a serious expression of intent to harm. The court below erred by treating Black as merely preserving a reasonable-speaker test, when Black’s own definition of a true threat points in the opposite direction. 538 U.S. at 359–60.

The government may contend that § 875(c) contains no express mens rea beyond transmission of a communication containing a threat. But this Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that silence on mens rea authorizes strict liability for serious crimes. Morissette, 342 U.S. at 250–52; Staples, 511 U.S. at 606–07. Where a statute is ambiguous, the Court ordinarily “read[s] into the statute only that mens rea which is necessary to separate wrongful conduct from otherwise innocent conduct.” Carter v. United States, 530 U.S. 255, 269 (2000). Applying that rule here, subjective intent is necessary to distinguish criminal threats from political hyperbole, angry venting, artistic expression, and crude online speech.

That requirement is especially vital in the social-media context. The First Amendment does not permit criminal punishment based on the listener’s reaction alone. Snyder v. Phelps, 562 U.S. 443, 458–61 (2011), confirms that speech may be deeply distressing and still protected when it does not cross the constitutional line. A subjective-intent requirement ensures § 875(c) punishes only those who knowingly or purposefully communicate threats, not speakers whose words are misread. See Elonis v. United States, 730 F.3d 321, 330–31 (3d Cir. 2013).

The jury instruction below omitted that essential element. It allowed conviction if petitioner “intentionally makes a statement in a context or under such circumstances wherein a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a serious expression of an intention to inflict bodily harm.” Elonis, 730 F.3d at 324. That standard permits punishment even when the defendant did not mean his words as a threat. Because criminal liability under § 875(c) requires proof that petitioner subjectively intended to threaten, the judgment should be reversed.
