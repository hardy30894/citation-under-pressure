ARGUMENT

I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, applying the same standard the district court should have applied. Turner v. Baylor Richardson Med. Ctr., 476 F.3d 337, 343 (5th Cir. 2007). Summary judgment is proper only when "there is no genuine issue as to any material fact." Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). Once the movant identifies the absence of evidence supporting an essential element of the nonmovant's claim, the burden shifts to the nonmovant to produce more than a "scintilla of evidence" from which a reasonable jury could find in its favor. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 252 (1986). A party opposing summary judgment must "do more than simply show that there is some metaphysical doubt as to the material facts." Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 586 (1986). Where the record contains no evidence on an essential element, summary judgment against the party bearing the burden of proof is required. Little v. Liquid Air Corp., 37 F.3d 1069, 1075 (5th Cir. 1994) (en banc).

II. The District Court Erred in Awarding Ybarra Summary Judgment on Calls 2 through 5

The TCPA prohibits "any call using any automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice" to a cell phone absent consent. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). Liability attaches only if the defendant's equipment actually "made" the offending call using the prohibited technology—capability alone is not enough. Satterfield v. Simon & Schuster, Inc., 569 F.3d 946, 954 (9th Cir. 2009). Whether a call violates the statute turns on how that particular call was placed and completed, not merely on what the dialing system was generally capable of doing. Meyer v. Portfolio Recovery Assocs., LLC, 707 F.3d 1036, 1043 (9th Cir. 2012).

DISH's Cisco Dialer was configured to play a prerecorded message only upon detecting a "positive voice"—either a live person or an answering-machine greeting. The call log shows that Calls 2, 3, and 4 registered as "Answering Machine," and Call 5 registered as "Data Error – No Value." Nothing in that log affirmatively establishes that a prerecorded message actually played on any of those four calls. Ybarra offered no voicemail recording, phone record, or testimony showing that DISH's message was delivered on Calls 2 through 5. That silence cannot carry his burden. Once DISH identified the absence of proof that a prerecorded voice was used on those calls, the burden shifted to Ybarra to "establish the existence of an element essential to [his] case." Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322. He failed to do so, and an ambiguous log entry is, at most, the kind of "scintilla" that cannot sustain judgment in his favor. Anderson, 477 U.S. at 252.

The district court avoided this evidentiary gap only by treating DISH's general discovery admission that it "used a prerecorded voice in calls from the 8047 number" as conclusive proof that the message played on every one of the seven calls without distinction. That was error. An admission describing how the system was generally designed to operate does not establish, as an undisputed fact, that the prerecorded voice actually played on calls whose own logged outcomes—"Answering Machine" and "Data Error"—are, at best, ambiguous as to whether the message was delivered. Ybarra needed evidence tying those specific outcomes to actual delivery of the prohibited voice; he had none. DISH was therefore entitled to judgment as a matter of law on Calls 2 through 5. Little, 37 F.3d at 1075.

III. DISH Concedes Liability on Calls 1, 6, and 7

DISH does not dispute that Calls 1, 6, and 7 registered "Positive Voice" and that its prerecorded message was delivered on each. Because the 3475 number had been reassigned from the original DISH customer who furnished the consent to Ybarra, and because the consent required under the TCPA runs with the current subscriber, not the intended debtor, Soppet v. Enhanced Recovery Co., 679 F.3d 637, 643 (7th Cir. 2012) (holding that the "called party" for consent purposes is the current subscriber, not the person the caller sought to reach), DISH lacked Ybarra's consent for these three calls. DISH accordingly does not contest the $1,500 in statutory damages attributable to Calls 1, 6, and 7.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment to Ybarra on Calls 2, 3, 4, and 5, render judgment for DISH as to those calls, affirm as to Calls 1, 6, and 7, and remand for entry of a corrected judgment awarding $1,500 in statutory damages.
