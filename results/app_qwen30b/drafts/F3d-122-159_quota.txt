**ARGUMENT**

I. THE BOP’S APPLICATION OF PROGRAM STATEMENT 5162.02 TO DENY ROUSSOS EARLY RELEASE VIOLATES THE CONGRESSIONAL POLICY OF PROMOTING DRUG TREATMENT AND CONSTITUTES AN UNREASONABLE INTERPRETATION OF 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) AND 28 C.F.R. § 550.58.

The Bureau of Prisons’ decision to deny Victor M. Roussos early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) based on a two-level sentencing enhancement for possession of a firearm during a drug conspiracy offense—without any actual use or threat of force—contravenes the plain language of the statute, the regulatory framework, and settled principles of administrative law. The BOP’s reliance on Program Statement 5162.02, which categorically deems any drug conspiracy conviction enhanced under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1(b)(1) as a “crime of violence,” is arbitrary, capricious, and inconsistent with the statutory and regulatory definitions of “nonviolent offense.” This interpretation exceeds the BOP’s authority and violates the principle that agency action must be grounded in a rational connection to the statutory text.

First, the statute itself defines eligibility for early release not by reference to sentencing enhancements, but by the nature of the offense. Section 3621(e)(2)(B) permits early release only for “a nonviolent offense.” The term “nonviolent offense” is not defined in the statute, but the BOP’s own regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, provides that an offense is not eligible if it is a “crime of violence” as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). That definition, in turn, requires either (A) the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force, or (B) the offense’s “nature” must involve a “substantial risk” that physical force may be used. See 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02, however, creates a per se rule that any drug conspiracy conviction enhanced for weapon possession is a “crime of violence,” regardless of whether the offense itself involved force or a substantial risk of force. This is a clear departure from the statutory standard.

The Supreme Court has long held that agencies may not interpret statutes in ways that contradict their plain meaning. *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842 (1984). Even under *Chevron*’s deference framework, the BOP’s interpretation fails at step one because the statute does not authorize such a categorical rule. The BOP’s position ignores the critical distinction between *sentencing enhancements* and *elements of the offense*. The enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1(b)(1) is not an element of the offense of conspiracy under 21 U.S.C. § 846; it is a fact-based adjustment made at sentencing. As the Court emphasized in *United States v. Booker*, 543 U.S. 220, 233 (2005), “the Sentencing Guidelines are advisory, not mandatory,” and enhancements do not alter the underlying nature of the offense. Thus, the mere fact that a sentencing court applied a weapon enhancement does not transform a nonviolent drug conspiracy into a “crime of violence” under § 924(c)(3).

Moreover, the BOP’s interpretation is inconsistent with the regulatory definition of “crime of violence.” The regulation explicitly ties eligibility to the offense’s *nature*, not to post-conviction sentencing decisions. See 28 C.F.R. § 550.58. The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02, by contrast, treats a sentencing enhancement as dispositive of the offense’s character—a move that effectively nullifies the regulatory framework. This is impermissible. In *Christensen v. Harris County*, 529 U.S. 576, 587 (2000), the Court held that an agency’s interpretation must be “consistent with the regulation’s plain language and purpose.” Here, the BOP’s interpretation is not consistent with the regulation’s focus on the offense’s inherent characteristics.

The BOP’s rule also fails under the arbitrary and capricious standard of the Administrative Procedure Act. *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983). The agency provided no reasoned explanation for why a mere enhancement—based on a weapon found in a residence unrelated to the arrest—should render a drug conspiracy a “crime of violence.” The Court in *State Farm* required agencies to “examine the relevant data and articulate a satisfactory explanation for its action.” The BOP did neither. It simply adopted a per se rule without considering whether the offense, in its actual commission, involved a substantial risk of force. This is precisely the kind of “unexplained departure from established practice” that the Court condemned in *State Farm*. Id. at 43.

Further, the BOP’s interpretation undermines Congress’s intent in enacting § 3621(e)(2)(B). The statute was designed to incentivize rehabilitation through drug treatment programs. See *United States v. Johnson*, 576 U.S. 531, 538 (2015) (noting that early release provisions are “designed to promote rehabilitation”). Denying early release to an inmate who completed a 500-hour treatment program—like Roussos—based on a technical sentencing enhancement that bears no relation to the actual commission of violence defeats that purpose. The Court has repeatedly held that “a regulation that frustrates the purpose of a statute is invalid.” *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 227 (2001).
