## ARGUMENT

Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA's jurisdiction only over "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." The question here is not whether the Coast Guard *could* regulate uninspected vessels like Rig 52, but whether it has *exercised* authority over the particular working conditions at issue. Because it has not, OSHA's citation was proper.

**I. The statutory text limits the exemption to fields the Coast Guard has actually regulated.**

Congress did not write a categorical exclusion for "vessels" or for maritime workplaces generally. It chose the word "exercise," a term of action, not potential. That choice must be respected according to its ordinary meaning. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (a statute's "plain, obvious, and rational meaning" governs unless it produces an absurd result). Congress could easily have exempted "working conditions of seamen" or "working conditions aboard vessels" outright, mirroring language this Court and the courts of appeals have used elsewhere to describe categorical maritime exemptions. It instead conditioned the exemption on the fact of another agency's regulatory action. That structural choice—jurisdiction turning on actual exercise rather than latent authority—must control here just as it controlled in *Maurer v. Hamilton*, 309 U.S. 598 (1940), where this Court sustained Pennsylvania's regulation of truck-trailer combinations notwithstanding the Interstate Commerce Commission's general statutory power over motor-carrier safety, because the Commission had not in fact promulgated a rule addressing that particular hazard. The mere existence of federal authority to regulate a subject does not, without more, displace regulation of that same subject by another body when the enabling statute conditions displacement on the exercise of that authority.

**II. Exemptions from remedial safety legislation are narrowly construed.**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act is remedial legislation designed "to assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions." Exemptions carved out of such statutes are not read expansively. This Court has repeatedly held, in the analogous context of the Fair Labor Standards Act, that "exemptions are to be narrowly construed against the employer who asserts them and their application limited to those establishments plainly and unmistakably within their terms and spirit." *Arnold v. Ben Kanowsky, Inc.*, 361 U.S. 388, 392 (1960). The same principle applies to an employer invoking § 4(b)(1) to escape OSHA coverage entirely. Mallard's uninspected barge is not "plainly and unmistakably" within the Coast Guard's exercised authority, because the Coast Guard regulates only discrete safety equipment aboard uninspected vessels—fire extinguishers, life preservers, backfire arrestors—and did not purport to regulate the blowout-prevention and drilling-safety procedures implicated by this explosion. A narrow construction of § 4(b)(1) therefore leaves OSHA's citation intact.

**III. Napier does not compel a contrary result, because Congress here expressly conditioned displacement on actual exercise.**

In *Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co.*, 272 U.S. 605 (1926), this Court held that the Boiler Inspection Act preempted state regulation of locomotive equipment even absent a specific federal rule on point, because that statute manifested an intent to occupy the entire field of locomotive-equipment safety. But the Boiler Inspection Act contained no textual limitation comparable to § 4(b)(1)'s "exercise" clause; its occupation-of-the-field character was inferred from the statute's structure and purpose. Section 4(b)(1), by contrast, expressly ties preemption to the fact of regulatory exercise. Where Congress has spoken so precisely, courts should not import a *Napier*-style field-occupation theory that Congress did not write into the text. *Maurer*, not *Napier*, supplies the governing analogy, because the statute in *Maurer*, like § 4(b)(1), preserved concurrent regulation except where the federal agency had actually acted.

**IV. The maritime-uniformity doctrine does not require exclusive Coast Guard jurisdiction over uninspected vessels.**

Petitioner's reliance on the general policy of uniformity in maritime law, see *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205, 216 (1917), is misplaced. *Jensen*'s uniformity principle concerns judicially fashioned substantive maritime law; it does not constrain Congress's own choices in allocating administrative jurisdiction by statute. This Court has long recognized that "Congress has paramount power to fix and determine the maritime law which shall prevail" throughout the country, and may alter or qualify that law by legislation as it sees fit. *Panama R. Co. v. Johnson*, 264 U.S. 375, 386 (1924). Congress exercised exactly that legislative prerogative in § 4(b)(1) by making the displacement of OSHA's remedial scheme contingent on another agency's actual exercise of regulatory authority—not on the vessel's status as a maritime workplace. Nothing in the constitutional interest in uniformity prevents Congress from filling regulatory gaps left open by the Coast Guard's limited, equipment-specific regulation of uninspected vessels.

**CONCLUSION**

Because the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over the working conditions at issue on this uninspected vessel, § 4(b)(1) does not withdraw OSHA's jurisdiction, and the citation should be sustained.
