ARGUMENT

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates the civil and criminal laws of adjacent States as surrogate federal law only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” The text and structure of the statute, confirmed by this Court’s decisions, establish that state law supplies a rule of decision solely when federal law leaves a gap.

In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court explained that Congress intended the OCSLA to treat fixed platforms “as though they were federal enclaves in an upland State.” Id. at 355. Because “federal law, because of its limited application to one type of problem, could never be a complete body of law,” id. at 357 (internal quotation marks omitted), the statute borrowed existing state law “to fill the substantial gaps” in federal coverage. Id. at 358. The Court therefore permitted Louisiana wrongful-death actions only after confirming that the Death on the High Seas Act supplied no remedy for accidents occurring on the platforms themselves. Id. at 360-61, 366.

Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Department of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19 (1988), reiterated the same premise. The Court observed that the OCSLA “defines a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” by extending federal law and, where necessary, “the law of the adjacent States.” Id. at 27 (quoting S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 2 (1953)). That borrowing occurs only to the extent needed to complete an otherwise incomplete federal regime.

The savings clause of the Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), does not alter the analysis. It preserves more protective state laws within the territorial jurisdiction of the States; it does not expand the geographic reach of those laws onto the outer Continental Shelf, an area placed under exclusive federal legislative authority by § 1333(a)(1). Where, as here, the FLSA and its implementing regulations already address minimum wage, overtime, and meal-period obligations, no gap remains for state law to occupy.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading—that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law—would convert the OCSLA’s limited assimilation provision into a general choice-of-law rule. That result cannot be reconciled with Rodrigue’s repeated emphasis on the need to “fill the substantial gaps” in federal law. 395 U.S. at 358. Because the FLSA comprehensively regulates the wage-and-hour claims at issue, California law has no role to play on the outer Continental Shelf.
