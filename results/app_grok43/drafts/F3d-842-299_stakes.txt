ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Lacked Subject Matter Jurisdiction.

Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction. Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of Am., 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994). Removal is proper only when the complaint, on its face, presents a federal question. Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams, 482 U.S. 386, 392 (1987) (quoting Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908)). The Pressls’ complaint seeks a declaration of rights under a 1960 Virginia flowage easement. It requests only that the court construe the easement’s language and confirm that the Pressls may use their land “in any manner not inconsistent with” APCO’s rights. No federal statute or regulation appears on the face of the complaint.

A federal defense or anticipated federal issue does not convert a state-law claim into one arising under federal law. Franchise Tax Bd. v. Constr. Laborers Vacation Tr., 463 U.S. 1, 10 (1983). APCO’s assertion that its FERC license supplies jurisdiction therefore fails. The complaint does not seek to enforce or set aside any FERC order, nor does it allege a violation of the Federal Power Act. Section 825p of Title 16 confers jurisdiction only over actions “to enforce compliance with” the Act or FERC orders. 16 U.S.C. § 825p. This private easement dispute falls outside that narrow grant.

Even if a federal issue were embedded, it would not be substantial enough to support jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331. Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). The easement’s meaning is governed by Virginia property law; any FERC-related question is collateral and can be addressed in the appropriate administrative forum. Because the district court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction, its judgment must be vacated. Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 94 (1998).
