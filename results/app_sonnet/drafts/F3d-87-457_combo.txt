ARGUMENT

I. THE DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS OF CHAPTER 93-121 ARE FACIALLY OVERBROAD AND MUST BE STRUCK DOWN.

A statute regulating speech "sweeps too broadly" when it reaches "a substantial amount of constitutionally protected conduct." NAACP v. Button, 371 U.S. 415, 432-33 (1963). Because lobbying is inseparable from petitioning and speech, this Court must scrutinize Chapter 93-121 with the same care the Supreme Court applied in Thornhill v. Alabama, 310 U.S. 88, 97 (1940), where a statute was voided because it "does not aim specifically at evils within the allowable area of state control but, on the contrary, sweeps within its ambit other activities that in ordinary circumstances constitute an exercise" of protected rights.

The only reason the federal lobbying act survived constitutional attack in United States v. Harriss, 347 U.S. 612 (1954), is that the Supreme Court narrowly construed it to reach only "direct communication" with Congress by persons whose "principal purpose" was to influence legislation, id. at 620, thereby avoiding "a construction of the Act which would raise serious constitutional questions." Id. at 622. Chapter 93-121 contains no such narrowing gloss. It compels disclosure of "all lobbying expenditures," Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a), by category, without regard to whether the expenditure bears any relation to direct communication with a legislator. The statute therefore reaches precisely the kind of ancillary, expressive, and associational activity that Harriss was careful to exclude.

The breadth of this financial-disclosure regime cannot be reconciled with NAACP v. Alabama, 357 U.S. 449 (1958), which held that compelled disclosure of associational ties works "an inhibition on the free exercise" of protected liberties. Id. at 462. Nor can it be reconciled with Bates v. City of Little Rock, 361 U.S. 516 (1960), where the Court held that "the free exercise . . . of protected activities" may not be conditioned on public disclosure absent a "'relevant correlation'" or "'substantial relation'" to a legitimate state interest, id. at 525, quoting NAACP v. Alabama, 357 U.S. at 464. Florida offers no such correlation for compelling disclosure of every category of expenditure, however remote from actual legislative persuasion.

This Court should likewise heed Shelton v. Tucker, 364 U.S. 479 (1960), which teaches that "even though the governmental purpose be legitimate and substantial, that purpose cannot be pursued by means that broadly stifle fundamental personal liberties when the end can be more narrowly achieved." Id. at 488. Florida's interest in preventing corruption is legitimate, but Chapter 93-121's blunderbuss disclosure requirement — extending to travel, lodging, entertainment, and publications wholly unconnected to any specific legislative appeal — is not the narrowly tailored means Shelton demands. Cf. Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60, 64-65 (1960) (invalidating an ordinance requiring identification on handbills because it was not limited to the specific evil the city sought to prevent).

II. THE BAN ON CONTINGENT-FEE LOBBYING VIOLATES THE FIRST AMENDMENT AND IS NOT SAVED BY NINETEENTH-CENTURY CONTRACT DECISIONS.

The panel below felt bound by "old pronouncements" — namely Trist v. Child, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 441 (1874), and Marshall v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co., 57 U.S. (16 How.) 314 (1854) — which refused, as a matter of common-law public policy, to enforce contracts for contingent lobbying fees because such arrangements tempted the lobbyist "to resort to corrupt means." Trist, 88 U.S. at 452. But those decisions addressed the enforceability of a private contract in a federal common-law diversity suit; they did not purport to adjudicate, and could not have adjudicated, a First Amendment challenge to a criminal or civil prohibition on political advocacy for hire. See Providence Tool Co. v. Norris, 69 U.S. (2 Wall.) 45, 54-55 (1865) (same, refusing to enforce contingent-fee contract as against public policy, without any First Amendment analysis whatever).

By contrast, Thomas v. Collins, 323 U.S. 516 (1945), squarely held that solicitation "for pecuniary reward" in connection with speech "no more furnishes the State with an excuse for barring [the speaker's] way . . . than it did in the case of the newspaper" seeking advertising revenue, and that the right to speak "for hire" is entitled to the same protection as speech given freely. Id. at 531, 544-45. And in NAACP v. Button, the Court held that solicitation of legal business for compensation, when undertaken to advance political and civil objectives, is "modes of expression and association protected by the First and Fourteenth Amendments," 371 U.S. at 428-29, notwithstanding a State's asserted interest in preventing the very evils — improper influence, conflicted loyalty — that Florida invokes here. If the prospect of a fee did not strip the NAACP's staff attorneys of constitutional protection, it cannot strip lobbyists of theirs.

Finally, the absolute ban cannot survive under Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 307 (1940), which condemned licensing schemes vesting officials with unchecked power over expressive conduct, or under Schware v. Board of Bar Examiners, 353 U.S. 232, 238-39 (1957), recognizing that the right to pursue a lawful occupation involving advocacy may not be arbitrarily foreclosed. Chapter 93-121's blanket prohibition, untethered to any showing of actual corruption, exceeds what Thomas, Button, Cantwell, and Schware permit.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
