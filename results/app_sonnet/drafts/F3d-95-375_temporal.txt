# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Striking the Testimony of Plaintiffs' Expert Witnesses as a Discovery Sanction.

The district court's decision to strike four of Plaintiffs' experts for failure to meet a deposition deadline was disproportionate to any fault attributable to Appellants and violated fundamental principles governing the imposition of discovery sanctions. The Supreme Court has long held that the harshest sanctions available under the discovery rules are reserved for cases of willfulness, bad faith, or fault, and are not to be visited upon a party for circumstances beyond its control. In *Societe Internationale v. Rogers*, 357 U.S. 197 (1958), the Court reversed a dismissal imposed for noncompliance with a production order, holding that dismissal "should not be construed to authorize" such a sanction "when it has been established that failure to comply has been due to inability, and not to willfulness, bad faith, or any fault of petitioner." *Id.* at 212. The same principle governs here: the record reflects that Plaintiffs' experts required additional time to review voluminous technical materials before they could be deposed, not that Appellants or their counsel acted in defiance of the court's order.

This limitation on the court's sanctioning power reflects due process concerns of the highest order. In *Hovey v. Elliott*, 167 U.S. 409 (1897), the Supreme Court held that a litigant may not be deprived of the opportunity to have a claim heard on its merits as a form of punishment for conduct unrelated to the substantive rights at issue. *Id.* at 413–14. Striking the testimony of four experts—effectively foreclosing Appellants' ability to prove causation and injury—was just such a punitive measure, imposed without regard to whether lesser sanctions, such as a further short continuance or cost-shifting, would have sufficed. The district court's own admonition that experts would be struck only if they demonstrated "no knowledge" or "insufficient knowledge" (Vol. 16, June 3, 1993 Tr. at 31–32) was never applied to the actual record; the court instead imposed the ultimate sanction for a scheduling delay alone.

## II. The District Court Erred in Excluding the Testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson.

The proper standard for assessing the admissibility of scientific expert testimony has long required only that the methodology employed be one that has achieved recognition within the relevant scientific community. As this Circuit's predecessor court articulated in *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923), "the thing from which the deduction is made must be sufficiently established to have gained general acceptance in the particular field in which it belongs." *Id.* at 1014. Dr. Schroeder's and Dr. Watson's methodologies—toxicological and epidemiological techniques for assessing chemical exposure and causation—reflect approaches generally accepted within their fields. The district court's wholesale exclusion of their testimony, rather than permitting the jury to weigh its persuasiveness against contrary evidence, invaded the jury's traditional province.

The Supreme Court has cautioned against precisely this sort of judicial usurpation of the fact-finder's role where expert opinion, even if contested, bears on a material question. In *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620 (1944), the Court held that a trial court errs in disregarding expert testimony merely because it might be characterized as interested or imperfect, explaining that such infirmities go to weight, not admissibility, and that "the court cannot properly weigh conflicting evidence" at the threshold stage. *Id.* at 627. Exclusion of Appellants' causation experts on this record deprived Appellants of the opportunity to have a jury assess the very evidence necessary to establish their claims.

## III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Remain.

Once the improperly excluded expert testimony is restored to the record, genuine issues of material fact preclude summary judgment. The burden rests on the moving party to demonstrate the absence of any genuine dispute, and the evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. *Cities Service Oil Co. v. Dunlap*, 308 U.S. 208, 210 (1939). Defendants cannot meet that burden where Appellants' experts—applying generally accepted methods under *Frye*—would testify to exposure and causation sufficient to create a jury question.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that summary judgment is an extraordinary remedy to be used sparingly where issues of causation, motive, or scientific interpretation are genuinely disputed. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). Here, the very evidence necessary to resolve the disputed issue of causation—Appellants' expert testimony—was stricken not because it lacked merit, but because of discovery scheduling difficulties and an erroneous admissibility ruling. Summary judgment premised on the absence of evidence that was wrongfully excluded cannot stand.

For these reasons, the orders striking Appellants' expert testimony, excluding Dr. Schroeder's and Dr. Watson's opinions, and granting summary judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
