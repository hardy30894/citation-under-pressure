**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit erred in construing FOIA Exemption 2 to encompass documents relating to the effects of explosions.**

The Freedom of Information Act ("FOIA") mandates disclosure of agency records unless they fall within one of nine enumerated exemptions. *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). Exemption 2 permits withholding of matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This exemption is narrow and must be strictly construed. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a "predominantly internal" standard to expand Exemption 2 beyond its statutory text contravenes Congress’s intent and this Court’s precedent.

This Court has long held that FOIA exemptions are to be narrowly construed. *EPA v. Mink*, 410 U.S. 73, 80 (1973) (citing *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361). The legislative history of FOIA confirms that exemptions are "explicitly exclusive" and should not be expanded by judicial construction. S. Rep. No. 89-813, at 3 (1965). The plain text of Exemption 2 limits its reach to "internal personnel rules and practices," not to all documents that might be used internally. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. The Ninth Circuit’s "predominantly internal" test improperly broadens Exemption 2 to cover documents that are merely used by an agency in its operations, even if not directed at personnel management or practices.

Moreover, this Court has repeatedly cautioned against interpreting FOIA exemptions to permit agencies to withhold information based on speculative risks. *Mink*, 410 U.S. at 80. The Ninth Circuit’s approach risks allowing agencies to shield any document they deem sensitive by labeling it "predominantly internal," thereby undermining FOIA’s core purpose of promoting transparency. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 372. The documents at issue here—Explosive Safety Quantity Distance ("ESQD") arcs—are technical safety calculations used to design and arrange storage facilities. They are not "personnel rules and practices" but operational safety data. The Navy’s reliance on Exemption 2 to withhold them is inconsistent with the statute’s text and purpose.

**II. The documents sought do not present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation.**

Exemption 2 applies only to documents the disclosure of which "may risk circumvention of agency regulation." *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. The Ninth Circuit’s expansive interpretation conflates operational safety data with personnel practices, erroneously suggesting that disclosure of ESQD arcs could allow circumvention of safety regulations. This reasoning is flawed.

This Court has recognized that circumvention risk must be real, not merely hypothetical. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 379. The documents here are technical in nature and do not prescribe rules or procedures for agency personnel. They are used for long-term planning and safety compliance, not for circumventing regulations. The Navy’s assertion that disclosure could aid in planning an attack or disrupt operations is speculative and insufficient to justify withholding under Exemption 2. *Mink*, 410 U.S. at 80.

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. *Hardy* involved law enforcement manuals governing search and seizure procedures—documents that directly prescribed how personnel should act. Id. at 655. ESQD arcs, by contrast, are not directives to personnel but technical data used for facility design. The Ninth Circuit’s extension of *Hardy* to operational safety documents exceeds the scope of Exemption 2.

**III. The documents are not exempt under any other FOIA provision.**

The Ninth Circuit did not address whether the documents might be exempt under Exemption 7(F), which permits withholding of records compiled for law enforcement purposes if disclosure could endanger life or safety. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(F). The documents here are not compiled for law enforcement purposes; they are safety planning tools. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 379. The Navy’s attempt to shoehorn them into Exemption 7(F) is unavailing.

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit’s construction of Exemption 2 to encompass operational safety documents like ESQD arcs is inconsistent with the statute’s text, legislative history, and this Court’s precedent. The documents sought are not "internal personnel rules and practices," nor do they present a real risk of circumvention. The judgment below should be reversed.
