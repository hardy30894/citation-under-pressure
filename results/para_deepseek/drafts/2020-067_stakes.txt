## ARGUMENT

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a federal appellate court conducting plain-error review may consult the entire record—including evidence not presented to the jury—to determine whether a Rehaif error affected the defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness of the trial. That conclusion follows from this Court’s plain-error precedents and from the nature of the prejudice inquiry.

### I. Plain-Error Review Permits Consideration of the Whole Record

Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), a court of appeals may correct an unpreserved error only if the defendant shows (1) error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affects substantial rights, and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732–36 (1993). The third prong requires the defendant to demonstrate “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different.” *Molina-Martinez v. United States*, 578 U.S. 189, 194 (2016) (quoting *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 81–82 (2004)).

That prejudice inquiry is not confined to the evidence the jury saw. This Court has repeatedly held that an appellate court reviewing for plain error “may consult the whole record” to assess the effect of an error. *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 59 (2002). In *Vonn*, the Court explained that Rule 52(b) “contemplates that the court of appeals will consult the record” and that the defendant’s burden of showing prejudice “cannot be satisfied by a record that is incomplete.” *Id.* at 63–64. Similarly, in *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 470 (1997), the Court considered the entire record—including evidence not before the jury—to conclude that a *Booker*-style error did not affect the defendant’s substantial rights. And in *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 633 (2002), the Court looked to “the evidence the Government introduced at trial” to determine that an indictment error was harmless. These cases establish that plain-error review is a record-based inquiry, not a trial-record-only inquiry.

The same principle governs here. The Eleventh Circuit did not hold a new evidentiary hearing or consider extrinsic facts. It reviewed the record on appeal—including Greer’s plea agreement, the presentence report, and his prior convictions—to determine whether the omission of a *Rehaif* instruction prejudiced him. That is precisely what *Vonn* and *Johnson* authorize.

### II. The Record Conclusively Shows That Greer Knew He Was a Felon

In *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191, 2200 (2019), this Court held that § 922(g) requires the government to prove the defendant knew he possessed a firearm and knew he belonged to the relevant prohibited status. Greer’s trial predated *Rehaif*, and the jury was not instructed on the knowledge-of-status element. The Eleventh Circuit correctly found plain error. But it also correctly found that Greer could not show prejudice because the record conclusively established that he knew he was a felon.

Greer had five prior convictions for aggravated burglary under Ohio law. He served substantial prison sentences for those offenses. The plea agreement in this case expressly stated that Greer was “punishable as an Armed Career Criminal” based on those convictions. The presentence report—which was part of the record—detailed his criminal history and his prior incarceration. A defendant who has been convicted of multiple felonies and imprisoned for them necessarily knows that he is a felon. *See United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. 285, 294 (2008) (noting that a person’s prior convictions are “the kind of evidence that would be probative of knowledge”). The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that Greer could not show a reasonable probability of a different outcome was therefore correct.

Greer’s contrary argument would require an appellate court to ignore the record and pretend that a defendant with five felony convictions and multiple prison terms might not have known his status. That is not the law. Plain-error review is designed to correct errors that “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings,” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734, not to reward defendants who cannot show any plausible prejudice.

### III. The Fairness, Integrity, and Public Reputation of the Trial Were Not Impaired

The fourth prong of plain-error review is discretionary, but it is not a backdoor for relief when the record forecloses prejudice. This Court has explained that the fourth prong is reserved for errors that “would result in a miscarriage of justice” or that “seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, 585 U.S. 129, 138 (2018) (quoting *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736). Where, as here, the record conclusively demonstrates that the defendant knew he was a felon, the omission of a *Rehaif* instruction did not undermine the fairness of the trial. The jury would have convicted even if properly instructed. *See United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258, 262 (2010) (plain-error review asks whether the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings”).

Greer’s argument that the court should have limited itself to the trial record would transform plain-error review into a technicality-driven windfall. This Court has never adopted such a rule, and it should not do so now. The Eleventh Circuit’s judgment should be affirmed.
