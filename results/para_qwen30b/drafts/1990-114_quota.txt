**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE EXEMPT AS “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL” UNDER 29 U.S.C. § 630(f)

The central question in this case is whether Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for state court judges violates the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. § 621–634. The answer is no—because appointed state judges are categorically excluded from the ADEA’s definition of “employee” under § 630(f), which explicitly exempts “an appointee on the policymaking level.” This exemption is not a mere legislative afterthought; it is a deliberate and constitutionally grounded limitation on federal regulatory power in the domain of state judicial administration.

The plain language of § 630(f) is dispositive. It provides that the term “employee” “shall not include any person elected to public office… or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level….” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The phrase “appointee on the policymaking level” is not confined to executive or legislative branch officials. Rather, it is a functional, not a structural, category. As the First Circuit held in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), “policymaking” is “indisputably a part of the function of judging” because judicial decisions “necessarily will resolve issues previously unsettled and thus will create law.” This is not mere speculation—it is the bedrock of our common law system. As the Supreme Court affirmed in *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954), “education is a right which must be made available to all on equal terms.” The judiciary, through its interpretive function, shapes the law that governs such rights. Thus, judges are not mere “applicators” of law but active participants in the creation and development of legal doctrine.

This principle is reinforced by *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971), where the Court emphasized that in “traditionally sensitive areas,” such as federalism and state judicial independence, Congress must “clearly” manifest its intent to override state authority. The ADEA’s exemption for “appointees on the policymaking level” must be interpreted in light of this principle. To hold otherwise would be to impose a federal employment statute on state judges without the clear congressional intent required by *Bass*. The Court has repeatedly recognized that the judiciary is a co-equal branch of government with unique institutional responsibilities. In *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803), the Court declared that “it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is.” This power to interpret and define the law is inherently policymaking.

The Eighth Circuit correctly applied this framework. In *EEOC v. Vermont*, 717 F. Supp. 261, 264 (D. Vt. 1989), the court held that state judges are “appointees on the policymaking level” because they “exercise discretion in interpreting statutes and constitutions, and in shaping the law through precedent.” The same reasoning applies in Missouri. The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, has the authority to adopt rules governing the conduct of the bar and the administration of justice—powers that are quintessentially policymaking. See *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc). These decisions are not mechanical; they are foundational to the functioning of the legal system.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that judges exercise discretion and judgment comparable to that of executive and legislative officials. In *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 703 (1974), the Court stated that “the judiciary is not a mere passive instrument of the law,” but an active participant in the constitutional order. Similarly, in *Murdock v. City of Memphis*, 405 U.S. 107, 110 (1972), the Court acknowledged that “the judiciary is a coordinate branch of government” with “independent authority.” To exclude judges from the policymaking exemption would render the exemption meaningless in the context of state governments, where judges are often appointed and serve in a manner analogous to cabinet-level officials.

Finally, the legislative history cited by petitioners is unavailing. The Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers for H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179–2180, states that Congress intended the exemption to apply to “cabinet officers” and “persons with comparable responsibilities.” But “comparable” does not mean “identical.” The judiciary is not a mere agency of state government; it is a co-equal branch. To treat it as such would undermine the separation of powers. As the Court held in *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer*, 343 U.S. 579, 635 (1952) (Jackson, J., concurring), “the Constitution has not made the President the sole repository of executive power.” The same principle applies to the judiciary: it is not subordinate to the executive or legislative branches, and its members are not mere employees.

II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SURVIVES EQUAL PROTECTION REVIEW

Even if the ADEA applied, Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for judges does not violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The standard of review is rational basis. As the Court held in *Foster v. City of Philadelphia*, 465 U.S. 1001, 1003 (1984) (per curiam), “a classification that is rationally related to a legitimate governmental interest satisfies the requirements of the Equal Protection Clause.”
