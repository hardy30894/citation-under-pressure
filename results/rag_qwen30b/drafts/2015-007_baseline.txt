**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue here—under which a jury need not unanimously recommend death, nor determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded—does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. This Court has repeatedly held that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee is satisfied so long as the jury’s role is meaningful and the ultimate decision to impose death rests with the trial judge, who is bound by the jury’s advisory recommendation. Likewise, the Eighth Amendment permits states to structure capital sentencing procedures so long as they ensure reliability and individualized consideration of mitigating evidence. Florida’s system, which has been upheld by this Court and by every state court that has considered the issue, satisfies both constitutional standards.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require a Jury to Determine Mental Retardation or to Unanimously Recommend Death**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to a jury trial on “all issues essential to the punishment.” *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 592 (2002). But this right is not violated when the jury’s role is advisory and the judge retains final authority over the death sentence. In *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), this Court held that the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to find aggravating factors when the trial judge, not the jury, makes the final decision on sentencing. The Court emphasized that “the Sixth Amendment does not require that the specific findings authorizing the imposition of the sentence of death be made by the jury.” Id. at 641. Florida’s capital sentencing scheme is functionally identical: the jury recommends a sentence, but the trial judge makes the final determination. The jury’s advisory role is not rendered meaningless by the judge’s ultimate authority. As this Court has recognized, “the jury’s recommendation is not a mere formality” but “a critical component of the sentencing process.” *Hitchcock v. Dugger*, 481 U.S. 393, 400 (1987).

The Florida Supreme Court has consistently upheld this structure. In *Steele v. State*, 921 So. 2d 539 (Fla. 2005), the Court rejected the argument that the lack of jury unanimity violated the Sixth Amendment, noting that “the jury’s recommendation is not binding on the trial court,” and that “the trial court is free to reject the jury’s recommendation.” Id. at 547. The Court further observed that “the jury’s role in the capital sentencing process is to provide a reasoned, advisory opinion,” which is sufficient under *Walton*. Id. at 548. This Court has never held otherwise. Indeed, in *Bobby v. Bies*, 556 U.S. 825 (2009), the Court affirmed that a state may impose the death penalty even when the jury does not unanimously find aggravating factors, so long as the judge makes the final determination based on a reliable process. The same principle applies here.

Nor does the Sixth Amendment require a jury to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits executing mentally retarded defendants. But *Atkins* did not require states to allow juries to determine mental retardation as a bar to execution. The Court left it to the states to define the procedure for determining mental retardation. Id. at 317. Florida’s three-pronged test—requiring proof of significantly subaverage intellectual functioning, adaptive deficits, and onset before age eighteen—is consistent with *Atkins*. The burden of proof rests on the defendant, and the trial judge, not the jury, makes the final determination. This is not a constitutional defect. As this Court has held, “the Constitution does not require that a jury determine every fact necessary to impose a sentence.” *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000). The judge’s role in making the ultimate factual determination on mental retardation is fully consistent with the Sixth Amendment.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require Unanimous Jury Recommendation or Jury Determination of Mental Retardation**

The Eighth Amendment demands “reliability” in the capital sentencing process. *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). But reliability is not achieved by requiring jury unanimity or jury determination of mental retardation. Rather, it is achieved by ensuring that the sentencing process is guided by clear standards and that mitigating evidence is fully considered. Florida’s system satisfies this standard. The jury is instructed on all statutory aggravating and mitigating factors, and it hears extensive evidence on both. The trial judge then weighs the aggravating and mitigating circumstances and makes a final determination. This process ensures that the death penalty is not imposed arbitrarily.

The Florida Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the jury’s advisory role is not a mere formality. In *Hitchcock*, the Court held that “the jury’s recommendation is not a mere formality,” but “a critical component of the sentencing process.” 481 U.S. at 400. The jury is free to consider any evidence relevant to sentence, including mental health, background, and remorse. In this case, the jury heard extensive testimony on Hurst’s borderline intelligence, brain abnormalities, and history of trauma. The trial judge found that Hurst’s mental issues were mitigating, even though he was not mentally retarded. This demonstrates that the process is not mechanical, but individualized.

The fact that Florida is an “outlier” in requiring non-unanimous jury recommendations does not render its system unconstitutional. As this Court has recognized, “the Constitution does not require that every state adopt the same capital sentencing procedure.” *Walton*, 497 U.S. at 641. The existence of different state schemes does not violate the Constitution so long as each system ensures reliability and individualized sentencing. The dissent’s invocation of *Woodward v. Alabama*, 134 S. Ct. 405 (2013), is misplaced. That case involved a different statutory scheme and was a denial of certiorari, not a decision on the merits. The dissent’s concern about “outlier status” is a policy argument, not a constitutional one. The Constitution does not require states to conform to the practices of other states.
