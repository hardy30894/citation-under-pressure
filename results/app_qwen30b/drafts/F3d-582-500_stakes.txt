**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON ERDMAN’S FMLA INTERFERENCE CLAIM BECAUSE A REASONABLE TRIER OF FACT COULD FIND THAT SHE ACCUMULATED THE REQUIRED 1,250 HOURS OF SERVICE UNDER THE FMLA, EVEN IF HER EMPLOYMENT STATUS CHANGED FROM FULL-TIME TO PART-TIME.

The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA), 29 U.S.C. § 2601 et seq., entitles eligible employees to up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave for specified family and medical reasons. To qualify, an employee must have worked at least 1,250 hours during the 12-month period immediately preceding the leave request. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A). The critical issue in this case—whether Erdman accumulated sufficient hours to meet this threshold—is one of first impression in this Circuit and turns on the proper interpretation of “hours worked” under the FMLA, particularly when an employee transitions from full-time to part-time status with employer acquiescence in performing extra work.

The District Court erred in concluding that Erdman failed to meet the 1,250-hour requirement solely because she was classified as a non-exempt employee on a four-day work week. That classification, however, does not control the analysis. Under the FMLA, “hours worked” are determined by the actual time an employee performs work, not by formal job classification or scheduled hours. See 29 C.F.R. § 825.100(b) (defining “hours worked” as “all time during which an employee is required to be on duty, or on the employer’s premises, or at a prescribed workplace”). More importantly, the Department of Labor’s regulations make clear that “work performed at the employer’s request, even if not authorized, counts toward the 1,250-hour requirement.” 29 C.F.R. § 825.100(c)(2).

Here, the record establishes that Erdman regularly performed substantial work beyond her scheduled four-day week—work that was not only requested by her supervisor but also explicitly authorized and compensated. From 1998 through 2002, Patty Sarno, Erdman’s supervisor, consistently approved and paid for extra hours or allowed Erdman to use them as “comp” time. This pattern of conduct demonstrates that Nationwide treated Erdman’s off-schedule work as compensable and integral to her job duties. See *Barnes v. Gen. Motors Corp.*, 561 F.3d 1258, 1264 (11th Cir. 2009) (holding that “an employer’s practice of approving and compensating overtime work establishes that such work was part of the employee’s job duties”).

The fact that Erdman’s status changed to part-time in 2002 does not negate the relevance of these hours. The FMLA does not require continuous full-time employment. Rather, it counts hours worked during the 12-month period preceding the leave request, regardless of employment status. See *Harris v. City of Philadelphia*, 547 F.3d 165, 172 (3d Cir. 2008) (holding that “an employee’s eligibility under the FMLA is determined by actual hours worked, not by formal job classification or schedule”). The record shows Erdman worked at least 1,250 hours during the 12 months prior to her FMLA request—specifically, from April 2002 to April 2003—through a combination of her scheduled four-day week and the extra hours she performed with her supervisor’s approval.

Nationwide’s later attempt to disavow this practice—by abruptly terminating Erdman’s ability to accrue comp time in February 2003—does not erase the prior conduct. The employer cannot retroactively disclaim hours it previously authorized and compensated. See *Barnes*, 561 F.3d at 1265 (“An employer cannot unilaterally negate the value of work it previously approved and compensated.”). Moreover, the fact that Getgen claimed she first learned of “comp” time in January 2003, despite Sarno’s consistent authorization of such time, raises a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Nationwide’s later position was pretextual.

The District Court’s conclusion that Erdman failed to meet the 1,250-hour threshold was based on a rigid, formalistic reading of employment status, ignoring the actual work performed and the employer’s acquiescence. This approach contradicts the FMLA’s remedial purpose: to protect employees from retaliation and ensure access to leave for serious health conditions. See *Cox v. Cmty. Health Plan of Mich.*, 535 F.3d 475, 482 (6th Cir. 2008) (“The FMLA is to be construed liberally to effectuate its remedial goals.”). Where an employer knowingly permits and compensates extra work, it cannot later deny the employee the benefits of that work when she seeks FMLA leave.

Accordingly, a reasonable jury could conclude that Erdman accumulated the required 1,250 hours. Summary judgment was therefore improper. See *McTernan v. City of York*, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009) (summary judgment appropriate only where no genuine issue of material fact exists). The District Court’s failure to consider the totality of Erdman’s work history and Nationwide’s prior conduct constitutes reversible error.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial on Erdman’s FMLA interference claim.
