**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Tax Court Erred in Concluding That Mrs. Davis’s Interest in the Trust Did Not Qualify as a “Qualifying Income Interest for Life” Under I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii).**

To qualify for the marital deduction under § 2056(b)(7), the surviving spouse must have a “qualifying income interest for life,” which requires that she be “entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals.” I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). The Tax Court erred in holding that Mrs. Davis’s interest failed this requirement because the trustee’s discretion to distribute income based on her “health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare” rendered her interest nonqualifying. *See Estate of Nicholson v. Commissioner*, 94 T.C. 666, 675 (1990) (holding that a surviving spouse’s interest in a trust that required distributions for her “support and maintenance” was a qualifying income interest where the trustee’s discretion was limited to determining the amount necessary for such purposes).

The Tax Court’s interpretation conflicts with the plain language of the statute and controlling precedent. In *Estate of Lester v. Commissioner*, 63 T.C. 314, 320 (1974), the Tax Court held that a surviving spouse’s right to receive “such amounts of income and principal as the trustee shall deem necessary for her comfortable support and maintenance” qualified as a “qualifying income interest for life” under former § 2056(b)(5), the predecessor to § 2056(b)(7). The court reasoned that the trustee’s discretion was circumscribed by an ascertainable standard, ensuring that the surviving spouse’s interest was not terminable. *Id.* at 321. Similarly, in *Estate of Spencer v. United States*, 58 A.F.T.R.2d 86-5539 (D. Kan. 1986), the court upheld a marital deduction where the surviving spouse’s interest was subject to a standard requiring distributions for her “support and maintenance,” holding that the standard prevented the interest from being terminable. *Id.* at 86-5542.

Here, the Amendment explicitly requires the trustee to distribute all net income to Mrs. Davis for her “health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare,” which is indistinguishable from the standard upheld in *Lester* and *Spencer*. The Tax Court’s contrary conclusion misapprehends the statutory requirement and disregards binding precedent.

II. **The Tax Court Erred in Holding That the Trustee’s Discretion to Consider Other Resources Rendered Mrs. Davis’s Interest Nonqualifying.**

The Tax Court further erred in relying on the trustee’s authority to consider “any other income or resources of the beneficiary known to the trustee and reasonably available” in determining distributions. *See* I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) (requiring only that the surviving spouse be entitled to all income payable at least annually). The Commissioner has consistently taken the position that such a provision does not disqualify a marital deduction where the surviving spouse’s right to income is otherwise unconditional. *See* Treas. Reg. § 20.2056(b)-5(f)(4) (stating that a surviving spouse’s right to income is not defeated by a trustee’s authority to consider other resources if the surviving spouse is otherwise entitled to all income).

This interpretation is consistent with the legislative history of § 2056(b)(7), which was enacted to liberalize the marital deduction for terminable interests. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 97-201, at 160 (1981), reprinted in 1981 U.S.C.C.A.N. 233, 360. Courts have repeatedly upheld marital deductions where the surviving spouse’s right to income is subject to a trustee’s discretion to consider other resources, so long as the surviving spouse is otherwise entitled to all income. *See, e.g., Estate of Powell v. Commissioner*, 89 T.C. 729, 735 (1987) (holding that a surviving spouse’s right to income subject to a trustee’s consideration of other resources qualified for the marital deduction); *Estate of Vissering v. United States*, 990 F.2d 572, 576 (8th Cir. 1993) (same).

III. **The Tax Court Erred in Holding That the Trustee’s Discretion to Invade Principal Rendered Mrs. Davis’s Interest Terminable.**

The Tax Court also erred in concluding that the trustee’s authority to invade principal for Mrs. Davis’s benefit rendered her interest terminable. *See* I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) (requiring that “no person has a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse”). The invasion power here is expressly limited to circumstances where Mrs. Davis has exhausted her own resources, and the invasion is for her benefit. Such a power does not create a terminable interest because it is exercisable only for the surviving spouse’s benefit and does not permit appointment to any other person. *See* Treas. Reg. § 20.2056(b)-5(f)(5) (stating that a power to invade principal for the surviving spouse’s benefit does not disqualify a marital deduction if the power is exercisable only for her benefit and does not permit appointment to others).

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Byrum*, 408 U.S. 125, 140 (1972), which held that a power exercisable only for the benefit of the surviving spouse does not create a terminable interest. *See also Estate of Van Horne v. Commissioner*, 72 T.C. 707, 712 (1979) (holding that a power to invade principal for the surviving spouse’s support and maintenance did not disqualify a marital deduction).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Tax Court erred in denying the marital deduction under § 2056(b)(7). The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
