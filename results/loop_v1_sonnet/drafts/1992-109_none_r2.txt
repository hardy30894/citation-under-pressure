**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit held that due process requires a heightened "reasoned choice" standard—more demanding than the standard for competency to stand trial—before a court may accept a waiver of counsel or a guilty plea. That holding is irreconcilable with this Court's competency doctrine, misapprehends this Court's waiver jurisprudence, and finds no support in the very decision the court of appeals invoked. The judgment below should be reversed.

**I. Due Process Requires Only the Competency Standard Set Forth in *Dusky v. United States*.**

This Court has defined the constitutional floor of competency in a single, uniform way. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that the test is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. *Dusky* announced the constitutional measure of competency itself; it did not create a floor applicable only to the decision to stand trial while leaving other decisions to a stricter test. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), confirmed that due process forbids subjecting a defendant to proceedings when he lacks that capacity, holding that a State's failure to provide adequate procedures for resolving doubts about a defendant's mental capacity denies him due process. *Id.* at 385–86.

Nothing in this framework changes when a defendant, rather than proceeding to trial, elects to plead guilty or to represent himself. The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard rests on the premise that the *decision* to waive rights demands greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But the competency inquiry has always asked whether a defendant can understand and participate in the proceedings against him—not whether his particular choice is the wisest one available. Once a defendant satisfies *Dusky*, due process asks whether his choice was voluntary and intelligent, not whether it was strategically sound.

**II. This Court's Waiver Decisions Confirm That the Relevant Inquiry Is Voluntariness and Knowledge, Not an Elevated Capacity Threshold.**

This Court's waiver decisions confirm that the constitutional inquiry concerns the character of the waiver, not a heightened capacity standard. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court explained that "[a] waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, and required courts to presume against such abandonment absent a clear showing to the contrary. *Id.* That standard asks whether a defendant knew of and intended to relinquish a right—a question distinct from his baseline mental capacity under *Dusky*.

*Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required a searching inquiry before a court accepts a waiver of counsel, so that the record demonstrates the defendant understood the charges, the range of punishment, and the risks of proceeding alone. *Id.* at 723–24. And in *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), the Court recognized that a defendant may competently elect to conduct his own defense, treating that election as a question of intelligent waiver rather than one demanding superior mental capacity. *Id.* at 279. *Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), confirms that the touchstone is whether the record affirmatively shows an informed decision, not whether the defendant possessed some elevated quantum of rationality beyond that required to stand trial. *Id.* at 174–75.

*Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), addressed the guilty-plea context directly. The Court explained that "[a] plea of guilty is more than a confession which admits that the accused did various acts; it is itself a conviction; nothing remains but to give judgment and determine punishment." *Id.* at 242 (quoting *Kercheval v. United States*, 274 U.S. 220, 223 (1927)). Because such a plea works so significant a waiver, *Boykin* required an affirmative showing on the record that the plea was voluntary and intelligent. *Id.* at 242–44. Nowhere did the Court suggest that this required a standard of rationality beyond *Dusky*'s "reasonable degree of rational understanding." 362 U.S. at 402.

**III. *Westbrook v. Arizona* Does Not Support a Heightened Competency Standard.**

The Ninth Circuit traced its "reasoned choice" language to *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), but that decision cannot bear the weight placed on it. *Westbrook* remanded because the record failed to show that the trial court had determined "whether petitioner had the capacity to waive his constitutional right to the assistance of counsel." *Id.* at 150. The Court did not articulate a standard distinct from that governing competency generally, nor did it hold that waiver of counsel demands a quantum of rationality beyond *Dusky*'s "reasonable degree of rational understanding." *Westbrook* establishes only that a trial court must separately determine whether a valid waiver occurred; it supplies no basis for a freestanding, elevated mental-capacity threshold applicable only to waiver decisions.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard governing whether a defendant may stand trial—articulated in *Dusky* and *Pate*—likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional protections required by *Zerbst*, *Von Moltke*, *Adams*, *Carter*, and *Boykin* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold, and *Westbrook* supplies no authority for one. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
