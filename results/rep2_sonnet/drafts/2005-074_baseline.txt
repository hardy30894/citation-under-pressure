# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Reach Reentries That Occurred Before IIRIRA's Effective Date

Because Congress did not "expressly prescribe[]" the reach of the reinstatement statute to reentries predating IIRIRA's 1997 effective date, this Court's two-step retroactivity framework governs. *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994). Under that framework, courts must first ask "whether Congress has expressly prescribed the statute's proper reach," and if it has not, must determine whether applying the statute to pre-enactment conduct "would have retroactive effect." *Id.* Nothing in the text of § 241(a)(5) speaks to the temporal reach of the statute; it says only that an order "shall be reinstated from its original date" against an alien who "has reentered the United States illegally." That silence is decisive, because "the presumption against retroactive legislation is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence." *Id.* at 265.

This Court has applied that presumption with particular force in the immigration context. In *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), the Court refused to read IIRIRA's repeal of discretionary relief under former § 212(c) to apply to aliens who had pleaded guilty before the repeal, explaining that "a statute that is ambiguous with respect to retroactive application is construed to be unambiguously prospective," *id.* at 320 (quoting *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 264), and that this canon applies with special force where, as here, "the aliens who are the subject of this case surely relied upon the state of the law" at the time they acted, *id.* at 325. Just as the *St. Cyr* petitioners relied on the availability of § 212(c) relief when they pleaded guilty, petitioner Fernandez-Vargas built a life in the United States for nineteen years—marrying a citizen and seeking adjustment of status—before Congress enacted § 241(a)(5). Absent a clear statement that Congress intended the reinstatement bar to reach back to reentries that predated the statute by fifteen years, the statute should be construed prospectively only.

## II. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Petitioner's Pre-Enactment Reentry Is Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statute's text were read to encompass pre-1997 reentries, applying it to Fernandez-Vargas would have an impermissible retroactive effect because it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. The touchstone of the retroactivity inquiry is "whether the new provision attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment," a determination guided by "familiar considerations of fair notice, reasonable reliance, and settled expectations." *Id.* at 270. Section 241(a)(5) fails that test in two independent respects.

First, the statute strips petitioner of a benefit—the opportunity to seek adjustment of status based on marriage to a citizen—that was available to him under the law as it stood when he reentered in 1982 and for fifteen years thereafter. This Court has recognized that a statute has impermissible retroactive effect where it "would impair rights a party possessed when he acted." *Id.* at 280. Petitioner possessed, at the time of his reentry and throughout the ensuing years, the ability to seek relief from deportation through the ordinary channels of immigration law. Section 241(a)(5) retroactively extinguishes that possibility by categorically denying "any relief" under the Act. Just as the Court in *St. Cyr* found it retroactive to eliminate relief for which an alien "would have been eligible" under prior law, 533 U.S. at 325, so too here: Congress may not, consistent with *Landgraf*, convert a completed act of reentry—lawful in its consequences at the time—into a permanent forfeiture of all future avenues of relief.

Second, § 241(a)(5) "increase[s] a party's liability for past conduct" by transforming what was, at the time of reentry, an act carrying the ordinary consequences of illegal presence into an act that now triggers automatic reinstatement of a decades-old order without any hearing, without eligibility for adjustment, and without regard to intervening equities such as marriage to a citizen. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. This Court has cautioned that "a statute does not operate 'retrospectively' merely because it is applied in a case arising from conduct antedating the statute's enactment," *id.* at 269, but where, as here, the statute reaches back to reattach dramatically enhanced legal consequences to a completed act, the presumption against retroactivity controls. See *Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp.*, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988) ("Retroactivity is not favored in the law," and congressional enactments "will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result.").

Finally, the reliance interests at stake here are precisely those that *Landgraf* and *St. Cyr* sought to protect. Petitioner did not merely reenter and remain passively; he married a United States citizen and affirmatively sought adjustment of status through channels that Congress had made available to aliens in his position. To apply § 241(a)(5) retroactively is to pull the rug out from under exactly the kind of "settled expectations" that this Court's retroactivity jurisprudence forbids Congress from disturbing absent unmistakably clear statutory language. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270; *Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 358 (1999) (retroactive effect assessed by reference to "the extent to which the new provision attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment"). Because Congress did not clearly authorize this retroactive application, and because such application impairs petitioner's rights and reliance interests arising from conduct completed well before 1997, the judgment below should be reversed.
