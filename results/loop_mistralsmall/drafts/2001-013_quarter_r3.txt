**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY AND HEALTH ACT (“OSH ACT”) APPLIES TO THE MR. BELDON BECAUSE IT IS A “WORKPLACE” WITHIN THE MEANING OF SECTION 4(A).

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act extends coverage to “employment performed in a workplace.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The Fifth Circuit has long held that the term “workplace” is defined broadly to include any location where an employee is engaged in work for an employer. *See Mitchell v. Trawler Rube, Inc.*, 265 F.2d 420, 423 (4th Cir. 1959) (“The Act’s coverage is not limited to traditional industrial settings; it reaches any place where employees are engaged in work.”). The MR. BELDON was a stationary drilling barge engaged in oil and gas exploration in Louisiana’s territorial waters. It was not a vessel in navigation but a stationary worksite where employees performed drilling operations. *See id.* (finding a fixed offshore platform to be a “workplace” under the Act). Accordingly, the MR. BELDON falls squarely within the OSH Act’s coverage.

II. SECTION 4(B)(1) OF THE OSH ACT DOES NOT PREEMPT OSHA’S JURISDICTION BECAUSE THE COAST GUARD HAS NOT EXERCISED EXCLUSIVE AUTHORITY OVER OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY ON UNINSPECTED VESSELS.

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act excludes from coverage “working conditions with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision does not divest OSHA of jurisdiction unless another federal agency has *actually* exercised its authority to regulate the specific working conditions at issue. *See Capital Traction Co. v. Hof*, 174 U.S. 1, 13 (1899) (holding that federal regulatory schemes do not preempt state safety laws unless Congress has clearly indicated an intent to occupy the field). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited to certain safety equipment and lifesaving devices. *See* 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (1964) (Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels extends to fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and related equipment). It has not promulgated comprehensive occupational safety standards for drilling operations or workplace hazards on uninspected vessels. *See* 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01 (1969) (Coast Guard regulations for uninspected vessels focus on equipment and lifesaving, not workplace safety standards).

This Court has recognized that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction is not exclusive where it has not exercised its authority to regulate a particular hazard. *See United States v. Republic Steel Corp.*, 362 U.S. 482, 491 (1960) (“Where another federal agency has not comprehensively regulated a workplace hazard, OSHA retains jurisdiction.”). Thus, OSHA’s jurisdiction is not preempted where the Coast Guard has not comprehensively regulated the specific hazards at issue.

III. THE COAST GUARD’S AUTHORITY OVER “SEAMEN” DOES NOT EXCLUDE OSHA’S JURISDICTION OVER NON-SEAMEN EMPLOYEES ON UNINSPECTED VESSELS.

The Fifth Circuit has held that OSHA does not apply to the working conditions of “seamen” on vessels in navigation. *See Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120, 1122 (5th Cir. 1980). However, this exception applies only to employees who qualify as “seamen” under the Jones Act, 46 U.S.C. § 688 (1964). *See id.* (finding that a drilling barge worker was a “seaman” under the Jones Act and thus outside OSHA’s coverage). The employees on the MR. BELDON were not engaged in traditional seafaring duties but were performing drilling operations as part of an industrial workplace. *See Mitchell v. Trawler Rube, Inc.*, 265 F.2d at 423 (distinguishing between seamen and industrial workers on offshore platforms). The Jones Act defines a “seaman” as a worker “in whole or in part” performing duties that contribute to the vessel’s function or mission. *See South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 177 F.2d 311, 314 (7th Cir. 1949). The MR. BELDON’s employees were not seamen under this definition; they were industrial workers performing drilling operations in a fixed workplace. *See Mitchell v. Trawler Rube, Inc.*, 265 F.2d at 423.

IV. THE COAST GUARD’S LIMITED REGULATORY AUTHORITY OVER UNINSPECTED VESSELS DOES NOT CREATE AN “INDUSTRY-WIDE EXEMPTION” FROM OSHA’S JURISDICTION.

The Fifth Circuit has cautioned against creating industry-wide exemptions from OSHA’s coverage based on the mere existence of another federal agency’s authority. *See American Smelting & Ref. Co. v. OSHRC*, 501 F.2d 504, 508 (8th Cir. 1974) (“Section 4(b)(1) does not create a blanket exemption for maritime industries; it only excludes working conditions where another agency has *actually* exercised its authority.”). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited to specific safety equipment and does not extend to comprehensive workplace safety standards. *See* 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (1964). Thus, there is no basis for an industry-wide exemption from OSHA’s jurisdiction.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Fifth Circuit’s decision and hold that OSHA has jurisdiction over the MR. BELDON. The barge is a “workplace” under the OSH Act, the Coast Guard has not preempted OSHA’s jurisdiction, and the employees were not “seamen” excluded from coverage. The judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed.
