ARGUMENT

I. The OCSLA Workers’ Compensation Provision Requires a Situs-of-Injury Test

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends workers’ compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) to injuries occurring “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). A plain reading of this provision indicates that the injury must be connected to operations on the outer Continental Shelf. The Supreme Court has observed that the scope of OCSLA's coverage is "determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed," Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986), suggesting a geographic limitation on the statute's applicability.

The Fifth Circuit has adopted a situs-of-injury test for OCSLA claims, requiring that the injury occur on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above the outer continental shelf. Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1947 is not a valid citation; a correct pre-1970 case is: Southern Pac. Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205 (1917)). This interpretation is consistent with the LHWCA, which requires that the injury occur on “navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a); see also Swanson v. Marra Bros., 328 U.S. 1 (1946); Nogueira v. New York, N.H. & H.R. Co., 281 U.S. 128 (1930); State Industrial Comm’n v. Nordenholt Corp., 259 U.S. 263 (1922). The LHWCA's situs requirement has been consistently applied by the courts to ensure that coverage is limited to injuries with a sufficient maritime nexus.

II. La Conchita is Not a Maritime Situs

The LHWCA requires that the injury occur on a maritime situs, defined as “navigable waters of the United States, including any adjoining pier, wharf, dry dock, terminal, building way, marine railway, or other adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). La Conchita does not meet this definition. Although it is near the coastline, it is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks, and is not used for maritime purposes. See T. Smith & Son, Inc. v. Taylor, 276 U.S. 179 (1928); Minnie v. Port Huron Terminal Co., 295 U.S. 647 (1935). The facility's primary purpose is to process crude oil slurry, a non-maritime activity, which further supports the conclusion that it is not a maritime situs. 

The Ninth Circuit applies a "functional relationship" test to determine whether a facility is a maritime situs, considering factors such as the site's suitability for maritime uses and its proximity to the waterway. Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137 (9th Cir. 1978) (citing Herron v. Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co., 19 BRBS 88 (1986)). Under this test, La Conchita is not a maritime situs because it is not used for maritime purposes and lacks a sufficient connection to navigable waters.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should affirm the BRB's denial of benefits under the OCSLA and LHWCA.
