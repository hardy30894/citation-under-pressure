ARGUMENT

I. Facebook Was Required to Prove Invalidity by Clear and Convincing Evidence, and the Jury’s Verdict Cannot Stand on This Record

A patent carries a statutory presumption of validity, and the burden of overcoming that presumption rests on the party asserting invalidity. Radio Corp. of America v. Radio Engineering Laboratories, Inc., 293 U.S. 1, 7-8 (1934). This Court has long recognized that the presumption is not lightly overcome; it demands more than a mere preponderance. Id. The Supreme Court reaffirmed in Mumm v. Jacob E. Decker & Sons, 301 U.S. 168, 171 (1937), that “the burden of proof to make good this defense” of invalidity rests upon the party urging it, and that burden does not shift merely because the patentee introduced its own product into commerce. Facebook’s proof here—resting almost entirely on a marketing white paper and inventor testimony describing the Leader2Leader® concept in general commercial terms—falls short of the “clear and cogent” showing required to strip an inventor of his statutory grant. Radio Corp. of America, 293 U.S. at 8.

II. The Record Does Not Clearly and Convincingly Establish That the Pre-Critical-Date Leader2Leader® Product Practiced Every Limitation of the Asserted Claims

It is axiomatic that the claims, not the accused or prior product’s general marketing description, measure the invention. General Elec. Co. v. Wabash Appliance Corp., 304 U.S. 364, 369 (1938). The scope of the patent grant is fixed by what the claims particularly point out and distinctly claim, and invalidity cannot be established by loose analogy to a commercial embodiment absent a limitation-by-limitation showing. Continental Paper Bag Co. v. Eastern Paper Bag Co., 210 U.S. 405, 419 (1908) (the patentee’s exclusive right extends to the invention as claimed). Every asserted claim requires “dynamically associating metadata” with data and “dynamically updating” that metadata as a user moves between environments. '761 patent, col.21 ll.42-56. The January 2002 white paper on which Facebook relied describes a “Big Board” and general “Input & Display Collaboration Devices,” J.A. 27210, but nowhere establishes that the system, as publicly offered before December 10, 2002, performed the specific, dynamic metadata-tracking function the claims require. A prior sale or use invalidates a claim only if it discloses each and every limitation; general commercial puffery describing a product as “operational” or “[f]ully developed,” J.A. 27204, 27207, does not satisfy that exacting standard. See Continental Paper Bag Co., 210 U.S. at 419.

III. Any Pre-Critical-Date Activity Was, at Most, Developmental and Experimental, Not a Public Use or Sale Within the Meaning of § 102(b)

The Supreme Court has long distinguished between a public use of a completed invention and use incidental to further development or testing. In Elizabeth v. Pavement Co., 97 U.S. 126 (1877), the Court held that use by the inventor, or under his direction, for the purpose of testing and perfecting the invention, is not the kind of public use contemplated by the patent laws. The Court there emphasized that the nature of the use, and whether the inventor retained control and was still perfecting the device, governs the analysis. Id. at 134-35. Leader’s presentation of a white paper offering licenses, and its demonstration of a system still described internally and to prospective customers as low-volume and evolving, is precisely the kind of pre-commercial activity Elizabeth v. Pavement Co. protects.

Nor does mere knowledge or use by others convert Leader’s conduct into invalidating public use. In Andrews v. Hovey, 123 U.S. 267, 274 (1887), the Court held that the public use or sale that bars a patent must be a use of the patented invention itself, in its patented form, and not merely of something resembling it. Similarly, in Coffin v. Ogden, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 120, 124 (1874), the Court explained that a single use by a stranger, without the inventor’s consent or acquiescence, may not constitute the kind of public use contemplated by the statute. And while Egbert v. Lippmann, 104 U.S. 333 (1881), found invalidating public use where an inventor delivered a completed device to another without restriction, this case is readily distinguishable: Leader retained control of its system, continued to develop it, and offered licenses to a system still being perfected—not a finished, unrestricted commercial article as in Egbert. 104 U.S. at 336.

IV. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Denying Leader’s Motion for a New Trial

Even if the evidence were sufficient to survive JMOL, the verdict was against the clear weight of the evidence, warranting a new trial. A trial court has both the power and the duty to grant a new trial where the verdict is contrary to the weight of the evidence and would result in a miscarriage of justice. Montgomery Ward & Co. v. Duncan, 311 U.S. 243, 251 (1940). The Supreme Court has cautioned that appellate review of such discretion should ensure the trial court did not permit an unjust verdict to stand, particularly where the evidence supporting the verdict is thin and largely inferential. Fairmount Glass Works v. Cub Fork Coal Co., 287 U.S. 236, 239-40 (1932). Here, the white paper and general testimony about the Leader2Leader® “engine,” J.A. 25585-86, provided at best a weak inferential basis for finding that every limitation of eleven distinct claims was practiced before the critical date. The district court’s refusal to grant a new trial under these circumstances was error.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of invalidity, or, in the alternative, vacate and remand for a new trial.
