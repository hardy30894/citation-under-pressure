ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Committed Reversible Error by Admitting Opinion Testimony from the ADAs and the Police Lieutenant on Credibility, Probable Cause, and the Weight of the Evidence.

A. Witnesses may not vouch for the credibility of other witnesses.

“The credibility of witnesses” has always been recognized as a matter “peculiarly within the province of the jury.” Napue v. Illinois, 360 U.S. 264, 269 (1959) (observing that “the jury's estimate of the truthfulness and reliability of a given witness may well be determinative of guilt or innocence”). That principle applies with equal force in civil cases such as this one, where the central dispute—whether Ramos and Rivera or Appellants told the truth about the events of July 2, 2005—turned entirely on the jury's independent assessment of credibility. When a court itself intrudes on that function, even through a passing comment, reversal follows, because “the influence of the trial judge on the jury is necessarily and properly of great weight,” and “his lightest word or intimation is received with deference.” Quercia v. United States, 289 U.S. 466, 470 (1933). If a judge's offhand remark on a defendant's veracity requires reversal, a fortiori the sworn opinion testimony of two Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant—witnesses whom jurors would naturally regard as neutral, informed government officials—that Ramos and Rivera were believable requires the same result. See Michelson v. United States, 335 U.S. 469, 480 (1948) (recognizing that the law carefully polices the introduction of opinion evidence bearing on a witness's truthfulness because of its potential to overwhelm the jury's own judgment).

B. Witnesses may not testify to legal conclusions such as the existence of probable cause.

The ADAs' and the lieutenant's testimony that probable cause existed to arrest and charge Appellants likewise invaded territory reserved for the jury and the court. Whether probable cause existed is a legal determination resting on the credited facts, not a matter on which a lay or expert witness may simply announce a conclusion. See United States v. Di Re, 332 U.S. 581, 594–95 (1948) (analyzing the lawfulness of an arrest as a legal question turning on the facts known to the officer, not on any witness's ipse dixit). Permitting government witnesses to tell the jury the ultimate legal answer effectively directed a verdict on the central issue in the case without the safeguards that direction of a verdict requires. See Tennant v. Peoria & Pekin Union Ry. Co., 321 U.S. 29, 35 (1944) (“It is not the function of a court to search the record for conflicting circumstantial evidence in order to take the case away from the jury.”).

C. The interpretive testimony about the evidence usurped the jury's fact-finding role.

Finally, the witnesses' commentary on whether particular pieces of evidence “strengthened” or “weakened” Appellants' case did nothing but tell the jury how to weigh evidence it was fully capable of weighing itself. That, too, trenches on the jury's exclusive domain. Tennant, 321 U.S. at 35.

D. The errors were not harmless.

These errors were not harmless. The proper standard asks whether “one cannot say, with fair assurance, after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole, that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error.” Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764–65 (1946). Here, the erroneously admitted testimony went to the two most hotly contested issues in the case—Ramos's and Rivera's credibility and the existence of probable cause. Given a week-long trial that hinged on which side the jury believed, this Court cannot say with fair assurance that the verdict was unaffected. Reversal and a new trial are required.

II. The District Court Correctly Denied Appellants' Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law.

On a motion for judgment as a matter of law, the evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, and the case may be withdrawn from the jury only when “the failure of proof is so complete that the trial judge would be justified in directing a verdict.” Wilkerson v. McCarthy, 336 U.S. 53, 62 (1949). Here, the surveillance photographs, while probative, do not conclusively foreclose Appellees' account; a jury could still credit Ramos's and Rivera's testimony about what the photographs do not show. Because reasonable jurors could differ, the District Court properly left the question to the jury. Tennant, 321 U.S. at 35.

III. On Remand, the Jury Should Be Instructed Consistently with Appellants' Requests.

First, because the offense of obstructing governmental administration requires that the officers obstructed be engaged in an authorized, lawful act, the jury must be told that Higgenbottom cannot be found to have obstructed governmental administration unless Cameron's underlying arrest was itself lawful. See Di Re, 332 U.S. at 594–95 (the lawfulness of official action is a predicate legal question for the fact-finder applying the correct standard).

Second, Appellants' version of events—that Ramos and Rivera fabricated the basis for the arrests—would permit a jury to find the kind of willful, malicious conduct that warrants punitive damages. Punitive damages have long been available where the wrongdoer's conduct is shown to be malicious or willfully wrongful, not merely negligent. Lake Shore & Mich. So. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893). Because a reasonable jury crediting Appellants' testimony could so find, the punitive damages instruction should have been given and should be given on retrial.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial, with instructions consistent with Parts III.A and III.B above.
