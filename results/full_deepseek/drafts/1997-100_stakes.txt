**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Prohibits Retrial of a Prior Conviction Allegation When the Sentencing Proceeding Is Functionally Equivalent to a Trial on Guilt or Innocence.**

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). Although sentencing is traditionally a less formal proceeding, the Supreme Court has held that when a sentencing scheme “has the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence,” the Double Jeopardy Clause applies. *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981). In *Bullington*, the Court held that a capital sentencing proceeding that required the prosecution to prove aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, and in which the defendant could present mitigating evidence, was “like a trial” for double jeopardy purposes. *Id.* at 438–439. The Court later extended this reasoning in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 209–210 (1984), holding that a sentencing determination that “is final and binding” and that “resembles a trial” bars retrial when the evidence is insufficient.

The California sentencing scheme for prior conviction allegations under the Three Strikes law is indistinguishable from the capital proceedings in *Bullington* and *Rumsey*. California Penal Code § 1025 grants defendants a statutory right to a jury trial on prior conviction allegations. This Court has held that the prosecution must prove such allegations beyond a reasonable doubt. *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993). The rules of evidence apply, *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal.4th 217, 224 (1996), and the defendant has the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses, *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal.3d 857, 863 n.5 (1974). These procedural protections mirror those of a criminal trial. As the Court of Appeal correctly held, the trial court’s finding of insufficient evidence to prove that petitioner personally used a deadly weapon was an “acquittal” of the sentencing enhancement. *See Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 10–11 (1978) (insufficient evidence bars retrial). Because the enhancement determination was “the functional equivalent of an acquittal on the merits,” *United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117, 130 (1980), retrial is prohibited.

**II. The California Supreme Court Erred in Holding That *Bullington* and *Rumsey* Are Limited to Capital Cases.**

The California Supreme Court refused to apply *Bullington* and *Rumsey* because those cases involved capital sentencing. But the Supreme Court has never held that the rationale of those cases is confined to capital proceedings. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994), the Court noted that “both *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases,” but it expressly declined to decide whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing. *Id.* at 393. The Court’s observation that its reasoning was “based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding” does not establish a categorical rule. To the contrary, the *Caspari* Court left the question open, and the reasoning of *Bullington* and *Rumsey*—focusing on the functional equivalence of the proceeding to a trial—applies with equal force to California’s Three Strikes scheme.

The California Supreme Court also relied on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), which held that the Due Process Clause does not require formal trial procedures at sentencing. But *Williams* addressed due process, not double jeopardy. *See id.* at 245–246. *Williams* does not preclude double jeopardy protection when the State voluntarily provides a trial-like proceeding. *Bullington* itself recognized that “a sentencing proceeding that is ‘like a trial’ ” triggers double jeopardy protections even though the State is not constitutionally required to provide such a proceeding. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438–439.

**III. The Court of Appeal’s Reversal of the Enhancement Finding Constitutes an Acquittal That Bars Retrial.**

When a reviewing court finds insufficient evidence to support a sentencing enhancement, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes a second attempt to prove the allegation. *See Burks*, 437 U.S. at 10–11. The Court of Appeal held that the evidence was insufficient to prove that petitioner personally used a deadly weapon, a necessary element of the “serious felony” enhancement. That determination is an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes. *See Hudson v. Louisiana*, 450 U.S. 40, 43 (1981). The State had a full and fair opportunity to present its case. Permitting retrial would subject petitioner to “the embarrassment, expense and ordeal of a second trial,” *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187 (1957), and would violate the core protection against multiple prosecutions.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to bar retrial of the prior conviction allegation.
