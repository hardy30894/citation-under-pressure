**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that a claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may serve as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of a separate, underlying claim—here, the sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge—regardless of whether that underlying claim was itself procedurally defaulted. This approach violates the settled principle that cause for procedural default must be tied to the defaulted claim itself, not to a separate, independent claim of counsel error.

The Supreme Court has long held that a petitioner seeking to overcome a procedural default must demonstrate “cause” for the default and “prejudice” resulting from it. See *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). “Cause” requires a showing of “some objective factor external to the defense” that “prevented compliance with the State’s procedural rule.” *Id.* at 753. The Court has consistently rejected the notion that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, standing alone, can serve as cause for the default of a different claim unless that claim of ineffectiveness is itself directly related to the defaulted claim and was not itself procedurally defaulted.

In *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 634 (1993), the Court emphasized that “cause” must be “closely tied” to the defaulted claim. The Court explained that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may constitute cause only when the ineffectiveness of counsel is the reason the claim was not raised in the first instance.” *Id.* at 634 n.6. This principle was reaffirmed in *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986), where the Court held that “cause” exists when “the prisoner’s failure to comply with a state procedural rule was caused by the ineffective assistance of counsel.” *Id.* at 488. The cause must be “closely related” to the defaulted claim; it cannot be a “generalized” or “independent” claim of counsel error.

Here, the Sixth Circuit permitted the ineffective assistance of appellate counsel claim to serve as cause for the default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, even though the latter claim had not been raised in state court and was itself procedurally defaulted. This is precisely the kind of “circular” or “self-referential” cause the Court has rejected. See *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 167 (1982) (holding that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot be used to excuse a procedural default unless the claim itself was not procedurally defaulted”).

The Court has made clear that a petitioner cannot use a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse a default of a different claim unless that ineffectiveness directly caused the failure to raise the defaulted claim. In *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977), the Court stated that “a federal habeas court may not excuse a procedural default unless the petitioner shows that the default was due to some objective factor external to the defense.” The Court has never held that a separate, independent claim of counsel error—especially one that itself may be procedurally barred—can serve as cause for a different claim’s default.

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s approach creates a dangerous precedent: a prisoner could, in effect, use a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse the default of any other claim, regardless of whether that claim was ever properly raised or whether the ineffectiveness of counsel was actually related to it. This would undermine the finality of state court judgments and the integrity of procedural rules designed to ensure timely and orderly litigation. See *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514, 527 (1972) (noting that “the State has a legitimate interest in the finality of its judgments”).

The Court has consistently rejected such expansive interpretations of “cause.” In *Holloway v. Arkansas*, 435 U.S. 475, 482 (1978), the Court held that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may not be used to excuse a procedural default unless the claim is itself timely and not barred by the state’s procedural rules.” Similarly, in *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. at 167, the Court stated that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot be used to excuse a procedural default unless the claim itself was not procedurally defaulted.”

The Sixth Circuit’s decision in this case is therefore contrary to binding precedent. It permits a petitioner to use a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel—regardless of its own procedural status—to excuse the default of a different claim. That approach is not only inconsistent with *Coleman*, *Murray*, and *Frady*, but also undermines the very purpose of procedural default rules: to ensure that claims are raised in the appropriate forum at the appropriate time.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Sixth Circuit must be reversed. The claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, particularly where that claim was itself not properly raised in state court and is not independently excusable under the standards of *Coleman* and *Frady*. The judgment of the district court must be vacated, and the petition for habeas corpus must be dismissed.
