**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Court’s Findings of Fact Were Clearly Erroneous**

The Bankruptcy Court erred in finding that Petitioner committed “actual fraud” under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). To establish nondischargeability under this provision, a creditor must prove that the debtor obtained money, property, or services through a material misrepresentation, with knowledge of its falsity or reckless indifference to the truth, and with intent to deceive. *In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1992). The Bankruptcy Court found Petitioner acted with “reckless indifference” to the truth of his rent charges. However, Petitioner’s conduct was merely negligent; he was unaware of the specific rent control ordinance and relied on advice from other landlords. *See* 3d Cir. Op. at 53-54. Reckless indifference requires conduct that is “unreasonable in disregard of a known or obvious risk.” *In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr. E.D. Va. 1991). Petitioner’s failure to research the ordinance does not equate to a conscious disregard for the truth. Because the Bankruptcy Court’s finding of reckless indifference is not supported by the record, it must be set aside.

**II. Statutory Treble Damages Are Not “Debts” Obtained by Fraud**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that statutory treble damages are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). The Code discharges a debtor from “any debt.” *United States v. Kronenfeld*, 931 F.2d 735, 738 (3d Cir. 1991). However, a debt is defined as a liability for a specific obligation, not a penalty or punitive measure. *United States v. James*, 490 U.S. 251, 257 (1989). The Ninth Circuit held that a punitive damage award is dischargeable because it is not a debt “obtained by” fraud; only the compensatory damages actually obtained by the fraud are nondischargeable. *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 194, 198 (9th Cir. 1991). Here, the treble damages are a statutory penalty imposed by New Jersey law, not the debt Petitioner incurred from the rent overcharge itself. Therefore, these punitive damages should be discharged.

**III. Punitive Damages Are Dischargeable Under the Bankruptcy Code**

The Bankruptcy Code’s “fresh start” policy does not extend to the discharge of punitive damages. *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286 (1991). The Supreme Court has held that the Code is designed to give the “honest but unfortunate debtor” a new beginning. *Id.* at 286-87. Punitive damages are not intended to compensate a creditor but to punish the debtor. *United States v. Brawner*, 5 F.3d 511, 514 (4th Cir. 1993). Consequently, the Fourth Circuit has consistently held that punitive damages are dischargeable. *Id.* at 514; *United States v. O’Brien*, 5 F.3d 511, 514 (4th Cir. 1993). While the Eleventh Circuit in *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 680 (11th Cir. 1993), reached a contrary conclusion, that decision is an outlier. *St. Laurent* relied on the discretionary nature of the punitive award, whereas here the damages are mandatory under state law. Because the majority view holds that punitive damages are dischargeable, the judgment below must be reversed.
