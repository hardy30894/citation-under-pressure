**Argument**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly refused to confine plain-error review to the trial record alone. Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), an appellate court may correct an unpreserved error only if the defendant shows (1) error, (2) that is plain, (3) affecting substantial rights, and then demonstrates that the court should exercise its discretion because the error “seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732-36 (1993). In applying those latter two prongs, courts of appeals are not required to blind themselves to information outside the trial transcript where that information is necessary to determine whether the defendant actually suffered prejudice or whether leaving the error uncorrected would undermine the administration of justice. See *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 466-70 (1997); *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 631-34 (2002).

This Court’s plain-error precedents show that the inquiry is not a rigid evidentiary exercise limited to what the jury heard. In *Johnson*, the Court held that an omitted element did not warrant reversal where the evidence on that element was “overwhelming” and “essentially uncontroverted.” 520 U.S. at 470. The Court reached that conclusion by looking to the record as a whole, not merely the instructions given to the jury. Id. Likewise, in *Cotton*, the Court evaluated whether an indictment defect affected substantial rights by examining whether the evidence of the omitted fact was overwhelming and uncontroverted. 535 U.S. at 632-33. Those decisions would make little sense if appellate courts were forbidden to consider the full record when deciding whether a plain error actually prejudiced the defendant.

The same is true here. Greer argues that because the jury was never asked to find that he knew he was a felon, the Eleventh Circuit had to ignore the rest of the record. But plain-error review has never been so artificial. To determine whether an unpreserved Rehaif error affected substantial rights, the court properly may consider whether the defendant had prior felony convictions and whether the surrounding record makes it implausible that he lacked knowledge of his status. See *Rehaif v. United States*, 588 U.S. ___, 139 S. Ct. 2191, 2194, 2200 (2019) (holding that the Government must prove knowledge of status, but not altering Rule 52(b)’s framework). A defendant who has multiple prior felony convictions faces an obvious hurdle in showing a reasonable probability of a different outcome. See *Cotton*, 535 U.S. at 633; *Johnson*, 520 U.S. at 470.

Nor does Greer’s theory fit the fourth prong. Whether an error seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings necessarily turns on the real-world likelihood that the defendant was convicted without proof of an essential fact. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736; *Cotton*, 535 U.S. at 632-34. If the appellate court could not consider uncontested record facts showing the defendant’s felony history, then it would be forced to grant relief even where the Rehaif error was plainly harmless in any practical sense. That is not plain-error review; it is automatic reversal. This Court has rejected that approach. *Cotton*, 535 U.S. at 631-34; *Johnson*, 520 U.S. at 466-70.

Greer’s contrary position also conflicts with the settled principle that on review for plain error the defendant bears the burden of persuasion on prejudice. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734-35. A defendant cannot meet that burden by insisting that the appellate court ignore dispositive information in the same case. The Court has repeatedly looked beyond the precise materials presented to the jury when necessary to assess whether an unpreserved error mattered. See *Johnson*, 520 U.S. at 470; *Cotton*, 535 U.S. at 633. Nothing in *Rehaif* abrogated that doctrine. See *Henderson v. United States*, 568 U.S. 266, 273-74 (2013).

Finally, the rule Greer proposes would place appellate courts in the position of knowingly reversing convictions where the defendant almost certainly knew he was a felon and where the jury’s omission of that uncontested point did not affect the verdict in any meaningful way. That result would undermine, not protect, the fairness, integrity, and public reputation of judicial proceedings. *Cotton*, 535 U.S. at 634. The Eleventh Circuit properly avoided that outcome by examining the full record and Greer’s prior convictions in deciding that he had not carried his burden under Rule 52(b). Its judgment should be affirmed.
