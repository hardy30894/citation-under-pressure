**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO INVOKE THE TENTH AMENDMENT.**

This Court has squarely held that a private party may not challenge a federal statute as violating the Tenth Amendment. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. TVA*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939). There, the Court explained that private utility companies “absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Id.* That holding controls here. Bond is a private individual, not a State or state officer. Her Tenth Amendment claim therefore fails at the threshold.

**II. THE THIRD CIRCUIT CORRECTLY HELD THAT PRIVATE PARTIES CANNOT RAISE TENTH AMENDMENT CLAIMS.**

A majority of circuits agrees that private parties lack Tenth Amendment standing. *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525-527 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005); *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004). These courts correctly recognize that the Tenth Amendment “is a truism that … states nothing that is not already assumed by the structure of the Constitution.” *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 34 (citation omitted). It “concerns the relationship between the federal government and the States, not the relationship between the federal government and individuals.” *Parker*, 362 F.3d at 1284. Accordingly, “[i]t is axiomatic that a private party … does not have standing to assert a Tenth Amendment claim.” *Brooklyn Legal Servs.*, 462 F.3d at 234.

Bond cannot salvage standing by arguing that *Tennessee Electric* is mere dicta. As the First Circuit observed, the Supreme Court “dismissed the Tenth Amendment claim after analyzing both the standing issue and the merits, and hence, the former holding is an alternative ground, rather than obiter dictum.” *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 34 (citing *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978)). Moreover, because standing implicates “the constitutional jurisdiction of a federal court, its resolution predominates any discussion of the merits.” *Id.* at 35.

**III. THE TENTH AMENDMENT PROTECTS STATE SOVEREIGNTY, NOT INDIVIDUAL RIGHTS.**

The Tenth Amendment reserves powers “to the States respectively, or to the people.” U.S. Const. amend. X. Its text thus identifies States and the people collectively as beneficiaries, not individual defendants. Consistent with this text, this Court has held that the Amendment “states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered.” *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). It “is not a font of individual rights,” *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 33, but instead “is designed to protect the States from federal overreach,” *id.* at 34.

Because the Tenth Amendment shields only state sovereign interests, only a State—or a state officer acting on the State’s behalf—may invoke it. *See Alden v. Maine*, 527 U.S. 706, 713 (1999) (recognizing that “the States have retained their sovereign immunity” from certain federal suits). A private criminal defendant like Bond, whose conviction the State of Pennsylvania has not challenged, cannot assert that the federal prosecution infringes Pennsylvania’s prerogatives. *See Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35 (“[T]he State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes.”).

**IV. BOND’S RELIANCE ON MISSOURI v. HOLLAND IS MISPLACED.**

Bond contends that *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), authorizes her Tenth Amendment challenge because it recognized limits on the treaty power. But *Holland* involved a suit by the *State of Missouri*—a sovereign—to enjoin enforcement of a federal statute implementing a migratory bird treaty. *Id.* at 430-31. Missouri, not a private citizen, invoked the Tenth Amendment. *Id.* at 431-32. *Holland* thus confirms that standing lies only for States, not individuals. Bond has no more standing to police federalism boundaries than “a bystander … could claim standing to enforce the separation of powers between Congress and the President.” *Parker*, 362 F.3d at 1284.

**V. THE STATUTE 18 U.S.C. § 229 IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S POWER.**

Even if Bond had standing, Section 229 is constitutional. The Necessary and Proper Clause authorizes Congress to enact laws “necessary and proper for carrying into Execution” the Treaty Power. U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18; *see Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432 (holding that a statute implementing a valid treaty “is a law of the United States proper to be passed” under the Necessary and Proper Clause). The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and Section 829 faithfully implements its obligation to “prohibit natural and legal persons … from undertaking any activity prohibited … under this Convention.” 32 I.L.M. 800, 810 (1993). Therefore, Section 229 is “necessary and proper to carry out the objectives of the treaty.” *United States v. Lue*, 134 F.3d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1998).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
