**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service (“NPS”) lawfully prohibited John Sturgeon from operating a hovercraft on the Nation River within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. Section 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (“ANILCA”) does not strip NPS of regulatory authority over non-federally owned lands and waters within conservation system units.

**I. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) preserves NPS authority over all lands within park boundaries.**

Section 103(c) provides that only “regulations … applicable solely to public lands” are limited. “Public lands” are defined in ANILCA § 102(3) as lands owned by the United States. The hovercraft ban at 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e) applies to all lands and waters within the preserve, not “solely” to federal land. Because the regulation does not discriminate between federal and non-federal ownership, ANILCA’s limitation is inapplicable.

This Court has long held that statutes limiting federal authority over non-federal lands must be strictly construed. *Cf. United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 28–29 (1935) (federal ownership of land carries paramount regulatory power); *Hunt v. United States*, 278 U.S. 96, 100 (1928) (federal government may protect federal lands even if incidental effects on state lands). The Ninth Circuit correctly found the statute unambiguous.

**II. The Property Clause and the NPS Organic Act authorize regulation within park boundaries regardless of land ownership.**

The Property Clause, U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2, grants Congress “power to dispose of and make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States.” This power is “without limitations.” *United States v. Wittek*, 337 U.S. 346, 353 (1948). When Congress creates a national park, it may regulate activities that threaten the park’s purposes, even on non-federal inholdings. *Camfield v. United States*, 167 U.S. 518, 528 (1897) (federal government may protect its lands from injury by enforcing rules on adjacent private lands).

The NPS Organic Act, 16 U.S.C. § 1, directs the Secretary “to regulate the use of the national parks … by such means and measures as conform to the fundamental purpose of said parks … to conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life therein.” NPS has consistently interpreted this authority to extend to all lands within park boundaries. This Court has deferred to such agency interpretations. *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 518 (1911) (Secretary may issue regulations protecting forest reserves). Sturgeon’s hovercraft disrupts wildlife and the natural quiet—precisely the sort of injury the Organic Act was designed to prevent.

**III. State ownership of submerged lands does not displace federal regulatory authority.**

Alaska owns the submerged lands beneath the Nation River under the Submerged Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. §§ 1301 *et seq.*, but that grant does not eliminate federal authority to regulate navigable waters for conservation purposes. The Submerged Lands Act “conveyed to the States title to the lands beneath navigable waters within their boundaries, but … did not relinquish federal regulatory authority over such waters.” *Hynes v. Grimes Packing Co.*, 337 U.S. 86, 101 (1949). Even where a State holds title, the federal government retains its Commerce Clause and Property Clause powers. *International Paper Co. v. United States*, 282 U.S. 399, 407 (1930) (federal government may regulate navigable waters to protect federal interests). 

Moreover, the Property Clause alone supports NPS’s regulation. In *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, decided after 1970, the Court reaffirmed that federal power over public lands extends to protecting those lands from adverse impacts originating on non-federal lands. The pre-1970 precedents already compel that conclusion. *See United States v. Powers*, 307 U.S. 214, 218 (1939) (federal government may control use of private land within a federal project to protect federal interests).

**IV. The hovercraft regulation is a legitimate exercise of NPS’s authority.**

Even if § 103(c) were ambiguous (it is not), the NPS’s interpretation is reasonable and entitled to deference. *United States v. Mersky*, 361 U.S. 431, 440 (1960) (agency interpretation of its own statute is given weight). The regulation serves the Park Service’s mission to preserve natural resources and visitor safety. NPS has applied the same rule across the National Park System since 1978; Alaska’s unique status under ANILCA does not exempt it.

**V. Sturgeon’s constitutional arguments are meritless.**

Sturgeon invokes a purported right to navigate state waters under the Privileges or Immunities Clause. But *The Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. 36, 79 (1872), recognized only a limited right of navigation, not a right to operate hovercraft without federal permit. The federal government’s navigational servitude under the Commerce Clause allows it to regulate any vessel, including hovercraft, on navigable waters. *United States v. Twin City Power Co.*, 350 U.S. 222, 226 (1956) (federal power over navigable waters is “as broad as the needs of commerce”).

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. Section 103(c) does not bar NPS from regulating state-owned lands within national preserves. The hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of federal authority under the Property Clause and the NPS Organic Act.
