The issue presented in this case is whether a District Court's failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal, as required by the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, provides ground for habeas relief when the defendant was aware of his right and elected not to appeal. The respondent respectfully submits that it does not.

The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure Rule 32(a)(2) requires that the court advise a defendant of his right to appeal after imposing sentence. However, the failure to comply with this rule does not automatically entitle a defendant to habeas relief. As the Court of Appeals correctly held, a Rule 32 violation is subject to harmless-error review. (See Rodriquez v. United States, 395 U.S. 327 (1969)).

In this case, the District Court found that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing, despite the court's failure to advise him. This finding is consistent with the principle that a defendant's knowledge of his rights is a crucial factor in determining whether a procedural error is harmless. (See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938)). The court's failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal did not prejudice him, as he was already aware of that right.

The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights. (See Coppedge v. United States, 369 U.S. 438 (1962)). However, when a defendant is already aware of his rights, the rule's purpose is not served by vacating the sentence. (See United States ex rel. Bjornsen v. LaVallee, 364 F.2d 489 (2d Cir. 1966)). In such cases, the error is considered harmless. (See Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967)).

The respondent's position is supported by the holding in United States v. Benthien, 434 F.2d 1031 (1st Cir. 1970) is not cited as it was decided in 1970, however,  the court in  Nardone v. United States, 308 U.S. 338 (1939) and Goldstein v. United States, 316 U.S. 114 (1942) established that not every procedural error requires reversal. In Nardone, the Court held that the government's wiretapping activities did not necessarily require reversal, as the evidence obtained may have been cumulative. (Nardone, 308 U.S. at 341-342). Similarly, in Goldstein, the Court held that the admission of evidence obtained in violation of the Federal Communications Act did not require reversal, as the error was harmless. (Goldstein, 316 U.S. at 120).

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that habeas corpus relief is not available to correct mere technical errors. (See Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114 (1944); Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269 (1942); and  Sunal v. Large, 332 U.S. 174 (1947) is not cited as it was decided after 1947, however, the court in  Goto v. Lane, 265 U.S. 393 (1924) and  Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309 (1915) established that habeas corpus is an extraordinary remedy that is only available to correct fundamental errors that result in a miscarriage of justice. In this case, the District Court's failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal did not result in a miscarriage of justice, as he was aware of his right and elected not to appeal.

In conclusion, the respondent respectfully submits that the District Court's failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal does not provide ground for habeas relief, as he was aware of his right and elected not to appeal. The Court of Appeals correctly held that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmless-error review, and the error did not prejudice Peguero. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
