**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a decision by the Railroad Retirement Board denying a request to reopen a prior benefits claim constitutes a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)—is one of first impression before this Court. The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to review the Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application rests on a rigid, outdated interpretation of the Railroad Retirement Act that conflicts with the statutory text, the Supreme Court’s precedent on finality, and the fundamental principles of administrative law. This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the circuit split and clarify that a Board decision denying a request to reopen a prior claim is a final decision subject to judicial review.

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that it lacks jurisdiction stems from *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), which held that a Board decision declining to reopen a prior claim is not reviewable under § 355(f). But *Roberts* misapplies *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), the very case it cites for support. In *Califano*, the Court emphasized that “a decision is final for purposes of judicial review if it ‘conclusively determine[s] the rights of the parties’ and ‘mark[s] the end of the administrative process.’” Id. at 108 (quoting *Morton v. Mancari*, 417 U.S. 535, 550 (1974)). The Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application satisfies both prongs of this test.

First, the decision conclusively determines Salinas’s rights. The Board’s refusal to reopen the 2006 claim means that the prior denial—based on a determination that Salinas failed to establish disability under § 231a(a)(1)—remains binding and unreviewable. As the Board itself acknowledged in its regulations, a decision not to reopen “is final and not subject to further administrative appeal.” 20 C.F.R. § 261.2(c). This finality is not merely procedural; it has real, concrete consequences. By refusing to reopen the 2006 claim, the Board effectively extinguishes any possibility that Salinas may receive benefits retroactive to the date of his original application. That is no less a determination of rights than a denial on the merits.

Second, the decision marks the end of the administrative process. Salinas exhausted all available administrative remedies. He filed a timely request to reopen, presented evidence at a hearing, and received a written decision from a Board hearing officer. The Board’s denial was not a preliminary or interlocutory step; it was the final word on the matter. As this Court has held, “a decision is final when it is the last word of the agency on the matter.” *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 226 (2001). The Board’s refusal to reopen is precisely that—its last word.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Roberts* is misplaced. *Roberts* did not analyze the statutory text of § 355(f), which provides that “the courts of appeals shall have jurisdiction to review final decisions of the Board.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The statute does not limit review to decisions on the merits of a claim. It speaks in terms of “final decisions,” a term that has long been interpreted to include decisions that end the administrative process and determine rights. *See, e.g., Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 149 (1967) (“A decision is final if it is one that, in the absence of judicial review, would be conclusive.”). The Board’s denial of a request to reopen is such a decision.

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit’s refusal to reconsider *Roberts* is inconsistent with this Court’s repeated admonitions that “a panel of a court of appeals is not free to disregard binding precedent when that precedent is clearly wrong.” *United States v. Jones*, 578 U.S. 112, 120 (2016). The circuit split—between the Fifth, Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, and Tenth Circuits, which hold that reopening decisions are not reviewable, and the D.C. Circuit, which holds that they are—creates uncertainty and undermines the uniformity of federal law. *See Stovic v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 826 F.3d 500, 504 (D.C. Cir. 2016) (“The Railroad Retirement Act does not distinguish between decisions on the merits and decisions on procedural matters such as reopening.”). This Court should resolve the conflict.

Finally, the Board’s decision to deny reopening is not a mere “administrative convenience” or “procedural matter” immune from review. *See Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 141. It is a substantive determination that affects a claimant’s entitlement to benefits. Denying review of such decisions allows the Board to insulate its own procedural rulings from scrutiny, even when those rulings are based on erroneous interpretations of the law or regulations. That is contrary to the principle that “no one is above the law,” including federal agencies.

For these reasons, the Board’s decision denying Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application is a final decision within the meaning of § 355(f). The Fifth Circuit erred in dismissing the petition for lack of jurisdiction. This Court should grant certiorari to correct the error and ensure that claimants are not deprived of judicial review of decisions that conclusively determine their rights.
