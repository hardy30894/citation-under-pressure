# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board's Application of an Identical "Reasonable Doubt" Standard to Polling, Withdrawal, and Elections Is Arbitrary and Cannot Stand.

The National Labor Relations Act does not mention employer polling; the practice, and any standard governing it, is a matter of Board policy. This Court has recognized that an employer confronted with evidence that an incumbent union may have lost majority support faces a genuine dilemma, caught between the risk of unlawfully refusing to bargain with a union that retains majority support and the risk of unlawfully continuing to bargain with one that does not. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 594–600 (1969). Polling exists precisely to allow employers to resolve that uncertainty without resort to the more drastic remedy of outright withdrawal.

An administrative rule, however, is entitled to deference only where "there must be a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made." *Burlington Truck Lines v. United States*, 371 U.S. 156, 168 (1962). No such rational connection supports the Board's decision to require the identical showing—objective evidence sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about majority status—for three categorically different employer actions: outright withdrawal of recognition, a Board-supervised RM election, and an internal poll. The Board itself has acknowledged that RM elections are the preferred vehicle for testing employee sentiment precisely because they carry procedural safeguards that polling lacks. That premise leads logically to a *lower* threshold for polling, not an identical one. An agency may not adopt a rule whose stated justification points in one direction while its operative effect points in another; the "grounds upon which the administrative agency acted" must be "clearly disclosed and adequately sustained." *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 318 U.S. 80, 94 (1943).

Nor can the Board defend its equivalence of standards as a permissible exercise of remedial discretion. The Board's discretion to fashion policy under the Act must be exercised in a manner consistent with the Act's purposes, not administrative convenience. *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. NLRB*, 313 U.S. 177, 194 (1941). And this Court has repeatedly required the Board to accommodate the legitimate, competing interests of employers rather than mechanically apply a single undifferentiated rule to conduct of markedly different character and consequence. *Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793, 797–98 (1945). A poll conducted with full procedural safeguards—secret ballot, neutral supervision, no threat of reprisal—poses none of the risks to employee free choice that attend an employer's unilateral withdrawal of recognition. Collapsing the two into a single evidentiary threshold, without any reasoned account of why the reduced risk of a properly conducted poll warrants no reduction in the evidentiary burden, is arbitrary agency action of the kind this Court has consistently refused to sanction.

## II. The Board's Finding That Allentown Lacked a Good-Faith Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence on the Record as a Whole.

Even accepting the Board's standard, its finding that Allentown lacked an objective, good-faith doubt cannot stand on this record. Judicial review of Board factfinding is not a rubber stamp. Congress intended that reviewing courts assume genuine responsibility for the reasonableness of Board decisions, and that "the substantiality of evidence must take into account whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The Board may not treat its chosen inference as conclusive merely because it selected among competing possibilities; the inference itself must be reasonable in light of the record as a whole. *Id.* at 490.

The Board's treatment of the evidence here reflects the same one-sided factfinding this Court condemned in *NLRB v. Brown*, 380 U.S. 278, 291–92 (1965), where an inference of unlawful motive rested not on a fair reading of the record but on a predetermined conclusion imposed upon ambiguous facts. The Board and the ALJ discounted every piece of evidence favorable to Allentown in succession: Ridgick's and Marsh's statements were dismissed as equivocal; Bloch's and Mohr's reports of coworkers' sentiments were excluded wholesale because the coworkers themselves did not testify; and employees no longer on the payroll were removed from the calculus entirely. Nothing favorable to the Union's position received comparable scrutiny. Board findings must rest on the actual dynamics of the record rather than on categorical rules that foreclose consideration of entire classes of evidence. *NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963).

The cumulative effect of the Board's approach is to impose an evidentiary threshold that no employer could realistically satisfy without conducting the very poll the Board would forbid it from taking. That circularity is not the "substantial evidence" this Court's precedents require; it is instead the sort of unreasoned, outcome-driven factfinding *Universal Camera* was designed to prevent. See also *Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944) (Board remedies must reflect the actual wishes of employees, not administrative presumption). The uncontradicted testimony here—far exceeding the seven statements credited by the Board—demonstrates that Allentown's doubt was genuine, objective, and reasonable.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
