**ARGUMENT**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (“FDCPA”) regulates “debt collectors,” not creditors collecting their own debts. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander Consumer USA, Inc. was not a “debt collector” when it sought to collect on debts it had purchased for its own account. The default status of a debt at the time of purchase is irrelevant to that determination.

**I. The Plain Text of the FDCPA Defines “Debt Collector” Based on Whether the Person Collects Debts Owed to Another**

The FDCPA defines “debt collector” as any person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due *another*.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). This language unambiguously focuses on the relationship between the collector and the debt: a debt collector collects debts for someone else. By contrast, a “creditor” is “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). When a person purchases a debt and then collects it for its own account, it is the creditor to whom the debt is now owed, not a debt collector. *See Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (defining debt collector as one who collects debts “owed to *another*”) (emphasis added); *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1315–16 (11th Cir. 2015) (“[A] person must regularly collect or attempt to collect debts for others in order to qualify as a ‘debt collector’ under the second definition of the term.”).

The statute’s structure confirms this reading. The definition of “debt collector” includes a threshold requirement: the person must be collecting debts owed to another. Only after that threshold is met do the exclusions in § 1692a(6)(A)–(F) come into play. *See id.* at 1314. One exclusion, § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), provides that a person collecting debts for another is not a debt collector if the debt was not in default when obtained. But that exclusion applies only to persons who *already* satisfy the threshold definition—i.e., those collecting debts for others. It does not transform a person collecting its own debts into a debt collector merely because the debt was in default when acquired. *See Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (“The statute is not susceptible to the interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another.’”).

Petitioners’ argument—that the default status of a debt determines whether a purchaser is a debt collector—reads the phrase “owed or due another” out of the statute. If Congress had intended default status to be dispositive, it would have said so directly. Instead, it expressly conditioned debt collector status on collecting debts for another, not on the timing of default. *See Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1318 (“[O]ur inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether [the defendant] regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to [it]; our inquiry is whether [it] regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection.”).

**II. The Fourth Circuit’s Interpretation Is Consistent with the FDCPA’s Purpose and Structure**

The FDCPA’s purpose is “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). Congress targeted abusive conduct by *third-party* collectors, not by creditors collecting their own debts. *See S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977)* (explaining that the definition “cover[s] all third persons who regularly collect debts for others”). When a company like Santander purchases a debt and collects it for itself, it stands in the shoes of the original creditor. Regulating such a creditor as a debt collector would conflate two distinct roles and subject ordinary creditors to liability for conduct that Congress did not intend to reach.

Petitioners’ interpretation would create a loophole of its own: any creditor that purchases defaulted debt—even a bank or finance company whose primary business is lending—would suddenly become a debt collector subject to the FDCPA, regardless of whether it collects debts for others. That would sweep in entities that Congress never intended to regulate as debt collectors. *See Fourth Circuit opinion at 14–15* (noting that such a reading would make every creditor that collects on its own loans and also collects for others “subject to the FDCPA . . . when it collects for itself”).

**III. Petitioners’ Contrary Interpretation Leads to Absurd Results**

Petitioners argue that because Santander purchased their debts after default, it must be a debt collector. But under that logic, any entity that purchases a defaulted loan—including a hedge fund, a bank, or even a consumer finance company like Santander—would be subject to the FDCPA’s strictures even if it never collects debts for others. That result is irreconcilable with the statute’s text, which defines debt collectors based on their *relationship* to the debt (whether they collect for another), not on the debt’s *condition* (whether it is in default). *See Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1318.

Petitioners also urge that because Santander serviced the loans before purchasing them, it remained a debt collector afterward. But the statute evaluates conduct at the time of collection. When Santander collected its own debts after purchasing them, it was acting as a creditor, not a debt collector. The Fourth Circuit correctly rejected this temporal bootstrapping argument. *See Fourth Circuit opinion at 16–17* (“[A] valid claim under the FDCPA inherently requires the coming together of all the statutory elements at the time of . . . the prohibited conduct.”).

**CONCLUSION**

The Fourth Circuit’s judgment should be affirmed.
