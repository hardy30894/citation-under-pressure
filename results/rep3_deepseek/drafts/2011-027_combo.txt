**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the Established Constitutional Test for Navigability for Title Purposes.**

The question of whether a riverbed passed to a State upon admission to the Union under the equal-footing doctrine turns on whether the river was navigable in fact at the time of statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). The test is whether the river, in its natural and ordinary condition, was used or was susceptible of being used as a highway for commerce, over which trade and travel were or could be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water. *The Daniel Ball*, 10 Wall. 557, 563 (1871). The Montana Supreme Court applied precisely this test, examining historical evidence of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers as they existed in 1889, the year Montana achieved statehood. The court did not err by focusing on the time of statehood; indeed, it was constitutionally required to do so. *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 591 (1922) (navigability for title purposes is determined as of the date of admission to the Union).

**II. The Trial Court Properly Considered Historical Evidence to Determine Navigability at Statehood.**

PPL’s contention that the trial court improperly relied on hearsay and unauthenticated historical documents is without merit. Courts have long recognized that navigability determinations must be made based on historical evidence, including maps, surveys, and contemporaneous accounts of river use. *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926) (holding that navigability is a question of fact to be determined by evidence of the river’s condition at the time of statehood). The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the historical documents submitted by the State—including newspapers, government reports, and other public records—were admissible under well-established hearsay exceptions for ancient documents and public records. *See* *United States v. Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co.*, 174 U.S. 690, 698 (1899) (relying on historical accounts to determine navigability). The court’s evidentiary rulings were consistent with federal practice. *See* *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 404 (1940) (navigability may be proved by evidence of prior use or by present susceptibility to use).

**III. The Equal-Footing Doctrine Vests Title to Navigable Riverbeds in the State.**

Upon admission to the Union, Montana acquired title to the beds of all rivers that were navigable at the time of statehood. *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 3 How. 212, 230 (1845). This principle is fundamental to the equal-footing doctrine, which ensures that new States enter the Union with the same sovereign rights as the original thirteen. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894). The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State’s title to the riverbeds at issue vested in 1889, and that PPL’s use of those riverbeds without compensation violated the State’s proprietary rights. *See* *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (State holds title to navigable riverbeds in trust for the public).

**IV. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt State Compensation Claims.**

PPL’s argument that the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempts Montana’s Hydroelectric Resources Act (HRA) is foreclosed by *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Cooperative v. Federal Power Commission*, 328 U.S. 152, 167-68 (1946), which recognized a “dual system” of control under the FPA. The FPA expressly preserves state law regarding property rights, including the right to compensation for the use of state-owned riverbeds. *Id.* at 175-76. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the HRA is not preempted, as it operates within the sphere of state property law that Congress intended to protect. *See* *Federal Power Commission v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239, 249-51 (1954) (FPA does not authorize uncompensated use of state property). The FPA’s requirement that licensees obtain state consent for use of riverbeds confirms that state compensation claims are not preempted. *See* *United States v. Central Stockholders’ Corp. of Vallejo*, 52 F.2d 322, 331-32 (9th Cir. 1931).

**V. The Federal Navigational Servitude Does Not Bar State Compensation.**

The federal navigational servitude is a power of Congress to regulate commerce, not a grant of authority to private parties to use state property without compensation. *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pacific R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596-97 (1941). The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the servitude does not preempt state law claims for compensation, as the servitude applies only to the federal government’s exercise of its commerce power, not to private licensees. *See* *United States v. Twin City Power Co.*, 350 U.S. 222, 224 (1956) (servitude does not extinguish state ownership of riverbeds). PPL’s reliance on the servitude to avoid paying compensation is misplaced.

**VI. The State’s Claims Are Not Barred by Laches, Estoppel, or the Statute of Limitations.**

PPL’s affirmative defenses of laches, equitable estoppel, and statute of limitations fail because the State, as sovereign, is not subject to such defenses when acting to protect public trust lands. *See* *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 40 (1947) (sovereign not bound by laches). Moreover, the State’s claim for compensation did not accrue until PPL’s use of the riverbeds became known to the State, and the State acted promptly upon discovering its rights. *See* *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) (State’s title to riverbeds is not lost by non-use). The Montana Supreme Court correctly rejected these defenses.

**VII. The Damages Award Was Proper.**

The trial court’s award of $40,956,180 in back lease payments was based on the fair market value of PPL’s use of state-owned riverbeds from 2000 through 2007. The Montana Supreme Court affirmed this award, finding it supported by substantial evidence. *See* *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 123 (1967) (compensation for use of state property is measured by fair market value). PPL’s challenge to the amount of damages is without merit, as the trial court properly applied the HRA’s statutory framework.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
