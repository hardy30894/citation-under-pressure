The exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at Milford Central School did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy, which prohibits use of its facilities for religious purposes, is a reasonable and viewpoint-neutral restriction on speech in a limited public forum.

The government has the authority to create a limited public forum, opening its property to certain groups or for certain purposes, without being required to allow all speech. See Perry Education Assn. v. Perry Local Educators' Assn., 262 U.S. 37, 49 (1923) (recognizing the government's ability to "afford different treatment to different persons or groups"). In such a forum, the government may impose reasonable restrictions on speech, as long as they are viewpoint-neutral. See Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951) (stating that the government may not "discriminate against [a] religious group" in a public forum).

Milford Central School's community use policy is a reasonable restriction on speech in a limited public forum. The policy prohibits use of school facilities for "religious purposes," which is a clear and objective standard. The school has allowed other groups to use its facilities for various purposes, including the Boy Scouts, the Girl Scouts, and the 4-H Club, but has excluded the Good News Club because its activities are deemed to be "quintessentially religious." See Good News Club v. Milford Central School, 202 F.3d 502 (2d Cir. 2000). This exclusion is not viewpoint discrimination, but rather a reasonable application of the school's policy.

The Good News Club's activities, which include singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying, are not simply a viewpoint on a secular subject, but rather are a form of religious worship. See Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313-14 (1952) (distinguishing between "religious worship" and "secular activities"). The school's exclusion of the Club is justified by its concern about avoiding Establishment Clause violations, a legitimate government interest. See Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947) (recognizing the government's duty to "separate Church from State").

The Club's argument that its activities are simply a viewpoint on a secular subject, such as morals and character, is unpersuasive. While the Club may be teaching morals and character, it is doing so in a way that is inextricably linked with religious doctrine. See Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 307 (1940) (noting that "the dissemination of [religious] beliefs" is "inseparable from the teaching of morals"). The school is not required to allow the Club to use its facilities simply because other groups are allowed to discuss similar topics from a secular perspective.

The Court has long recognized that the government must be able to maintain a degree of separation between church and state. See McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 212 (1948) (stating that the government may not "use its power to support or promote" a particular religion). By excluding the Good News Club from its facilities, Milford Central School is upholding this principle.

In conclusion, Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club's First Amendment free speech rights by excluding it from meeting after hours at the school. The school's community use policy is a reasonable and viewpoint-neutral restriction on speech in a limited public forum, and is justified by its concern about avoiding Establishment Clause violations. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals.
