# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text and Structure of the Civil Service Reform Act Channel Mixed-Case Review to District Court Regardless of the Ground for the Board's Disposition.

The Civil Service Reform Act defines a "mixed case" by what the employee brought to the Board, not by what the Board ultimately decided. A case is mixed if the employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the [Board]" and "alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination." 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). This Court confirmed in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), that Congress meant this definition to control the choice of forum in every mixed case, explaining that "[n]o one here contests that Kloeckner brought a mixed case—that she was affected by an action (i.e., removal) appealable to the MSPB and that she alleged discrimination." *Id.* at 604. Nothing in that definition asks whether the Board's eventual dismissal rested on timeliness, on the merits, or on jurisdiction. The inquiry is backward-looking to the nature of the claim presented, not forward-looking to the label the Board happens to attach to its disposition.

This textual approach follows directly from *Chandler v. Roudebush*, 425 U.S. 840 (1976), which established that federal employees alleging discrimination are entitled to the same de novo judicial remedy available to private-sector employees under Title VII. That guarantee cannot depend on the fortuity of whether the Board characterizes its dismissal as jurisdictional rather than procedural or merits-based. As this Court has recognized, the CSRA constitutes "an integrated scheme of administrative and judicial review" designed to channel federal employment disputes through a coherent process. *Elgin v. Dep't of Treasury*, 567 U.S. 1, 11 (2012); see also *United States v. Fausto*, 484 U.S. 439, 445 (1988) (the CSRA reflects "an elaborate remedial system" that Congress crafted comprehensively). A rule that varies the forum based on the reason for dismissal would fracture that integrated scheme precisely where uniformity is most needed—at the threshold question of where an employee may even be heard.

## II. *Kloeckner*'s Reasoning Eviscerates *Powell* and Compels the Same Result for Jurisdictional Dismissals.

The D.C. Circuit below acknowledged that *Kloeckner* addressed only procedural dismissals, but the reasoning of that decision sweeps far more broadly than its facts. This Court in *Kloeckner* granted certiorari to "resolve a Circuit split on whether an employee seeking judicial review should proceed in the Federal Circuit or in a district court when the MSPB has dismissed her mixed case on procedural grounds," 133 S. Ct. at 603, and among the decisions it cited on the district-court side of that split were *Harms v. IRS*, 321 F.3d 1001 (10th Cir. 2003), and *Downey v. Runyon*, 160 F.3d 139 (2d Cir. 1998)—both of which had drawn the very procedural/jurisdictional line that Petitioner now challenges. *Kloeckner* rejected that line. Its holding rests on the statutory text's focus on what the employee alleged, not on any distinction between reasons a Board might invoke to avoid reaching a discrimination claim. There is no principled basis to preserve *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), when the very premise underlying it—that mixed-case review should track whichever tribunal is best suited to police the Board's own jurisdictional boundaries—was the same premise this Court rejected for procedural dismissals.

The Federal Circuit's post-*Kloeckner* effort to rescue that distinction in *Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board*, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013), fares no better. *Conforto* reasoned that jurisdictional dismissals differ because the Board "may not hear a case over which it lacks jurisdiction," while it may excuse procedural defects. *Id.* at 1118 n.1. But that distinction addresses only the Board's own remedial discretion; it says nothing about which court is best positioned to review a dismissal that turns—as it does here—on contested factual questions of coercion and discrimination. Indeed, voluntariness determinations frequently overlap with the merits of the discrimination claim itself, since an employee's allegation that she was coerced into a settlement by discriminatory pressure is functionally indistinguishable from the discrimination claim she seeks to litigate.

## III. A Rule Turning on the Board's Chosen Label Reintroduces the Very Confusion Congress and This Court Sought to Eliminate.

Sound statutory interpretation favors a "readily administrable" rule over one that invites satellite litigation about which label applies. *King v. St. Vincent's Hospital*, 502 U.S. 215, 221 (1991). A jurisdictional/procedural distinction is precisely the sort of rule that generates wasteful threshold litigation, forcing employees to guess—often incorrectly—which tribunal may hear their case, only to be told years later, after the statute of limitations has run, that they guessed wrong. That is exactly the predicament Petitioner now faces: transferred from the D.C. Circuit, uncertain whether the Federal Circuit possesses authority to adjudicate his discrimination claims at all. Congress could not have intended such a result when it enacted a scheme meant to give federal employees the same discrimination remedies available to all other employees under Title VII. *Chandler*, 425 U.S. at 864.

## CONCLUSION

Because Perry's mixed case satisfies the statutory definition regardless of the ground for the Board's dismissal, review belongs in district court. The judgment below should be reversed.
