The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern polling, but the NLRB has established a standard for allowing employers to poll employees. The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have rejected the NLRB's standard, holding that an employer may poll its employees if it has "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support." See NLRB v. A.W. Thompson, Inc., 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1962) (citing Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1964); Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1962). This standard is more in line with the NLRA's goal of protecting employees' rights to choose their representatives.

The NLRB's standard, on the other hand, renders polling "useless" as it requires an employer to have "objective indications raising a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status" before conducting a poll. See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987) (citing NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990) (predecessor case)). This is an overly stringent standard that fails to account for the employer's legitimate interest in determining the union's majority status.

In this case, Allentown Mack had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support. Several employees expressed anti-union sentiments, and the company had a good-faith doubt about the union's majority status. See NLRB v. Little Rock Downtowner, Inc., 341 F.2d 1020, 1022 (8th Cir. 1965) (holding that an employer's good-faith doubt is a valid defense to a refusal to bargain charge). The NLRB's finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith doubt is not supported by substantial evidence.

The Supreme Court has held that an employer may rebut the presumption of a union's continuing majority status with "sufficient objective evidence." See Brooks v. NLRB, 348 U.S. 96, 104 (1954). Allentown Mack presented such evidence, including statements from employees indicating a loss of support for the union. See NLRB v. John S. Barnes Corp., 379 F.2d 703, 705 (7th Cir. 1967) (holding that employee statements can be used to rebut the presumption of majority status).

Furthermore, the NLRB's standard is inconsistent with the NLRA's goal of protecting employees' rights. By making it difficult for employers to poll employees, the NLRB's standard can lead to employers bargaining with a minority union, which is a violation of § 8(a)(2) of the NLRA. See NLRB v. Local 404, Int'l Bhd. of Teamsters, 205 F.2d 99, 103 (1st Cir. 1953) (holding that an employer may not bargain with a minority union).

In light of the above, this Court should hold that the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard is not rational and consistent with the NLRA, and that Allentown Mack had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support. The NLRB's factual determination to the contrary is not supported by substantial evidence. See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951) (holding that the substantial evidence standard requires a reviewing court to consider the entire record).

The Court should reverse the NLRB's order and remand for further proceedings. The NLRB's decision is not supported by substantial evidence, and its standard for polling is overly stringent and inconsistent with the NLRA. See NLRB v. Purity Food Stores, Inc., 354 F.2d 926, 931 (1st Cir. 1965) (reversing an NLRB order due to a lack of substantial evidence).
