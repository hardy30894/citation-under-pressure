**ARGUMENT**

The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA—though no longer serving the purpose of retirement savings for the beneficiary—remains exempt from the bankruptcy estate under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). The Court of Appeals misread the statutory text, ignored controlling precedent, and failed to respect the foundational principle that exemptions in bankruptcy are narrowly construed. The correct interpretation of the Bankruptcy Code is that an inherited IRA is not “retirement funds” within the meaning of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it no longer functions as a vehicle for the beneficiary’s retirement. The exemption applies only to funds that are, at the time of bankruptcy, being held for the debtor’s own retirement. This interpretation is compelled by the plain language of the statute, consistent with the Supreme Court’s directive that exemptions must be interpreted in light of their purpose, and supported by a clear line of circuit precedent.

**I. The Statutory Text Requires That the Funds Be Held for the Debtor’s Retirement**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The key phrase is “retirement funds,” a term not defined in the Bankruptcy Code. But the Supreme Court has long held that “where a statute does not define a term, we look to its context and purpose.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989). The purpose of the exemption is to protect funds set aside for the debtor’s own retirement, not to shield assets that have lost their retirement function. As the Court explained in *Barnes v. Ryan*, 536 U.S. 135, 142 (2002), “the Bankruptcy Code’s exemptions are designed to preserve a debtor’s ‘fresh start’ by protecting essential assets, not to reward pre-bankruptcy planning.” The exemption is not a windfall for heirs; it is a safeguard for the debtor’s future.

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary view—that the exemption applies because the funds were once retirement funds—ignores the temporal and functional requirements of the statute. The Court of Appeals analogized to a trustee holding retirement funds for another, but that analogy fails. In *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312, 318 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), the court correctly observed that “a trustee is not the owner of the funds, but holds them for the benefit of another.” But Heidi Heffron-Clark is not a trustee; she is the sole owner of the inherited IRA. The funds are not held for her mother’s benefit, nor are they held for any future retirement of the original owner. They are held for her own use, and under the Internal Revenue Code, they must be distributed within five years of the original owner’s death. 26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A). This is not a retirement vehicle—it is a time-limited tax-deferral mechanism that must be liquidated before the beneficiary reaches retirement age.

**II. The Seventh Circuit’s Interpretation Contradicts the Supreme Court’s Precedent on Statutory Construction**

The Seventh Circuit’s approach—extending the exemption to assets that were once retirement funds but are no longer—runs afoul of the Supreme Court’s principle that “a statute should not be interpreted to produce absurd or unintended results.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters.*, 489 U.S. at 242. If the exemption applied to any asset that had once been a retirement fund, then a debtor could inherit a $1 million IRA, withdraw the funds, spend them on a yacht, and still claim the entire amount exempt from creditors. That would render the exemption meaningless and allow debtors to circumvent the bankruptcy process. The Court has repeatedly rejected such expansive interpretations. In *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 101 (2000), the Court held that “a statute must be read in a way that gives effect to its purpose and avoids absurd results.” The Seventh Circuit’s rule produces precisely such an absurd result.

Moreover, the Court has emphasized that “the meaning of a statute is not to be determined by isolated words or phrases, but by the context and the overall structure.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). The Bankruptcy Code distinguishes between exemptions that depend on the debtor’s interest at the time of filing (e.g., §522(b)(3)(B), which refers to “the debtor’s interest”) and those that do not (e.g., §522(b)(3)(C)). The absence of a temporal qualifier in §522(b)(3)(C) does not mean that the exemption applies to assets that were once retirement funds but are no longer. Rather, it reflects Congress’s intent to protect ongoing retirement savings, even if new contributions are made during bankruptcy. *In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985). The exemption is not a retroactive shield for assets that have ceased to serve their original purpose.

**III. The Seventh Circuit’s Decision Creates a Circuit Split and Contradicts Binding Precedent**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision in *Clark* directly conflicts with the Fifth Circuit’s holding in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), which held that inherited IRAs are exempt under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because they were once retirement funds. But the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning is flawed. It relies on the fact that the statute does not say “the debtor’s retirement funds,” but that omission does not justify a reading that ignores the functional reality.
