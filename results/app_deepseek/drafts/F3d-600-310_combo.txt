**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Court Lacked Subject Matter Jurisdiction Over Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs.**

A bankruptcy court is a court of limited jurisdiction, possessing only the authority Congress has conferred. *Cline v. Kaplan*, 323 U.S. 97, 98 (1944); *United States v. Emery*, 237 U.S. 28, 32 (1915). That jurisdiction is further confined to the property and controversies of an active bankruptcy estate. *See Harris v. Zion’s Savings Bank & Trust Co.*, 317 U.S. 447, 450 (1943). Once the estate is closed and the trustee discharged, there is no estate and no administration to oversee.

Here, Kirkland’s Chapter 13 case was closed in February 2006, after the discharge order was entered. The adversary proceeding was not filed until June 2007. By that time, the bankruptcy court had no authority to determine any matter “concerning the administration of the estate,” 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2)(A), because there was no longer an estate. The post-petition interest and collection costs at issue are not assets of the estate; they are personal obligations purportedly arising from Kirkland’s post-petition default. They are not part of the claims-allowance process and are not within the bankruptcy court’s core jurisdiction.

The only issue properly before the bankruptcy court was whether the loan principal had been discharged. Kirkland’s complaint expressly acknowledged that post-petition interest was nondischargeable and prayed only for a declaration that the principal had been paid and discharged. ECMC’s answer contested dischargeability. No party sought an award of interest or a determination of collection costs. The bankruptcy court nevertheless *sua sponte* awarded $184.40 in interest and denied costs. A court is confined to the issues made by the pleadings. *Keokuk & Hamilton Bridge Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 125, 128 (1922). The bankruptcy court exceeded that limit.

The dischargeability jurisdiction conferred by the Bankruptcy Act does not carry with it authority to fix the amount of ancillary, nondischargeable liabilities. *Katchen v. Landy*, 382 U.S. 323, 328-29 (1966). Dischargeability is a binary inquiry: the debt is either discharged or it is not. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). If it is not discharged, the creditor’s right to collect the full amount owed, including interest and costs, is left to the ordinary courts. The Act is to be construed strictly against the creditor seeking to expand bankruptcy jurisdiction. *Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915).

Finally, post-petition interest is not a provable claim in bankruptcy. *Sexton v. Dreyfus*, 219 U.S. 339, 344 (1911); *Vanston Bondholders Protective Committee v. Green*, 329 U.S. 156, 163 (1946). Collection costs are likewise not administration expenses. They arise, if at all, from a post-discharge default. A discharge does not release a debtor from such obligations. *Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 236 U.S. 549, 554 (1915). Because these obligations are not part of the bankruptcy estate, the bankruptcy court had no jurisdiction over them.

**II. Even If Jurisdiction Existed, the Bankruptcy Court Erred in Its Determination of Interest and Costs.**

Alternatively, the bankruptcy court’s awards cannot stand. The court awarded $184.40 in post-petition interest without any finding as to the contract rate or the accrual period. Under governing law, interest is allowed at the contract rate until payment. *Vanston Bondholders*, 329 U.S. at 165. The court’s bare observation that Kirkland “did not dispute” the sum is insufficient; ECMC’s documentation may have demonstrated a different amount. The court also denied collection costs for lack of a “statutory or factual basis” despite ECMC having submitted the loan documents. Had jurisdiction existed, the proper course would have been an evidentiary hearing. But because the bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction, the district court’s judgment must be reversed and the case remanded with instructions to vacate the interest award and the cost denial.
