**Argument**

Peguero is not entitled to habeas relief because the District Court’s failure to advise him of his right to appeal caused him no prejudice. A district court’s omission under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32 is error, but not every Rule 32 violation warrants collateral relief. Habeas relief requires more than a technical breach; it requires a showing that the error affected substantial rights. See *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 783–85 (1979) (holding that collateral relief is unavailable for a technical Rule 11 violation absent a complete miscarriage of justice); *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (holding that a collateral attack may not rest on “a formal violation” of the rules where no prejudice is shown).

This Court has long recognized that criminal procedure rules are designed to protect important rights, but collateral review is not a vehicle for setting aside judgments on purely formal grounds. In *Timmreck*, the Court rejected a § 2255 claim based on a Rule 11 omission, emphasizing that “the concern with finality served by the limitation on collateral attack has special force with respect to convictions based on guilty pleas.” 441 U.S. at 784. The same principle controls here. Peguero does not allege that he was unaware of his right to appeal at the time judgment was entered. To the contrary, after an evidentiary hearing, both the District Court and the court of appeals found that he knew of his appellate rights when sentenced and chose not to appeal. Where the defendant already knows of the right the court failed to mention, the omission does not deprive him of an opportunity to seek review and therefore does not justify habeas relief.

The Court of Appeals correctly treated the Rule 32 omission as subject to harmless-error analysis. That approach accords with this Court’s cases recognizing that not every deviation from a rule of criminal procedure requires reversal. See *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 623, 637–38 (1993) (habeas relief unavailable unless the error had a “substantial and injurious effect or influence”); *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946) (harmless-error standard asks whether the error had substantial influence). A district court’s failure to advise of appellate rights is harmless when the defendant already knew of those rights and knowingly elected not to appeal. That is precisely what the courts below found here.

Peguero’s contrary rule—that any Rule 32 violation automatically entitles a prisoner to relief—cannot be squared with this Court’s collateral-review precedent. In *United States v. Addonizio*, 442 U.S. 178, 185 (1979), the Court held that a sentence may not be disturbed on habeas absent a fundamental defect resulting in a complete miscarriage of justice. Likewise, in *Timmreck*, the Court rejected the view that a technical rule violation alone suffices. 441 U.S. at 783–85. Peguero identifies no miscarriage of justice. He does not contend that he lost a meritorious appeal because of the omission; instead, the record shows he was already aware of the right and declined to pursue it.

The purpose of Rule 32 was fully served here. The rule’s notice requirement exists to ensure that a defendant understands the availability of appellate review. That purpose is satisfied when the defendant in fact knows of the right. See *Rodriquez v. United States*, 395 U.S. 327, 330–32 (1969) (recognizing that the denial of an appeal to one who desires to appeal is a serious deprivation, but distinguishing cases in which the defendant has knowingly forgone review). Because Peguero knew of his right, he was not denied appellate review in any meaningful sense. He received exactly the protection Rule 32 is meant to provide: awareness of the right to appeal.

Nor does the rule’s mandatory phrasing require automatic vacatur. This Court has repeatedly rejected formalism in postconviction review where the defendant suffered no actual prejudice. See *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428; *Timmreck*, 441 U.S. at 784–85. The habeas statute does not authorize setting aside convictions for errors that are harmless in fact. If relief were available whenever a district judge omitted a perfunctory advisement, even when the defendant already knew of the right, final criminal judgments would be unnecessarily destabilized without serving any compensating fairness interest.

Because Peguero knew of his right to appeal and elected not to do so, the Rule 32 omission did not prejudice him and does not support collateral relief. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
