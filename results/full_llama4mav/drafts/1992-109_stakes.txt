The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court's failure to apply this heightened "reasoned choice" standard to Moran's case was a due process violation.

The competency standard for waiving constitutional rights is distinct from the standard for determining competency to stand trial. As this Court has noted, a defendant is competent to stand trial if they have a "rational and factual understanding of the proceedings" and are "capable of assisting [their] counsel." Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). In contrast, a defendant is competent to waive counsel or plead guilty only if they have the capacity for "reasoned choice" among the available alternatives. See Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); Chavez v. United States, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981).

The trial court's failure to apply the "reasoned choice" standard to Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty plea was error. The state court's post-conviction ruling, which relied on psychiatric evaluations conducted to determine Moran's competency to stand trial, did not cure this error. Those evaluations did not assess Moran's capacity for "reasoned choice," and therefore did not provide a sufficient basis for determining his competency to waive counsel or plead guilty. See Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211, 214-15 (9th Cir. 1973).

Furthermore, the record raises significant doubts about Moran's competency to waive counsel and plead guilty. Moran had attempted suicide just months before the plea hearing, was taking multiple medications at the time of the hearing, and gave monosyllabic responses to the court's questions. Under these circumstances, due process required the trial court to hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights. See Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 180-81 (1975); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385-86 (1966).

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court's post-conviction ruling did not constitute a valid finding that Moran was competent to waive his constitutional rights. The state court applied the wrong legal standard, relying on the psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran's competency to stand trial rather than his capacity for "reasoned choice." See Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963).

In light of the record and the applicable law, it is clear that the trial court's failure to apply the "reasoned choice" standard to Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty plea was a due process violation. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
