# ARGUMENT

## I. Article III Confines Federal Courts to Live Controversies Capable of Specific Redress

The judicial power of the United States extends only to genuine "Cases" and "Controversies." U.S. Const. art. III, § 2. This Court has long insisted that a federal court's role is "the right to determine actual controversies arising between adverse litigants, duly instituted in courts of proper jurisdiction." *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346, 356–57 (1911). A dispute is justiciable only where it is "definite and concrete" and "admit[s] of specific relief through a decree of a conclusive character, as distinguished from an opinion advising what the law would be upon a hypothetical state of facts." *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240–41 (1937). These principles apply with equal force at every stage of a case: a controversy that is live when filed can become moot, and once it does, the federal courts lack power to proceed. *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895) ("The duty of this court, as of every judicial tribunal, is limited to determining rights of persons or of property, which are actually controverted in the particular case before it.").

GGC's revision of the Freedom of Expression Policy and elimination of the challenged Student Code of Conduct provision extinguished the only live controversy in this case—the constitutionality of policies that no longer exist. Petitioners do not dispute that their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief became moot. The only question is whether a bare demand for nominal damages, untethered to any actual loss, can manufacture a controversy where none remains.

## II. A Claim for Nominal Damages Alone Cannot Sustain an Otherwise Moot Case

This Court has repeatedly refused to retain jurisdiction merely because a party seeks an abstract or nominal form of relief once the substantive dispute has evaporated. In *California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R. Co.*, 149 U.S. 308 (1893), the Court dismissed a tax case as moot after the underlying obligation was satisfied, explaining that "[t]his court is not empowered to decide moot questions or abstract propositions, or to declare, for the government of future cases, principles or rules of law which cannot affect the result as to the thing in issue in the case before it." *Id.* at 314. Critically, the Court held that even a residual claim tied to the litigation—there, a question of costs—could not revive a case once the true controversy disappeared. *Id.*

The same logic governs nominal damages. Nominal damages, by definition, compensate no actual injury; they exist only to mark a technical legal wrong. See *Mills*, 159 U.S. at 653 (courts decide only "actually controverted" rights, not symbolic ones). Once GGC rescinded the Prior Policies, any declaration of their invalidity would "affect [no] result as to the thing in issue" — there is no policy left to enjoin, no injury left to compensate, and no future application to prevent. *San Pablo & Tulare R.R. Co.*, 149 U.S. at 314. A request for a token award divorced from any continuing or compensable harm is the paradigmatic "abstract proposition" this Court has refused to entertain.

This case is thus readily distinguishable from *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), where a live claim for back pay preserved jurisdiction after the plaintiff's expulsion from Congress rendered his claim for reinstatement moot. The backpay claim in *Powell* rested on a concrete, quantifiable pecuniary loss—salary actually withheld—not a symbolic sum untethered to injury. *Id.* at 495–96, 512–14. Petitioners here identify no analogous loss. The First Amended Complaint's request for nominal damages, as both the district court and the Eleventh Circuit correctly recognized, sought only a token sum to mark a constitutional violation, not compensation for any pled injury such as lost wages, medical expense, or quantifiable harm of the sort that sustained jurisdiction in *Powell*.

## III. Golden v. Zwickler Confirms That a Policy Change Extinguishes the Controversy Notwithstanding a Continuing Request for Relief

This Court's decision in *Golden v. Zwickler*, 394 U.S. 103 (1969), controls. There, the plaintiff sought a declaration that a handbill-distribution statute was unconstitutional, but intervening events made it "most unlikely" that the circumstances giving rise to the dispute would recur. *Id.* at 109. The Court held the case moot notwithstanding the continued formal request for declaratory relief, because "the case has lost its character as a present, live controversy." *Id.* at 108. The same is true here: GGC's superseding policies eliminated the very restrictions Petitioners challenged, and there is no reasonable expectation the college will revert to them. A continuing prayer for relief—whether declaratory or nominal—cannot resurrect a controversy that the facts themselves have resolved.

## IV. Collateral or Incidental Requests for Relief Do Not Preserve Jurisdiction

Finally, this Court has consistently held that incidental or collateral aspects of a lawsuit—such as costs—do not present a "substantial controversy" sufficient to avoid dismissal once the primary dispute is moot. *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42–43 (1943) (dismissing as moot despite arguments concerning ancillary consequences of the underlying judgment). Nominal damages, no less than costs, are incidental to the substantive claim; they cannot serve as the tail that wags the mootness dog.

## CONCLUSION

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that Petitioners' claim for nominal damages could not rescue their otherwise moot challenge to policies GGC had already abandoned. The judgment below should be affirmed.
