ARGUMENT

I. The Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to determine whether a capital defendant is mentally retarded.

Ring v. Arizona held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find any fact that increases the maximum punishment. 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002). Mental retardation, however, operates solely as a restriction on the State’s power to execute, not as an element that increases punishment. Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002) (“[W]e leave to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction upon [their] execution of sentences.”). Because the absence of retardation is not a fact that exposes the defendant to a greater sentence, it falls outside Ring’s rule. See Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000) (requiring jury finding only of facts “that increase the prescribed range of penalties”).

Florida’s statutory definition of mental retardation, Fla. Stat. § 921.137(1), and the clear-and-convincing-evidence standard, id. § 921.137(4), are therefore consistent with the Eighth Amendment. The trial court’s determination that Hurst failed to meet that standard did not violate the Sixth Amendment.

II. The Sixth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury recommendation of death.

The Sixth Amendment’s jury-trial guarantee incorporates no unanimity requirement in state capital sentencing. See McDonald v. City of Chicago, 561 U.S. 742, 765 n.14 (2010) (noting that the Court has never held unanimity applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment). Ring itself addressed only who must find aggravating circumstances, not whether those findings must be unanimous. 536 U.S. at 609. Florida’s scheme, which permits a death recommendation by a majority of the jury and requires the trial judge to make independent findings, satisfies Ring because the jury’s advisory verdict is not the sole basis for the sentence. See Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447, 465 (1984) (upholding Florida’s pre-Ring procedure).

Nor does the Eighth Amendment compel unanimity. The Court has never held that the “evolving standards of decency” require every capital-sentencing fact to be found by a unanimous jury. See Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality). Florida’s outlier status among death-penalty jurisdictions does not itself establish an Eighth Amendment violation. See Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957, 990 (1991) (plurality) (rejecting the notion that state practices alone dictate constitutional minima).

The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
