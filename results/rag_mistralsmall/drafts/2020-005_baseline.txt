**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Permits Specific Jurisdiction Where a Plaintiff’s Claims “Relate To” a Defendant’s Forum Activities, Even If Those Activities Did Not Cause the Underlying Harm.**

The Due Process Clause does not require that a defendant’s forum contacts be the *proximate cause* of the plaintiff’s injury to support specific jurisdiction. Instead, it requires only that the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum activities. *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011) (quoting *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984)). This Court has long recognized that the “relate to” standard is broader than the “arise out of” standard, encompassing any claim that has a “substantial connection” to the defendant’s forum activities. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472 (1985) (citing *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 317 (1945)).

Here, Ford’s extensive contacts with Montana—including its nationwide network of dealerships, its advertising and marketing of replacement parts, and its ongoing business relationships with Montana consumers—provide a sufficient basis for specific jurisdiction. The fact that the vehicle at issue was not originally sold in Montana does not defeat jurisdiction, as Ford’s forum activities are sufficiently connected to the claims. *See id.* at 475 (holding that a contract with a forum resident may support jurisdiction even if the underlying dispute did not arise from forum activities).

**II. Ford’s Nationwide Sales and Marketing Activities in Montana Satisfy the “Purposeful Availment” Requirement.**

Due process requires that a defendant have “purposefully availed” itself of the forum’s laws before a state may exercise specific jurisdiction. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475. Ford’s nationwide distribution system, which includes sales and marketing of replacement parts in Montana, clearly meets this standard. Ford has purposefully availed itself of Montana’s market by advertising, selling, and servicing its vehicles and parts in the state. *See id.* at 473 (finding purposeful availment where a franchise agreement subjected the defendant to the forum’s laws).

Moreover, Ford’s ongoing business relationships with Montana consumers—including the sale of replacement parts and provision of warranty services—demonstrate that it has invoked the benefits and protections of Montana’s laws. *See id.* at 475-76. This is sufficient to satisfy the due process requirement.

**III. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the “Substantial Connection” Test for Specific Jurisdiction.**

The Montana Supreme Court properly found that Ford’s forum activities had a “substantial connection” to the claims, as required by *International Shoe* and its progeny. *See id.* at 472. The court’s analysis was consistent with this Court’s precedents, which permit jurisdiction where the defendant’s forum activities are related to the plaintiff’s claims, even if those activities did not cause the underlying harm. *See Goodyear*, 564 U.S. at 919.

Ford’s argument that jurisdiction is improper because the vehicle was not originally sold in Montana ignores the broader context of its nationwide business operations. Ford’s extensive contacts with Montana—including its marketing, sales, and service activities—provide a sufficient basis for jurisdiction under the Due Process Clause.

**IV. The “Stream of Commerce” Theory Supports Jurisdiction in This Case.**

This Court has recognized that a defendant’s placement of a product into the “stream of commerce” with the expectation that it will be sold in the forum state may support jurisdiction. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297-98 (1980). Ford’s nationwide distribution system, which includes sales of replacement parts in Montana, clearly satisfies this standard. Ford placed its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they would be sold and used in Montana, and it has purposefully availed itself of the state’s market. *See id.* at 297-98.

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford is subject to specific jurisdiction in Montana. Ford’s extensive contacts with the state—including its nationwide sales and marketing activities—satisfy the due process requirements for specific jurisdiction. The judgment below should be affirmed.
