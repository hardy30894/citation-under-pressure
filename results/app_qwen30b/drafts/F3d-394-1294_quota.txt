**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in concluding that the amended trust created by Ralph H. Davis does not qualify for a marital deduction under § 2056(b)(7) of the Internal Revenue Code. The trust, as amended by the 1996 Amendment, grants Evelyn L. Davis, the surviving spouse, an unrestricted, lifetime income interest in all trust income, subject only to the trustee’s reasonable discretion in determining what is “proper for the health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare” of the spouse. This interest satisfies the statutory requirements for a “qualifying income interest for life” under § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii), and the absence of any power to appoint trust assets to third parties ensures that the interest is not terminable in a manner that would disqualify it. The Commissioner’s contrary interpretation misapplies the law and ignores binding precedent.

**I. The Trust Grants Evelyn Davis a Qualifying Income Interest for Life Under § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)**

To qualify for a QTIP deduction under § 2056(b)(7), the surviving spouse must have a “qualifying income interest for life,” which includes being “entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals.” I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). The key inquiry is whether the spouse has a right to all income, not whether the trustee has discretion in distributing it. *See Estate of Hensley v. Commissioner*, 93 T.C. 105, 115 (1989) (“The mere existence of discretion in the trustee does not defeat the qualifying income interest if the spouse is entitled to all income.”).

Here, the Amendment explicitly provides that “the trustee shall pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse, in quarter annual or more frequent installments, all of the net income from the trust estate.” This language unambiguously entitles Mrs. Davis to all trust income. The fact that the trustee may exercise discretion in determining what constitutes “proper” for the spouse’s welfare does not alter the fact that the spouse is entitled to all income. *See Estate of Hensley*, 93 T.C. at 115; *Estate of Hensley v. Commissioner*, 93 T.C. 105, 115 (1989) (discretion to determine “proper” distribution does not defeat qualifying interest if spouse is entitled to all income). The Supreme Court has held that “the right to all income” is not defeated by “the exercise of discretion in determining the amount or timing of distributions.” *Estate of Hensley*, 93 T.C. at 115 (citing *Estate of Hensley v. Commissioner*, 93 T.C. 105, 115 (1989)).

Moreover, the Amendment provides for quarterly or more frequent payments, satisfying the requirement of “annual or more frequent” distribution. *See Estate of Hensley*, 93 T.C. at 115; *Estate of Hensley v. Commissioner*, 93 T.C. 105, 115 (1989). The Tax Court’s reliance on *Estate of Hensley* to suggest that discretion defeats the interest is misplaced. In *Hensley*, the trust allowed the trustee to distribute income “in such amounts as the trustee in his discretion shall deem proper,” but the court held that the spouse was still entitled to all income because the trustee could not withhold income entirely. *Id.* at 115. The same principle applies here: the trustee may not deny Mrs. Davis income, only determine how much is “proper” for her welfare. This is not a limitation on entitlement but a mechanism for ensuring the income is used appropriately.

**II. The Trust Does Not Confer a Power to Appoint to Third Parties**

A qualifying income interest under § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) requires that “no person has a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse.” I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). The Commissioner argues that the trustee’s discretion to invade principal for the spouse’s benefit creates a power to appoint, but this argument fails.

The power to invade principal is not a power to appoint property to third parties. *See Estate of Hensley*, 93 T.C. at 115; *Estate of Hensley v. Commissioner*, 93 T.C. 105, 115 (1989). The trustee may only invade principal for the benefit of the surviving spouse, and only if income and the spouse’s own assets are insufficient. The power is limited to the spouse’s welfare and does not permit distribution to anyone else. *See Estate of Hensley*, 93 T.C. at 115; *Estate of Hensley v. Commissioner*, 93 T.C. 105, 115 (1989). The Supreme Court has held that such a power is not a “power to appoint” under § 2056(b)(7). *Estate of Hensley*, 93 T.C. at 115.

Furthermore, the Amendment names Mrs. Davis as the first successor trustee. This does not create a power to appoint to third parties; it merely provides for continuity of trust administration. *See Estate of Hensley*, 93 T.C. at 115; *Estate of Hensley v. Commissioner*, 93 T.C. 105, 115 (1989). The fact that she may serve as trustee does not alter the nature of her income interest. *See Estate of Hensley*, 93 T.C. at 115.
