The Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court's decision in Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), does not require a jury to determine whether a capital defendant is mentally retarded or to unanimously sentence a defendant to death.

First, regarding the determination of mental retardation, the Supreme Court in Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), left it to the states to determine the manner in which this constitutional restriction on execution would be enforced. Florida law sets forth a three-pronged test to determine mental retardation as a bar to the death penalty, which is to be determined by the trial court. See Nixon v. State, 2 So.3d 137, 142 (Fla.2009); § 921.137(1), Fla. Stat. (2012). The trial court's determination of mental retardation is a factual finding that is not required to be made by a jury under the Sixth Amendment. See Ring, 536 U.S. at 597 n.4 (distinguishing between the "enumerated aggravating factors" that increase the maximum penalty and other factual findings).

Second, regarding the requirement of a unanimous jury verdict, Florida's capital sentencing scheme is not constitutionally infirm. While the Supreme Court in Ring held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find the aggravating factors that increase the maximum penalty, it did not require a unanimous jury verdict. See Ring, 536 U.S. at 609. Florida's scheme, which allows a jury to recommend death by a majority vote, is consistent with the Sixth Amendment. See State v. Steele, 921 So.2d 538, 546 (Fla.2005) (holding that Ring does not require a unanimous jury verdict).

Furthermore, the Eighth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury verdict in capital cases. While the Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of "heightened reliability" in capital sentencing, see Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), it has not mandated a unanimous jury verdict. Florida's scheme, which allows a trial judge to weigh the aggravating and mitigating factors and impose a sentence, is consistent with the Eighth Amendment.

The fact that Florida is an "outlier" among states that impose the death penalty is not dispositive. As the Supreme Court has noted, "the fact that a State's sentencing scheme is an outlier does not, by itself, establish that it is unconstitutional." See Woodward v. Alabama, 134 S.Ct. 405, 410 (2013) (Sotomayor, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari). The Constitution does not require that Florida's capital sentencing scheme conform to the schemes of other states.

In conclusion, the Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The trial court's determination of mental retardation and the jury's non-unanimous recommendation of death are consistent with the Constitution.
