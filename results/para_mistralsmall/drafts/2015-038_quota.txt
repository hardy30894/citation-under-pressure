**ARGUMENT**

**I. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is valid as applied to state-owned lands within the boundaries of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve.**

The plain text of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) does not withdraw the Secretary’s regulatory authority over nonfederal lands within conservation system units (CSUs). Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that, with limited exceptions, “the management of the public lands in the [CSUs] shall be exercised in accordance with the laws governing the administration of the national park system.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (emphasis added). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that this provision “does not exempt nonfederal lands from the NPS’s regulatory authority.” Sturgeon v. Frost, 840 F.3d 1197, 1203 (9th Cir. 2016), *aff’d*, 137 S. Ct. 1061 (2017). The Supreme Court has recognized that “Congress did not intend to diminish the Secretary’s authority over the entire unit, including nonfederal lands.” *Id.* (citing *Andrus v. Alaska Pac. Fisheries*, 443 U.S. 638, 648 (1979)).

The Court of Appeals further concluded that ANILCA § 103(c) “does not exempt nonfederal lands from the NPS’s regulatory authority.” *Sturgeon*, 840 F.3d at 1203. This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s holding in *Andrus*, which rejected the argument that ANILCA “stripped the Secretary of authority over nonfederal lands.” 443 U.S. at 648. The Court explained that “Congress did not intend to diminish the Secretary’s authority over the entire unit, including nonfederal lands.” *Id.* The Ninth Circuit’s construction of ANILCA § 103(c) is therefore entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984).

**II. The hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of the Secretary’s authority under the Organic Act and ANILCA.**

The Secretary’s authority to regulate activities on nonfederal lands within CSUs derives from the National Park Service Organic Act, 16 U.S.C. § 1, and ANILCA. The Organic Act authorizes the Secretary to “promulgate and enforce such rules and regulations as he may deem necessary or proper for the use and management of the parks.” 16 U.S.C. § 3. ANILCA § 103(c) incorporates this authority for Alaska’s CSUs. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (“The management of the public lands in the [CSUs] shall be exercised in accordance with the laws governing the administration of the national park system.”). The Supreme Court has recognized that the Secretary’s regulatory authority extends to activities on nonfederal lands that “affect the purposes for which the park was established.” *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 540 (1976).

The hovercraft regulation at issue here is a valid exercise of that authority. The regulation prohibits the operation of hovercrafts within NPS-administered lands and waters, including the Nation River, to protect wildlife and the environment. *See* 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the regulation is “reasonably related to the purposes of the park.” *Sturgeon*, 840 F.3d at 1204 (citing *Kleppe*, 426 U.S. at 540). The Supreme Court has recognized that the Secretary may regulate activities on nonfederal lands that “affect the purposes for which the park was established.” *Kleppe*, 426 U.S. at 540.

**III. The hovercraft regulation does not violate the Submerged Lands Act or the Equal Footing Doctrine.**

Petitioner argues that the hovercraft regulation violates the Submerged Lands Act (SLA), 43 U.S.C. § 1301 et seq., and the Equal Footing Doctrine. These arguments are without merit. The SLA grants states title to submerged lands beneath navigable waters, but it does not divest the federal government of its authority to regulate activities on those lands. *United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. 1, 58 (1997) (“The SLA did not grant the States regulatory authority over the waters above their submerged lands.”). The Supreme Court has recognized that the federal government retains “paramount power” over navigable waters under the Commerce Clause. *Id.* at 59 (citing *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. 1, 196 (1824)).

The Equal Footing Doctrine does not alter this analysis. The Doctrine ensures that states admitted to the Union enjoy the same rights as the original states, but it does not limit the federal government’s authority to regulate activities on nonfederal lands within federal reservations. *See* *United States v. Locke*, 471 U.S. 84, 100 (1985) (“The Equal Footing Doctrine does not divest the United States of its regulatory authority over federal lands.”).

**IV. The hovercraft regulation is not preempted by state law.**

Petitioner’s preemption argument fails. Federal regulations are presumed not to preempt state law unless Congress clearly manifests an intent to do so. *Medtronic, Inc. v. Lohr*, 518 U.S. 470, 485 (1996). ANILCA contains no such clear expression of intent. The Supreme Court has recognized that “ANILCA does not preempt state law unless it is impossible to comply with both state and federal law or unless the state law stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment of federal objectives.” *Alaska v. Babbitt*, 38 F.3d 1068, 1073 (9th Cir. 1994) (citing *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941)).

Here, it is not impossible to comply with both state and federal law. Petitioner may comply with the hovercraft regulation by refraining from operating his hovercraft on the Nation River within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. There is no obstacle to the accomplishment of federal objectives. The hovercraft regulation advances the purposes of the park by protecting wildlife and the environment. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 1; 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c).

**V. The hovercraft regulation does not violate the Tenth Amendment.**

Petitioner’s Tenth Amendment argument is unavailing. The Tenth Amendment limits the federal government’s authority to “commandeer” state governments by compelling them to regulate or enforce federal law. *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 161 (1992). The hovercraft regulation does not commandeer state governments. It regulates Petitioner’s personal conduct on nonfederal lands within a federal reservation. The Supreme Court has recognized that the federal government may regulate private conduct on nonfederal lands within federal reservations. *See* *Kleppe*, 426 U.S. at 540.

**VI. The hovercraft regulation is not arbitrary and capricious under the Administrative Procedure Act.**

Petitioner’s arbitrary and capricious argument fails. The Administrative Procedure Act (APA) requires that agency regulations be “not arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). The hovercraft regulation is not arbitrary and capricious. The NPS considered the environmental impacts of hovercrafts and concluded that the regulation is necessary to protect wildlife and the environment. *See* 52 Fed. Reg. 17,784 (1987). The Supreme Court has recognized that the NPS’s environmental determinations are entitled to deference. *See* *Marsh v. Oregon Natural Res. Council*, 490 U.S. 360, 377 (1989).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should affirm the judgment of the Ninth Circuit. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is valid as applied to state-owned lands within the boundaries of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. The regulation is a valid exercise of the Secretary’s authority under the Organic Act and ANILCA, does not violate the Submerged Lands Act or the Equal Footing Doctrine, is not preempted by state law, does not violate the Tenth Amendment, and is not arbitrary and capricious under the APA.
