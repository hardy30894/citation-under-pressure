**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing this putative class action as moot after Bureau of Collection Recovery, Inc. (“BCR”) made an unaccepted Rule 68 offer for Lucero’s individual claim before the class-certification deadline. Under Supreme Court and Tenth Circuit precedent, a defendant may not unilaterally extinguish a live class controversy by “picking off” the named plaintiff before the court has had a fair opportunity to rule on certification. See *Sosna v. Iowa*, 419 U.S. 393 (1975); *U.S. Parole Comm’n v. Geraghty*, 445 U.S. 388 (1980); *Deposit Guaranty Nat’l Bank, Jackson, Miss. v. Roper*, 445 U.S. 326 (1980); *Reed v. Heckler*, 756 F.2d 779 (10th Cir. 1985); *Clark v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 590 F.3d 1134 (10th Cir. 2009).

First, an unaccepted offer of complete relief does not itself moot a case. The Supreme Court later confirmed what Rule 68 already implies: “an unaccepted settlement offer or offer of judgment does not moot a plaintiff’s case.” *Campbell-Ewald Co. v. Gomez*, 577 U.S. 153, 165 (2016). Even before *Campbell-Ewald*, the Court had recognized that a defendant’s tender of relief cannot defeat jurisdiction where the plaintiff has not received the remedy sought. In *Roper*, the Court held that named plaintiffs whose individual claims had been satisfied retained a live interest in appealing denial of class certification because they possessed a personal stake in shifting litigation costs and representing the class. 445 U.S. at 332–34. The same concern exists here: BCR’s Rule 68 offer was designed to terminate the litigation before certification, not to resolve the class claims on the merits.

Second, the district court’s bright-line rule conflicts with *Geraghty* and *Sosna*. In *Sosna*, the Court held that a certified class action does not become moot when the named plaintiff’s individual claim expires because the class acquires an independent legal status. 419 U.S. at 399–402. *Geraghty* extended that principle to the certification context, holding that a plaintiff may appeal the denial of class certification even though his personal claim has become moot. 445 U.S. at 404. The Court explained that the “relation back” doctrine may be necessary where the challenged conduct is too transitory to allow full litigation before mootness would otherwise occur. *Id.* at 398–99. A live controversy therefore remains where, as here, the defendant’s conduct threatens to evade review by mooting the named plaintiff before certification can be decided.

Third, this Court’s own decision in *Reed* does not support dismissal. *Reed* recognized that “where a defendant tenders complete relief to the named plaintiff prior to certification, the case may become moot,” but the decision must be read in light of the specific posture there and the broader class-action cases discussed above. 756 F.2d at 785–86. More importantly, later Tenth Circuit authority rejected the notion that a defendant can always moot a class case by satisfying the named plaintiff’s individual demand. In *Clark*, the Court reiterated that mootness does not defeat a class action when the plaintiff has diligently pursued certification and the defendant attempts to “pick off” the representative. 590 F.3d at 1137–39. *Clark* is especially controlling here because Lucero had not delayed unduly; the parties’ scheduling order expressly sequenced class-certification discovery and set a certification deadline, and Lucero filed his motion the day before that deadline. Under *Clark*, he acted within a reasonable time.

Fourth, the district court improperly ignored the relation-back doctrine recognized in Supreme Court precedent and applied by multiple courts of appeals. Where a defendant’s tactics threaten to moot a named plaintiff before certification can reasonably be adjudicated, the certification motion relates back to the filing of the complaint. *Sosna*, 419 U.S. at 402 n.11; *Geraghty*, 445 U.S. at 398–99; *Weiss v. Regal Collections*, 385 F.3d 337, 348–49 (3d Cir. 2004); *Susman v. Lincoln Am. Corp.*, 587 F.2d 866, 870–71 (7th Cir. 1978). That doctrine prevents “picking off” and preserves the utility of Rule 23. Here, BCR’s early Rule 68 offer came only weeks after removal, before class discovery had begun, and before Lucero’s certification motion was due under the court’s own schedule. The relation-back doctrine is therefore necessary to prevent BCR from defeating class adjudication through unilateral action.

Finally, dismissal here conflicts with the basic rule that Article III requires a concrete dispute at the time suit is filed, not at the time the defendant chooses to capitulate. *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189–92 (2000). Lucero’s complaint alleged statutory violations and sought both damages and class-wide relief. Those allegations created a live controversy. BCR’s rejected offer did not eliminate the controversy because Lucero sought to represent absent class members, and his claim had not been adjudicated or satisfied by judgment. See *Genesis Healthcare Corp. v. Symczyk*, 569 U.S. 66, 73–75 (2013) (distinguishing collective-action context and noting the importance of whether a claim is actually satisfied). The district court’s contrary rule would allow defendants to insulate unlawful conduct from judicial review whenever they can afford to pay off one named plaintiff.

Because BCR’s Rule 68 offer did not moot Lucero’s timely pursued class claims, the dismissal should be reversed and the case remanded for consideration of class certification on the merits.
