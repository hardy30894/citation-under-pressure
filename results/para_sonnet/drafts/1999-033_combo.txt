# ARGUMENT

## THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE A "CLASS OF ONE" CAUSE OF ACTION WHERE THE PLAINTIFF ALLEGES NO MEMBERSHIP IN ANY CLASS OR GROUP

### A. The Equal Protection Clause Has Always Been Understood to Guard Against Discrimination Among Classes, Not to Redress the Grievances of a Single Individual

From its earliest applications, this Court has treated the Equal Protection Clause as a guarantee running to persons "similarly circumstanced," not as a general charter against arbitrary treatment of any one citizen. In *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27 (1885), the Court explained that "[c]lass legislation, discriminating against some and favoring others, is prohibited; but legislation which, in carrying out a public purpose, is limited in its application, if within the sphere of its operation it affects alike all persons similarly situated, is not within the amendment." *Id.* at 32. The touchstone is treatment of similarly situated *groups*, not the treatment of one person measured against everyone else.

*Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), is not to the contrary. Although the Court there condemned administration of a facially neutral ordinance "with an evil eye and an unequal hand," *id.* at 373, the discrimination condemned was systematic exclusion of an entire identifiable class—Chinese laundry operators—from the benefit of licenses freely granted to others. The wrong redressed was discrimination against a class defined by race, not an isolated permitting dispute involving a single applicant's easement.

Similarly, *Missouri v. Lewis*, 101 U.S. 22 (1880), confirms that the Amendment addresses differential treatment of classes of persons under like circumstances, not the particularized grievance of an individual dissatisfied with a discretionary decision applied to her alone.

### B. This Court's Modern Equal Protection Doctrine Requires Proof of Discrimination Based on an Impermissible Classification, Not Merely Idiosyncratic or Ad Hoc Treatment

*Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), squarely addressed the situation presented here—a plaintiff complaining that state officials misapplied a facially valid rule to her disadvantage. The Court held: "The unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination." *Id.* at 8. Critically, *Snowden* did not hold that any intentional deviation from evenhanded treatment suffices; the discrimination must operate against persons "entitled to be treated alike"—that is, members of a class or group whose common characteristic explains the differential treatment. Olech's complaint contains no such allegation; she claims only that she, individually, was treated worse than unspecified other applicants, without alleging that this differential treatment tracked any classification at all.

*Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448 (1962), reinforces this requirement. There, the Court held that "the conscious exercise of some selectivity in enforcement is not in itself a federal constitutional violation." *Id.* at 456. Rather, a plaintiff must show that the selection was "deliberately based upon an unjustifiable standard such as race, religion, or other arbitrary classification." *Id.* Selectivity alone—even intentional selectivity—does not establish an equal protection violation absent proof that the selection rested on a forbidden classificatory basis. Olech has identified no such standard; she alleges only a dispute over easement width, untethered to any class-based motive save the conclusory label "spite."

### C. Rational-Basis Review, Which Presupposes a Classification, Cannot Be Applied to a Claim That Alleges No Classification at All

The very structure of equal protection analysis presumes a classification to be tested. In *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61 (1911), the Court articulated the framework still governing garden-variety equal protection claims: legislative or administrative classifications are presumptively valid, and a challenger "must carry the burden of showing that [the classification] does not rest upon any reasonable basis, but is essentially arbitrary." *Id.* at 78–79. That framework functions only where two or more classes exist to be compared.

*F. S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia*, 253 U.S. 412 (1920), makes the same point: a classification "must rest upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation, so that all persons similarly circumstanced shall be treated alike." *Id.* at 415 (emphasis added). Likewise, in *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), the Court held "[i]t is no requirement of equal protection that all evils of the same genus be eradicated or none at all," *id.* at 110—a principle that necessarily assumes comparison between defined categories of persons or conduct, not between one plaintiff and an amorphous universe of "others."

Olech's theory asks this Court to conduct rational-basis review of a decision affecting one person, compared solely to herself. That is not a classification; it is a disagreement about the wisdom of a discretionary municipal decision—precisely the kind of claim *Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141 (1940), teaches courts should not convert into a constitutional cause of action merely because a plaintiff feels aggrieved by differential treatment in an individual instance.

### Conclusion

Because Olech alleges no membership in any class, race, religion, or other group, and no classification for a court to test against the similarly-situated requirement these decisions establish, her complaint fails to state an equal protection claim. The judgment below should be reversed.
