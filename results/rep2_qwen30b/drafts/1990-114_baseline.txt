**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL” UNDER 29 U.S.C. § 630(f)

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) does not extend to appointed state court judges because they are “appointees on the policymaking level” within the meaning of § 630(f). This statutory exception is not limited to executive or legislative branch officials; rather, it applies to any individual whose duties involve the exercise of significant discretion and judgment in shaping the law and administering justice—precisely the role of a state judge.

The text of § 630(f) explicitly excludes from the definition of “employee” any “appointee on the policymaking level.” The phrase “policymaking level” is not confined to the executive or legislative branches. As the First Circuit held in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), “policymaking” is “indisputably a part of the function of judging” because judicial decisions often involve filling “interstices of authority” in statutes, constitutions, and precedent. *See also* *EEOC v. Illinois*, 721 F. Supp. 156, 160 (N.D. Ill. 1989) (“Judges are not mere automatons applying pre-existing rules; they interpret and apply laws, often in novel or ambiguous contexts, thereby shaping the law.”). The role of a judge is not merely to “decide cases” in a mechanical sense, but to interpret legal texts, resolve conflicts, and establish binding precedent—activities that are quintessentially policymaking.

The Missouri judges here are not mere adjudicators of discrete disputes. They serve as the final arbiters of state law, with the power to interpret constitutional provisions, overturn legislative enactments, and create binding legal doctrine. *Cf.* *Cruzan v. Harmon*, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988) (en banc), cert. granted, --- U.S. ----, 109 S.Ct. 3240, 106 L.Ed.2d 587 (1989), where the Missouri Supreme Court confronted profound questions of medical ethics and constitutional liberty. Such decisions are not routine; they are foundational to the legal order and have far-reaching societal consequences. The Court has long recognized that judicial decisions “may be as important as legislative enactments in shaping the law.” *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 703 (1974).

Moreover, Missouri judges exercise substantial supervisory and administrative authority over the state judiciary. The Missouri Constitution vests the Supreme Court with broad powers to adopt rules governing judicial conduct, manage court operations, and resolve internal disputes among judges. *Mo. Const. art. V, § 4*. The Chief Justice, Chief Judges, and Presiding Judges exercise policymaking authority over court administration, case management, and judicial ethics. *See In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985); *In re Rules of Circuit Court of Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 757 S.W.2d 590 (Mo. 1988). These responsibilities are not administrative minutiae; they are core functions of governance that shape how justice is delivered across the state.

The petitioners’ argument that judges are not “on the policymaking level” because they are not “elected” or “partisan” is unpersuasive. The ADEA’s exception is not based on electoral status but on functional role. The legislative history cited by the petitioners—“the conferees’ intent that this exemption shall be construed narrowly”—does not support a narrow reading of the policymaking exception. *Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746*, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180. That statement refers to the narrow construction of the *entire* exemption scheme, not to the exclusion of judges from the policymaking category. Indeed, the very existence of the exception for “appointees on the policymaking level” suggests Congress intended to preserve the independence of high-level officials across all branches, including the judiciary.

To hold otherwise would render the exception meaningless in the context of state courts. If appointed judges were covered by the ADEA, then the only judges excluded would be those elected by the people—creating a perverse result where the most politically accountable judges are protected, while the most insulated are not. This cannot be the intent of Congress. *See EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 680 F. Supp. 455, 460 (D. Mass. 1988) (“It is implausible that Congress intended to exclude elected judges from the ADEA while including appointed judges in its coverage.”).

Thus, under the plain language of § 630(f), the petitioners are excluded from the ADEA’s definition of “employee.” The lower court correctly dismissed the statutory claim.

II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SURVIVES EQUAL PROTECTION REVIEW

Even if the ADEA applied, Missouri’s mandatory retirement at age 70 for state judges is rationally related to a legitimate state interest and therefore satisfies the rational basis test under the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Equal Protection Clause does not require that laws be “perfect” or “optimal,” only that they be “reasonable.” *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 n.4 (1938).
