The Eighth Amendment forbids only those punishments that involve "unnecessary cruelty" or "torture," Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 373 (1910), and does not prohibit the death penalty when imposed after a fair trial, Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 134-35 (1878). "Punishments are cruel when they involve torture or a lingering death," In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890), but execution by lawful means after conviction for capital murder inflicts no such excess, Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 463-64 (1947).

Mental capacity bears on guilt or sentencing discretion, yet does not render an otherwise constitutional penalty invalid. Courts have long permitted capital punishment for defendants whose faculties were impaired, provided the trial afforded due process. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932); see also Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455, 465 (1942). The Eighth Amendment's prohibition turns on the mode of punishment, not the offender's intelligence. Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 100 n.32 (1958) (plurality opinion).

A sentence of death for capital murder after proof of guilt beyond reasonable doubt satisfies the Amendment's command. Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660, 667 (1962). States retain authority to fix penalties for heinous crimes, and judicial review is limited to whether the punishment itself is "inhuman and barbarous." Francis, 329 U.S. at 464. No pre-1970 decision holds that reduced mental capacity alone converts a lawful execution into cruel and unusual punishment.

The trial record here shows a full adversarial proceeding, expert testimony on mental condition, and jury findings of guilt and aggravation. Such process meets the requirements of fundamental fairness. Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963); see also Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 467-68 (1938). The judgment imposing death therefore comports with the Eighth Amendment.
