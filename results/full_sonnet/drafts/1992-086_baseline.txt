# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT'S EXCLUSION OF LAMB'S CHAPEL'S FILM SERIES WAS VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION FORBIDDEN BY THE FIRST AMENDMENT

### A. Once a Limited Public Forum Is Created, Content-Based Exclusions Directed at Religious Viewpoints on Otherwise Permitted Subjects Violate the Free Speech Clause

This Court has long recognized that when government opens its property to expressive activity by the public, it creates a forum in which speech restrictions are constrained by the First Amendment. *Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983). Even where a forum is not a traditional public forum, once government "opens a forum to some speakers"—even a limited one—it may not exclude other speakers based on viewpoint. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267-70 (1981). In *Widmar*, this Court held that a university that created a forum generally open to student groups could not deny access to a religious student group, because doing so amounted to viewpoint discrimination. *Id.* at 277.

The Second Circuit acknowledged that the District's facilities constitute a "limited public forum," open for use by community and civic groups after hours. Pet. App. at __. Indeed, the District permitted the Boy Scouts, a garden club, a chapter of the American Red Cross, and a group performing musical entertainment with religious content (the "Southern Harmonizers") to use its property. Once the District opened its facilities for such community use—including discussion of childrearing and family issues through the "Psychology and the Unknown" lecture series—it could not selectively exclude Lamb's Chapel's contribution to that same discourse merely because its perspective on family values was religious.

This is precisely the discrimination this Court condemned in *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788 (1985), where the Court explained that even in a limited forum, "the State may not exclude speech where its distinction is not reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum," *id.* at 806, and, critically, may never discriminate against speech on the basis of viewpoint, *id.* at 812. The District permitted secular discussions of family values, childrearing, and psychology—the precise subject matter of the Dobson film series—but excluded Lamb's Chapel's contribution to that discourse solely because its viewpoint on those subjects was religious. That is textbook viewpoint discrimination.

### B. The District's Blanket Exclusion of "Religious Purposes" Is Not a Reasonable, Viewpoint-Neutral Time-Place-Manner Restriction

The District cannot escape this conclusion by characterizing its restriction as a content-neutral subject-matter limitation rather than viewpoint discrimination. This Court has cautioned that "the State's power to restrict speech in such [limited] forums is not without limits," *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 800, and any restriction must be "reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum," *id.* at 806, while remaining neutral as to viewpoint.

Here, the District did not merely restrict access by subject matter—it excluded an entire viewpoint on subjects it otherwise permitted. The film series' topic, family values and childrearing, was plainly within the range of subjects the District allowed other groups to address. The District's own correspondence confirms this: School Clerk Alice Schoener rejected the application not because family values was an impermissible topic, but because the film "does appear to be church related." Pet. App. at __. This is not subject-matter regulation; it is viewpoint discrimination dressed as content neutrality.

This Court's reasoning in *Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), though decided under the Equal Access Act, illuminates the constitutional stakes. There, the Court recognized that permitting some student groups to meet while excluding a religious student group based on its religious character would raise serious equal-access concerns. *Id.* at 247. The logic applies with equal force here: a school cannot open its doors to secular discussions of family values while closing them to religious perspectives on the identical subject.

### C. Denial of Access Was Not Compelled by the Establishment Clause

The Second Circuit did not rely on the Establishment Clause to justify exclusion, and rightly so, for no Establishment Clause concern justifies this discrimination. This Court's decision in *Widmar* forecloses any suggestion that permitting equal access to religious speech violates the Establishment Clause. There, the Court held that an "equal access policy would not be incompatible with the Establishment Clause," because such access, "granted to all forums on the same terms," serves the secular purpose of accommodating a broad range of community expression, and because "a religious organization's enjoyment of merely 'incidental' benefits does not violate the prohibition against the 'primary advancement' of religion." 454 U.S. at 273-75.

Extending access to Lamb's Chapel on the same terms afforded to other community groups discussing family values would not signal state endorsement of religion. The forum would remain open to secular and religious viewpoints alike, and any benefit to Lamb's Chapel would be incidental to a neutral policy of permitting after-hours community use. Excluding the Chapel's viewpoint, by contrast, affirmatively disfavors religious speech relative to secular speech on the same topics—precisely the kind of discrimination the Free Speech Clause forbids.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions that the District be enjoined from denying Lamb's Chapel access to school facilities on the same terms afforded to other community organizations.
