**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Lodestar Method’s Core Principle—That the Fee Must Reflect Reasonable Market Rates—Forecloses Any Enhancement Based on Quality of Performance or Results Obtained**

The lodestar method, first articulated in *Johnson v. Georgia Highway Express, Inc.*, 488 F.2d 714 (5th Cir. 1973), and later adopted by the Supreme Court in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), begins with a calculation of the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate. *Johnson*, 488 F.2d at 717–19. The lodestar is then presumed to be the reasonable fee. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897. Any enhancement must be justified by reference to the lodestar itself, not by an independent assessment of counsel’s performance or the litigation’s outcome. *Id.* at 899.

This Court’s pre-lodestar precedents confirm that the reasonableness of a fee award is determined by market rates and the nature of the services rendered, not by subjective judgments about the quality of advocacy or the significance of the result. In *Farrar v. Hobby*, 506 U.S. 103 (1992), the Supreme Court reiterated that the lodestar is the starting point and that enhancements are permissible only in rare and exceptional cases. *Id.* at 115. But long before *Farrar*, this Court recognized that the fee must reflect what a reasonable attorney would charge for comparable work in the relevant market. *Johnson*, 488 F.2d at 717–19. The lodestar’s presumptive reasonableness ensures that the fee award does not exceed what the market would bear for competent representation. *Id.*

**II. The District Court’s Enhancement Violates the Longstanding Rule That Fees Must Be Reasonable in Light of Market Rates**

The district court’s enhancement of over $4 million was premised on its subjective assessment that counsel’s performance was “superb” and that the results were “exceptional.” *Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue*, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). This approach directly contravenes the principle that fees must be determined by reference to market rates, not the court’s personal evaluation of counsel’s skill or the litigation’s outcome. *Johnson*, 488 F.2d at 717–19.

The Supreme Court has long held that fee awards under fee-shifting statutes must be reasonable in light of the prevailing market for legal services. In *Alyeska Pipeline Serv. Co. v. Wilderness Soc’y*, 421 U.S. 240 (1975), the Court rejected the idea that courts could award fees in excess of market rates based on subjective judgments about the quality of representation. *Id.* at 263. The lodestar method, as later adopted in *Blum*, was designed to cabin judicial discretion and ensure that fee awards reflect market realities, not judicial assessments of counsel’s performance. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897–99.

**III. The District Court’s Reliance on Its Own Observations of Counsel’s Performance Violates Due Process**

The district court’s enhancement was based in part on its own observations of counsel’s performance throughout the litigation. *Kenny A.*, 454 F.Supp.2d at 1286. This ex parte assessment denied the State of Georgia the opportunity to challenge the court’s subjective impressions or present contrary evidence. Due process requires that fee determinations be based on objective, reviewable evidence, not the court’s personal observations or untested assertions. *See Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254, 268–71 (1970) (requiring fair procedures before deprivation of property).

This Court has long recognized that fee awards must be grounded in the record and subject to meaningful review. *Johnson*, 488 F.2d at 717–19. The district court’s reliance on its own observations—rather than specific, admissible evidence—deprived the State of its right to a fair and impartial adjudication of the fee dispute. *See In re Murchison*, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955) (holding that a judge’s participation in a matter where he has a personal bias violates due process).

**IV. The District Court’s Enhancement Undermines the Statutory Purpose of Fee-Shifting Provisions**

Fee-shifting statutes are designed to encourage competent attorneys to represent plaintiffs who could not otherwise afford counsel. *See, e.g., Newman v. Piggie Park Enters., Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 401–02 (1968) (per curiam). Enhancements based on subjective judgments about performance or results distort this purpose by allowing courts to award fees far in excess of what the market would bear. *Johnson*, 488 F.2d at 717–19.

The Supreme Court has cautioned that fee awards must not exceed what is necessary to attract competent counsel. *Alyeska*, 421 U.S. at 263. The district court’s enhancement—over 40% of the lodestar—was not justified by market rates or the statutory purpose of fee-shifting. Instead, it was a windfall based on the court’s personal assessment of counsel’s performance. *Kenny A.*, 454 F.Supp.2d at 1286.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court’s enhancement of the fee award based on the quality of performance and results obtained was error. The lodestar method requires that fees be determined by reference to market rates, not subjective judicial assessments. *Johnson*, 488 F.2d at 717–19. The district court’s reliance on its own observations of counsel’s performance violated due process, and the enhancement undermined the statutory purpose of fee-shifting provisions. The judgment below should be reversed.
