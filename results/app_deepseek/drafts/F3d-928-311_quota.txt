**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in designating Mr. Furlow an armed career criminal under the ACCA and a career offender under the Guidelines. Neither his Georgia first-degree arson conviction nor his South Carolina crack cocaine distribution conviction qualifies as a predicate offense under the categorical approach.

**I. The Georgia Arson Conviction Is Not a Predicate “Violent Felony” or “Crime of Violence”**

The ACCA defines a “violent felony” as any crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year that “is burglary, arson, or extortion, involves use of explosives, or otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). The Guidelines’ “crime of violence” definition is identical. USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). When a statute lists an enumerated offense like arson, courts must apply the generic, contemporary meaning of that offense. *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 598 (1990). Generic arson requires the intentional burning of a building or structure. *United States v. Vann*, 660 F.3d 771, 776 (4th Cir. 2011) (en banc). It does not include the burning of personal property or non-structural items. *Id.* at 777.

Georgia’s first-degree arson statute, O.C.G.A. § 16-7-60, criminalizes damaging “a dwelling house, building, or other structure” by fire or explosion, but also covers damaging “any property” with the intent to defraud an insurer. This latter prong reaches conduct far beyond generic arson—for example, burning a vehicle or a piece of furniture for insurance fraud. Because the statute is indivisible (it sets out alternative means, not elements), the court must compare the full scope of the statute to the generic definition. *Mathis v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 2243, 2251 (2016). If the statute criminalizes conduct that falls outside generic arson, it cannot serve as a predicate. *Descamps v. United States*, 570 U.S. 254, 261 (2013). The government presented no evidence that Mr. Furlow’s conviction rested on the narrower structural-burning prong. The district court therefore erred in concluding that the Georgia arson conviction qualifies as a violent felony or crime of violence. *See United States v. Spivey*, 958 F.3d 253, 260 (4th Cir. 2020) (holding that a state arson statute covering non-structural property is not a violent felony under the ACCA).

**II. The South Carolina Drug Conviction Is Not a Predicate “Serious Drug Offense” or “Controlled Substance Offense”**

The ACCA defines a “serious drug offense” as a state offense “involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance” for which the maximum term of imprisonment is ten years or more. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” similarly, requiring that the offense prohibit the manufacture, distribution, or possession with intent to distribute “a controlled substance.” USSG § 4B1.2(b). Under the categorical approach, a state drug offense qualifies only if the state’s controlled substances schedule is coextensive with the federal schedules. *United States v. Dozier*, 848 F.3d 180, 184 (4th Cir. 2017). If the state schedule includes substances not listed federally, the state statute is overbroad and cannot serve as a predicate. *United States v. Bynum*, 669 F.3d 880, 886 (4th Cir. 2012).

South Carolina’s drug schedule, S.C. Code Ann. § 44-53-190, includes substances that are not federally controlled, such as certain isomers and analogs. The statute under which Mr. Furlow was convicted—S.C. Code Ann. § 44-53-370—criminalizes distribution of any substance on that broader state schedule. Because the statute is indivisible, it is categorically overbroad. *Mathis*, 136 S. Ct. at 2251. The government bore the burden of proving that the conviction qualified, *United States v. Harcum*, 587 F.3d 677, 682 (4th Cir. 2009), and it failed to show that the substance involved was a federally controlled substance. Moreover, the government did not establish that the offense carried a maximum term of ten years or more, as required for the ACCA. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The district court therefore erred in treating this conviction as a predicate.

**Conclusion**

Because neither the Georgia arson conviction nor the South Carolina drug conviction qualifies as a predicate under the ACCA or the Guidelines, Mr. Furlow lacks the requisite number of prior convictions for either enhancement. This Court should vacate the sentence and remand for resentencing without the armed career criminal and career offender designations.
