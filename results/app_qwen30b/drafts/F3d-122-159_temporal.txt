**ARGUMENT**

I. THE BOP’S INTERPRETATION OF “NONVIOLENT OFFENSE” IN PROGRAM STATEMENT 5162.02 VIOLATES CONGRESSIONAL INTENT AND THE TEXT OF 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) BY EXPANSIVELY CLASSIFYING DRUG CONSPIRACY CONVICTIONS AS “CRIMES OF VIOLENCE” BASED ON A SENTENCING ENHANCEMENT THAT WAS NOT A FACTOR IN THE OFFENSE OF CONVICTION.

The core issue before this Court is whether the Bureau of Prisons (BOP) may, under the guise of administrative regulation, reclassify a drug conspiracy conviction as a “crime of violence” for purposes of early release eligibility—solely because the sentencing court applied a two-level enhancement under the United States Sentencing Guidelines for possession of a firearm during the offense, despite the fact that the firearm was discovered after the offense and played no role in the arrest or conviction. The answer is no. The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02, which deems such a conviction a “crime of violence” under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), is inconsistent with the plain language of the statute and violates the principle that administrative agencies may not expand statutory definitions beyond their text.

Congress enacted 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) to encourage rehabilitation by allowing early release for inmates who successfully complete drug treatment programs, but only if their offense is “nonviolent.” The statute does not define “nonviolent offense,” but it does define “crime of violence” in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), which provides that such an offense must either (A) have as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force, or (B) by its nature involve a substantial risk that physical force may be used. The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02, however, creates a new category: a drug conspiracy conviction becomes a “crime of violence” if the sentencing court enhanced the sentence for possession of a firearm during the offense—regardless of whether the weapon was used, possessed in connection with the offense, or even known to the defendant at the time of the offense.

This construction is impermissible. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), the Supreme Court held that “the meaning of a statute is to be gathered from the language used, and not from the supposed purpose of the legislature.” Here, Congress did not define “nonviolent offense” in terms of sentencing enhancements, nor did it authorize the BOP to use post-conviction judicial findings—such as a sentencing enhancement based on a separate, unrelated discovery of a firearm—to reclassify the offense as violent. The enhancement in this case was not based on any conduct that formed the basis of the conviction. The firearm was found in Roussos’ vacation home after the offense was complete and was not used, possessed in furtherance of the conspiracy, or even known to the government at the time of arrest. The enhancement was applied solely to increase the sentence under the Guidelines, not to establish a new element of the offense.

The BOP’s reliance on the enhancement to trigger the “crime of violence” designation is thus a legislative act, not an administrative one. As this Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), “an administrative agency may not, by regulation, expand the scope of a statute beyond its plain meaning.” The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 does precisely that. It transforms a nonviolent drug conspiracy into a “crime of violence” based on a fact—possession of a firearm during the offense—that was not an element of the offense, was not proven at trial, and was not even part of the conduct that led to the conviction. This is not interpretation; it is redefinition.

Moreover, the BOP’s rule contradicts the very purpose of the early release provision. Congress intended to reward rehabilitation, not punish inmates for circumstances beyond their control. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), the Court emphasized that “the construction of a statute must be such as to give effect to its purpose.” The purpose of § 3621(e)(2)(B) is to incentivize drug treatment and reduce recidivism. To deny early release to an inmate who has completed a 500-hour treatment program—based solely on a sentencing enhancement that did not affect the nature of the offense—undermines that purpose. It is a punishment for a fact that was not part of the offense and that the defendant did not even know about at the time of conviction.

The BOP’s argument that the enhancement “indicates a substantial risk of force” is also flawed. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, the Court rejected the idea that a mere possibility of future harm could justify a broad interpretation of a criminal statute. The risk of force must be inherent in the offense itself, not inferred from a post-hoc sentencing decision. The possession of a firearm in a drug conspiracy is not inherently violent; it is a separate, often collateral, act. The mere fact that a court may enhance a sentence for such possession does not mean the offense itself is violent.

Therefore, the BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02, by equating a sentencing enhancement with a “crime of violence,” exceeds its statutory authority and violates the principle of non-delegation. As the Court held in *United States v. Morgan*, 307 U.S. 183 (1939), “an administrative agency cannot be permitted to exercise legislative power.” The BOP has done so here.

For these reasons, the BOP’s denial of early release to Victor M. Roussos is unlawful. The conviction under 21 U.S.C. § 846 was not a “crime of violence” under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), and the BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 is invalid to the extent it relies on a sentencing enhancement to reclassify such offenses. The district court erred in upholding the BOP’s decision. Relief must be granted.
