# ARGUMENT

## I. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Establish That Nieves-Castaño Knew the Rifle Had the Characteristics of a Machine Gun

This Court reviews sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenges by asking "whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979).

Section 922(o) is not a strict-liability offense. In *Staples v. United States*, the Supreme Court held that the government "must prove that the defendant knew the weapon he possessed had the characteristics that brought it within the scope of the Act"—namely, its capacity to fire automatically. 511 U.S. 600, 619 (1994). This mens rea requirement flows from the longstanding presumption that Congress does not intend to criminalize otherwise innocent conduct absent a showing of guilty knowledge. *See Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (crime ordinarily requires "concurrence of an evil-meaning mind with an evil-doing hand"); *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994) (courts presume a scienter requirement even absent express statutory language, particularly where severe penalties attach to otherwise innocent conduct).

The government's proof here established only that Nieves-Castaño once looked into the golf bag, saw "there was a rifle in it," and "knew it was an AK-47." Nothing in the record shows she knew, or had reason to know, that the particular rifle was capable of automatic fire rather than semi-automatic operation—a distinction *Staples* makes dispositive, since civilian AK-47-pattern rifles are commonly manufactured and sold as semiautomatic weapons. No witness testified to a selector switch, markings, or any other feature observed by Nieves-Castaño indicating automatic capability. Absent such proof, no rational jury could find beyond a reasonable doubt that she possessed the knowledge *Staples* requires. 511 U.S. at 619. The § 922(o) conviction must be reversed.

## II. Section 922(q)(2)(A) Is Unconstitutionally Vague Because It Provides No Ascertainable Method for Measuring the 1,000-Foot School Zone

A penal statute violates due process if it fails to "define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited" and fails to establish "minimal guidelines to govern law enforcement." *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357–58 (1983). As the Supreme Court explained in *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, "a vague law impermissibly delegates basic policy matters to policemen, judges, and juries for resolution on an ad hoc and subjective basis, with the attendant dangers of arbitrary and discriminatory application." 408 U.S. 104, 108–09 (1972). Criminal statutes must give "fair warning . . . in language that the common world will understand." *United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259, 265 (1997) (quoting *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931)).

Section 922(q)(2)(A) prohibits possession of a firearm "at a place that the individual knows is a school zone," defined as within "a distance of 1,000 feet from the grounds of a public, parochial or private school." 18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A), (q)(2)(B). The statute is silent on how that distance is to be measured—whether as a straight line, by pedestrian route, or by some other metric. This is not a trivial omission: the choice of methodology can determine whether a given residence, such as Building 9 of the housing project, falls inside or outside the zone. Where the statute itself supplies no method, juries are left to select among competing measures without any legislative or judicial standard, precisely the "ad hoc and subjective" decision-making *Grayned* forbids. 408 U.S. at 108–09.

Some courts have addressed this gap by judicially adopting a straight-line measure and rejecting vagueness challenges on that basis. *See United States v. Roberts*, 62 F.3d 495, 499 (2d Cir. 1995). But that judicial gloss cannot cure the statute's facial vagueness for a defendant who received no instruction employing that methodology, or any methodology at all. This Court has itself recognized, in construing the analogous "schoolyard" enhancement, that the method used to measure distance to school property materially affects the fairness of the statute's application. *See United States v. Soto*, 8 F.3d 15, 19 (1st Cir. 1993). Unlike § 860, however, § 922(q)(2)(A) contains no textual or regulatory guidepost whatsoever directing how the 1,000 feet is to be calculated. The jury here was left not merely to resolve a factual dispute, but to divine the governing legal standard itself—an impermissible delegation of "basic policy matters" condemned in *Kolender*, 461 U.S. at 358, and *Grayned*, 408 U.S. at 108–09.

Because § 922(q)(2)(A) fails to give citizens or enforcing officials "fair warning . . . in language that the common world will understand," *Lanier*, 520 U.S. at 265, the statute as applied—and as written—is unconstitutionally vague. The Count Two conviction must be vacated.

## III. Alternatively, the Government Failed to Prove the Distance Element Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

Even crediting the government's chosen measurement approach, the record contains no reliable evidence, subject to rational-juror scrutiny under *Jackson*, 443 U.S. at 319, establishing that Building 9 lay within 1,000 feet of a qualifying school under any defined method. The conviction cannot stand on such an evidentiary void.

## CONCLUSION

This Court should reverse the § 922(o) conviction for insufficient evidence of mens rea under *Staples*, and vacate the § 922(q)(2)(A) conviction as void for vagueness or, alternatively, for insufficient evidence.
