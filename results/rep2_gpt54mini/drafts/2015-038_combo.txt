ANILCA does not strip the National Park Service of all regulatory authority over Alaska lands and waters that happen to lie within park boundaries. The statute’s text, structure, and history show a narrower rule: Congress preserved certain state, Native, corporate, and private lands from being transformed into “public lands” for purposes of Alaska-specific subsistence management, but it did not create a regulatory vacuum inside National Park System units. See Alaska v. United States, 545 U.S. 75, 125 S. Ct. 2137, 162 L. Ed. 2d 57 (2005). The better reading is that ANILCA § 103(c) limits land status, not all federal regulation, and certainly not regulations that govern conduct within the external boundaries of a park unit. See 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c).

The starting point is statutory text. Congress often uses geographic boundaries to identify the reach of federal authority without converting every parcel inside those boundaries into a uniform regulatory regime. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470 (1917); United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76 (1820). Here, ANILCA distinguishes between “public lands” and lands “conveyed” or “selected” under Alaska land laws, and it separately governs “conservation system units” by placing special limits on how federal agencies may treat nonfederal inholdings. 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). But nothing in the statute states that the National Park Service loses all authority to apply park-wide use restrictions to conduct occurring within unit boundaries. When Congress intends to divest the Executive of otherwise applicable regulatory power, it says so clearly. See Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935); Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935).

This Court should also construe ANILCA in harmony with the longstanding principle that federal authority over public lands is broad absent a clear congressional withdrawal. See United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506 (1911); Light v. United States, 220 U.S. 523 (1911). The National Park Service has long exercised delegated authority to promulgate and enforce regulations protecting park resources and visitor uses. Regulations addressing hovercraft use fall comfortably within that traditional police-power-like federal management of federal reservations. Grimaud, 220 U.S. at 516-17; Light, 220 U.S. at 535-36. ANILCA did not abrogate that settled authority by implication. Repeals by implication are not favored. See Posadas v. National City Bank, 296 U.S. 497 (1936); Wood v. United States, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 342 (1842).

Nor does the fact that the Nation River may be state-owned or navigable alter the result. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that navigation and surface use on waters traversing federal lands may remain subject to federal regulation where Congress has so provided. See United States v. Arizona, 295 U.S. 174 (1935); United States v. California, 332 U.S. 19 (1947). Alaska’s sovereignty interests cannot displace a federal statute and valid federal regulations within the boundaries of a national preserve. See McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819). The Constitution forbids state action that frustrates valid federal law. Id. at 436.

Sturgeon’s contrary reading would also upset the evident purpose of ANILCA. Statutes should be read so that each part has effect and the whole is coherent. See The Emily and The Caroline, 9 U.S. (5 Cranch) 381 (1815); Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457 (1892). Congress created Alaska conservation units to conserve extraordinary lands and waters while accommodating local uses. It did not enact a blanket exemption from federal regulation for every state or private parcel inside park boundaries. That would create patchwork enforcement and frustrate uniform park management. See Alaska Pacific Fisheries v. United States, 248 U.S. 78 (1918).

Finally, even if ambiguity remained, the statute should be construed to avoid defeating the very conservation purposes Congress enacted ANILCA to advance. See United States v. Union Pacific R.R. Co., 91 U.S. 72 (1875); United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc., 272 U.S. 1 (1926). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 103(c) is not a talisman wiping out NPS regulations within Alaska park units. Its reading preserves both the statutory text and Congress’s design. The judgment should be affirmed.
