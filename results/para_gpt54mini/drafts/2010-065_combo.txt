**Argument**

Bond lacks standing to mount a Tenth Amendment attack on her conviction. The Constitution’s federal judicial power is limited to “Cases” and “Controversies,” and a litigant must show a personal legal injury before invoking the courts. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife* postdates the cutoff, but the underlying principle is longstanding: one may not litigate generalized constitutional objections divorced from a concrete personal right. See *Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 488-89 (1923). A private criminal defendant prosecuted under a federal statute may challenge the statute’s validity only to the extent that the alleged defect invades her own protected interests. Here, Bond asserts only that Congress exceeded an enumerated power and intruded on state sovereignty. That is not a personal right of hers.

This Court and the Supreme Court have repeatedly held that the Tenth Amendment is a structural limitation designed to preserve the federal-state balance, not a personal liberty provision enforceable by private parties in the abstract. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939), the Court held that private utilities, “absent the states or their officers,” had “no standing” to raise a Tenth Amendment objection. That controlling statement squarely forecloses Bond’s claim. The same principle appears in *Frothingham*, where the Court rejected taxpayer standing to complain that federal spending invaded state prerogatives. 262 U.S. at 487-89. And in *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 485-86 (1923), the Court explained that a plaintiff cannot “be heard to complain” of an alleged constitutional injury that is public in nature and not personally inflicted.

Nor does Bond escape this rule by pointing to her status as a criminal defendant. The Court has long distinguished between a defendant’s right to insist that the government prove the statute she violated is valid, and a generalized power to invoke federalism on behalf of the States. In *United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1, 68 (1936), the Court described the Tenth Amendment as confirming that powers not delegated are reserved to the States or the people, but it did not suggest private enforcement of the reservation apart from a concrete personal injury. Likewise, in *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941), the Court held that the Tenth Amendment is “but a truism,” reinforcing that it does not itself create an individualized cause of action. These decisions confirm that Bond’s objection is structural, not personal.

Bond’s reliance on the alleged “federalism” limits of Congress’s enumerated powers does not change the standing analysis. The Court has recognized that not every constitutional limitation is privately enforceable by anyone subject to a statute. In *Fairchild v. Hughes*, 258 U.S. 126, 129-30 (1922), the Court denied standing where the plaintiff asserted only a public grievance concerning constitutional compliance. Similarly, *Bailey v. Patterson*, 369 U.S. 31, 32-33 (1962) (per curiam), held that litigants may not obtain review of abstract constitutional claims when no personal right is infringed beyond that shared by the public at large. Bond’s complaint that Congress should have left this conduct to state prosecutors is exactly that kind of generalized grievance.

The better reading of *Tennessee Electric* is reinforced by cases recognizing that federal courts do not adjudicate the rights of absent sovereigns at the behest of private parties. In *Georgia v. Evans*, 316 U.S. 159, 162 (1942), the Court allowed a State, as a direct sovereign and market participant, to invoke federal law; by contrast, private parties may not invoke sovereign interests they do not hold. Likewise, *Colegrove v. Green*, 328 U.S. 549, 552-56 (1946), rejected judicial resolution of disputes that were essentially political in nature, underscoring that federal courts do not become forums for broad structural objections untethered to individualized injury. Bond asks for precisely that kind of advisory judgment.

Finally, Bond cannot manufacture standing by characterizing her challenge as one to the “constitutionality” of the statute. The question is not whether § 229 may be validly enacted, but who may object that it allegedly offends federalism. Under *Tennessee Electric*, only the States or their officers may press that claim. 306 U.S. at 144. Because no State has challenged this prosecution, Bond lacks standing to assert the Tenth Amendment on the State’s behalf. See also *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. at 485-86; *Frothingham*, 262 U.S. at 488-89.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be affirmed on the ground that Bond lacks standing to pursue her Tenth Amendment challenge.
