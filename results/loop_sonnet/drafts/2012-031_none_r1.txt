**ARGUMENT**

**A district court may not certify a class under Rule 23(b)(3) without admissible common evidence establishing that damages are capable of measurement on a class-wide basis tied to the theory of liability actually certified.**

**I. Predominance under Rule 23(b)(3) requires proof, not assumption, that class-wide damages can be established through evidence common to the class.**

Class certification is not a procedural formality; it determines whether absent individuals will be bound by a judgment they never personally litigated. Because absent class members are bound without their day in court, due process requires that their interests be genuinely aligned with the class representatives on every element of the claim, including damages. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32 (1940). That guarantee traces to the earliest representative suits in equity, which permitted one party to sue on behalf of a class only where the class shared a truly common right or interest in the controversy. *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288 (1853). A class certified on a damages theory that cannot in fact be proved with evidence common to the class fails this basic requirement: it smuggles individualized inquiry into what is supposed to be a unitary proceeding.

This Court has long distinguished between proof of the *fact* of damage and proof of the *amount* of damage. While the amount of antitrust injury may be estimated with some imprecision, the fact that the alleged wrong caused a discernible, common injury must rest on a sound evidentiary foundation, not speculation. *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555 (1931); *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359 (1927). Only after that threshold showing is made may a factfinder "make a just and reasonable estimate of the damage based on relevant data." *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946). The relaxed standard for calculating the *amount* of damages does not relieve plaintiffs of the burden to connect the alleged violation to the claimed injury in the first instance. *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100 (1969). And while a court evaluating an antitrust plaintiff's proof must consider the evidence as an integrated whole rather than crediting isolated fragments in isolation, *Continental Ore Co. v. Union Carbide & Carbon Corp.*, 370 U.S. 690 (1962), that principle presupposes that the evidence, taken together, actually supports the theory of liability the plaintiffs pressed — not a broader or different theory that happens to be convenient at the certification stage.

**II. The damages model approved below cannot satisfy predominance because it measures injury from theories of liability that were never certified.**

The courts below inverted these principles. The District Court's certification order narrowed the class's theory of antitrust impact to a single theory — that Comcast's clustering deterred entry by overbuilders. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154, 191 (E.D. Pa. 2010). Yet the damages model offered by Plaintiffs' expert, Dr. McClave, was built to quantify harm flowing from four distinct theories of anticompetitive conduct, three of which were rejected as bases for class-wide proof. No admissible evidence isolated what portion, if any, of the model's aggregate damages figure was attributable to the single surviving overbuilder-deterrence theory as opposed to the discarded theories.

That gap is fatal under this Court's precedents. *Story Parchment* and *Eastman Kodak* require that the fact of common injury — not merely some undifferentiated damages number — be established by competent proof before any estimate of amount is permitted. A model that may capture *some* injury, filtered through theories the class was never permitted to pursue, does not establish that the injury common to the class is the injury the class actually alleged. It is not "relevant data" supporting "a just and reasonable estimate," *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264; it is speculation dressed in the language of regression analysis — precisely what *Zenith Radio* and *Continental Ore* forbid when they require that proof of injury be causally tethered to the certified theory of liability.

**III. Even under a deferential standard of review, an evidentiary gap of this magnitude constitutes reversible error.**

The Court of Appeals compounded the error by reviewing the District Court's acceptance of the damages model under an unduly deferential lens without first confirming that the model was even capable, as a matter of law, of proving what Rule 23(b)(3) requires. But deference to factual findings has never extended to findings that lack any genuine evidentiary mooring. A finding is clearly erroneous when the reviewing court is "left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). A predominance finding resting on a damages methodology that cannot be disaggregated to match the certified theory of liability is exactly such a mistake: the record contains no admissible basis to conclude that common evidence — as opposed to speculation imported from rejected theories — can measure the injury the class is actually permitted to prove.

**CONCLUSION**

Rule 23(b)(3) demands proof, not assumption, that damages can be established on a class-wide basis consistent with the class's own certified theory of liability. *Story Parchment*, *Eastman Kodak*, *Bigelow*, *Zenith Radio*, *Continental Ore*, *Hansberry*, and *Smith v. Swormstedt* together establish that antitrust damages, however imprecise in amount, must be causally and evidentially connected to the specific wrong the class is certified to litigate. That connection was never established here, and the finding to the contrary was clearly erroneous under *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.* The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
