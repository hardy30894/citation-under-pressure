ARGUMENT

A conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his words as a threat. Due process forbids criminal liability for speech absent such intent. See Cole v. Arkansas, 333 U.S. 196, 201 (1948) (“conviction upon a charge not made would be sheer denial of due process”); Rogers v. Richmond, 365 U.S. 534, 544 (1961) (due process demands that “the state establish the guilt of an accused” by proof meeting constitutional standards).

The objective standard applied below permits conviction whenever a reasonable person might perceive a threat, regardless of the speaker’s actual purpose. That rule cannot stand. In Lee v. Mississippi, 332 U.S. 742, 745 (1948), this Court held that a conviction resting on an involuntary confession violates due process because “the confession was the product of coercion.” The same principle governs here: speech is not a threat unless the speaker intends it to instill fear. See Waley v. Johnston, 316 U.S. 101, 104 (1942) (per curiam) (habeas relief required where plea was induced by threats that overcame the defendant’s will).

Precedent confirms that the speaker’s subjective state controls. Brown v. Pierce, 74 U.S. 205, 214 (1869), recognized that duress per minas exists only when the threatened party’s will is actually overcome. Sexton v. California, 189 U.S. 319, 324 (1903), likewise treated the extortionate threat as criminal only when the speaker acted with the purpose of inducing fear. Iron Mountain & Helena Railroad v. Johnson, 119 U.S. 608, 611 (1887), emphasized that the law punishes the use of force or terror precisely because the actor intends to disturb the peace by his own hand.

Equity reinforces the point. Dombrowski v. Pfister, 380 U.S. 479, 486 (1965), permitted federal intervention to prevent prosecutions that chill protected expression. Hygrade Provision Co. v. Sherman, 266 U.S. 497, 503 (1925), acknowledged that threatened enforcement of a statute may itself inflict irreparable injury when the statute sweeps too broadly. An objective-threat instruction produces precisely that injury by criminalizing speech the speaker never meant to threaten.

Because the jury was instructed only on objective foreseeability, the convictions rest on an impermissible standard. The judgment should be reversed.
