The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates the civil and criminal laws of adjacent States only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law. This assimilation is limited to circumstances in which federal law leaves a gap. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (federal law on the Shelf is “exclusive” except where state law is needed “to fill a significant void”); United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286, 294 (1958) (assimilation statutes operate only when Congress has not “provided a complete body of law”).

The same rule governs federal enclaves. When the United States acquires land with a State’s consent, “only the state laws in effect at the time of the transfer of jurisdiction continue in force” as surrogate federal law. Pacific Coast Dairy, Inc. v. Dep’t of Agric., 318 U.S. 285, 294 (1943); see also James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94, 100 (1940) (“subsequent statutes of the State are not part of the body of laws in the ceded area”); Murray v. Joe Gerrick & Co., 291 U.S. 315, 318 (1934) (state workmen’s-compensation statute enacted after cession “has no application”); Arlington Hotel Co. v. Fant, 278 U.S. 439, 454 (1929) (later state legislation “cannot be extended” over federal land); Chicago, R.I. & P. Ry. v. McGlinn, 114 U.S. 542, 546 (1885) (“the law in force at the time of the transfer remains in force” until displaced by Congress).

Howard v. Commissioners of Sinking Fund, 344 U.S. 624, 627 (1953), confirms that state law applies within an enclave solely “so far as the same is not inconsistent with” federal statutes. Because the Fair Labor Standards Act supplies a comprehensive scheme of wage-and-hour regulation, California’s later-enacted meal-period and overtime rules are not assimilated. No gap exists for state law to fill.

The text and structure of § 1333(a)(2)(A) mirror these enclave precedents. State law therefore governs on the Shelf only when federal law is silent.
