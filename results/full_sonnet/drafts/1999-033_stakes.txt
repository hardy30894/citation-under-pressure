# ARGUMENT

## I. The Equal Protection Clause Guarantees Equal Treatment Among Similarly Situated Persons or Classes, Not Freedom from Individualized Government Decisions.

The Equal Protection Clause was designed to prevent government from treating groups of citizens differently based on impermissible criteria. As this Court explained in *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the Clause "does not take from the states the power to classify in the adoption of police laws," and a mere allegation of an erroneous or even arbitrary administrative act, without more, does not amount to a denial of equal protection. *Id.* at 8. The Clause instead protects against "intentional and arbitrary discrimination" between persons or classes similarly circumstanced. *Id.* at 8–9. Respondent Olech does not allege that Willowbrook singled her out because of her membership in any race, class, or group, or because of the exercise of any fundamental right. She alleges only that the Village treated *her*, an individual, differently from other applicants for a water hookup. That is a garden-variety land-use dispute, not a constitutional injury.

This Court's equal protection cases have consistently analyzed claims of unequal treatment through the lens of classification—differential treatment of defined groups—not idiosyncratic, one-off decisions affecting a single citizen. In *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256 (1979), the Court reiterated that equal protection analysis requires identifying "whether the challenged classification is the product of a purpose to discriminate against" a cognizable group. *Id.* at 272. Likewise, in *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), the Court's equal protection inquiry turned on discriminatory purpose directed at a class of persons defined by a forbidden criterion. *Id.* at 239–42. Extending the Clause to cover every citizen's belief that she, individually, was treated worse than her neighbor untethers equal protection doctrine from its textual and historical anchor in group-based discrimination.

## II. Recognizing a "Class of One" Cause of Action Would Convert Every Local Land-Use and Licensing Decision into a Federal Constitutional Case.

Rational-basis review exists precisely so that federal courts do not sit as a "super" zoning board or licensing commission second-guessing routine municipal decisions. *See New Orleans v. Dukes*, 427 U.S. 297, 303 (1976) (states are afforded "wide latitude" in classification, and legislative choices are not subject to courtroom fact-finding). This Court has repeatedly cautioned that rational-basis scrutiny is highly deferential precisely because it must accommodate the practical reality that government routinely draws lines between similarly situated applicants—granting one variance and denying another, requiring one developer a wider easement and another a narrower one—for reasons of administrative judgment that need not, and often cannot, be reduced to a formula. *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993) ("a classification neither involving fundamental rights nor proceeding along suspect lines is accorded a strong presumption of validity"). Under *Beach Communications*, the government "has no obligation to produce evidence to sustain the rationality of a statutory classification," and a classification survives "if there is any reasonably conceivable state of facts that could provide a rational basis." *Id.* at 315.

The Seventh Circuit's "class of one" theory inverts this presumption. By allowing any individual property owner to sue merely by alleging that a municipal employee treated her differently than some other applicant, the decision below invites federal litigation over every discretionary line-drawing decision a municipality makes—easement widths, permit conditions, inspection schedules—so long as a plaintiff can find one similarly situated comparator treated more favorably. That result cannot be squared with *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), where the Court upheld an ordinance drawing distinctions between types of advertising vehicles, explaining that "it is no requirement of equal protection that all evils of the same genus be eradicated or none at all." *Id.* at 110.

## III. The Seventh Circuit's "Spite" Standard Is Unmoored from Rational-Basis Doctrine and Cannot Salvage the Claim.

Even accepting, arguendo, some narrow class-of-one theory, the Seventh Circuit's formulation—permitting suit whenever government action is "motivated solely by a spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective"—improperly imports a subjective motive inquiry into what has always been an objective rationality test. Rational-basis review under this Court's precedents asks whether *any* conceivable legitimate purpose supports the classification, not whether the decisionmaker's subjective state of mind was benign. *Beach Communications*, 508 U.S. at 315. A motive-based "spite" test would require federal courts to adjudicate the personal animus of local officials in every property dispute, a task for which state tort and administrative law—not the Fourteenth Amendment—are the proper vehicles. Nothing in this Court's equal protection jurisprudence, from *Snowden* through *Feeney*, contemplates transforming isolated administrative friction into a federal constitutional tort.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be reversed.
