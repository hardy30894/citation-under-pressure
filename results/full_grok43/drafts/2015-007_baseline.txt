ARGUMENT

I. Florida’s capital sentencing scheme satisfies the Sixth Amendment.

Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find any fact that increases the maximum punishment to death. Id. at 609. Florida’s statute, however, channels the jury’s role through an advisory recommendation that identifies the existence of aggravating circumstances. See § 921.141, Fla. Stat. Because the jury’s recommendation rests on its determination that at least one statutory aggravator has been proved, the scheme complies with Ring. See id. at 597-98 (distinguishing schemes in which the jury’s finding is “necessary” to impose death).

The Court has never required that the jury’s finding be unanimous. In Apodaca v. Oregon, 406 U.S. 404 (1972), a plurality upheld non-unanimous verdicts in state criminal trials, and that holding remains undisturbed in the capital context. See also McDonald v. City of Chicago, 561 U.S. 742, 766 n.14 (2010) (reaffirming Apodaca). Florida’s requirement of a majority recommendation therefore satisfies the Sixth Amendment.

II. The Eighth Amendment does not mandate jury unanimity or jury resolution of mental-retardation claims.

Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), left “to the States the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction” on executing intellectually disabled offenders. Id. at 317 (quoting Ford v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399, 416-17 (1986)). Florida’s three-prong statutory test, administered by the trial judge with clear-and-convincing-evidence burden, is one such procedure. See § 921.137, Fla. Stat. Nothing in Atkins or subsequent decisions requires that the jury make the ultimate determination.

The same principle governs sentencing procedures. The Eighth Amendment demands “heightened reliability,” Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), but has never imposed a unanimity requirement. States remain free to allocate fact-finding between judge and jury so long as the ultimate sentence rests on constitutionally sufficient aggravating circumstances. See Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 195 (1976) (plurality opinion). Florida’s scheme meets that standard.

III. Hurst’s sentence rests on valid jury findings.

The jury’s 7–5 recommendation necessarily determined that the two proven aggravators—heinous, atrocious, or cruel murder and murder during a robbery—existed beyond a reasonable doubt. The trial judge’s independent findings merely confirmed that determination. Because the jury performed the fact-finding function Ring requires, no constitutional violation occurred.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
