# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEXT AND STRUCTURE OF OCSLA REQUIRE A GAP-FILLING ANALYSIS BEFORE STATE LAW MAY BE BORROWED AS FEDERAL LAW

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act declares that "[t]he Constitution and laws and civil and political jurisdiction of the United States are extended to" the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and to fixed structures thereon "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). This is the operative jurisdictional command. Only after establishing exclusive federal jurisdiction does the Act provide, as a secondary and subordinate mechanism, that state law may be borrowed "[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws." *Id.* § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Ninth Circuit's holding—that state law applies whenever it is not inconsistent with federal law, without regard to whether federal law already occupies the field—inverts this structure and converts a scheme of exclusive federal jurisdiction into one of concurrent state and federal regulation.

This Court's decision in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), confirms that OCSLA's borrowing provision operates as a gap-filler, not an independent source of concurrent regulatory authority. The Court explained that Congress's purpose was "to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf," *id.* at 355, precisely because federal law standing alone was "never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself." 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953) (quoted in Pet. App.). Congress did not intend wholesale importation of state regulatory regimes; it intended state law to supply content only where federal law left an area unaddressed. This is confirmed by *Rodrigue*'s holding that state wrongful-death law could be borrowed as federal law only because the federal Death on the High Seas Act did not reach fixed platform accidents at all—there was no federal remedy whatsoever, and hence no obstacle to borrowing state law "by incorporation as federal law through § 1333(a)(2)." *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 366.

The Fifth Circuit, which has the longest and most extensive experience applying OCSLA's choice-of-law provision, has consistently required a threshold showing that federal law does not already address the relevant subject matter. In *Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners' Mutual Insurance Ass'n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law may be adopted as surrogate federal law only where there is a "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" in federal coverage. *Id.* at 1036. That gap-filling framework respects the primacy of federal law on the Shelf while permitting resort to state law where Congress has not spoken. The Ninth Circuit's rejection of this approach as a "prerequisite," Pet. App. 4a, cannot be reconciled with either the statutory text's subordination clause—state law applies only "[t]o the extent" gaps exist, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A)—or with *Rodrigue*'s own analytic sequence, which first asked whether federal admiralty law covered the claim before turning to state law. 395 U.S. at 360, 366.

## II. THE FLSA IS A COMPREHENSIVE FEDERAL SCHEME THAT LEAVES NO GAP FOR CALIFORNIA'S WAGE AND HOUR LAWS TO FILL

The Fair Labor Standards Act comprehensively regulates minimum wages, overtime, and recordkeeping for covered employees, including offshore platform workers like Respondent. 29 U.S.C. §§ 206–207. Congress addressed the precise subject matter at issue—compensation for time spent on duty, including standby and waiting time—through the FLSA and the Department of Labor regulations implementing it. Where Congress has enacted a comprehensive scheme addressing a subject, there is no "void or gap" for state law to fill under the *Continental Oil* framework. 417 F.2d at 1036.

That the FLSA contains a savings clause permitting more generous state wage floors, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), does not itself create a "gap" under OCSLA. A savings clause operates within ordinary preemption analysis to permit state law to operate concurrently in the several States; it says nothing about whether OCSLA's distinct gap-filling mechanism authorizes wholesale borrowing of state regulatory schemes onto the Shelf. The savings clause reflects Congress's judgment about the relationship between federal and state law *within the States*—a judgment made against the backdrop of ordinary federalism, not against OCSLA's extension of exclusive federal jurisdiction to a discrete federal enclave. Treating the savings clause as opening a gap under OCSLA would mean that any FLSA savings clause automatically imports the entire body of state wage and hour law onto the Shelf, effectively nullifying the exclusivity command of § 1333(a)(1).

The district court correctly recognized this distinction, holding that, notwithstanding the savings clause, California's law offered Respondent no additional protection because the FLSA occupies the field of the wage and hour claims at issue. Pet. App. 6a–7a. The Ninth Circuit's contrary holding, permitting state law whenever merely "not inconsistent" with federal law, sweeps far more broadly than OCSLA's text or this Court's precedent permits.

## III. THE PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCES OF THE NINTH CIRCUIT'S RULE CONFIRM ITS ERROR

The Ninth Circuit's "applicable and not inconsistent" test, untethered from any gap requirement, would subject platform operators to the patchwork wage and hour regimes of whichever State lies adjacent to their operations, notwithstanding Congress's enactment of a uniform federal wage and hour scheme in the FLSA. This result directly undermines OCSLA's core purpose of establishing "a body of law" for the Shelf, *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355, rather than fifty potentially divergent bodies of law depending on adjacency. This Court should reverse and hold that state law applies under OCSLA only when federal law leaves a significant gap that state law is necessary to fill—a gap that does not exist here given the FLSA's comprehensive regulation of the subject matter of Respondent's claims.
