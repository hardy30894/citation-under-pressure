**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code exempts “retirement funds” from a debtor’s estate. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The question presented is whether an inherited individual retirement account—an “inherited IRA”—qualifies as such funds. It does not. An inherited IRA is not held for the debtor’s retirement; it is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle that must be distributed to the debtor immediately, regardless of age. The plain meaning of “retirement funds” requires that the funds be reserved for use after the owner’s cessation of work. Because an inherited IRA lacks that essential characteristic, it is not exempt.

**I. The plain language of § 522 exempts only funds that are, in the debtor’s hands, truly “retirement funds.”**

Statutory interpretation begins with the language of the statute. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940). The term “retirement” has a well-settled ordinary meaning: the period when an individual stops working. *See Railroad Retirement Board v. Alton Railroad Co.*, 295 U.S. 330, 361 (1935) (defining “retirement” as “the status of an employee who has been removed from service”). “Retirement funds” are therefore sums set aside for that period. An inherited IRA, by contrast, is not designed to fund the debtor’s retirement. Under the Internal Revenue Code, the beneficiary must begin withdrawing money within one year of the original owner’s death and may be required to complete distribution within five years. 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B); § 402(c)(11)(A). The debtor cannot postpone withdrawals until her own retirement. The funds are thus available for current consumption, not savings for later years. The plain meaning of the statute cannot be stretched to cover assets that are not held for the debtor’s own retirement.

**II. The structure of the Bankruptcy Code confirms that exemptions depend on the debtor’s use of the property, not on the property’s history.**

The Bankruptcy Code’s other exemptions tie eligibility to the debtor’s personal relationship with the asset. For example, the homestead exemption protects only a “domicile” in which the debtor lived for at least 730 days before filing. § 522(b)(3)(A). A home inherited from a parent and rented out is not exempt simply because it once was the parent’s home. *In re Boutin*, 150 F. 299, 300 (D. Mont. 1907) (homestead exemption requires “actual occupancy” by the debtor). Similarly, the exemption for tools of the trade protects only implements the debtor uses in her own occupation. *In re Slater*, 11 F. 46, 47 (D. Mass. 1882) (tools must be “used in the debtor’s trade”). These exemptions are personal privileges, not hereditary entitlements. *In re Zell*, 107 F. 563, 564 (D. Ill. 1901) (exemptions are “personal to the debtor”). The same principle governs retirement funds. The statute asks whether the funds are for *the debtor’s* retirement, not whether they were once for someone else’s. An inherited IRA was the decedent’s retirement fund; after her death, it is no one’s retirement fund. It is merely a tax-deferred account that must be drained before the debtor reaches retirement age. To hold otherwise would transform the exemption into a blanket inheritance shield, a result Congress did not enact.

**III. The inherited IRA lacks the fundamental attributes of a retirement savings vehicle.**

A genuine retirement fund is defined by restrictions that ensure it is not dissipated before retirement: no early withdrawals without penalty, limits on contributions, and required minimum distributions only after age 70½. An inherited IRA has none of these features. It cannot receive new contributions; it cannot be rolled over into the debtor’s own retirement account; and it must begin distributing assets immediately. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). These characteristics make it a vehicle for short-term income deferral, not long-term retirement savings. The tax code itself distinguishes between an owner’s IRA and an inherited IRA, treating the latter as a distinct category. That distinction is dispositive for bankruptcy purposes. The exemption for “retirement funds” cannot be read to include assets that the debtor is forced to consume before retirement.

**Conclusion**

The plain language, statutory structure, and economic reality all compel the same conclusion: an inherited IRA is not exempt from the bankruptcy estate. The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
