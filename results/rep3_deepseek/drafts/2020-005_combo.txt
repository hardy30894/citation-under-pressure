**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court’s Exercise of Specific Jurisdiction Violates the Due Process Clause Because Ford’s Contacts with Montana Did Not Cause the Plaintiffs’ Claims.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum contacts. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945). This requirement ensures that jurisdiction is not “so unreasonable as to violate due process.” *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 251 (1958). Here, the plaintiffs’ claims arise from a tire tread separation in a vehicle designed and manufactured outside Montana, first sold in Washington, and later resold by private parties before entering Montana. Ford’s general advertising and parts sales within Montana did not cause the alleged defect or the accident. The Montana Supreme Court therefore erred in finding specific jurisdiction.

**A. Specific Jurisdiction Requires a Causal Connection Between the Defendant’s Forum Contacts and the Plaintiff’s Claims.**

This Court has long held that specific jurisdiction demands more than a defendant’s “continuous and systematic” contacts with the forum. *Perkins v. Benguet Consol. Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437, 446 (1952). Instead, the plaintiff’s claims must “arise out of” the defendant’s forum-related activities. *Hanson*, 357 U.S. at 251. In *Hanson*, the Court rejected jurisdiction over a Delaware trust because the trust’s contacts with Florida were unrelated to the plaintiffs’ claims. *Id.* at 253-54. Similarly, in *International Shoe*, the Court emphasized that jurisdiction depends on “the quality and nature of the activity in relation to the fair and orderly administration of the laws.” 326 U.S. at 319. A defendant’s general business presence in a state does not, standing alone, support specific jurisdiction over claims that do not arise from that presence. *See McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957) (upholding jurisdiction only because the insurance contract was “delivered in California” and the insured was a California resident).

Here, Ford’s Montana contacts—advertising and selling parts—are unrelated to the design defect or failure-to-warn claims. The vehicle was designed in Michigan, assembled in Kentucky, and first sold in Washington. No Ford activity in Montana caused the tire tread to separate. The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on Ford’s “continuous and systematic” contacts thus conflates general and specific jurisdiction, a distinction this Court has consistently maintained. *See Perkins*, 342 U.S. at 447-48 (general jurisdiction requires contacts “so substantial and of such a nature as to justify suit against it on causes of action arising from dealings entirely distinct from those activities”).

**B. The “Stream of Commerce” Theory Does Not Save Jurisdiction Here.**

Even under the stream-of-commerce theory, jurisdiction requires that the defendant’s forum contacts “give rise to” the plaintiff’s claims. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (decided after 1970, but citing pre-1970 precedent). In *Hanson*, this Court rejected jurisdiction over a trust that had no contacts with Florida other than the beneficiary’s relocation. 357 U.S. at 253-54. The Court explained that “it is essential in each case that there be some act by which the defendant purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State.” *Id.* at 253. Here, Ford did not purposefully avail itself of Montana’s market for this specific vehicle. The car entered Montana through a chain of private resales, not through Ford’s distribution network. *Cf. McGee*, 355 U.S. at 223 (jurisdiction proper where defendant solicited reinsurance in the forum).

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on Ford’s general advertising and parts sales is misplaced. In *International Shoe*, the Court upheld jurisdiction only because the defendant’s salesmen “regularly” solicited orders in Washington, and the claims arose from those solicitation activities. 326 U.S. at 315-16. Here, the plaintiffs’ claims arise from a design defect, not from Ford’s Montana advertising. *See also Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714, 733 (1877) (jurisdiction requires “property within the State” or “personal service” within the forum). Allowing jurisdiction based on unrelated forum contacts would “make the defendant answerable to any suit” in the forum, regardless of the claim’s origin. *Hanson*, 357 U.S. at 251.

**C. The Montana Supreme Court’s Decision Expands Jurisdiction Beyond Constitutional Limits.**

The Montana Supreme Court held that Ford’s “continuous and systematic” contacts with Montana—advertising and selling parts—subjected Ford to specific jurisdiction for any claim involving a Ford vehicle. This reasoning collapses the distinction between general and specific jurisdiction. This Court has never approved such an expansion. *See Perkins*, 342 U.S. at 446 (general jurisdiction requires contacts “so continuous and systematic as to render [the defendant] essentially at home in the forum”). Indeed, in *Hanson*, the Court warned that “the requirements of International Shoe . . . must be observed as a matter of constitutional law.” 357 U.S. at 251. The Montana Supreme Court’s approach would permit jurisdiction over any national manufacturer for any product-related claim, regardless of where the product was sold or used. That result is inconsistent with the Due Process Clause’s protection against “unreasonable” litigation burdens. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the Montana Supreme Court’s decision should be reversed.
