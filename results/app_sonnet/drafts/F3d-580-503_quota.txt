ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion, and Violated Winbush's Due Process Rights, by Denying His Motion for a Fingerprint Expert and Continuance on the Eve of Trial.

Due process guarantees a criminal defendant "a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense." Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 294 (1973). That guarantee includes access to expert assistance necessary to test the government's forensic case. Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68, 76-77 (1985) (holding that due process requires access to expert assistance where it is "a significant factor" in the defense). Winbush's entire theory of defense on the possession counts turned on the absence of his fingerprints on the recovered jacket, gun, and crack cocaine. He was entitled to an expert who could explain to the jury what that absence meant, not merely to stipulate to the government's characterization of it.

The magistrate judge nonetheless denied Winbush's motion for a fingerprint expert and his companion motion to continue, reasoning only that the request came "woefully late." That ruling did not weigh the considerations this Court and the Supreme Court require. While "the matter of continuance is traditionally within the discretion of the trial judge," "it is not every denial of a request for more time that violates due process." Ungar v. Sarafite, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964). The constitutional line is crossed when the denial is "arbitrary" and works an "unreasoning and arbitrary insistence upon expeditiousness in the face of a justifiable request for delay." Morris v. Slappy, 461 U.S. 1, 11-12 (1983) (quoting Ungar, 376 U.S. at 589). This Court examines factors including the reasons for the delay, whether the requested continuance addresses those reasons, and the prejudice to the defendant. United States v. Miller, 327 F.3d 598, 601 (7th Cir. 2003).

Those factors favored Winbush. The government's own expert notice, filed October 3, was the first formal confirmation that the absence of fingerprints would be litigated through Shay's and Reilly's testimony; Winbush's November 8 motion followed close on its heels. Denying any opportunity to counter that expert testimony—rather than, for example, granting a short continuance—was not a proportionate response to a scheduling problem of the government's own making. The prejudice was concrete: the jury heard unrebutted expert testimony that the absence of fingerprints was unremarkable, while Winbush was barred from offering any competing explanation. That one-sidedness is precisely what Chambers forbids. 410 U.S. at 294.

II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Sustain Winbush's Conviction for Possessing a Firearm in Furtherance of a Drug Trafficking Crime.

On sufficiency review, this Court asks "whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). Even under that deferential standard, the government's proof on Count charging a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 924(c) fails.

Mere possession of a firearm during a period in which a defendant also possesses drugs is not enough; the statute requires a nexus between the gun and the drug offense. See United States v. Kitchen, 57 F.3d 516, 520-21 (7th Cir. 1995) (constructive possession requires proof of a substantial connection between the defendant and the item, not merely proximity). The Supreme Court has likewise cautioned against equating simple presence with the conduct Congress targeted in § 924(c). See Bailey v. United States, 516 U.S. 137, 143 (1995) (the statute demands "active employment" of the weapon in the offense, not passive presence); Smith v. United States, 508 U.S. 223, 237-38 (1993) (the firearm must facilitate or have some purpose or effect with respect to the drug crime).

Here, the sale to Jones was already complete when police stopped Winbush's car; no drug transaction was in progress. Winbush's gun surfaced only when the police pulled him over, at which point he used it—if at all—not to protect a drug deal but to extricate himself from the traffic stop before discarding both the weapon and the Kentucky crack in his jacket as he fled on foot. That sequence shows a gun deployed to escape arrest, not a gun possessed "in furtherance of" distributing crack. A rational jury could not, on this record, find beyond a reasonable doubt the required nexus between the firearm and the completed drug sale. Jackson, 443 U.S. at 319; Kitchen, 57 F.3d at 520-21.

The same infirmity taints the jury's attribution of the Tennessee crack to Winbush. That cocaine was found a block away, with no forensic or eyewitness link to him beyond location along a chase route also traveled by others. Constructive possession cannot rest on proximity alone. Kitchen, 57 F.3d at 520-21.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, Winbush respectfully requests that this Court vacate his convictions, or in the alternative remand for a new trial at which he may present expert testimony in his defense.
