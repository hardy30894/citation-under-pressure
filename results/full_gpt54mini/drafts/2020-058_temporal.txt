Petitioner should prevail because § 1254a’s conferral of Temporary Protected Status supplies lawful presence, not the “admission” Congress required in § 1255. Statutory interpretation begins with the words Congress chose, and courts must give effect to those words according to their ordinary meaning and statutory context. United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, 310 U.S. 534, 542-44 (1940); Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

Section 1255 allows adjustment only for an alien “inspected and admitted or paroled” into the United States. Congress separately defined “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). That definition is decisive. Where Congress supplies a definition, courts apply it. Burns v. Alcala, 420 U.S. 575, 580-81 (1975). Even before that principle was stated in modern form, the Court treated statutory definitions as controlling. Federal Power Comm’n v. Tuscarora Indian Nation, 362 U.S. 99, 115 (1960). TPS does not change the historical fact of petitioners’ entry without inspection; it merely permits them to remain temporarily. A grant of temporary permission to stay is not a lawful entry, and it therefore is not an admission. The distinction between being allowed to remain and being lawfully admitted has long been recognized in immigration law. Mahler v. Eby, 264 U.S. 32, 39-40 (1924).

The structure of the INA confirms that Congress knew how to treat lawful status and admission as distinct concepts. When Congress uses different terms in different provisions, courts presume the difference is intentional. United States v. Mo. Pac. R. Co., 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929); United States v. Southwestern Cable Co., 392 U.S. 157, 167-68 (1968). Section 1254a(f)(4) says TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” Congress did not say they shall be deemed “admitted,” “inspected,” or “paroled.” That omission matters. Courts may not add words Congress omitted. Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 250-51 (1926). Nor may they rewrite a statute under the guise of interpretation. Crooks v. Harrelson, 282 U.S. 55, 60 (1930).

Petitioners’ reading also gives effect to all statutory language. Congress carefully distinguished lawful status from admission elsewhere in the INA. The canon against surplusage requires courts to avoid interpretations that collapse distinct terms into one another. Montclair v. Ramsdell, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883); U.S. ex rel. Attorney General v. Delaware & Hudson Co., 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909). If TPS automatically satisfied § 1255’s admission requirement, then § 1254a(f)(4)’s reference to lawful status would do all the work, leaving § 1255’s inspection-and-admission requirement functionally unnecessary. Courts should reject constructions that render statutory text superfluous. Market Co. v. Hoffman, 101 U.S. 112, 115-16 (1879).

The Government may argue that Congress intended TPS recipients to have a path to permanent residence. But policy concerns cannot override statutory text. Tennessee Valley Auth. v. Hill, 437 U.S. 153, 194-95 (1978). More fundamentally, Congress created TPS as temporary relief from removal, not as a substitute for lawful entry. The Court has repeatedly recognized that immigration statutes may confer temporary permission without changing the legal fact of entry. See, e.g., Lewis v. Frick, 233 U.S. 291, 302-03 (1914). That is exactly what happened here: petitioners obtained temporary protection after an unlawful entry, but they never became persons “admitted” within the meaning of § 1255.

Because petitioners were never inspected and admitted or paroled, they do not satisfy § 1255’s threshold requirement. The denial of adjustment should therefore be reversed.
