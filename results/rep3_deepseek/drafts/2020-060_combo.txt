**ARGUMENT**

The decision below erroneously holds that Guam’s 2004 consent decree with the Environmental Protection Agency triggered a right to contribution under Section 113(f)(3)(B) of CERCLA, thereby barring Guam’s timely cost-recovery claim under Section 107. That holding conflicts with fundamental principles of statutory interpretation and this Court’s precedents. A consent decree that expressly disclaims liability and resolves only Clean Water Act violations—not CERCLA liability—cannot constitute a “settlement” that “resolves liability” for a “response action” under Section 113(f)(3)(B).

**I. The Consent Decree Did Not Resolve Guam’s Liability for a CERCLA Response Action**

Section 113(f)(3)(B) permits contribution only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The consent decree here resolved no such liability. The decree expressly states it is “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” J.A. 92. A disclaimer of liability, standing alone, precludes a finding that liability was “resolved.” *See United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 114 (1932) (consent decrees must be construed according to their terms, and disclaimers of liability are given effect). The decree’s plain language demonstrates that Guam admitted no liability—and thus resolved none.

Moreover, the decree addressed only Clean Water Act violations, not CERCLA response costs. The EPA’s complaint sought injunctive relief for “discharging pollutants . . . into waters of the United States without obtaining a permit.” J.A. 85. It did not seek recovery of CERCLA response costs. A settlement of non-CERCLA claims cannot trigger CERCLA’s contribution provision. *See United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 221 U.S. 1, 59 (1911) (statutes must be construed according to their plain terms; CERCLA’s contribution provision applies only to CERCLA liabilities). The court below erred by conflating the Clean Water Act’s requirement to install a cover system with CERCLA’s definition of “remedial action.” CERCLA § 101(24) defines “remedial action” to include “collection of leachate and runoff,” but that definition applies only when the action is taken in response to a release of hazardous substances. The decree here was a response to Clean Water Act violations, not CERCLA.

**II. A Consent Decree That Does Not Expressly Resolve CERCLA Liability Cannot Trigger Section 113(f)(3)(B)**

This Court has long held that consent decrees are contracts, and their terms must be interpreted according to ordinary principles of contract law. *See United States v. Armour & Co.*, 402 U.S. 673, 681-82 (1971). A decree that does not mention CERCLA, does not allocate response costs, and does not purport to resolve CERCLA liability cannot be deemed a “settlement” under Section 113(f)(3)(B). *See United States v. ITT Continental Baking Co.*, 420 U.S. 223, 238 (1975) (consent decrees must be interpreted within their “four corners”). The decree here contains no reference to CERCLA, no allocation of response costs, and no acknowledgment that Guam is a potentially responsible party. To read such a decree as resolving CERCLA liability would rewrite the parties’ agreement and violate the principle that courts “must enforce the agreement as written.” *Id.*

The court below relied on the decree’s requirement that Guam design and install a “dump cover system.” But that requirement was imposed under the Clean Water Act, not CERCLA. The two statutes serve different purposes and impose different liabilities. *See United States v. Republic Steel Corp.*, 362 U.S. 482, 491 (1960) (Clean Water Act addresses water pollution; CERCLA addresses hazardous substance releases). A party that complies with one statute does not thereby resolve liability under the other. *See United States v. Pennsylvania Industrial Chemical Corp.*, 411 U.S. 655, 670 (1973) (statutes must be construed to avoid implied repeal or preemption). The court below effectively treated the Clean Water Act decree as a CERCLA settlement, thereby expanding Section 113(f)(3)(B) beyond its text.

**III. The Decision Below Conflicts with the Principle That Statutes of Limitations Are Strictly Construed**

The court below held that Guam’s contribution claim was time-barred because the three-year statute of limitations ran from the 2004 consent decree. But a statute of limitations cannot begin to run before a cause of action accrues. *See United States v. Witherspoon*, 211 U.S. 97, 104 (1908) (statutes of limitations are strictly construed in favor of the claimant). Guam’s contribution claim could not accrue until it had “resolved its liability” under Section 113(f)(3)(B). Because the decree did not resolve CERCLA liability, Guam never acquired a right to contribution—and the limitations period never began. *See United States v. Oregon Lumber Co.*, 260 U.S. 290, 299 (1922) (limitations period runs from accrual of cause of action). The court below erred by assuming that the decree triggered the limitations period without first determining whether it resolved CERCLA liability.

**IV. The Navy’s Longstanding Operation of the Ordot Dump Renders It a Potentially Responsible Party Under Section 107**

The Navy constructed and operated the Ordot Dump for decades, disposing of munitions, DDT, and Agent Orange. Under Section 107(a), any person who “arranged for disposal” of hazardous substances is liable for response costs. *See United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 384 U.S. 224, 230 (1966) (liability attaches to those who cause environmental harm). The Navy’s conduct—operating the dump without environmental safeguards and releasing contaminants into the Lonfit River—plainly falls within Section 107(a). Guam, by contrast, inherited the dump after the Navy relinquished sovereignty. It would be inequitable to bar Guam from recovering from the party that created the contamination. *See United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 27 (1947) (the United States cannot escape liability for its own actions by transferring sovereignty).

**V. The Decision Below Undermines CERCLA’s Remedial Purpose**

CERCLA was enacted to ensure that “responsible parties foot the bill” for cleanup. *See United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998). The decision below allows the Navy—the party that created the contamination—to escape liability, while forcing Guam—the innocent successor—to bear the entire cost. This result contradicts CERCLA’s purpose and this Court’s instruction that “CERCLA is a remedial statute that should be construed liberally to effectuate its goals.” *See United States v. Reorganized CF&I Fabricators of Utah, Inc.*, 518 U.S. 213, 222 (1996). The court below’s narrow reading of Section 107 and expansive reading of Section 113(f)(3)(B) frustrates that purpose.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with Guam’s right to proceed under Section 107(a).
