The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law. Instead, the OCSLA allows state law to apply only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the outer Continental Shelf "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. The Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to mean that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law to fill gaps in federal law. In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court held that the OCSLA's purpose was to provide a body of law for the outer Continental Shelf, and that state law would be adopted as federal law where necessary.

However, the Court also emphasized that the adoption of state law is not unlimited. In United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), the Court held that the assimilation of state law into federal law is proper where there is a gap in federal law that needs to be filled. The Court in Sharpnack was interpreting the Assimilative Crimes Act, 18 U.S.C. § 13, which is similar to the OCSLA's choice of law provision.

The Fifth Circuit has also interpreted the OCSLA's choice of law provision to require a gap in federal law before state law can be applied. In Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners' Mutual Insurance Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law only when there is a "significant void or gap" in federal law.

Other cases have also supported the idea that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law to fill gaps in federal law. In Chicago, R.I. & P.R. Co. v. McGlinn, 114 U.S. 542 (1885), the Court held that state law is adopted as federal law on federal enclaves only to the extent that it is not inconsistent with federal law and is necessary to fill a gap in federal law. Similarly, in Pacific Coast Dairy v. Department of Agriculture, 318 U.S. 285 (1943), the Court held that state law is not adopted as federal law on federal enclaves if there is already federal law governing the subject matter.

The Ninth Circuit's holding that state law can be applied whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law is inconsistent with these precedents. Under this holding, state law would be applied even when there is comprehensive federal law governing the subject matter, such as the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) in this case.

The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour claims, and it does not leave room for state law to address these claims on the outer Continental Shelf. As the district court recognized, the FLSA contains a savings clause that allows for more protective state wage and overtime laws, but this does not mean that state law can be applied in addition to the FLSA.

In Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), the Court held that federal law is supreme and that state law cannot be applied in a way that is inconsistent with federal law. Similarly, in Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52 (1941), the Court held that federal law preempts state law when there is a conflict between the two.

Therefore, the Ninth Circuit's holding should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of Newton's claims should be reinstated. The OCSLA does not permit the application of California's wage and hour laws to Newton's claims because the FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour claims on the outer Continental Shelf.
