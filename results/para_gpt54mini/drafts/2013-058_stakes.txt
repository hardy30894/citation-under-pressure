**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code does **not** exempt inherited IRAs from a debtor’s estate. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and § 522(d)(12) protect only “retirement funds,” and an inherited IRA is not retirement funds in the debtor’s hands. As this Court explained below, inherited IRAs are “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013). That conclusion follows from the statute’s text, structure, and purpose, and it is consistent with this Court’s prior approach to exemptions, which turns on the debtor’s present interest and use of the property at bankruptcy, not on how a predecessor used it. *See In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985).

The statutory phrase “retirement funds” must be given its ordinary meaning. *Davis v. Michigan Dep’t of Treasury*, 489 U.S. 803, 809 (1989). Funds that must be distributed within a short period after the original owner’s death, and that cannot be replenished or rolled over by the beneficiary, are not funds set aside for the beneficiary’s retirement. 26 U.S.C. §§ 402(c)(11)(A), 408(d)(3)(C), 401(a)(9)(B); *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 561–62. The beneficiary may be required to withdraw money long before retirement age, which means the account serves present consumption, not retirement savings. *Id.* at 562. A retirement exemption for such accounts would stretch the term “retirement funds” beyond its ordinary sense and beyond Congress’s chosen language.

The surrounding structure of § 522 confirms that Congress knew how to tie exemptions to the debtor’s own interest when it wanted to do so. Section 522(b)(3)(A), for example, exempts “any interest in property in which the debtor had, immediately before the commencement of the case, an interest” as a tenant by the entirety or joint tenant. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A). This Court correctly recognized that such language has a temporal effect: the exemption depends on the debtor’s interest as of bankruptcy, not on what a predecessor once did with the property. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. The same reasoning applies here. A debtor cannot transform nonexempt property into exempt property after filing, and a beneficiary cannot convert an inherited account into “retirement funds” simply because the account once belonged to someone who saved for retirement. *See In re Barker*, 768 F.2d at 196.

The contrary view would also create a mismatch between the exemption and the Code’s purpose. Congress enacted § 522(b)(3)(C) and § 522(d)(12) to protect genuine retirement savings from pre-retirement creditors. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 560. But inherited IRAs do not function as retirement savings for the beneficiary; they are immediately accessible, subject to mandatory minimum distributions, and unavailable for additional contribution or rollover. 26 U.S.C. §§ 402(c)(11)(A), 408(d)(3)(C); *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 561. As the Seventh Circuit observed, treating such accounts as exempt would “shelter from creditors a pot of money that can be freely used for current consumption.” *Id.* at 562.

The debtors may argue that the phrase “retirement funds” should be read broadly because the money originally came from a retirement account. But a term’s historical source does not control its current statutory character. *See In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. Once the original owner dies, the inherited account no longer functions as the owner’s retirement fund, and it is not the beneficiary’s retirement fund either. *Id.* That is why the Seventh Circuit rejected the Fifth Circuit’s contrary analysis in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012). This Court should adhere to the better reading of the statute.

Finally, this Court should reject any invitation to resolve the case by invoking a debtor-friendly canon. There is no need to resort to such a principle where the statutory text and structure are clear. *See Milner v. Dep’t of the Navy*, 562 U.S. 562, 572 (2011) (“It is text that matters.”). And where Congress has spoken with specificity, courts may not rewrite the statute to expand an exemption beyond its terms. *Lamie v. U.S. Tr.*, 540 U.S. 526, 534 (2004). Because inherited IRAs are not “retirement funds” within the meaning of § 522, they are not exempt from the bankruptcy estate. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
