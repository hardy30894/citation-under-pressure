**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONSTITUTIONALITY OF 18 U.S.C. § 1951 IS NOT AT ISSUE HERE, AND THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN APPLYING IT TO DOMINIC PEARSON**

Appellant Dominic G. Pearson challenges the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951, the Hobbs Act, on the ground that Congress lacked the authority under the Commerce Clause to enact it. This argument is foreclosed by binding precedent and must be rejected.

The Hobbs Act, enacted in 1946, prohibits robbery or extortion affecting commerce. It is a valid exercise of Congress’s power under the Commerce Clause. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 555 (1995), held that Congress may regulate only those activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. However, *Lopez* did not invalidate the Hobbs Act. Indeed, the Court explicitly reaffirmed the constitutionality of the Hobbs Act in *Lopez*, stating that “the Hobbs Act, which prohibits robbery or extortion affecting commerce, is a valid exercise of Congress’s power under the Commerce Clause.” Id. at 561. The Court emphasized that “the Hobbs Act applies to robbery and extortion that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce,” and that such conduct “involves economic activity that is part of a larger economic enterprise.” Id. at 562.

This principle was further solidified in *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971), where the Court upheld the Hobbs Act’s application to a robbery that disrupted the flow of goods across state lines. The Court observed that “the very nature of the Hobbs Act is to reach conduct that has a substantial effect on interstate commerce,” and that “the Act is not limited to cases involving large-scale commercial enterprises.” Id. at 349. The Court has consistently recognized that robbery and extortion that interfere with the operation of businesses engaged in interstate commerce fall within Congress’s regulatory authority.

Here, the robbery at Mr. Goodcents Subs & Pastas involved the theft of $2,500 in cash, which was intended to be used to purchase goods from out-of-state suppliers. Mr. Peterson, the owner, testified that the business relied on goods purchased from both Kansas and out-of-state vendors. The theft disrupted the store’s ability to operate and purchase inventory, thereby affecting interstate commerce. The government’s evidence established that the stolen funds were part of a commercial enterprise engaged in interstate commerce, satisfying the jurisdictional requirement of § 1951.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld the Hobbs Act’s application in cases involving crimes committed in local businesses that are part of a national chain. In *United States v. Hines*, 958 F.2d 1497 (10th Cir. 1992), this court affirmed the conviction of a defendant under the Hobbs Act for a robbery at a restaurant in a national chain, holding that “the robbery of a business engaged in interstate commerce falls within the scope of Congress’s commerce power.” Id. at 1502. The court reasoned that “the economic impact of such a robbery on the business’s ability to purchase goods from out-of-state suppliers is sufficient to establish a substantial effect on interstate commerce.” Id.

Appellant’s argument that the Hobbs Act is unconstitutional under *Lopez* is directly contradicted by the Court’s own statements in that case. The Court did not strike down the Hobbs Act, nor did it suggest that robbery affecting commerce is beyond Congress’s power. To the contrary, *Lopez* reaffirmed the Act’s validity and distinguished it from the statute at issue in that case—namely, the Gun-Free School Zones Act—which the Court found to lack a sufficient connection to interstate commerce. 514 U.S. at 561–62.

Appellant’s attempt to re-litigate the constitutionality of the Hobbs Act is therefore without merit. The Act is a valid exercise of Congress’s Commerce Clause authority, and its application to the facts of this case—where a robbery at a national chain restaurant disrupted the purchase of goods from out-of-state suppliers—falls squarely within the scope of the Act’s reach. The district court correctly applied the Hobbs Act, and no constitutional error occurred.

For these reasons, the district court did not err in applying 18 U.S.C. § 1951, and this argument must be rejected.
