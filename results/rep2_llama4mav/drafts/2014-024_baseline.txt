ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in affirming Anthony Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) because the trial court's jury instruction on "true threats" did not require the government to prove Elonis' subjective intent to threaten. The Supreme Court's decision in Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), requires a subjective intent to threaten for a statement to be considered a "true threat" outside the protection of the First Amendment.

In Virginia v. Black, the Court held that a "true threat" is a statement where "the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intention to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." 538 U.S. at 359. The Court emphasized that the speaker's intent is crucial in determining whether a statement is a "true threat." Id. at 360. The Third Circuit's contrary holding in United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), which requires only an objective intent to threaten, is no longer good law in light of Virginia v. Black.

The Third Circuit's decision to uphold Elonis' conviction despite the trial court's failure to instruct the jury on the subjective intent requirement is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's holding in Virginia v. Black. The trial court's instruction, which defined a "true threat" as a statement made in a context or under circumstances that would lead a reasonable person to foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat, is insufficient to ensure that Elonis' First Amendment rights were protected.

Requiring a subjective intent to threaten is essential to distinguish between speech that is protected by the First Amendment and speech that is not. As the Supreme Court noted in Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), "a statute such as this one, which makes criminal a form of pure speech, must be interpreted with the commands of the First Amendment clearly in mind." A subjective intent requirement ensures that individuals are not prosecuted for speech that they did not intend to be threatening.

The government's failure to prove Elonis' subjective intent to threaten raises significant concerns about the protection of free speech. Elonis' Facebook posts, while disturbing and violent, were made in the context of a rap lyrics-inspired artistic expression, and he testified that he did not intend to threaten anyone. The jury's verdict, based on an objective intent standard, may have convicted Elonis for speech that was protected by the First Amendment.

In conclusion, the Third Circuit's decision to uphold Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) without requiring proof of subjective intent to threaten is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's holding in Virginia v. Black. To protect the First Amendment rights of individuals, a conviction under § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten another person.
