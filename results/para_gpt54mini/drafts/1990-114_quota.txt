**ARGUMENT**

I. Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision does not conflict with the ADEA because appointed state judges are “appointee[s] on the policymaking level” and thus outside the Act’s definition of “employee.”

The ADEA’s text controls. It defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office in any State or political subdivision of any State by the qualified voters thereof,” as well as “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). In *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 604–10 (8th Cir. 1990), the court correctly held that appointed Missouri judges fall within the policymaking-level exception. That construction accords with Congress’s exemption of officials whose office entails the exercise of substantial discretionary authority. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 54–56 (1st Cir. 1988), held that judicial officers are policymakers because judging necessarily involves “lawmaking” and the exercise of discretion. The same conclusion was reached in *EEOC v. Illinois*, 721 F. Supp. 156, 158–60 (N.D. Ill. 1989), *EEOC v. Vermont*, 717 F. Supp. 261, 264–66 (D. Vt. 1989), *Schlitz v. Virginia*, 681 F. Supp. 330, 334–36 (E.D. Va. 1988), rev’d on other grounds, 854 F.2d 43 (4th Cir. 1988), *In re Stout*, 521 Pa. 571, 559 A.2d 489, 492–94 (1989), and *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 143–46 (1988).

The legislative history confirms, rather than narrows, that reading. Congress intended to exempt appointed officials “to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments,” and directed that the exception be construed narrowly. H.R. Conf. Rep. No. 92-899, at 15 (1972), reprinted in 1972 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2179, 2180. But a narrow construction does not mean an artificial one. As *Gregory* explained, judges decide unsettled questions of law, create precedent, and exercise broad supervisory authority over the judicial system. 898 F.2d at 607–09. Missouri judges therefore occupy a policymaking level within the meaning of § 630(f).

Petitioners’ contrary argument fails because it would treat judges as outside every exemption in § 630(f), a result Congress could not have intended. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 609–10. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against reading federal statutes to intrude on sensitive state functions absent a clear statement. *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971); *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d at 604–06. The ADEA contains no unmistakable command displacing state control over judicial qualifications and tenure.

II. The Missouri Constitution’s age-70 retirement rule survives equal protection review because age is not a suspect classification and the rule is rationally related to legitimate state interests.

Age classifications are subject only to rational-basis review. *Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313–14 (1976) (per curiam). Under that deferential standard, a law “must be upheld against equal protection challenge if there is any reasonably conceivable state of facts that could provide a rational basis” for it. *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993). Missouri’s policy easily satisfies that test.

The State has legitimate interests in maintaining a competent judiciary, avoiding the difficulty and embarrassment of individualized fitness proceedings, assuring continuity and public confidence, and promoting orderly transitions in judicial office. Those concerns have been recognized in upholding mandatory retirement rules for judges and other officials. *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 315–17; *Vance v. Bradley*, 440 U.S. 93, 97–112 (1979); *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d at 612–15. Courts have also recognized that mandatory retirement can reduce delay, avoid the burdens of case-by-case removal, and encourage renewal in public office. *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979); *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 571–73 (3d Cir. 1980), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980); *Apkin*, 517 N.E.2d at 146.

Petitioners cannot prevail by showing that other policy choices might also be rational. The Equal Protection Clause does not authorize courts to second-guess the wisdom of a state’s line-drawing where the classification is not suspect and no fundamental right is implicated. *Dandridge v. Williams*, 397 U.S. 471, 485–87 (1970); *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 109 (1949). Missouri’s judgment that judges should retire at seventy is therefore constitutionally permissible.

III. In any event, the ADEA should not be construed to override Missouri’s constitutional regulation of its judiciary absent a clear congressional statement.

Federalism principles require a clear statement before Congress may be read to displace a state’s sovereign authority over the structure and tenure of its courts. *Atascadero State Hospital v. Scanlon*, 473 U.S. 234, 242–47 (1985); *Bass*, 404 U.S. at 349; *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d at 604–06. The Eighth Circuit correctly declined to infer that Congress silently subjected state judges to the ADEA. Because Missouri judges fall outside the Act in the first place, and because the retirement provision is rational under the Fourteenth Amendment, the judgment should be affirmed.
