# ARGUMENT

## I. AMENDMENT 73 IMPOSES AN ADDITIONAL QUALIFICATION FOR CONGRESSIONAL OFFICE THAT THE CONSTITUTION DOES NOT PERMIT STATES TO IMPOSE

The Qualifications Clauses of Article I set forth an exhaustive list of the requirements to serve in Congress. Article I, Section 2 provides that a Representative must be twenty-five years of age, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the state from which elected. Article I, Section 3 imposes analogous requirements for Senators. Amendment 73 adds a further disqualification—prior electoral service—found nowhere in the constitutional text.

This Court has long recognized that where the Constitution enumerates qualifications for federal office, that enumeration is exclusive. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), this Court held that the House of Representatives could not exclude a duly elected member on grounds other than the standing qualifications set forth in Article I, explaining that "the Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution." *Id.* at 522. Although *Powell* addressed Congress's own power, its logic controls here: if the body vested with authority over its own membership cannot add qualifications, an individual State—which has no textual grant of authority over congressional qualifications at all—cannot do so either.

The Framers deliberately rejected proposals to allow the qualifications for Congress to vary by fiat. As the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly observed below, "the action finally taken by the framers of the constitution, following exhaustive debates, is strong evidence that term limits for senators and representatives was rejected." The historical record confirms this: at the Constitutional Convention, delegates debated and rejected rotation requirements for Congress precisely because uniform, minimal qualifications were thought essential to a national legislature representing a unified people rather than a confederation of separately-regulated state delegations.

This structural understanding follows directly from *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), which teaches that the federal government's powers, and the structure through which they are exercised, cannot be undermined by the "sovereign" prerogatives that States otherwise retain. Just as Maryland could not tax the Bank of the United States merely because taxation is ordinarily a state prerogative, Arkansas cannot regulate ballot access to congressional office merely because ballot regulation is ordinarily a state prerogative. The Elections Clause, Article I, Section 4, grants States authority over the "Times, Places and Manner" of holding elections—not authority to add substantive qualifications for the office itself. As Justice Story recognized, and as the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly concluded, "[c]ongressional officeholders partake of the same national character as the President of the United States." Uniformity is not merely preferable; it is constitutionally compelled, for otherwise fifty States could each impose their own patchwork of eligibility requirements—age limits, durational residency beyond the constitutional minimum, wealth qualifications, or, as here, term limits—fragmenting the national legislature into fifty different institutions.

Amendment 73's proponents attempt to characterize Section 3 as a mere "ballot access" restriction rather than a qualification. This is a distinction without constitutional substance. As the court below recognized, "the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point" to escape constitutional scrutiny, particularly because "[a]lthough section 3 is couched in ballot-access terminology," its practical effect is identical to a qualification: it renders certain persons—those who have served the specified number of terms—ineligible to hold the office regardless of the will of the electorate. A state cannot achieve through the ballot what it is forbidden to achieve directly. *Cf. Powell*, 395 U.S. at 547-48 (rejecting formalistic distinctions that would allow circumvention of substantive constitutional limits).

## II. SECTIONS 1 AND 2 OF AMENDMENT 73 ARE NOT SEVERABLE FROM THE UNCONSTITUTIONAL SECTION 3

Even accepting that Arkansas retains plenary authority to limit the terms of its own state officers, Amendment 73's severability clause cannot rescue Sections 1 and 2 from the constitutional infirmity of Section 3, because the voters were never given a fair, independent choice on state term limits standing alone.

Arkansas law is clear that "the test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion." *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). Here, there is no way to know whether Arkansas voters would have approved state-officer term limits divorced from the far more publicly salient issue of congressional term limits. The Preamble refers throughout to "elected officials" without distinguishing state from federal offices, and Section 6 applies uniformly to "the offices specified in this Amendment"—which include both categories without differentiation.

This Court has recognized that a severability inquiry must respect the actual choice presented to the enacting body, not a hypothetical choice never actually offered. The proponents "created this uncertainty and, therefore, had the onus to furnish this court something to go on besides speculation." No such evidence exists in this record. To the contrary, the structure of the Amendment—joining a constitutionally dubious congressional provision with an unquestionably valid state provision—reflects a calculated strategy: sponsors "knew that section 3 was of questionable constitutionality" and therefore "chose to blanket the two groups...into one unified package," leaving voters "no choice to approve one without the other."

This Court's severability jurisprudence, exemplified in cases such as *Alaska Airlines, Inc. v. Brock*, 480 U.S. 678 (1987), asks whether the legislature would have enacted the valid provisions independent of the invalid one. Arkansas's own courts, applying materially identical principles, have refused to presume severability where, as in the analogous "States' Rights Amendment" considered in *Hoban v. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 316 S.W.2d 185 (1958), a legitimate objective was deliberately coupled with a constitutionally doubtful one. The practice of "coupling a legitimate objective with one of doubtful legality, papered over with a severability clause, is not fair to voters" and, as the court below held, "is misleading at the very least, if not downright deceptive."

Because Sections 1, 2, and 3 were "not only inextricably linked" but "systemically fused in such a manner that each ceased to have a separate existence for voting purposes," the unconstitutionality of Section 3 requires invalidation of the entire Amendment.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court holding Amendment 73 unconstitutional in its entirety.
