**ARGUMENT**

**I. Furlow’s South Carolina crack-cocaine conviction is not a qualifying “serious drug offense” or “controlled substance offense” because the statute of conviction is broader than the federal definitions.**

The ACCA requires a predicate “serious drug offense” to be an offense “under State law, involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance” carrying a ten-year maximum. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Guidelines similarly require a “controlled substance offense” that “prohibits” specified drug conduct. USSG § 4B1.2(b). In evaluating whether a prior conviction qualifies, courts apply the categorical approach, asking whether the state statute’s elements match the generic federal definition. Descamps v. United States, 570 U.S. 254, 257 (2013); Mathis v. United States, 579 U.S. 500, 504–05 (2016).

South Carolina’s distribution-of-crack-cocaine statute is broader than the federal definitions because it criminalizes conduct beyond the federal generic forms of distribution. Where a statute is overbroad, it cannot serve as a predicate unless it is divisible and the modified categorical approach reveals a qualifying alternative element. Mathis, 579 U.S. at 517–18. Here, the government did not establish a Shepard-approved basis showing that Furlow necessarily admitted a qualifying element. Shepard v. United States, 544 U.S. 13, 16, 26 (2005). Absent that showing, the conviction cannot support either enhancement.

This Court has repeatedly held that prior drug convictions must satisfy the elements-based inquiry demanded by the ACCA and the Guidelines, not a factual recitation of the defendant’s conduct. United States v. Price, 777 F.3d 700, 708–09 (4th Cir. 2015); United States v. Dozier, 848 F.3d 180, 183–84 (4th Cir. 2017). Because the South Carolina conviction sweeps more broadly than the federal predicates, it should not have been counted.

**II. Furlow’s Georgia first-degree arson conviction is not categorically “arson” under the ACCA or the Guidelines.**

Both the ACCA and Guidelines list “arson” as a predicate offense. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). But “arson” for federal sentencing purposes means the generic offense, not whatever a State labels arson. Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575, 599 (1990); United States v. Stitt, 139 S. Ct. 399, 405 (2018). A state offense qualifies only if its elements are the same as, or narrower than, generic arson. Mathis, 579 U.S. at 504–05.

Georgia’s first-degree arson statute is broader because it reaches conduct beyond generic arson, including burning or causing to burn various enumerated structures and circumstances that do not necessarily require the elements of generic arson as historically understood. When a state statute extends beyond the generic definition, it fails the categorical test. Descamps, 570 U.S. at 261; United States v. McLeod, 808 F.3d 972, 975–76 (4th Cir. 2015). The district court therefore erred in counting the Georgia arson convictions as ACCA violent felonies and Guidelines crimes of violence.

The Supreme Court has emphasized that the categorical approach is necessary to avoid “mini-trials” about old convictions and to preserve uniformity. Taylor, 495 U.S. at 601; Shepard, 544 U.S. at 23. Those concerns are especially acute here, where the record does not establish that Furlow’s Georgia convictions correspond to generic arson.

**III. Because Furlow lacked the requisite predicate convictions, his ACCA and career-offender designations cannot stand, and the error was not harmless.**

An ACCA sentence enhancement requires three qualifying predicates. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1). The career-offender guideline requires two qualifying prior convictions. USSG § 4B1.1(a). If either challenged conviction is excluded, the enhancement fails; if both are excluded, the enhancements fail a fortiori. The district court’s contrary ruling therefore affected the advisory range and the statutory minimum. Under this Court’s precedents, an erroneous enhancement is not harmless where it changes the sentencing framework. United States v. Newbold, 791 F.3d 455, 460–61 (4th Cir. 2015); United States v. Winston, 850 F.3d 677, 682–83 (4th Cir. 2017).

The judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing without the ACCA and career-offender enhancements. See United States v. Rumley, 952 F.3d 538, 550 (4th Cir. 2020); United States v. Carthorne, 726 F.3d 503, 516–17 (4th Cir. 2013).
