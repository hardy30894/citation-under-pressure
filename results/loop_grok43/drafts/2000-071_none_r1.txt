The Milford Central School's exclusion of the Good News Club from its facilities after hours did not violate the First Amendment. The Club's proposed activities—prayer, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, and invitations to accept Christ—constituted religious instruction and worship rather than discussion of moral values from a religious viewpoint. See McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 209 (1948).

This distinction is dispositive. The district opened its limited public forum only for social, civic, and recreational uses pertaining to community welfare, not for religious purposes. See Davis v. Massachusetts, 167 U.S. 43, 47 (1897). The school had permitted secular groups like the Boy Scouts but properly excluded sectarian teaching. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 304 (1940).

Even if the exclusion implicated speech rights, it was justified by the Establishment Clause. The First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state. Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947). Permitting the Club's meetings would have placed the state's facilities in the service of advancing religion. Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 430 (1962). The district could not prefer one religion over another or aid all religions. Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952); Reynolds v. United States, 98 U.S. 145, 164 (1879).

The Establishment Clause forbids government from sponsoring religious exercises in public schools. Abington School District v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 223 (1963). It likewise bars the state from composing official prayers or requiring their recitation. Engel, 370 U.S. at 431. Allowing after-hours access for the Club's devotional program would have entangled the school with religious indoctrination of children. Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158, 165 (1944); West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 631 (1943).

The district's policy thus respects both free speech and establishment principles without abridging constitutional rights.
