**ARGUMENT**

**I. This Court's precedents establish that a reasonable fee under a federal fee-shifting statute may be enhanced for superior performance and exceptional results in rare cases supported by specific evidence.**

The lodestar—reasonable hours multiplied by a reasonable rate—is only the "initial estimate" of a reasonable fee. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 888 (1984). This Court has never held that the lodestar figure is immutable. To the contrary, in *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), the Court explained that "where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee," and that "in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified." *Id.* at 435.

*Blum* reaffirmed rather than repudiated that principle. The Court held that the "quality of representation" is "generally reflected in the reasonable hourly rate," and that it is therefore "no more appropriate to award a bonus for good performance than it would be to penalize an attorney with a fee reduction if the quality of his work were below average." *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899. But the Court did not stop there. It went on to explain that upward adjustment remains available "in some cases of exceptional success," and that a party seeking such an adjustment must "offer specific evidence" that "the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged." *Id.* If quality-based enhancements were categorically foreclosed, this instruction—specifying the evidentiary showing necessary to obtain one—would have been superfluous. This Court does not write surplus sentences into its opinions.

*Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986) ("*Delaware Valley I*"), confirms this reading. The Court there stated that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are ... proper only in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." *Id.* at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898–901). The Court cautioned that "the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar," *id.* at 566 (emphasis added)—language that itself presupposes a non-ordinary case in which such an adjustment is permissible. Had the Court meant to announce a categorical bar, it would not have qualified its holding with "ordinarily," nor would it have preserved the "rare" and "exceptional" case exception drawn directly from *Blum*.

Nothing in *City of Burlington v. Dague*, 505 U.S. 557 (1992), disturbs this framework. *Dague* addressed only whether a fee could be enhanced to reflect the contingent, risky nature of the representation, and held that it could not, because such a risk enhancement would "provide a windfall to attorneys" whose expected value was already built into the marketplace. *Id.* at 565. *Dague* did not purport to overrule *Blum* or *Delaware Valley I*, and did not address enhancement for superior performance and exceptional results at all. This Court "does not normally overturn, or ... dramatically limit, earlier authority sub silentio." *Shalala v. Illinois Council on Long Term Care, Inc.*, 529 U.S. 1, 18 (2000).

**II. Every court of appeals to consider the question has read *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* the same way Respondents urge here.**

The consensus among the courts of appeals confirms the correctness of this reading. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits have all held that a district court retains discretion, in the rare and exceptional case supported by specific record evidence, to enhance a fee award based on the quality of representation and the results obtained. *See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794–95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). No circuit has held to the contrary. This unbroken uniformity reflects the correct understanding of this Court's guidance: enhancement is available, but only upon a showing—grounded in specific, articulable evidence rather than generalized praise—that the lodestar, calculated using rates that already account for attorney skill and experience, nonetheless fails to capture the exceptional quality of representation or the extraordinary result achieved.

**III. The district court's enhancement here rested on precisely the kind of specific findings that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* require.**

The district court did not enhance the fee award merely because it found counsel to be skilled advocates. It made detailed, case-specific findings—reciting counsel's investigation, trial preparation, and negotiation of a consent decree providing sweeping structural relief for Georgia's foster children—and it found that the result achieved and the caliber of representation "far exceeded what could reasonably be expected" given the hourly rates used to calculate the lodestar. These are precisely the kind of particularized, record-based findings that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* contemplate as a predicate for enhancement. That the district judge measured counsel's performance against his own decades of experience practicing and presiding over fee-shifting litigation in the Atlanta market does not transform his judicial findings into improper "testimony"; it reflects the very institutional competence—firsthand observation of trial-level advocacy—that makes the district court, not a reviewing court operating at a distance, best positioned to identify the rare case warranting enhancement. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the district court did not abuse its discretion in making that determination, and this Court should affirm.
