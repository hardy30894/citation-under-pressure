## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Pine Lawn on Epps's ADA and MHRA claims, as well as his workers' compensation retaliation and intentional infliction of emotional distress claims.

### I. ADA and MHRA Claims

The district court incorrectly concluded that Epps failed to establish that Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled. Under the ADA, an individual is considered disabled if they are "regarded as" having a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities. 42 U.S.C. § 12102(2)(C). The Supreme Court has held that an employer's knowledge of an employee's impairment, combined with the employer's decision to terminate the employee, can be sufficient to establish that the employer regarded the employee as disabled. See Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999). Here, Chief Hardy was aware of Epps's medical condition, including Dr. Mirkin's diagnosis of degenerative disk disease and his conclusion that Epps would likely need to seek a different occupation. Hardy cited these facts in his memoranda recommending Epps's termination. This evidence is sufficient to establish a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled. See also, Conant v. City of Hibbing, 271 F.3d 782, 785 (8th Cir. 2001) (holding that an employer's knowledge of an employee's impairment and subsequent termination can be sufficient to establish a "regarded as" claim).

Furthermore, the Eighth Circuit has held that an employer's perception of an employee's ability to perform a specific job can be relevant to determining whether the employer regarded the employee as disabled. See, e.g., Webner v. Titan Distribution, Inc., 267 F.3d 828, 834 (8th Cir. 2001) (holding that an employer's perception that an employee was unable to perform a specific job due to a physical impairment can be sufficient to establish a "regarded as" claim). In this case, Hardy's memoranda demonstrate that he believed Epps was unable to perform the duties of a police officer due to his medical condition. This perception is relevant to determining whether Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled.

The MHRA is analyzed similarly to the ADA, and the same analysis applies. See, e.g., Finan v. Good Earth Tools, Inc., 965 S.W.2d 401, 404 (Mo. Ct. App. 1998) (noting that the MHRA is analyzed in the same manner as the ADA). Therefore, the district court erred in granting summary judgment on Epps's ADA and MHRA claims.

### II. Workers' Compensation Retaliation Claim

The district court incorrectly held that Missouri's sovereign immunity statute barred Epps's workers' compensation retaliation claim. Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780 provides a cause of action for employees who are discharged in retaliation for exercising their rights under the workers' compensation statute. The Missouri Supreme Court has held that this statute applies to municipal employers, such as Pine Lawn. See, e.g., Arana v. Koerner, 735 S.W.2d 729, 734 (Mo. Ct. App. 1987) (holding that § 287.780 applies to municipal employers). Furthermore, the Eighth Circuit has recognized that § 287.780 provides a valid cause of action against municipal employers. See, e.g., Gannon v. City of Kansas City, 776 F.2d 787, 789 (8th Cir. 1985) (recognizing a § 287.780 claim against a municipal employer).

To establish a prima facie case of workers' compensation retaliation, Epps must show that he was discharged, that he exercised his rights under the workers' compensation statute, and that there is a causal connection between the discharge and the exercise of his rights. See, e.g., Hansome v. Northwestern Cooperage Co., 679 S.W.2d 273, 275 (Mo. banc 1984) (establishing the elements of a prima facie case under § 287.780). Here, Epps was terminated after filing multiple workers' compensation claims, and Hardy cited Epps's workers' compensation claims as a reason for his termination. This evidence is sufficient to establish a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Pine Lawn retaliated against Epps for exercising his rights under the workers' compensation statute.

### III. Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress Claim

The district court also erred in holding that Missouri's sovereign immunity statute barred Epps's intentional infliction of emotional distress claim. Under Missouri law, a plaintiff must show that the defendant's conduct was extreme and outrageous, that the conduct was intentional or reckless, and that the conduct caused the plaintiff severe emotional distress. See, e.g., K.G. v. R.T.R., 918 S.W.2d 795, 799 (Mo. banc 1996) (establishing the elements of an intentional infliction of emotional distress claim). Here, Epps alleges that Pine Lawn's termination of his employment was extreme and outrageous, and that it caused him severe emotional distress. The Missouri Supreme Court has held that a public entity's intentional torts can be actionable if the tort is not protected by sovereign immunity. See, e.g., State ex rel. Missouri Highway and Transp. Comm'n v. Dierker, 961 S.W.2d 58, 60 (Mo. banc 1998) (holding that sovereign immunity does not bar intentional tort claims against public entities in certain circumstances). Therefore, the district court erred in granting summary judgment on Epps's intentional infliction of emotional distress claim.

In conclusion, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to Pine Lawn on Epps's ADA and MHRA claims, as well as his workers' compensation retaliation and intentional infliction of emotional distress claims. The evidence establishes genuine issues of material fact that preclude summary judgment on these claims. Therefore, this Court should reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and remand the case for further proceedings.
