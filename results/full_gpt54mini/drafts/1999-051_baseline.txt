## Argument

### I. Section 922(g)(3) is not unconstitutionally vague as applied to Edwards.

A criminal statute satisfies due process if it gives “a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that his contemplated conduct is forbidden.” *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954); *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357 (1983). In a non-First Amendment vagueness challenge, the Court examines the statute “in light of the facts of the case at hand.” *United States v. Mazurie*, 419 U.S. 544, 550 (1975); *Chapman v. United States*, 500 U.S. 453, 467 (1991).

Measured by that standard, 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(3) easily passes constitutional scrutiny. The statute prohibits possession of a firearm by “any person” who “is an unlawful user of or addicted to any controlled substance.” 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(3). The phrase “is an unlawful user” does not require the government to prove drug use at the exact instant the firearm is possessed; rather, it refers to ongoing, contemporaneous drug use. Courts of appeals have repeatedly so held. See *United States v. McCowan*, 469 F.2d 1153, 1155-56 (5th Cir. 1972); *United States v. Bennett*, 975 F.2d 305, 308-09 (6th Cir. 1992); *United States v. Jackson*, 280 F.3d 403, 406-07 (4th Cir. 2002); *United States v. Augustin*, 376 F.3d 135, 138-39 (3d Cir. 2004).

Edwards’s conduct falls comfortably within that settled construction. The record shows repeated drug use in the months surrounding his firearm possession: marijuana use in May, marijuana found in his car in July, marijuana found in the police car after a November detention, and marijuana and cocaine found at his residence on the night of the firearm offense. On this record, any ordinary person would understand that he was an “unlawful user” within the meaning of § 922(g)(3). See *United States v. Ocegueda*, 564 F.2d 1363, 1366 (9th Cir. 1977) (rejecting vagueness attack where “prolonged use of heroin, occurring before, during and after” firearm possession placed defendant within statute).

Edwards’s reliance on *United States v. Weissman*, 373 F.2d 799 (9th Cir. 1967), is misplaced. *Weissman* involved a materially different statute and predates the Court’s modern vagueness cases limiting facial invalidation outside the First Amendment context. See *Kolender*, 461 U.S. at 358 n.8; *Village of Hoffman Estates v. Flipside, Hoffman Estates, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 489, 495-96 (1982). Likewise, *United States v. Reed*, 114 F.3d 1067 (10th Cir. 1997), did not hold § 922(g)(3) void; it addressed the impropriety of deciding constitutionality on a pretrial motion before the government had presented evidence of the defendant’s conduct. Id. at 1070-71.

Accordingly, the judgment of conviction should be affirmed because § 922(g)(3), as applied here, gave Edwards ample notice and set an intelligible standard of enforcement.

### II. Edwards cannot rely on a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse any other default.

Even if Edwards could identify some independent procedural default in this habeas case, a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as “cause” unless that ineffective-assistance claim itself has been properly exhausted and preserved in state court. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488-89 (1986); *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). This Court squarely rejected the contrary rule in *Edwards v. Carpenter*, 529 U.S. 446, 451-53 (2000), holding that “an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim asserted as cause for the procedural default of another claim can itself be procedurally defaulted.” Id. at 453. Unless the petitioner can also show cause and prejudice for the default of the ineffectiveness claim itself, it cannot excuse the default of the underlying claim. Id. at 451-52; see also *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977).

The rule is straightforward. Federal habeas review respects state procedural bars, and a petitioner may not use a defaulted constitutional claim to bootstrap review of another defaulted claim. *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 730-31, 750. If the ineffective-assistance allegation was itself untimely or otherwise forfeited in state court, it is no different from any other defaulted claim. *Edwards*, 529 U.S. at 452-53. Only a properly preserved ineffectiveness claim, or one whose default is independently excused, can supply cause.

Edwards therefore is entitled to no relief on any theory that depends on a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim. But in any event, the merits of his vagueness challenge fail, and the conviction should be affirmed on that independent ground.
