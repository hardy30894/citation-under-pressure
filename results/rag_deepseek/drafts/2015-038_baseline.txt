**Argument**

The National Park Service (“NPS”) properly enforced its regulation prohibiting hovercraft operations within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. Section 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (“ANILCA”) does not strip NPS of authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands and waters within park boundaries. The plain text, structure, and purpose of ANILCA confirm that NPS retains regulatory jurisdiction over all lands within conservation system units, regardless of ownership.

**I. Section 103(c) Does Not Eliminate NPS Authority Over Non-Federal Lands**

ANILCA § 103(c) provides that “only those lands within the boundaries of any conservation system unit which are public lands . . . shall be treated as . . . included in such unit.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). This provision addresses land *status* for purposes of the Act’s land-use designations, not NPS’s pre-existing regulatory authority. The hovercraft ban is a regulation of general applicability, not a rule that applies solely to public lands. See 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e). Nothing in § 103(c) purports to repeal or limit NPS’s organic authority to protect park resources and visitors. *Amoco Production Co. v. Village of Gambell*, 480 U.S. 531, 549 (1987) (ANILCA must be viewed “in the context of the Act as a whole,” and its primary purpose was to allocate federal lands, not to strip federal regulatory power).

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 103(c) “does not preclude NPS from regulating activities on non-public lands” within park units. Sturgeon’s contrary reading would create an absurd patchwork: federal law would govern federally owned parcels but not adjacent state-owned rivers or private inholdings, thwarting NPS’s ability to manage wildlife, prevent habitat degradation, and ensure visitor safety. Such a result cannot be squared with ANILCA’s overarching goal of preserving Alaska’s natural resources. *See Amoco*, 480 U.S. at 549.

**II. NPS Has Long Regulated Non-Federal Lands Within Park Boundaries**

This Court has consistently upheld NPS authority to regulate non-federal lands within park units. In *Colorado v. Toll*, 268 U.S. 228, 230 (1925), the Court affirmed NPS regulations governing private property and commercial activities within Rocky Mountain National Park, rejecting the State’s argument that such regulations “interfere with the sovereign rights of the State.” The Court recognized that Congress may delegate to NPS the power to protect park values across all lands within the park’s boundaries, regardless of ownership. *Id.*

Similarly, in *Hynes v. Grimes Packing Co.*, 337 U.S. 86, 109 (1949), this Court upheld the Secretary’s authority to regulate fishing in Alaska’s waters, even when such waters were not federally owned. The Court emphasized that “the United States has jurisdiction” over activities that affect federal interests, including navigable waters and park resources. *Id.* at 108-09.

Sturgeon’s reliance on *Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government*, 522 U.S. 520 (1998), is misplaced. That case addressed whether land conveyed to Native corporations remained “Indian country” for purposes of tribal jurisdiction, not whether NPS may regulate non-federal lands within parks. *Id.* at 522-23. It does not speak to the scope of federal regulatory authority under ANILCA.

**III. The Hovercraft Ban Is a Valid Exercise of NPS Authority**

The hovercraft ban is a content-neutral safety and environmental regulation that applies uniformly to all persons within park boundaries. It is authorized by the NPS Organic Act, 54 U.S.C. § 100751, and the general regulations at 36 C.F.R. § 1.2. These regulations protect park resources, including wildlife, water quality, and the quiet enjoyment of the preserve. Hovercrafts generate noise, disturb wildlife, and erode riverbanks—harms that NPS may permissibly prevent across the entire park unit.

Sturgeon’s claim that the Nation River is state-owned does not exempt him from federal regulation. As this Court held in *United States v. Pacific & Arctic Railway & Navigation Co.*, 228 U.S. 87, 106 (1913), “we may control such citizens and corporations operating in our territory,” even if the underlying land is not federally owned. The Federal Government’s navigational servitude and commerce clause authority provide ample basis for regulating activities on navigable waters. *See South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 693 (1993) (federal takings and regulatory authority can diminish state control over lands within federal enclaves).

**IV. Congress Did Not Intend to Strip NPS of Regulatory Authority in Alaska**

ANILCA’s legislative history confirms that Congress intended NPS to retain full regulatory authority over all lands within park units. The Act’s purpose was to “complete the allocation of federal lands in the State of Alaska,” *Amoco*, 480 U.S. at 549, not to create a regulatory vacuum on non-federal lands. Section 103(c) merely clarifies that only public lands are “treated as included” for purposes of *land status*—e.g., for mineral leasing or wildlife refuge designations. It does not address NPS’s regulatory jurisdiction.

Moreover, ANILCA § 1319 provides that “[n]othing in [the Act] shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing . . . existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. This savings clause preserves NPS’s pre-existing regulatory authority, including its power to regulate hovercrafts, which predates ANILCA.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. NPS’s regulation of hovercrafts on state-owned waters within the Yukon-Charley Preserve is a lawful exercise of federal authority under ANILCA and the NPS Organic Act. Section 103(c) does not bar such regulation, and Sturgeon’s as-applied challenge fails.
