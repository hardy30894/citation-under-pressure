**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an uncounseled tribal court conviction may be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)—requires this Court to resolve a critical tension between federal criminal law, tribal sovereignty, and constitutional due process. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that such convictions cannot be used to establish an essential element of a federal felony offense, rests on a flawed interpretation of *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), and misapplies the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel to a context where it does not apply. The Court should reverse.

### I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT MISAPPLIES *NICHOLS* BY EXTENDING A SENTENCING RULING TO THE GUILT PHASE

The Ninth Circuit’s central error lies in treating *Nichols* as a broad constitutional license to use uncounseled convictions in any context, including the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution. That is not what *Nichols* held. There, the Court upheld the use of an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction to enhance a defendant’s sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746–47, 114 S.Ct. 1921. The Court explicitly grounded its holding in the fundamental distinction between the guilt and punishment phases of criminal adjudication: while the government must prove every element of an offense beyond a reasonable doubt, see *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364, 90 S.Ct. 1068, 25 L.Ed.2d 368 (1970), it need only prove sentencing enhancements by a preponderance of the evidence. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 748, 114 S.Ct. 1921.

The Court in *Nichols* acknowledged that uncounseled convictions raise reliability concerns, but concluded that those concerns were adequately addressed by the sentencing process: the defendant could challenge the reliability of the prior conviction, and the preponderance standard reflected a lower burden. Id. at 747–48, 114 S.Ct. 1921. Nothing in *Nichols* suggests that the same standard applies when the prior conviction is used to establish an essential element of a new offense. Indeed, to permit such use would violate *Winship*’s core principle: “the government must prove every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged beyond a reasonable doubt.” 397 U.S. at 364, 90 S.Ct. 1068.

The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Nichols* on the ground that tribal court convictions are constitutionally valid because the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal proceedings is legally incoherent. The fact that a conviction is constitutionally valid under tribal law does not mean it is constitutionally reliable for use in a federal prosecution. The issue is not whether the tribal court complied with the Sixth Amendment—because it need not—but whether the use of such a conviction in a federal felony prosecution comports with due process. The Ninth Circuit’s approach collapses this distinction, treating constitutional validity under one system as equivalent to constitutional reliability under another.

### II. THE SIXTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT APPLY TO TRIBAL COURT PROCEEDINGS

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that tribal court convictions are “constitutionally infirm” because they lack a Sixth Amendment right to counsel is fundamentally mistaken. The Sixth Amendment applies only to federal and state courts. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323–24, 98 S.Ct. 1079, 55 L.Ed.2d 303 (1978). The Court has repeatedly affirmed that “the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.” *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337, 128 S.Ct. 2709, 171 L.Ed.2d 457 (2008). Tribal courts operate under a distinct legal regime governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. § 1302, which provides a right to counsel only in certain circumstances—specifically, when the tribal court exercises expanded jurisdiction over felonies or special domestic violence cases. See id. § 1302(a)–(c), § 1304.

The fact that a tribal court did not provide counsel in a misdemeanor case does not render the conviction invalid under federal constitutional law. The Supreme Court has made clear that “a conviction may be valid under tribal law even if it would not satisfy the requirements of the Sixth Amendment.” *Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654, 661–62, 122 S.Ct. 1764, 152 L.Ed.2d 888 (2002). In *Shelton*, the Court affirmed that a state may invalidate a prison sentence imposed after an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, but it left the underlying conviction intact. Id. at 662, 122 S.Ct. 1764. The same principle applies here: the absence of counsel in a tribal court proceeding does not invalidate the conviction for purposes of federal law, especially when the conviction is used to establish a prior offense under a recidivist statute.
