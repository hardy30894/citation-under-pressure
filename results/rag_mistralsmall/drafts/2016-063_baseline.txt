**ARGUMENT**

**I. The D.C. Circuit Erred in Transferring This Case to the Federal Circuit**

The D.C. Circuit held that this case must be transferred to the Federal Circuit because it concluded that *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), remains controlling after *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012). That conclusion is incorrect. *Kloeckner* resolved the precise jurisdictional question presented here—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) dismissal for lack of jurisdiction over a mixed case must be reviewed in district court—and held that such dismissals are reviewable in district court. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 53 ("[W]e conclude that §7703(b)(2) permits judicial review in district court when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds."). The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* on the ground that it involved a procedural dismissal rather than a jurisdictional one is unavailing. *Id.* at 52-53 (rejecting the argument that the nature of the dismissal—procedural or jurisdictional—matters, and holding that "the only discrimination cases that can go to district court pursuant to §7703(b)(2) are those the MSPB has decided on the merits" is incorrect).

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is misplaced. *Powell* predated *Kloeckner* and did not consider the Supreme Court’s later holding. *Powell*, 158 F.3d at 597. The Supreme Court in *Kloeckner* explicitly addressed and rejected the argument that the nature of the dismissal—whether procedural or jurisdictional—determines the proper forum for review. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 52-53. The Court’s holding controls this case.

**II. The Statutory Framework Mandates Review in District Court**

The governing statute, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2), provides that "[a]ctions which must be filed in district court" include cases in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and [alleges] that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" certain federal statutes. The Supreme Court has held that this provision applies to all MSPB dismissals of mixed cases, regardless of whether the dismissal is on procedural or jurisdictional grounds. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 53 ("[W]e conclude that §7703(b)(2) permits judicial review in district court when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds.").

Here, Perry’s appeal to the MSPB alleged discrimination and sought review of an adverse action that he contended was involuntary. The MSPB dismissed his appeal for lack of jurisdiction, finding that his retirement and suspension were voluntary. This dismissal falls squarely within the mixed-case exception of § 7703(b)(2). The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner* makes clear that such dismissals must be reviewed in district court, not the Federal Circuit. *Id.* at 53.

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Powell* is no longer good law. *Kloeckner* abrogated the reasoning of *Powell* by rejecting the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals for purposes of § 7703(b)(2). *Id.* The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to preserve *Powell* by distinguishing *Kloeckner* is unpersuasive and inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s holding.

**III. The Federal Circuit Lacks Jurisdiction Over This Case**

The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction over MSPB decisions is limited to cases that do not involve discrimination claims. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A). The Supreme Court has held that § 7703(b)(2) carves out an exception for mixed cases—those involving both an appealable action and a discrimination claim—regardless of whether the MSPB reaches the merits. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 53. Because Perry’s case is a mixed case, the Federal Circuit lacks jurisdiction, and the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring this case to it.

**IV. The D.C. Circuit’s Decision Creates Uncertainty and Undermines the Statutory Scheme**

The D.C. Circuit’s decision creates a circuit split and undermines the statutory scheme established by Congress. The Supreme Court in *Kloeckner* resolved the very question presented here and held that mixed cases dismissed by the MSPB—whether on procedural or jurisdictional grounds—must be reviewed in district court. *Id.* The D.C. Circuit’s refusal to follow *Kloeckner* creates confusion and deprives federal employees of the clear statutory right to district court review in mixed cases.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s decision and hold that Perry’s case must be reviewed in district court. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner* controls, and the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring this case to the Federal Circuit.
