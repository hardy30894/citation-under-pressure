# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN LANGUAGE OF SECTION 1407(a) REQUIRES REMAND TO THE TRANSFEROR COURT AND ADMITS OF NO EXCEPTION FOR SELF-ASSIGNMENT UNDER SECTION 1404(a)

Section 1407(a) provides that any action transferred by the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings to the district from which it was transferred." 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The word "shall" is not a suggestion. As this Court has repeatedly recognized, when Congress uses mandatory language in a statute, courts must give it its ordinary, obligatory meaning. *Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947) ("shall" is "the language of command"). Congress could easily have written that the Panel "may" remand, or that remand is discretionary once pretrial proceedings conclude. It did not. Instead, it chose the mandatory "shall," a choice this Court must respect.

The statutory design confirms that remand is non-discretionary. Section 1407(a) creates a temporally limited grant of authority: transfer is permitted only "for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings," and the transferee court's power terminates "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). Nothing in the statute authorizes the transferee court to retain the case for trial, nor does it authorize the transferee court to use its temporary custody of the case as a springboard for permanently relocating it under a different statutory provision. Section 1404(a), by contrast, is a general venue-transfer statute that says nothing about multidistrict litigation and contains no exception to § 1407(a)'s remand command. See 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). This Court should decline to read into § 1404(a) an implicit repeal of § 1407(a)'s unambiguous remand mandate. Repeals by implication are disfavored, and courts must give effect to both statutes where possible. *Morton v. Mancari*, 417 U.S. 535, 551 (1974) ("courts are not at liberty to pick and choose among congressional enactments" and repeal by implication is found "only where provisions in two statutes are in irreconcilable conflict").

There is no irreconcilable conflict here. Section 1407(a) governs the temporal scope of the transferee court's authority over the case; § 1404(a) governs venue transfers generally. The two statutes coexist perfectly if § 1404(a) transfer motions—like other pretrial matters—are resolved by the transferee court *during* the pretrial period but the case is nonetheless remanded for trial as § 1407(a) commands. The Ninth Circuit's approach inverts this relationship, allowing the transferee court to use § 1404(a) to swallow the remand requirement whole.

## II. MULTIDISTRICT LITIGATION RULE 14(b) AND THE COURTS OF APPEALS THAT HAVE SANCTIONED SELF-TRANSFER CANNOT OVERRIDE THE STATUTE'S PLAIN TEXT

The Ninth Circuit below rested its decision largely on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and on decisions such as *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810, 820 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 125 (2d Cir. 1971). *Lexecon Inc. v. United States Dist. Court*, 61 F.3d 911 (9th Cir. 1995) (unpublished). But a procedural rule promulgated by the Panel cannot expand the Panel's own statutory authority beyond what Congress granted. It is axiomatic that "an agency may not confer power upon itself" and that rules must yield to the statute they implement. *Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp.*, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988) (agency rules cannot contradict unambiguous statutory text). The Panel's authority is *created* by § 1407; it cannot use rulemaking to rewrite the very statute that defines the limits of its jurisdiction.

Nor does *Pfizer*'s reasoning withstand scrutiny. That court candidly acknowledged it was departing from the statute for reasons of policy and convenience, reasoning that requiring remand before resolution of § 1404(a) motions would be "absurd." *Pfizer*, 447 F.2d at 124–25. But as Judge Kozinski recognized in dissent below, "[i]t's hard to imagine a clearer statutory command, or one more widely ignored." *Lexecon*, 61 F.3d at 911 (Kozinski, J., dissenting). Even the district court in *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire* admitted that "there is nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial." 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971). Policy disagreements with a statute's plain command are properly addressed to Congress, not remedied by judicial improvisation. *Board of Governors, FRS v. Dimension Financial Corp.*, 474 U.S. 361, 374 (1986) ("Policy arguments are properly addressed to Congress, not to this Court.").

## III. SELF-TRANSFER UNDER SECTION 1404(a) UNDERMINES THE STRUCTURAL PROTECTIONS SECTION 1407(a) WAS DESIGNED TO PRESERVE

Section 1407(a)'s remand requirement reflects a deliberate congressional choice to permit efficient coordination of pretrial matters while preserving the plaintiff's traditional right to select the forum for trial. Permitting transferee courts to invoke § 1404(a) to assign cases to themselves converts a temporary, efficiency-driven arrangement into a permanent forum change achieved through the back door. As Judge Kozinski observed, "[c]ases may be transferred to remote locations, but only for the duration of pretrial procedures. When it comes to conducting a trial . . . plaintiffs retain their ancient right to select the forum." *Lexecon*, 61 F.3d at 911 (Kozinski, J., dissenting). Here, Lexecon was transferred to Arizona solely for coordinated pretrial proceedings; it never chose that forum for trial, and the transferee court's self-assignment deprived it of the forum selection to which the statute entitled it.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment below should be reversed.
