Arizona’s rule is consistent with due process. The Fourteenth Amendment requires the State to prove the elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt, but it does not dictate the particular evidentiary doctrines by which a defendant may attempt to rebut the State’s proof. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), articulated the burden-of-proof principle, but the Constitution leaves States substantial latitude to define defenses and to regulate the use of mental-health evidence at trial. See Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952); Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895).

This Court has repeatedly recognized that insanity is a distinct legal doctrine that States may define and administer. In Davis, the Court held that, in federal prosecutions, sanity may be an issue the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt once properly raised, 160 U.S. at 484-85. But Davis did not constitutionalize a separate diminished-capacity defense or hold that psychiatric evidence must always be admissible to negate intent. To the contrary, Davis reflects the traditional understanding that the law of mental responsibility belongs to the sovereign defining the offense. Leland, 343 U.S. at 798-99, is even more directly on point: the Court rejected the claim that due process forbids requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. If due process permits a State to place the full burden of proving insanity on the accused, it necessarily permits the State to confine psychiatric evidence to that insanity defense rather than allow it also to operate as a partial negation of mens rea. See also Salinger v. United States, 272 U.S. 542, 548-49 (1926).

Petitioner’s theory would transform a state-law evidentiary limitation into a constitutional entitlement. But this Court has not treated every restriction on a defendant’s preferred mode of proof as a due process violation. Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), invalidated a rule that arbitrarily barred a defendant from calling an accomplice witness and thereby denied “the testimony of witnesses to establish a defense.” Id. at 19. That holding rested on the exclusion of a witness altogether, not on a State’s choice to restrict the legal effect of admissible expert testimony. Likewise, Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284 (1973), involved a unique combination of hearsay and cross-examination restrictions and is not controlling in any event for a pre-1970 case analysis. The pre-1970 decisions show no constitutional rule requiring States to recognize diminished capacity or to permit psychiatric evidence to be used for whatever purpose the defense prefers.

The Court’s insanity and competency cases point the same way. Blackburn v. Alabama, 361 U.S. 199 (1960), concerned the voluntariness of a confession taken from a mentally ill defendant under coercive circumstances; it did not announce a right to use psychiatric testimony to rebut specific intent. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), addressed competency to stand trial, not the scope of trial evidence. Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), required a competency hearing when the evidence raised a bona fide doubt about the defendant’s ability to proceed. None of those cases holds that due process compels a State to let expert mental-health evidence be used as a partial defense to mens rea distinct from insanity.

Arizona’s rule is also consistent with the common-law distinction between criminal intent and legal insanity. The State did not prevent Clark from contesting the prosecution’s case; it allowed him to present an insanity defense and to offer evidence bearing on that issue. What Arizona refused to permit was the use of psychiatric testimony to create a separate excuse short of insanity. That limitation is well within a State’s power to define the elements and defenses of its criminal law. See Holt v. United States, 218 U.S. 245, 252-53 (1910) (rejecting the proposition that evidence of mental condition automatically negates criminal responsibility); Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105-06 (1934) (due process is reserved for procedures that offend principles “so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental”).

Nor does petitioner’s theory find support in the Court’s broader due process cases. The Court has recognized that due process protects against fundamentally unfair procedures, but it has never held that fundamental fairness requires a State to admit expert psychiatric evidence for the limited purpose of negating intent when the State channels such evidence into insanity. Fisher v. United States, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946), confirmed that the criminal process may be structured by rules that serve the administration of justice; it did not create a constitutional right to a diminished-capacity defense. The Constitution demands a fair trial, not the particular evidentiary regime petitioner prefers.

Because Arizona allowed Clark to present an insanity defense, but reasonably limited psychiatric testimony to that defense, there was no Fourteenth Amendment violation. The judgment should be affirmed.
