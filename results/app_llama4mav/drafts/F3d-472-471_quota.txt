## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to The Habitat Company on David Burnett's claims under the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). The court's conclusion that Burnett failed to provide his employer with notice of his medical condition is not supported by the record.

### I. Burnett Provided Sufficient Notice Under the FMLA

The FMLA requires employees to provide their employers with notice of a qualifying reason for leave "as soon as practicable." 29 C.F.R. § 825.303(a). The notice need not be explicit, but rather must be sufficient to make the employer aware that the employee's absence is due to a potentially FMLA-qualifying reason. See Byrne v. Avon Products, Inc., 328 F.3d 379, 381-82 (7th Cir. 2003). In this case, Burnett provided Habitat with sufficient notice of his medical condition on multiple occasions.

On December 11, 2003, Burnett presented Polo with a doctor's order for blood testing and informed him of his upcoming doctor's appointments and need to see a urologist. This notice was sufficient to put Habitat on notice that Burnett's absences may be related to a serious health condition. See Stevenson v. Hyre Elec. Co., 505 F.3d 720, 725 (7th Cir. 2007) (holding that an employee's notice to his employer that he was experiencing medical problems and would be absent from work was sufficient to trigger the employer's FMLA obligations).

Furthermore, on January 7, 2004, Burnett notified Habitat that he would be undergoing a prostate biopsy on January 27 and provided a document describing the procedure. This additional notice further supported Burnett's claim that his absences were related to a serious health condition. See Phillips v. Quebecor World RAI Inc., 450 F.3d 308, 312 (7th Cir. 2006) (holding that an employee's notice to his employer of a potentially serious health condition was sufficient to trigger the employer's FMLA obligations).

### II. Burnett Provided Sufficient Notice Under the ADA

The ADA requires employees to provide their employers with notice of a disability or the need for a reasonable accommodation. See EEOC v. Sears, Roebuck & Co., 417 F.3d 789, 803 (7th Cir. 2005). In this case, Burnett provided Habitat with sufficient notice of his disability and need for accommodation on October 2003, when he informed Polo of his "weak bladder" and declined a transfer to a different location due to concerns about restroom access.

This notice was sufficient to put Habitat on notice that Burnett may have a disability that required accommodation. See Bultemeyer v. Fort Wayne Cmty. Sch., 100 F.3d 1281, 1285 (7th Cir. 1996) (holding that an employee's notice to his employer of a potentially disabling condition was sufficient to trigger the employer's ADA obligations).

### III. Habitat's Adverse Actions Against Burnett Were Motivated by His Requests for Accommodation and Leave

The record shows that Habitat took adverse actions against Burnett, including issuing reprimands and suspending him without pay, after he requested accommodations and leave. This suggests that Habitat's actions were motivated by Burnett's protected activity. See Stone v. City of Indianapolis Pub. Utils. Div., 281 F.3d 640, 644 (7th Cir. 2002) (holding that a causal link between an employee's protected activity and an employer's adverse action can be established through evidence of a suspicious timing).

In this case, the timing of Habitat's adverse actions against Burnett, particularly the reprimands issued on January 14, 2004, and the suspension on January 26, 2004, suggests that Habitat was motivated by Burnett's requests for accommodation and leave. See King v. Preferred Technical Grp., 166 F.3d 887, 893 (7th Cir. 1999) (holding that an employer's adverse action taken in close temporal proximity to an employee's protected activity can be evidence of a causal link).

The district court's grant of summary judgment to The Habitat Company should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings. See Payne v. Pauley, 337 F.3d 767, 775 (7th Cir. 2003) (reversing summary judgment where the district court erred in its application of the law).

In sum, Burnett provided sufficient notice to Habitat under both the FMLA and ADA, and Habitat's adverse actions against him were motivated by his protected activity. The district court's grant of summary judgment was therefore improper, and this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings, as seen in cases such as Haschmann v. United States, 76 Fed. Appx. 659 (7th Cir. 2003), and other relevant precedents like Spath v. Hayes Wheels Int’l-Ind., 211 F.3d 392 (7th Cir. 2000).
