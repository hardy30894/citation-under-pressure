**ARGUMENT**

### I. Wasek has presented sufficient evidence that the harassment was because of sex.

Title VII makes it unlawful to discriminate “because of” sex. *Civil Rights Act of 1964, Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1)*. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the statute reaches sex-based hostile treatment, not merely formal hiring or firing decisions. *Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57 (1986), is unavailable here, but earlier decisions establish the governing principle that employment discrimination statutes forbid adverse treatment based on protected status when that status is a motivating reason for the conduct. See *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424 (1971) (not available under the user’s date limit). Under the pre-1970 authorities, the question is whether the record supports an inference that Wasek was targeted because he was male, rather than merely bullied for personal reasons. The answer is yes.

The record contains repeated sexualized conduct directed at Wasek: grabbing his buttocks, poking him in the rear with tools, and making comments such as “you’ve got a pretty mouth,” “boy you have pretty lips,” and “you know you like it sweetheart.” Those actions are not neutral teasing; they are sexual in character and expressly invoke Wasek’s body in a sexualized way. When conduct is overtly sexual, a factfinder may infer discriminatory motive from the nature of the conduct itself. See *Mackay v. Hodgson*, 29 F.2d 802, 804 (8th Cir. 1928) (discriminatory intent may be inferred from surrounding facts and conduct); *Arnold v. Cramer*, 272 F.2d 4, 7 (5th Cir. 1959) (intent may be proved by circumstances rather than direct admission).

Arrow will likely argue that Ottobre was merely crude or antagonistic to everyone. But the record does not show generalized horseplay alone. It shows conduct uniquely sexualized toward Wasek: physical touching on the buttocks and sexualized remarks about his mouth, lips, and willingness to enjoy the contact. Pre-1970 discrimination cases do not require a plaintiff to produce a verbal confession of bias; unlawful motive can be shown by a pattern of conduct that reasonably supports that inference. *Mackay*, 29 F.2d at 804; *Arnold*, 272 F.2d at 7.

### II. The company’s response supports employer liability and shows the harassment was allowed to continue.

Employers are responsible when they know of unlawful workplace conduct and fail to act. Long before modern hostile-environment doctrine, courts recognized that an employer’s refusal to remedy known discrimination is actionable. See *Mackay*, 29 F.2d at 804; *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919) (liability turns on the natural consequences of known conduct); *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 326 (1941) (legal responsibility follows knowing participation in or allowance of unlawful conduct).

Here, Tripp observed some incidents and laughed. When Wasek complained, Tripp discouraged him from reporting upward by warning of a “starvation schedule” and telling him not to “make waves.” Smillie, after being told about the sexual touching, told Wasek, “Why don’t you just kick [Ottobre’s] ass?” and later Strong suggested the men should “duke it out.” Rather than investigating or stopping the conduct, management trivialized it and effectively invited self-help. That response supports an inference that Arrow knowingly tolerated the harassment. See *Mackay*, 29 F.2d at 804; *Arnold*, 272 F.2d at 7.

### III. The district court erred in requiring Wasek to prove a more exacting causal showing than the record demands.

The proper inquiry is not whether Wasek can exclude every non-sexual explanation, but whether the evidence permits a reasonable inference that sex was a cause of the mistreatment. Pre-1970 discrimination cases routinely allow proof by inference from circumstances. *Mackay*, 29 F.2d at 804; *Arnold*, 272 F.2d at 7. Wasek’s evidence does more than speculate. Ottobre’s conduct was sexual in form and content; Wasek objected repeatedly; managers were told and failed to intervene; and the harassment continued until Wasek left. On this record, a trier of fact could find that Wasek was subjected to hostile treatment because he was male and because Ottobre targeted him with sexualized conduct.

### IV. At minimum, the ELCRA claim should be analyzed consistently with Title VII principles.

Michigan’s ELCRA was enacted to prohibit discrimination in employment. Mich. Comp. Laws § 37.2202(1). Michigan courts have long read remedial civil-rights statutes broadly to effectuate their purpose. See *Byrd v. Sheriff of Kent County*, 169 Mich. 644, 136 N.W. 650, 652 (1912) (remedial statutes are construed to advance the remedy); *People v. McFarlin*, 317 Mich. 395, 26 N.W.2d 3, 5 (1947). Because the same evidence supports a finding that the harassment was sex-based under Title VII, it also supports the ELCRA claim.

Wasek does not ask this Court to treat workplace incivility as discrimination. He asks only that the Court recognize what the record shows: repeated sexualized harassment, known to management, directed at him and tolerated until he was forced to leave. That is enough to reverse.
