**ARGUMENT**

**I. Because the ACCA and career-offender enhancements are penal in character, any doubt as to whether Furlow's prior convictions qualify as predicates must be resolved in his favor.**

The enhancements at issue transform a ten-year maximum sentence into a fifteen-year mandatory minimum, and likewise inflate the advisory Guidelines range. Such consequences demand the strictest fidelity to the text defining a qualifying predicate. As this Court's predecessors have long recognized, "[t]he rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself." *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820). That rule rests on "the tenderness of the law for the rights of individuals," *id.*, and it applies with full force here, where the label "serious drug offense" or "violent felony" carries the same life-altering weight as any substantive criminal charge.

The same solicitude for fair notice underlies *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451 (1939), which held that "[n]o one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes." 306 U.S. at 453. A defendant facing a mandatory fifteen-year floor is entitled to know, from the statutory elements of his prior convictions alone, whether those convictions will trigger that floor — not to have the classification supplied after the fact from the underlying facts of his case.

Where ambiguity remains after the statute is read as written, it must be resolved against the harsher result. "This policy of lenity means that the Court will not interpret a federal criminal statute so as to increase the penalty that it places on an individual when such an interpretation can be based on no more than a guess as to what Congress intended." *Ladner v. United States*, 358 U.S. 169, 178 (1958). Similarly, in *United States v. Universal C.I.T. Credit Corp.*, 344 U.S. 218 (1952), the Court declined to adopt the harsher of two readings absent clear congressional direction. 344 U.S. at 221–22. And in the analogous context of deportation — another consequence attached to a prior "conviction" rather than to newly adjudicated conduct — the Court in *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6 (1948), resolved doubt in the individual's favor, explaining that courts should not assume Congress intended a result "beyond that which is required by the narrowest of several possible meanings of the words used." 333 U.S. at 10.

Finally, because these enhancements attach solely by virtue of the *fact* of a prior conviction, courts must look to what that conviction necessarily established — its statutory elements — rather than to the defendant's actual conduct. This is the same principle that animated *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728 (1948), in which the Court explained that a recidivist enhancement is "not to be viewed as either a new jeopardy or additional penalty for the earlier crimes," but rather "a stiffened penalty for the latest crime, which is considered to be an aggravated offense because a repetitive one." 334 U.S. at 732. That characterization confirms that the prior offense must be examined as a matter of law — by its elements — not relitigated as a matter of fact.

**II. Furlow's South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine does not categorically qualify as a "serious drug offense" or "controlled substance offense."**

Consistent with the foregoing, when Congress or the Sentencing Commission use a term denoting a category of prior state offenses, courts must ask whether the state statute of conviction necessarily involves the same conduct the federal definition targets — not whether Furlow's own conduct happened to satisfy it. This is precisely the method the Court employed in *United States v. Nardello*, 393 U.S. 286 (1969), when construing which state offenses fell within the Travel Act's reference to "extortion": the Court looked past labels to the generic understanding of the offense across the states, rather than to any single jurisdiction's idiosyncratic definition. 393 U.S. at 289–90. So too here: South Carolina's distribution statute, unlike the generic "distribution" contemplated by 21 U.S.C. § 841(a)(1) and USSG § 4B1.2(b), sweeps in conduct — including certain forms of simple transfer without the elements of commercial distribution — that the federal definition does not reach. Under *Jordan v. De George*, 341 U.S. 223 (1951), where a categorical classification is at issue, the classification must rest on what the state offense necessarily entails, not on the worst-case facts of a particular defendant's case. 341 U.S. at 227–29. Because the South Carolina statute is broader than the generic federal offense, the conviction cannot categorically serve as a predicate.

**III. Furlow's Georgia first-degree arson conviction does not categorically match the generic "arson" enumerated in the ACCA and the Guidelines.**

The same generic-offense methodology defeats reliance on the Georgia arson conviction. Both the ACCA and the Guidelines enumerate "arson" without further definition, requiring courts to identify the generic, contemporary meaning of that offense — the burning of a dwelling or structure under circumstances endangering habitation — and then to compare it to the elements of the Georgia statute. *Nardello*, 393 U.S. at 289–90; *Jordan*, 341 U.S. at 227. Georgia's first-degree arson statute, however, extends to burning of one's own property under conditions the generic offense does not require, and does not require the elements the generic definition demands. Because the Georgia statute punishes a broader swath of conduct than generic arson, it cannot categorically qualify — and under *Fong Haw Tan*, 333 U.S. at 10, and *Ladner*, 358 U.S. at 178, any doubt on that score must be resolved against the enhancement.

For these reasons, Furlow lacks the requisite predicate convictions, and his sentence must be vacated.
