**ARGUMENT**

**THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S “PREDOMINANTLY INTERNAL” STANDARD FOR EXEMPTION 2 VIOLATES THE TEXT, STRUCTURE, AND PURPOSE OF FOIA.**

The Freedom of Information Act mandates “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure,” *Dept. of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976), subject only to nine “explicitly exclusive” exemptions. *Dept. of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989). Exemption 2 shields matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This Court has squarely held that Exemption 2 “does not apply to matters subject to . . . a genuine and significant public interest.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 362-69.

The Ninth Circuit erred by adopting a “predominantly internal” test that rewrites the statute. The word “solely” in Exemption 2 “connects the phrase ‘internal personnel rules and practices’ to the whole of § 552(b)(2) only by the phrase ‘related solely to.’” *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051, 1056 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc) (Wilkey, J., dissenting). The modifier “predominantly” cannot supplant Congress’s chosen adverb “solely.” *See Schwaner v. Dept. of Air Force*, 898 F.2d 793, 795 (D.C. Cir. 1990) (acknowledging that “predominantly” is a “gloss” on the text). No authority supports substituting a looser standard for the statutory requirement that withheld material relate *only* to internal personnel matters.

The ESQD arcs at issue are maps and data “designed to be a long term planning tool” for ammunition storage and safety. They do not concern employment matters, parking, lunch hours, or sick leave—the “minor or trivial” items Congress contemplated for Exemption 2. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 363. Nor do they concern rules governing agency personnel conduct, such as an agency’s litigation strategy, *Schiller v. NLRB*, 964 F.2d 1205, 1208 (D.C. Cir. 1992), or training manuals for law enforcement agents, *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980). The ESQD arcs govern physical infrastructure, not personnel. As the dissent correctly observed, “information is not related to ‘internal personnel rules and practices of an agency’ simply because the agency uses it internally.” Pet. App. A-48.

The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to anchor its test in *Hardy* is misplaced. *Hardy* held that “law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation, are exempt under Exemption 2.” 631 F.2d at 656. But *Hardy* did not hold—and could not have held—that *non*-law enforcement materials wholly unrelated to personnel matters qualify for exemption. The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 to cover safety planning documents transforms a narrow exemption for “personnel rules and practices” into a general “internal agency information” exemption. That result cannot be squared with *Rose*, which emphasized that Exemption 2 “is not a blanket exemption for all internal matters.” *Maricopa Audubon Soc’y v. U.S. Forest Serv.*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085 (9th Cir. 1997).

The risk-of-circumvention prong cannot salvage the Ninth Circuit’s error. Even assuming that disclosure of the ESQD arcs could enable someone to circumvent safety regulations, that consideration is irrelevant unless the materials first satisfy the threshold statutory requirement of relating “solely” to internal *personnel* rules and practices. As the D.C. Circuit recognized, “the threshold inquiry” is whether the information “relates to an agency’s personnel rules and practices.” *Judicial Watch, Inc. v. FBI*, 522 F.3d 364, 366 (D.C. Cir. 2008). The Navy concedes that ESQD arcs contain data about blast ranges and explosive storage—information about *land and munitions*, not about *employees*.

Finally, the Ninth Circuit’s standard invites agencies to withhold any document they deem “predominantly internal” by merely asserting a risk of circumvention. That approach “defies the Supreme Court’s instruction that FOIA exemptions ‘must be narrowly construed.’” Pet. App. A-53. Congress provided Exemptions 1 and 7(F) to protect national security and physical safety. The Navy declined to classify these documents under Exemption 1 and did not meet Exemption 7(F)’s threshold requirement that documents be compiled for law enforcement purposes. The Ninth Circuit may not stretch Exemption 2 to cover what Congress specifically excluded from other exemptions.

This Court should reverse and hold that Exemption 2 does not apply to documents, like ESQD arcs, that relate to physical infrastructure and safety planning rather than “internal personnel rules and practices.”
