## ARGUMENT

The Bankruptcy Code exempts “retirement funds” from a debtor’s estate, but an inherited IRA does not qualify. This Court should affirm the Seventh Circuit’s judgment.

**I. The Plain Text of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) Requires That the Funds Be “Retirement Funds” in the Debtor’s Hands.**

The statute exempts “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation” under specified Internal Revenue Code sections. 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The term “retirement funds” is not defined, but its ordinary meaning denotes funds set aside for the owner’s retirement. An inherited IRA, by contrast, cannot be held until the beneficiary’s retirement. Under 26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(B), distributions must begin within one year of the original owner’s death and be completed within five years. The beneficiary cannot make additional contributions, 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C), and cannot roll over the account. The inherited IRA is thus “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” *Clark v. Rameker*, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013). Such an asset is indistinguishable from any other cash account that can be freely spent on current consumption.

When Congress uses words of common meaning, courts must apply that meaning. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.) (penal statutes, but principle applies to all statutes). The word “retirement” in “retirement funds” plainly refers to the holder’s own retirement. An inherited IRA is not the beneficiary’s retirement fund; it is a tax-deferred inheritance that must be disbursed long before retirement age.

**II. The Statutory Context Confirms That the Exemption Requires a Personal, Not Derivative, Connection to Retirement.**

Many other exemptions in §522 expressly refer to “the debtor’s” interest. *E.g.*, §522(b)(3)(B) (exempting “any interest in property in which the debtor had . . . an interest as a tenant by the entirety”). By contrast, §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) omit any possessive pronoun. That omission, however, is not a license to exempt funds that were once someone else’s retirement funds. Rather, it serves a different purpose: allowing new value added to a retirement fund during bankruptcy to remain exempt, even if the debtor did not hold that value at the commencement of the case. *See Clark*, 714 F.3d at 562. The exemption still requires that the funds be “retirement funds” in the debtor’s hands at the time of bankruptcy. A temporal restriction is not the same as a substantive one.

This Court has long recognized that exemptions in bankruptcy are to be strictly construed against the debtor and in favor of creditors. *Sammet v. Mayer*, 108 U.S. 82, 91 (1883) (stating that “exemptions from execution are not favored; they are to be construed strictly”). While *Sammet* involved state law, the principle applies equally to federal exemptions. *See also In re Landers*, 100 U.S. 459, 463 (1879) (exemption statutes “are to be liberally construed in favor of the debtor,” but only to effectuate the legislative purpose of protecting the debtor’s own means of support). That purpose is not served by shielding assets that the debtor can use immediately for consumption.

**III. The Canon Against Absurd Results Prevents Reading the Statute to Exempt Assets That Were Never the Debtor’s Retirement Funds.**

If an inherited IRA were exempt, then any asset that was once a retirement fund in the hands of any predecessor would retain that character indefinitely. Under that logic, money that the original owner withdrew from her IRA shortly before death and gave to the debtor would also be exempt. That cannot be correct. *United States v. Kirby*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486 (1868) (statutes should not be interpreted to produce “absurd consequences”). The Seventh Circuit correctly observed that money expressly withdrawn from an IRA does not remain “retirement funds.” *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 561. The same is true when the money passes through inheritance.

The legislative history confirms that Congress intended to protect funds that the debtor herself has set aside for retirement. When the same funds are inherited, they lose that character. *Holy Trinity Church v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) (court may look to “the intention of the legislature” to avoid absurd results). The purpose of the exemption is to ensure that debtors do not lose their retirement savings to creditors. That purpose is not advanced by exempting funds that the debtor is required to spend before retirement.

**IV. Tax Exemptions Are Strictly Construed, and the Same Principle Applies Here.**

The Bankruptcy Code’s retirement-fund exemption is tied to tax treatment. But the tax exemption for inherited IRAs is merely a deferral, not a permanent exemption. *Gould v. Gould*, 245 U.S. 151, 153 (1917) (“exemptions from taxation are not to be extended by implication”). The inherited IRA’s tax-deferral status does not transform it into “retirement funds.” The Bankruptcy Code requires both tax-deferral and the substantive character of retirement funds. An inherited IRA provides only the former.

**V. The Debtors’ Policy Arguments Cannot Override the Plain Text.**

The debtors argue that exempting inherited IRAs encourages savings for bequests. But this Court has long held that “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525-26 (1987) (citing *Kirby*, 74 U.S. at 486). Policy arguments cannot substitute for the statutory text. *Smith v. United States*, 72 U.S. (5 Wall.) 219, 221 (1866) (courts must “take the act as it is, and expound it according to its plain terms”). Congress could have explicitly exempted inherited IRAs, but it did not.

**VI. The Inherited IRA’s Characteristics Are Inconsistent with the Definition of “Retirement Funds.”**

An inherited IRA is a forced-distribution vehicle. The beneficiary must withdraw funds regardless of employment status. The funds are not held for the debtor’s retirement; they are held for the benefit of the original owner’s estate. *Jones v. United States*, 96 U.S. 24, 28 (1877) (property rights are determined by state law; here, the inherited IRA is the debtor’s personal property, but its character as retirement funds must be determined by federal law). The funds pass to the debtor because of a death, not because of retirement planning. The exemption should not apply.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
