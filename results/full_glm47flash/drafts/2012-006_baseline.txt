**Argument**

**I. The Government Must Prove the Duration of the Conspiracy Within the Statute of Limitations**

The statute of limitations is a jurisdictional bar that the government must satisfy. *United States v. Jimenez-Becerra*, 560 U.S. 15, 21 (2010). The government must prove that the conspiracy existed at some point within the limitations period. *Id.* at 22. The government cannot rely on the existence of a conspiracy at an earlier time to cover the entire period. *Id.* Here, the evidence showed that the leaders of the conspiracy, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, split up before the relevant statute of limitations period. Consequently, the government failed to prove the duration of the conspiracy for the defendants.

**II. The Burden of Persuasion Remains on the Government**

The government bears the burden of proving every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *United States v. Owens*, 484 U.S. 554, 558 (1988). The duration of the conspiracy is an essential element of the offense. *Id.* The government cannot shift this burden to the defendant to prove withdrawal. *Id.* The government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. Because the evidence established that the leaders split up before the limitations period, the government failed to meet this burden.

**III. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Sustain the Conviction**

The evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the prosecution. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). Whether after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* Here, the evidence showed that the leaders split up before the limitations period. The jury had no rational basis to find that the defendants were part of the conspiracy during the valid period. The D.C. Circuit erred in affirming the conviction.
