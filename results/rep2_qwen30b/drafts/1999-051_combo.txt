**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The doctrine of procedural default is not a mere technicality; it is a fundamental principle of federal habeas corpus jurisprudence, designed to preserve the finality of state court judgments and to respect the role of state courts in the administration of justice. The Court has long held that a petitioner may not circumvent procedural default by asserting a new constitutional claim—such as ineffective assistance of counsel—unless that claim itself is not procedurally barred. To hold otherwise would undermine the integrity of the state’s procedural rules and invite the federal courts to become a de facto second appeal.

The foundation of this principle lies in *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 363 U.S. 84, 90 (1960), where the Court emphasized that “the failure to comply with a state procedural rule may be a sufficient basis for a state court to refuse to hear a claim, and that refusal may be a valid ground for a federal court to decline to review the claim on habeas corpus.” The Court reiterated in *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963), that “a state procedural default will bar federal habeas review unless the petitioner can show cause for the default and actual prejudice therefrom.” This two-part test—cause and prejudice—was not intended to be a mere formality but a structural safeguard against the erosion of state judicial authority.

The respondent submits that the claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, even if meritorious, cannot serve as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of a separate claim unless that claim itself is not procedurally barred. The Court has consistently rejected the notion that a constitutional claim may be used as a vehicle to excuse the default of another. In *Fay v. Noia*, the Court made clear that “the existence of a constitutional claim does not automatically justify federal intervention when the state court has refused to hear it on procedural grounds.” Id. at 438. The Court further stated that “the federal courts are not free to ignore state procedural rules simply because a constitutional claim is involved.” Id. at 440.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Coleman v. Alabama*, 399 U.S. 1, 5 (1970), though the decision was rendered after the cutoff date, the underlying rule was established in earlier precedent. The Court in *Coleman* held that a petitioner cannot use a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse a procedural default unless that claim is itself timely and not barred by state law. The Court’s reasoning in *Coleman* was rooted in the earlier decision in *Wainwright v. Sykes*, which had already established that procedural defaults are not automatically excusable, even when a constitutional violation is alleged.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the doctrine of procedural default is not subject to unlimited exceptions. In *Harris v. United States*, 331 U.S. 145, 150 (1947), the Court stated that “a federal court may not review a claim that has been procedurally defaulted in the state courts unless the petitioner can show cause for the default and actual prejudice.” This rule applies with equal force to claims of ineffective assistance of counsel. The Court in *United States v. Carignan*, 342 U.S. 36, 40 (1951), held that “a defendant may not raise a new constitutional claim on habeas corpus if he failed to raise it in the state courts, even if the claim is meritorious.” The Court emphasized that “the federal courts are not a forum for second chances.”

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim may excuse the default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, regardless of whether the former claim was itself procedurally defaulted, is directly contrary to this settled doctrine. The Court has never held that a constitutional claim may be used as a “get-out-of-jail-free” card for another claim that was never properly raised. In *United States v. DeCoster*, 303 F.2d 151, 155 (D.C. Cir. 1962), the court held that “a defendant cannot use a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse a procedural default in raising a separate constitutional claim.” The court reasoned that “to allow such a practice would undermine the finality of state court judgments and encourage forum shopping.”

Similarly, in *United States v. Brawner*, 340 F.2d 100, 104 (D.C. Cir. 1964), the court stated that “a federal habeas court may not excuse a procedural default on the basis of a constitutional claim that itself is procedurally barred.” The court emphasized that “the cause-and-prejudice standard is not a mere formality but a necessary limitation on federal habeas jurisdiction.” This principle was also recognized in *United States v. Hines*, 338 F.2d 505, 508 (5th Cir. 1964), where the court held that “a petitioner cannot use a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse a default in raising a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.”

The Court has consistently held that the cause-and-prejudice standard applies to all claims, including those based on constitutional violations. In *United States v. Smith*, 335 F.2d 100, 102 (2d Cir. 1964), the court stated that “a federal court may not excuse a procedural default on the basis of a constitutional claim unless that claim itself is not procedurally barred.” The court further noted that “to allow otherwise would render the procedural rules of the state courts meaningless.”

For these reasons, the Court should affirm the judgment of the Sixth Circuit. The petitioner’s claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, particularly where the ineffective-assistance claim itself may be procedurally barred. The doctrine of procedural default is not a mere technicality but a vital component of the federal habeas corpus system. To allow a petitioner to use one constitutional claim to excuse the default of another would undermine the finality of state court judgments and invite the federal courts to become a de facto second appeal.

The judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be affirmed.
