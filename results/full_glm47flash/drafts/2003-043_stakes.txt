**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Statute of Limitations Applies Separately to the Partnership and the Individual Partners**

The IRS argues that its timely assessment of taxes against the Partnership extended the ten-year statute of limitations to the individual partners. This argument fails because the Internal Revenue Code treats partnerships and individual partners as distinct taxpayers, and a valid assessment requires the specific identification of the taxpayer.

Under 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), a “taxpayer” is “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1). Consequently, an individual partner is a separate “taxpayer” from the partnership itself. The statute of limitations for assessment is governed by 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), which provides a three-year period from the filing of the return. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) then extends the collection period to ten years from the date of assessment. Because the partners are distinct taxpayers, the assessment against the Partnership could not toll the limitations period applicable to the partners.

**II. A Valid Assessment Requires the Identification of the Taxpayer**

Furthermore, 26 U.S.C. § 6203 requires that an assessment “shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer in the office of the Secretary.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The implementing regulation clarifies that a valid assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The IRS failed to make an individualized assessment against the partners; it assessed only the Partnership. Therefore, the statutory ten-year period did not begin to run for the partners.

**III. Precedent Supports the Requirement of Individualized Assessments**

This interpretation is consistent with Ninth Circuit precedent. In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the court held that a tax lien does not attach to an individual's property unless the tax obligation has been assessed against that individual. The court stated, “this court is of the opinion that such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property... unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him.” 286 F.2d at 676. Similarly, the Seventh Circuit in *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), required assessments against both the partnership and the partners to extend the limitations period. The Ninth Circuit distinguishes *Wright* because the government had assessed the partners there. The Ninth Circuit also distinguishes *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), which only addressed a refund claim, not the collection period.

**CONCLUSION**

Because the IRS did not assess the partners individually, the ten-year collection period did not begin to run for them. Consequently, the bankruptcy court properly disallowed the IRS’s claims under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).
