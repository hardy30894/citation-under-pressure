ARGUMENT

A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot excuse the default of another habeas claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself has been properly exhausted and preserved in state court. That rule follows from the basic structure of federal habeas review, which requires a prisoner to present his federal claims through the state courts before seeking federal relief. Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204-05 (1950); Smith v. Bennett, 365 U.S. 708, 712-13 (1961); Newsom v. Smyth, 365 U.S. 604 (1961). A federal court may not use a claim that the state courts never had a fair opportunity to adjudicate as “cause” to reach a different, defaulted claim. See Darr, 339 U.S. at 204-05; Nowakowski v. Maroney, 386 U.S. 542 (1967).

1. Cause must itself be a valid, exhausted federal claim.  
This Court has long treated habeas as an extraordinary remedy available only after state remedies have been exhausted. Darr, 339 U.S. at 204-05; Smith, 365 U.S. at 712-13. That principle is not a technicality; it reflects comity and the “ordinary” rule that federal courts do not intervene until the state process has run its course. Darr, 339 U.S. at 204-05. If a petitioner could bypass that requirement by styling an unexhausted or defaulted assertion of ineffective assistance as “cause,” the exhaustion doctrine would be emptied of force. See Newsom, 365 U.S. 604; Smith, 365 U.S. 708.

The Court’s cases also show that habeas claims are evaluated in the procedural posture in which they are presented. In United States ex rel. McCann v. Adams, 320 U.S. 220, 222-23 (1943), the Court permitted habeas review only because the petitioner’s waiver issue “has never been adjudicated on its merits by the lower courts” and was “fairly tendered” below. Likewise, in Holiday v. Johnston, 313 U.S. 342 (1941), the Court emphasized that habeas review depends on the actual merits and record of the claim presented. A defaulted ineffective-assistance claim, never properly adjudicated in state court, is not a valid predicate for excusing another default.

2. Carpenter’s rule would collapse the default doctrine.  
The Sixth Circuit’s approach would allow a prisoner to evade a state procedural bar simply by recasting every default as attorney error. But this Court has never permitted federal habeas to proceed in that fashion. In Price v. Johnston, 334 U.S. 266 (1948), the Court made clear that habeas is not a device for endless relitigation of issues that could and should have been pursued earlier. In Darr, the Court likewise rejected any notion that federal courts should entertain habeas before the petitioner has properly exhausted his remedies. 339 U.S. at 204-05. If ineffective assistance could excuse any default regardless of whether the ineffectiveness claim itself was preserved, then every procedural default would become self-excusing whenever counsel had failed earlier in the process.

3. The Court’s custody cases do not support reaching a defaulted claim through an unexhausted one.  
Peyton v. Rowe, 391 U.S. 54 (1968), and Walker v. Wainwright, 390 U.S. 335 (1968), expanded access to habeas by allowing prisoners to challenge future or collateral restraints while still in custody. But those decisions did not dilute exhaustion or default principles. Peyton allowed a challenge to a later sentence because the petitioner was already “in custody,” 391 U.S. at 67; Walker rejected premature refusal to consider claims simply because other sentences remained outstanding, 390 U.S. at 336-37. Neither case suggests that a prisoner may rely on a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to resurrect a different defaulted claim.

4. Finality and comity require adherence to the state courts’ procedural ruling.  
This Court has repeatedly respected the state courts’ role in enforcing their own procedural rules. Smith, 365 U.S. at 712-13; Darr, 339 U.S. at 204-05. Nowakowski, 386 U.S. 542, and Long v. District Court of Iowa, Lee Cty., 385 U.S. 192 (1966), confirm that federal habeas procedure is carefully structured to ensure meaningful review, but only within established limits. Those limits include default and exhaustion. A petitioner who failed to present an ineffective-assistance claim properly in state court has not satisfied the prerequisites for using that claim as cause.

The better rule, consistent with this Court’s precedents, is straightforward: a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance cannot excuse the procedural default of another claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself has been properly exhausted and is not itself procedurally barred. Darr, 339 U.S. at 204-05; Smith, 365 U.S. at 712-13; Newsom, 365 U.S. 604; McCann, 320 U.S. at 222-23. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
