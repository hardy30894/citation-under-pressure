# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF § 1254a(f)(4) ESTABLISHES THAT A GRANT OF TPS SATISFIES § 1255(a)'S ADMISSION REQUIREMENT

Congress commanded that once TPS is granted, "the alien shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This Court has long recognized that "nonimmigrant status" is a status that, by definition, arises only upon admission to the United States. *See Landon v. Plasencia*, 459 U.S. 21, 25 (1982) (describing the admission process as the gateway through which nonimmigrant status is conferred). Section 1255(a) requires that an alien be "inspected and admitted or paroled." When Congress directs that TPS recipients be treated *as if* they hold nonimmigrant status, it necessarily directs that they be treated as if admitted, because one cannot hold nonimmigrant status without having been admitted in the first instance.

The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized this logic in *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954 (9th Cir. 2017), holding that "[b]y the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted." *Id.* at 960. The Sixth Circuit reached the identical conclusion in *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548 (6th Cir. 2013), holding that "an individual who has TPS has, by statutory definition, been 'admitted' as that term is defined by the statute." *Id.* at 553–54. These courts properly gave effect to the statutory fiction Congress created—one that operates precisely like the fiction this Court approved in *Fedorenko v. United States*, 449 U.S. 490 (1981), where Congress's use of deeming language was held to accomplish exactly what it said, without need for extratextual limitation.

This Court's admonition in *BedRoc Ltd. v. United States*, 541 U.S. 176 (2004), that "the preeminent canon of statutory interpretation requires us to 'presume that [the] legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there,'" *id.* at 183 (quoting *Conn. Nat'l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253–54 (1992)), compels the same result here. Congress said TPS recipients are "considered as being in ... lawful status as a nonimmigrant." § 1254a(f)(4). It did not qualify that direction by reference to the manner of the alien's original entry.

## II. THE THIRD CIRCUIT'S ARTIFICIAL DISTINCTION BETWEEN "STATUS" AND "ADMISSION" CANNOT BE RECONCILED WITH § 1254a(f)(4)'S DEEMING CLAUSE

The court below held that "admission" and "status" are "fundamentally distinct concepts," relying on the Fifth Circuit's reasoning in *Gomez v. Lynch*, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016). But that generic distinction cannot survive the specific statutory command of § 1254a(f)(4), which expressly deems TPS recipients to hold nonimmigrant status—a status that under the ordinary operation of the INA arises only from admission. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15) (defining nonimmigrant classifications by reference to admission categories).

This Court has cautioned against interpretations that render statutory language superfluous. *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) ("A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous, void, or insignificant."). If § 1254a(f)(4)'s deeming clause does no work toward satisfying § 1255(a)'s admission requirement, then Congress's decision to specifically invoke "nonimmigrant" status—rather than simply lawful presence—becomes inexplicable surplusage. The provision's entire function is to substitute for the ordinary admission process that TPS recipients, by definition, never underwent through inspection at a port of entry.

## III. THE STATUTORY PURPOSE OF TPS CONFIRMS THAT CONGRESS INTENDED TO PROVIDE A PATHWAY TO ADJUSTMENT

This Court has repeatedly instructed that statutes must be read in light of their remedial purpose. *See King v. Burwell*, 576 U.S. 473, 486 (2015) ("If the statutory language is plain, we must enforce it according to its terms.... But oftentimes the meaning—or ambiguity—of certain words or phrases may only become evident when placed in context."). TPS was enacted to provide humanitarian protection to nationals of countries suffering armed conflict or natural disaster. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Construing § 1254a(f)(4) to permit adjustment of status effectuates that humanitarian purpose by allowing individuals who have lawfully resided in the United States for years, often married to citizens or parents of citizens, to regularize their status rather than remain in perpetual limbo.

The Third Circuit's own citation to *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421 (1987), for the proposition that Congress acts intentionally when it includes language in one section but omits it in another, *id.* at 432, cuts against the decision below. Congress specifically chose to use the term "nonimmigrant status" in § 1254a(f)(4)—language with well-understood admission-based connotations—rather than a more generic phrase such as "lawful presence." This choice should be honored, not disregarded.

## IV. THIS COURT'S ADMISSION-STATUS FRAMEWORK IN ANALOGOUS CONTEXTS SUPPORTS PETITIONERS

In *Judulang v. Holder*, 565 U.S. 42 (2011), this Court rejected an agency's arbitrary distinctions among aliens based on procedural technicalities unrelated to statutory purpose, holding that the agency's approach was "not tied to the purposes of the immigration laws or the appropriate operation of the immigration system." *Id.* at 55. The Third Circuit's distinction here—treating identically situated TPS recipients differently based solely on the happenstance of how they originally entered the country decades earlier—suffers from the same defect condemned in *Judulang*. An alien who entered without inspection but was granted TPS is, for all functional purposes relevant to § 1255(a), in the same position as one who entered with a visa and later received TPS: both are nonimmigrants under § 1254a(f)(4).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and hold that a grant of Temporary Protected Status constitutes an admission for purposes of 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a).
