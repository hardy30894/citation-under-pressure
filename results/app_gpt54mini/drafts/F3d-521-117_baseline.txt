**Argument**

The Fourth Amendment permits a protective frisk only when officers have a reasonable, articulable basis to believe the particular person they are searching is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27, 30 (1968). That standard is individualized; police may not frisk simply because they are working a traffic stop in a high-crime neighborhood or because a passenger appears annoyed, verbally hostile, or nervous. *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85, 91–94 (1979); *Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 62–64 (1968). Here, the district court upheld the frisk of Michael Soares on far less than the Constitution requires. The handgun must therefore be suppressed.

I. **The officers lacked particularized reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous.**

A lawful frisk requires “specific reasonable inferences” drawn from the facts known to the officer, not a generalized hunch. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21, 27. The government relied on four facts: the stop occurred late at night in a high-crime area; the occupants made movements in the car before it stopped; Soares became agitated and used profanity; and he moved his hands despite commands to keep still. None of those facts, alone or together, established a reasonable belief that Soares was armed and dangerous.

First, the location and time of the stop do not justify a frisk. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected searches based on mere presence in a suspicious neighborhood. In *Brown v. Texas*, 443 U.S. 47, 52 (1979), the Court held that officers may not detain and search a person without “specific objective facts” linking that person to wrongdoing. Likewise, *Ybarra* held that a person’s presence at a location associated with criminal activity does not authorize a pat-down absent individualized suspicion. 444 U.S. at 91. A “high-crime area” may color the analysis, but it cannot supply the missing nexus between Soares and a weapon. *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119, 124 (2000).

Second, the occupants’ movements in the car were too ambiguous to justify a frisk of Soares. Officers described “furtive movements,” but the record shows only that the passengers shifted and reached down and left before the stop completed. Such movements may justify caution, but they do not automatically establish that a passenger is armed. This Court has recognized that nervous behavior and movement are “ambiguous” and must be assessed in context, not converted into probable cause or reasonable suspicion by hindsight. *United States v. McKoy*, 428 F.3d 38, 39–41 (1st Cir. 2005). Here, the officers saw no weapon, no bulge, no exchange, and no threat directed at them before the frisk began.

Third, Soares’s verbal hostility cannot support a frisk. The Supreme Court has made clear that “mere words” or belligerence do not create reasonable suspicion of dangerousness. *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 497–98 (1983). Nor may officers frisk a person simply because he is uncooperative or argumentative. *Sibron*, 392 U.S. at 62–64. Soares’s profanity and accusation of racial profiling were constitutionally protected expressions of displeasure, not evidence that he was armed.

Fourth, Soares’s hand movements after being ordered to stay still did not create a sufficient basis for the frisk. Officers may not bootstrap their own commands into reasonable suspicion. *Terry* requires objective facts suggesting danger, not escalation produced by police presence. 392 U.S. at 21–22. Soares’s movement occurred in the middle of a traffic stop, while officers stood around the vehicle with drawn or partially drawn guns, repeatedly ordering compliance. In that coercive setting, agitation was not surprising. The record does not show a reaching motion toward a weapon, an attempt to flee, or any prior knowledge that Soares was armed.

II. **The frisk was not justified by concerns for officer safety under the circumstances of this stop.**

The government will likely invoke officer safety, but *Terry* does not authorize a frisk whenever an officer “feels uneasy.” 392 U.S. at 21–27. The question is whether a reasonably prudent officer, given the specific facts, would believe the person was armed and dangerous. *Adams v. Williams*, 407 U.S. 143, 146 (1972). That standard was not met here.

The officers had already secured the driver, called for backup, and had multiple officers on scene when Soares was removed from the vehicle. The stop was for defective headlights, not a violent offense. The record contains no evidence that Soares threatened anyone, made a weapon-like gesture, or refused a lawful order in a way that suggested immediate access to a gun. The Supreme Court has warned against “sweeping intrusions” on the basis of generalized safety concerns. *Ybarra*, 444 U.S. at 93–94. That warning applies with special force to a passenger who was searched after being removed from a car without any individualized evidence of a weapon.

III. **Because the frisk violated the Fourth Amendment, the gun must be suppressed.**

Evidence obtained through an unconstitutional frisk is inadmissible. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 12; *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961). The district court’s denial of suppression therefore cannot stand. Because the firearm was the direct product of an unlawful pat-down, Soares’s conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) must be vacated.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded with instructions to grant the motion to suppress.
