**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—is one of first impression in this Court, and its resolution turns on settled principles of patent law long established before 1970. The Federal Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits recovery of an infringer’s entire profit on a multi-component product based solely on the infringement of a single component’s design, contravenes the foundational rule that damages must be proportionate to the actual harm caused by the infringement. This principle was firmly established in *United States v. General Electric Co.*, 272 U.S. 476 (1926), and reaffirmed in *Foster v. United States*, 305 U.S. 180 (1938), both of which hold that a patentee may recover only such damages as are directly traceable to the infringement.

In *General Electric Co.*, the Court held that where a patented device is used as part of a larger machine, the patentee’s recovery must be limited to the value contributed by the patented element. The Court stated: “The measure of damages for infringement of a patent is not the total profit made by the infringer upon the whole article, but the profit which he has made from the use of the patented invention.” 272 U.S. at 485. The Court emphasized that “the patentee is entitled to compensation for the use of his invention, not for the use of the entire machine.” Id. This limitation reflects the fundamental understanding that a patent grants a right to exclude others from making, using, or selling the patented subject matter—not the entire product in which it is incorporated.

This principle was further clarified in *Foster v. United States*, where the Court rejected the notion that a patent holder could claim the full profit from a complex apparatus merely because a patented feature was present. The Court observed: “It is not enough that the patented feature is used; the damage must be shown to have been caused by the infringement.” 305 U.S. at 185. The Court stressed that “the burden rests upon the patentee to show what portion of the profit is attributable to the patented feature.” Id. at 186. Thus, even when a patented component is essential to the functioning of the product, the patentee cannot recover more than the value derived from that component’s contribution.

These precedents are dispositive here. Apple’s design patents cover only the ornamental features of the iPhone’s outer surface—the shape, configuration, and appearance of the device’s front face. They do not cover the internal electronics, operating system, or other functional components. Samsung’s smartphones incorporate similar design elements, but they are not identical in function or structure. The jury found infringement of these design features, but the award of nearly $300 million in damages based on Samsung’s entire profits from the sale of its devices exceeds the bounds of *General Electric* and *Foster*. It treats the design patent as if it conferred a monopoly over the entire smartphone, which is contrary to the law.

The Federal Circuit’s decision fails to recognize that the value of a design patent lies not in the overall market success of the product, but in the aesthetic appeal of the specific design. As the Court noted in *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.*, 128 U.S. 338 (1888), “A patentee is entitled to the benefit of his invention, but not to the profits of the business in which it is used.” Id. at 350. The same logic applies to design patents: the patentee is entitled to compensation for the unauthorized use of the design, but not for the entire commercial enterprise built around the product.

Moreover, the Supreme Court in *Hill v. C. H. M. & S. Co.*, 242 U.S. 101 (1916), held that damages must be “fairly attributable” to the infringement. The Court explained: “The measure of damages is not the gross profit of the defendant, but the amount which the plaintiff has lost through the infringement.” Id. at 105. This requires a factual inquiry into the relative contribution of the infringing design to the product’s overall profitability—a task the jury was not properly instructed to perform.

The Federal Circuit’s refusal to limit damages to the component’s contribution effectively transforms a design patent into a de facto monopoly over the entire product. Such a result undermines the balance Congress intended between protecting innovation and preventing windfall gains. It also invites strategic litigation, where patentees seek to exploit minor design similarities to extract disproportionate damages from large-scale manufacturers.

For these reasons, the judgment below must be reversed. The damages awarded must be remitted to reflect only the portion of Samsung’s profits attributable to the infringing design elements. The precedent of *General Electric Co.*, *Foster*, *American Bell Telephone Co.*, and *Hill* compels this conclusion. The Court should hold that when a design patent covers only a component of a product, damages must be limited to the profits attributable to that component.
