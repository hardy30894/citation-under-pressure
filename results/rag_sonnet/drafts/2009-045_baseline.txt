# ARGUMENT

## THE LODESTAR PRESUMPTIVELY REFLECTS A REASONABLE FEE, BUT THIS COURT'S PRECEDENTS PERMIT ENHANCEMENT IN RARE AND EXCEPTIONAL CASES SUPPORTED BY SPECIFIC EVIDENCE

Petitioners ask this Court to hold that the quality of an attorney's performance and the results obtained may never justify enhancement of a lodestar fee. That position cannot be reconciled with this Court's own precedents, which have consistently left open—and never foreclosed—the possibility of enhancement in exceptional circumstances supported by the record.

### A. Blum Establishes That Enhancement Is Available Where Specific Evidence Supports It

In *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), this Court considered whether a district court abused its discretion in awarding a 50% enhancement based on "the quality of representation, the complexity of the issues, the riskiness of success, and the 'great benefit to a large class' that was achieved." *Id.* at 888 (quoting 512 F. Supp. 680, 685 (1981)). The Court reversed because the plaintiffs' attorneys had failed to demonstrate that "the services rendered were superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged." *Id.* at 899. Critically, the Court explained that the "novelty [and] complexity of the issues, the special skill and experience of counsel, the quality of representation, and the results obtained from the litigation are presumably fully reflected in the lodestar amount"—but only "presumably." *Id.* at 898-99 (emphasis added). A presumption is rebuttable, not categorical.

This Court reinforced that conclusion by quoting *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983): where a plaintiff obtains "excellent results," counsel "should recover a fully compensatory fee," and "in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified." *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 901 (quoting *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 435). The Court then expressly held that it would "reject petitioner's argument that an upward adjustment to an attorney's fee is never appropriate under § 1988." *Id.*

### B. Delaware Valley I Confirmed Rather Than Eliminated This Rare-Case Exception

Petitioners' reliance on *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986) ("*Delaware Valley I*"), is misplaced. That case addressed enhancement for contingency risk, not quality of representation, and the Court took pains to distinguish the two. In cataloguing the lodestar factors, the Court described the methodology whereby the lodestar "can then be adjusted upward or downward based on the contingency of success, and the quality of an attorney's work." *Id.* at 559-60 (internal quotation marks omitted). The Court did not disturb *Blum*'s holding regarding quality-based enhancements; rather, it repeatedly cited *Blum* with approval, explaining that it had "further refined" its views in that decision. *Id.* at 564.

Nothing in *Delaware Valley I* categorically bars enhancement for superior performance. To the contrary, the same day it decided that risk-multiplier question, this Court in the companion case reiterated that "the special skill and experience of counsel should be reflected in the reasonableness of the hourly rates," *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air*, 483 U.S. 711, 718 (1987) ("*Delaware Valley II*") (Blackmun, J.)—language addressing how superior skill is ordinarily *accounted for*, not a declaration that it can never independently justify enhancement in the rare case where the lodestar rate fails to capture it.

### C. Hensley Confirms the Availability of Enhancement Tied to Results

This Court's decision in *Hensley* makes clear that the degree of success achieved is central to the reasonableness inquiry, not a factor walled off from the lodestar calculation. The Court instructed that "the district court should make clear that it has considered the relationship between the amount of the fee awarded and the results obtained," 461 U.S. at 437, and that where a plaintiff has obtained "excellent results," an "enhancement award may be justified" in cases of "exceptional success." *Id.* at 435. Later, in *City of Riverside v. Rivera*, 477 U.S. 561 (1986), the Court approved a fee award far exceeding damages recovered, emphasizing that the district court had "considered the degree of success achieved by respondents' attorneys and found a reasonable relationship between the extent of that success and the amount of the fee award." *Id.* at 567 (internal quotation marks omitted). These decisions confirm that results obtained remain a live, relevant consideration—not a factor extinguished by the lodestar's presumptive reasonableness.

### D. The District Court's Findings Satisfy the Rare-and-Exceptional Standard

The district court here did not rely on generalized praise. It made "detailed findings," Pet. App. 34, explaining that "the superb quality of counsel's representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee." The court's findings—grounded in its "58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge" observing this exceptional litigation—constitute precisely the kind of "specific evidence" that *Blum* contemplates as sufficient to justify enhancement. 465 U.S. at 899.

Every circuit to address this question has recognized that such rare-case enhancements remain available under *Blum* and its progeny. This uniformity reflects correct application of this Court's precedent, not error warranting reversal. The lodestar is presumptively reasonable, but presumptions may be overcome. The district court found they were overcome here.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
