## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in dismissing this case for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The commercial activity exception to the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (“FSIA”), 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), applies because (1) the Government of Punjab engaged in “commercial activity” by sending a representative to the United States to offer Beg an alternative parcel of land, and (2) the expropriation itself, when combined with the subsequent failure to compensate, caused a “direct effect in the United States.”

### I. The Offer of Alternative Land in the United States Constitutes Commercial Activity

The FSIA defines “commercial activity” as “either a regular course of commercial conduct or a particular commercial transaction or act.” 28 U.S.C. § 1603(d). The character of the activity is determined by reference to “the nature of the course of conduct or particular transaction or act, rather than by reference to its purpose.” *Id.* The Supreme Court has held that a foreign sovereign engages in commercial activity when it acts “in the manner of a private player within the market,” not when it exercises “powers peculiar to sovereigns.” *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992) (quotation marks omitted). Expropriation of property for a public purpose is typically a sovereign act. *See* *Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina*, 965 F.2d 699, 712 (9th Cir. 1992) (expropriation is “regardless of the State’s motivation . . . a uniquely sovereign act”). However, the subsequent offer of an alternative land parcel—made by a representative of the Government of Punjab who traveled to the United States—is a distinct transaction. That offer was a commercial negotiation akin to a private land swap. The purpose of the offer (resolving a dispute) does not matter; the *nature* of the act—a proposal to exchange property—is the type of activity a private party could undertake. *See* *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614-15 (foreign government’s issuance of bonds to refinance debt was commercial because it was “the type of lending that a private bank might engage in”); *Millicom Int’l v. Republic of Costa Rica*, 70 F.4th 1320, 1328 (11th Cir. 2023) (commercial activity includes “contracts, agreements, and dealings with private parties”).

Moreover, the offer was made in the United States, satisfying the exception’s first clause: “commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Government of Punjab’s representative physically entered the United States to negotiate the exchange. This act alone constitutes “commercial activity” within the United States. *See* *O’Bryan v. Holy See*, 556 F.3d 361, 380 (6th Cir. 2009) (negotiations in the United States can constitute commercial activity).

### II. The Expropriation and Failure to Compensate Caused a Direct Effect in the United States

Even if the initial expropriation were sovereign, the combined conduct—taking Beg’s property and then refusing to compensate him—caused a “direct effect in the United States.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2) (third clause). A direct effect is one that “follows as an immediate consequence of the defendant’s activity.” *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618 (cleaned up). The effect need not be “substantial” or “foreseeable,” but must be “more than a mere ‘remote’ or ‘fortuitous’ consequence.” *Adler v. Federal Republic of Nigeria*, 219 F.3d 869, 876 (9th Cir. 2000).

Here, Beg is a resident of the United States. The expropriation deprived him of property worth $10 million, and the subsequent refusal to compensate him left him with a financial loss that he feels in his U.S. bank accounts and investments. The failure to pay compensation for taken property is a “direct effect” on the owner’s pocketbook. *See* *De Sanchez v. Banco Central de Nicaragua*, 770 F.2d 1385, 1393 (5th Cir. 1985) (failure to pay on a certificate of deposit held by a U.S. resident caused a direct effect in the United States). The Supreme Court has recognized that a breach of a financial obligation to a U.S. resident creates a direct effect because the “money that should have been paid” is not received in the United States. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618-19. The same logic applies here: Beg was entitled to compensation for his land, and the Pakistani government’s failure to pay that compensation directly affects his U.S. financial interests.

Additionally, the offer of alternative land—made in the United States—was itself a direct effect of the expropriation. The offer was an attempt to resolve the dispute, and when it was later rejected by the Supreme Court of Pakistan, Beg was left without any remedy. The initial offer in the United States was a “but for” cause of the litigation; the district court should have considered the entire chain of events. *See* *Frolova v. Union of Soviet Socialist Republics*, 761 F.2d 370, 374 (7th Cir. 1985) (direct effect may be found where the foreign state’s conduct “creates a legal obligation in the United States”). Here, the offer created a legal relationship between Beg and the Government of Punjab that was negotiated and partially performed (by the Lahore High Court’s acceptance) in the United States.

### III. The District Court Erred in Failing to Consider the “Commercial Activity” Exception Holistically

The district court treated the expropriation as a single, sovereign act and ignored the subsequent commercial dealings. Courts must examine “the entire course of conduct” to determine whether the exception applies. *See* *Hanil Bank v. PT. Bank Negara Indonesia*, 148 F.3d 1274, 1277 (11th Cir. 1998) (commercial activity exception requires a “nexus” between the suit and the commercial activity). The Government of Punjab’s offer—made in the United States through a representative—is a separate commercial transaction. The FSIA does not require that the entire controversy be commercial; only that the claim “is based upon” a commercial activity. *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 356 (1993) (the “based upon” language requires a “causal link” between the commercial activity and the plaintiff’s injury). Beg’s claim for compensation is based on the expropriation *and* the subsequent failure to honor the offer of alternative land. The offer was a commercial act, and it is the basis for his claim that he is entitled to monetary compensation.

### CONCLUSION

The district court erred in dismissing for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. This Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.
