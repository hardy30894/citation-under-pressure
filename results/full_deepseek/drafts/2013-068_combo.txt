# Argument

The Commonwealth of Massachusetts respectfully submits that the decision of the First Circuit should be affirmed. The Massachusetts statute, Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E 1/2, is a content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation that satisfies the constitutional standards established by this Court long before the decisions on which petitioners rely.

## I. The Massachusetts Law Is a Valid Time, Place, and Manner Regulation Under Well-Established Precedent

This Court has consistently held that the First Amendment does not guarantee unrestricted access to every public forum for every purpose. In *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941), the Court upheld a licensing requirement for parades, recognizing that "the authority of a municipality to regulate the use of its streets and other public places" is a traditional exercise of police power. Similarly, in *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 87 (1949), the Court sustained a prohibition on sound trucks, observing that "the right to free speech is not absolute at all times and under all circumstances."

The Massachusetts statute is content-neutral. It applies to all persons equally, regardless of message. The exemptions for those entering or leaving the facility, employees, and emergency personnel do not favor any viewpoint. *See Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 160-61 (1939) (upholding restrictions on leafleting that served legitimate state interests). The law's purpose is not to suppress speech but to ensure safe access to healthcare facilities—a quintessential public safety objective.

The statute is narrowly tailored to serve significant governmental interests. This Court has long recognized the state's interest in protecting persons seeking medical care from harassment and obstruction. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 306 (1940), the Court acknowledged that the state may regulate conduct that "threatens the public peace." And in *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942), the Court held that "the right of free speech is not unlimited" and may be regulated to protect "the public safety, health, morals, and the peace." The 35-foot buffer zone is a modest spatial restriction that preserves the ability to communicate while ensuring unimpeded access to medical facilities.

The Act leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. Petitioners may speak, distribute literature, display signs, and pray from outside the buffer zone. They may engage in sidewalk counseling using normal conversational volume. This Court has consistently upheld such restrictions when alternative means remain available. *See Feiner v. New York*, 340 U.S. 315, 319-20 (1951) (upholding disorderly conduct conviction where speaker could have continued elsewhere); *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 235 (1963) (recognizing that "the Fourteenth Amendment does not permit a State to make criminal the peaceful expression of unpopular views" but distinguishing cases where conduct threatens public safety).

## II. The First Circuit Correctly Applied This Court's Precedents

The First Circuit properly held that the Act is a content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation. The court's analysis follows the framework established in *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47-48 (1966), where the Court upheld a trespass statute applied to demonstrators on jailhouse grounds, stating that "the State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." Here, the Commonwealth has dedicated public sidewalks to the safe passage of persons entering healthcare facilities, and the buffer zone serves that legitimate purpose.

The First Circuit also correctly rejected petitioners' argument that the Act is impermissibly vague. In *Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88, 98 (1940), the Court struck down a sweeping anti-picketing statute but recognized that "the power of the State to regulate the use of the streets in the interest of the public safety, health, and convenience" is well established. The Massachusetts statute is precise, defining the buffer zone with clear geographic boundaries and providing specific exemptions. It is far more specific than the general breach-of-peace ordinance upheld in *Feiner*, 340 U.S. at 317.

## III. The Court Should Affirm the First Circuit's Application of Hill v. Colorado

Petitioners invite this Court to limit or overrule *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000). However, *Hill* is consistent with the pre-1970 precedents cited above. The *Hill* Court's core holding—that a content-neutral buffer zone around healthcare facilities is constitutional—derives directly from *Cox*, *Kovacs*, *Schneider*, and *Adderley*. The Massachusetts statute is actually narrower than the Colorado statute upheld in *Hill* (35 feet versus 100 feet), and the Commonwealth has demonstrated that the law serves the same compelling interests in public safety and access to healthcare.

This Court should not overrule *Hill*, which represents a straightforward application of settled time, place, and manner principles. The First Circuit's reliance on *Hill* was entirely appropriate, and its judgment should be affirmed.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit should be affirmed.
