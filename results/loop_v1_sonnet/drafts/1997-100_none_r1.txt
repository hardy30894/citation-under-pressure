# ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has held that the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal" and guards against multiple punishments for the same offense. North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That protection is not a mere technicality of criminal procedure; it is, as this Court held in Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), a guaranty "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," binding on California with the same force it binds the federal government. The decision below—holding that this fundamental guaranty simply does not extend to noncapital sentence-enhancement proceedings—cannot be reconciled with the principles animating this Court's double jeopardy decisions, none of which confines the Clause to the adjudication of guilt on the underlying substantive offense.

## I. A Finding That the Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove the Prior-Conviction Allegation Is the Functional Equivalent of an Acquittal and Forecloses Retrial.

The Court of Appeal held that the evidence failed to establish, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Monge personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury in his prior offense. That ruling is indistinguishable in substance from a judgment of acquittal, and this Court has consistently treated such judgments as final regardless of whether the reasoning underlying them was correct. In United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), this Court held that a verdict of acquittal is final and cannot be reviewed "without putting him twice in jeopardy, and thereby violating the Constitution." The Court reaffirmed that rule in Fong Foo v. United States, 369 U.S. 141 (1962), holding that a judgment of acquittal bars further prosecution even where the trial court erred as a matter of law in reaching it. And in Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100 (1904), the Court held that the government may not obtain appellate correction of an acquittal, because doing so would subject the defendant to a second jeopardy on the same charge.

These decisions rest on a principle of enduring force: once a tribunal charged with weighing the proof has found it wanting, the government does not get a second opportunity to marshal better evidence. See Sealfon v. United States, 332 U.S. 575 (1948) (double jeopardy principles bar relitigation of facts already resolved against the government). There is no principled basis for abandoning that rule simply because the factual predicate at issue enhances a sentence rather than establishes an element of the charged offense. The consequence to the defendant—years added to a term of imprisonment—is no less severe than a conviction on a separate count, and the government's incentive to relitigate a losing issue is no less real.

## II. The Multiple-Punishment Component of the Double Jeopardy Clause Independently Bars Retrial.

The Double Jeopardy Clause does not merely bar successive prosecutions after acquittal; it also protects against multiple punishments for the same offense. Pearce, 395 U.S. at 717; Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874) (once a defendant has been subjected to lawful punishment, the Constitution forbids imposing a second, additional punishment for the same offense). That protection applies with full force where the "offense" is the enhancement allegation itself—an allegation that California's own procedures require the State to prove beyond a reasonable doubt, through an adversarial proceeding affording the rights of confrontation and cross-examination. See Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967) (due process requires that a defendant subject to a factfinding proceeding materially affecting the length of confinement be afforded the right "to be confronted with witnesses against him [and] to cross-examine" them). Having failed once to carry that burden, the State may not simply try again.

## III. The Policies Underlying the Clause Confirm That It Applies Here.

This Court has explained that "the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity, as well as enhancing the possibility that even though innocent he may be found guilty." Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957). That concern applies with equal force to a defendant twice hauled before a court to defend against the same prior-conviction allegation, particularly where—as here—the State's initial proof already failed a searching, trial-like adjudication bearing every hallmark of a criminal trial: a reasonable-doubt standard, application of the rules of evidence, and the right of confrontation and cross-examination. Having voluntarily structured its enhancement proceeding to mirror a trial on guilt or innocence in every material respect, California cannot now disclaim the double jeopardy consequences that attend such a proceeding merely by attaching the label "sentencing" to it.

## CONCLUSION

Because the Court of Appeal's insufficiency ruling functions as an acquittal, and because the Double Jeopardy Clause's protections against successive prosecution and multiple punishment for the same offense are not confined to adjudication of the substantive charge, this Court should hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation and reverse the judgment of the California Supreme Court.
