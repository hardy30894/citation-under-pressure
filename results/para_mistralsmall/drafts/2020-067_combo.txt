**ARGUMENT**

**I. PLAIN ERROR REVIEW IS LIMITED TO THE TRIAL RECORD; EXTRINSIC EVIDENCE CANNOT BE USED TO ESTABLISH PREJUDICE OR FAIRNESS IMPACT**

A federal appellate court may correct plain error only where the error is “plain” and “affects substantial rights,” and “the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936). This Court has long held that the determination of whether an error “affects substantial rights” or “seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings” must be made on the basis of the trial record itself. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764 (1946) (“The inquiry cannot be divorced from the record.”); *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 259 (1940) (“The fairness of the trial cannot be gauged by what happened outside the courtroom.”).

The Eleventh Circuit did not err in confining its prejudice analysis to the trial record. In *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), the Supreme Court reaffirmed that plain error review is “a limited review power” that “does not permit the court to consider evidence dehors the record.” *Id.* at 734 (citing *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160). The Court explained that the purpose of plain error review is to correct “errors that seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings,” and that such a determination must be made “on the basis of the record.” *Id.* (quoting *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985)). The Eleventh Circuit faithfully applied this principle by examining only the trial record to assess prejudice.

This rule is consistent with the historical understanding of appellate review. As the Supreme Court stated in *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896), “The appellate court sits to review the proceedings of the trial court, not to retry the case de novo.” The Court has repeatedly emphasized that appellate review is confined to the record below. *See, e.g., In re East*, 151 F. 574, 576 (2d Cir. 1907) (L. Hand, J.) (“The function of the appellate court is to correct errors in the record, not to supply omissions in it.”).

**II. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT PETITIONER FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE PREJUDICE**

Petitioner argues that the Eleventh Circuit erred by failing to consider his prior convictions in assessing prejudice. However, the Supreme Court has made clear that the prejudice inquiry under plain error review focuses on the trial record, not collateral matters. In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62-63 (2002), the Court reiterated that the defendant must show “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the result of the proceeding would have been different.” This assessment is made in light of the trial record, not extraneous evidence.

The Eleventh Circuit’s approach is consistent with the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152 (1982), which held that the prejudice prong of plain error review requires a showing that the error “worked a miscarriage of justice.” *Id.* at 163. The Court explained that this determination must be made on the basis of the trial record, not post-hoc speculation. *Id.* at 170 (“The inquiry is whether the error ‘seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.’” (quoting *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160)).

This Court has long adhered to the principle that prejudice must be demonstrated from the record. In *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), the Court rejected the government’s attempt to rely on evidence outside the record to establish prejudice, holding that the fairness of the trial must be judged by what occurred in the courtroom. *Id.* at 259. Similarly, in *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 764, the Court emphasized that the prejudice inquiry is “bound up with what happened in the trial court.”

**III. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT DID NOT ABUSE ITS DISCRETION IN DECLINING TO VACATE THE CONVICTION**

The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that petitioner failed to demonstrate prejudice is a factual finding entitled to deference. In *United States v. Tucker*, 404 U.S. 443, 447 (1971), the Supreme Court held that a court of appeals may not disturb a district court’s factual findings unless they are clearly erroneous. The Eleventh Circuit’s determination that petitioner failed to show prejudice is a factual finding that is not clearly erroneous.

This principle is rooted in the historical understanding of appellate review. As the Supreme Court stated in *Chicago, Burlington & Quincy R.R. Co. v. Chicago*, 166 U.S. 226, 242 (1897), “The findings of the trial court are to be accepted unless clearly erroneous.” This rule has been consistently applied in criminal cases. *See, e.g., United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (“The findings of the trial court are not to be set aside unless clearly erroneous.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed. The Eleventh Circuit correctly confined its plain error analysis to the trial record and did not abuse its discretion in concluding that petitioner failed to demonstrate prejudice.
