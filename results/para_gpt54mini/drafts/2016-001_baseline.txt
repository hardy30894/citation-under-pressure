**Argument**

The Patent Act does not permit a design-patent plaintiff to recover the infringer’s entire profits when the patented design is only a component of a larger product and the design is not the basis of the product’s value. Section 289 authorizes recovery of the “total profit” from an “article of manufacture,” but it does not say that the relevant article is always the end product sold to consumers. 35 U.S.C. § 289. The statute’s text, history, and this Court’s precedent confirm that damages must be tied to the particular article of manufacture that incorporates the patented design. *Samsung Elecs. Co. v. Apple Inc.*, 580 U.S. 53, 137 S. Ct. 429, 436–37 (2016); *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439, 444–46 (1885); *Whitman Saddle Co. v. Buffum*, 145 F. 466, 467–68 (1st Cir. 1906).

This Court has already rejected the notion that design-patent profits may be awarded untethered to the patented article. In *Samsung*, the Court held that “an article of manufacture” under § 289 “need not be the end product sold to the consumer but may be only a component of that product.” 137 S. Ct. at 432. The Court explained that, because a product may contain multiple articles of manufacture, “the relevant ‘article of manufacture’” for § 289 purposes can be something less than the whole product. Id. at 436. That holding necessarily forecloses an automatic award of total profits on the entire finished product whenever the patent covers only part of it.

The historical development of § 289 points the same way. The predecessor statutes were enacted to override *Dobson*, which had required apportionment and had limited recovery to profits attributable to the patented design. *Dobson*, 114 U.S. at 444–46; *Samsung*, 137 S. Ct. at 433–35. But Congress’s response was not to eliminate all limits on design-patent damages. It replaced the *Dobson* framework with a rule authorizing recovery of the infringer’s “total profit” from the relevant “article of manufacture.” Act of Feb. 4, 1887, ch. 105, § 1, 24 Stat. 387; 35 U.S.C. § 289. The statutory word “article” does work. As this Court explained, it identifies the thing to which total profits are attached; it does not erase the question what the article is in a multi-component product. *Samsung*, 137 S. Ct. at 436–37.

Nor does *Nike, Inc. v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 138 F.3d 1437, 1440–41 (Fed. Cir. 1998), support awarding a patentee all profits from a complex product merely because the infringing design appears on a part of it. *Nike* recognized that § 289 is a special remedy, but it did not hold that the article of manufacture is always the consumer product as sold. Id. at 1440. Indeed, once the Court in *Samsung* confirmed that the article may be a component, *Nike* cannot be read to compel whole-product recovery in every case. 137 S. Ct. at 436–37.

A rule of whole-product profit recovery for a component design is also inconsistent with basic principles of patent damages. Patent remedies are meant to compensate for the invention, not to confer a windfall. *Aro Mfg. Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co.*, 377 U.S. 476, 507–08 (1964); *Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co.*, 225 U.S. 604, 617–18 (1912). Where the patented design covers only a portion of a smartphone, tablet, or other multifeatured device, awarding all of the device’s profits assumes, contrary to reality, that the design accounts for the value of the entire product. This Court has repeatedly rejected such overbroad recoveries in patent cases. *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884); *Westinghouse*, 225 U.S. at 617–18.

The better reading of § 289 is that when the patented design is applied only to a component of a multicomponent product, the factfinder must determine the relevant article of manufacture and award total profits only on that article. *Samsung*, 137 S. Ct. at 436–37. That approach respects the statutory text, preserves Congress’s abrogation of *Dobson* without converting design patents into monopolies over entire products, and avoids the “absurd” result that a small ornamental feature could capture profits generated by unrelated technology, brand, and function. *Id.* at 436; *Garretson*, 111 U.S. at 121.

This Court should therefore reverse the judgment below and hold that § 289 limits recovery to the profits attributable to the infringed article of manufacture—not the infringer’s total profits on the finished product whenever the patented design covers only a component.
