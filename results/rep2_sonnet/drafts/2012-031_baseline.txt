# ARGUMENT

## I. RULE 23(b)(3) REQUIRES ADMISSIBLE EVIDENCE THAT DAMAGES ARE SUSCEPTIBLE OF MEASUREMENT ON A CLASS-WIDE BASIS, AND THE COURTS BELOW ERRONEOUSLY DISPENSED WITH THAT REQUIREMENT

Rule 23(b)(3) permits certification only where "questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that Rule 23 is not a mere pleading standard but demands rigorous factual scrutiny. In *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338 (2011), this Court held that "Rule 23 does not set forth a mere pleading standard," and that a party seeking certification "must affirmatively demonstrate his compliance with the Rule." *Id.* at 350. Certification is proper only after "rigorous analysis" establishes that the prerequisites of Rule 23 have been satisfied. *Id.* (quoting *General Telephone Co. of Southwest v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982)).

Critically, this rigorous analysis "will frequently entail overlap with the merits of the plaintiff's underlying claim." *Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 351. That overlap is not a reason to relax scrutiny at certification; it is a reason to intensify it. When, as here, a plaintiff's theory of class-wide antitrust impact and damages rests on a particular economic methodology, the district court must determine—based on admissible evidence—whether that methodology can actually measure damages attributable to the plaintiffs' theory of liability, as opposed to damages arising from other, non-common causes.

The Third Circuit's decision below permitted certification without requiring that plaintiffs' damages model be tied to their sole surviving theory of antitrust impact. The District Court had narrowed the case to a single theory—that Comcast's clustering "deter[red] the entry of overbuilders in the Philadelphia DMA." *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 178 (E.D. Pa. 2010). Yet plaintiffs' expert, Dr. James McClave, offered a regression model that calculated damages based on four distinct theories of anticompetitive conduct—not merely the overbuilder-deterrence theory that survived. The model made no attempt to isolate damages attributable to the one theory the District Court accepted from damages attributable to the three theories it rejected.

This defect is fatal under this Court's antitrust precedents governing proof of damages. In *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100 (1969), this Court held that a plaintiff must prove "the fact of damage" with evidence connecting the alleged antitrust violation to the injury claimed, and that damages calculations must be tied to the specific theory of liability at issue. Likewise, in *Comcast's* own trial context, the same principle that undergirds *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), requires that expert testimony "fit" the facts of the case—meaning there must be a "valid scientific connection to the pertinent inquiry." *Id.* at 591-92. A methodology that cannot distinguish damages caused by a viable theory from damages caused by rejected theories does not "fit" the case and cannot constitute admissible evidence supporting predominance.

The Third Circuit majority acknowledged that Dr. McClave's model "assumes the validity of the four theories of antitrust impact initially claimed by plaintiffs." Pet. App. 21a. It nevertheless held that any resulting imprecision "goes to the weight of the evidence," not its admissibility, and that "at the class certification stage we do not require that Plaintiffs tie each theory of antitrust impact to an exact calculation of damages." Pet. App. 22a. This holding cannot be reconciled with *Wal-Mart*'s command that Rule 23 findings must be made, not assumed, and that plaintiffs must "actually prove"—not merely allege—that common questions predominate. 564 U.S. at 350-51.

## II. A DAMAGES METHODOLOGY THAT DOES NOT MEASURE DAMAGES ATTRIBUTABLE TO THE ACCEPTED THEORY OF LIABILITY CANNOT SATISFY PREDOMINANCE

Predominance requires more than a showing that some common questions exist; it requires that common questions "predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). Where the only common evidence of damages conflates a valid theory with three invalid ones, the individualized inquiry necessary to disentangle them overwhelms any common proof. The Third Circuit's own precedent, *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), instructed that a district court must undertake a "thorough examination of the factual and legal allegations," *id.* at 317, and must resolve factual disputes relevant to certification by a preponderance of the evidence, not by "assuming" a model's validity, *id.* at 318. The panel majority's approach—crediting a damages model without requiring it to correspond to the certified theory of liability—cannot be squared with *Hydrogen Peroxide*'s own "rigorous analysis" standard, let alone with *Wal-Mart*.

This Court's decision in *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564 (1985), does not rescue the decision below. *Anderson* forbids appellate reweighing of properly admitted evidence, *id.* at 573-74, but it does not authorize district courts to certify classes on the basis of inadmissible or non-responsive evidence in the first instance. Where, as here, the expert model is legally insufficient because it fails to isolate damages attributable to the certified theory, no deference is owed, because the error is one of law—misapplication of the *Daubert* "fit" requirement and Rule 23(b)(3)'s predominance standard—reviewed de novo under *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 312.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions that class certification be denied absent admissible evidence of a damages methodology capable of measuring harm attributable solely to the accepted theory of antitrust impact.
