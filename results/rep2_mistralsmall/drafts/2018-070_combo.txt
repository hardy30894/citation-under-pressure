**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF LANDS ACT (OCSLA) PERMITS THE APPLICATION OF STATE LAW ONLY WHEN NECESSARY TO FILL A SIGNIFICANT VOID OR GAP IN FEDERAL LAW.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates state law only to the extent it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. This Court has long held that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law under the OCSLA only where federal law fails to address the subject matter at issue. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 359 (1969) (state wrongful death law applied because federal law did not provide a remedy); *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 293 (1958) (state criminal law applied only where federal law was “deficient”).

This principle was first articulated in *Rodrigue*, where the Court explained that state law fills gaps in federal coverage, not to supplement it. 395 U.S. at 366. The Fifth Circuit later formalized this rule in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), holding that state law applies under the OCSLA only when “necessary to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. This interpretation comports with the OCSLA’s purpose: to provide a complete federal legal framework for activities on the Outer Continental Shelf. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355.

II. THE FLSA IS A COMPREHENSIVE STATUTORY SCHEME THAT LEAVES NO GAP FOR STATE WAGE AND HOUR LAWS TO FILL.

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a comprehensive federal statute governing minimum wages, overtime, and working conditions. *Mitchell v. Lublin, McGaughy & Assocs.*, 358 U.S. 207, 211 (1959) (FLSA is “a comprehensive federal statute designed to eliminate labor conditions detrimental to the maintenance of the minimum standard of living necessary for health, efficiency, and general well-being of workers”). The FLSA expressly preserves more protective state wage laws, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), but this savings clause does not create a gap for state law to fill where federal law already provides a remedy. *See generally* *Goldberg v. Daniels*, 238 F. Supp. 734, 737 (D. Conn. 1965) (FLSA’s savings clause allows state law to supplement, not supplant, federal protections).

The FLSA’s regulatory scheme is detailed and exhaustive. The Supreme Court has recognized that the FLSA’s overtime and wage provisions are “elaborate and complex,” *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 136 (1944), and its enforcement mechanisms are comprehensive. *See Mitchell v. Robert DeMario Jewelry, Inc.*, 361 U.S. 288, 292 (1960) (FLSA provides “full complement of sanctions and remedies”). Because the FLSA already governs wage and hour disputes, there is no “significant void or gap” for state law to fill under the OCSLA. *Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036.

III. CALIFORNIA’S WAGE AND HOUR LAWS ARE INCONSISTENT WITH THE FLSA’S COMPREHENSIVE SCHEME.

Even if state law were “applicable” under § 1333(a)(2)(A), it must not be “inconsistent” with federal law. The FLSA’s savings clause permits more protective state laws, but it does not allow state laws that conflict with federal standards. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 218(a); *Mitchell v. Louisiana ex rel. Dep’t of Highways*, 286 U.S. 501, 507 (1932) (federal law preempts state law where the two are in conflict).

California’s meal period requirement conflicts with the FLSA’s framework. The FLSA does not mandate meal periods, and its overtime provisions are calculated differently than California’s. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 207(a)(2) (federal overtime based on 40-hour workweek); *Cal. Lab. Code § 512* (state meal period requirement). Because California’s law imposes obligations beyond the FLSA’s scope, it is inconsistent with federal law. *Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. at 293 (state law inconsistent if it “stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress”).

IV. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S INTERPRETATION OF THE OCSLA IS CONTRARY TO THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT.

The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that state law applies under the OCSLA whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent,” without requiring a gap in federal law. *Parker Drilling Mgmt. Servs., Ltd. v. Newton*, 928 F.3d 1199, 1206 (9th Cir. 2019). This interpretation conflicts with *Rodrigue* and *Sharpnack*, which require a gap in federal law before state law may apply. 395 U.S. at 366; 355 U.S. at 293. The Fifth Circuit’s *Continental Oil* rule is the correct interpretation of the OCSLA. 417 F.2d at 1036.

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA permits state law to apply only when federal law leaves a significant gap. The FLSA is a comprehensive statute that leaves no such gap. California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA and thus cannot apply on the Outer Continental Shelf. The Ninth Circuit’s decision should be reversed.
