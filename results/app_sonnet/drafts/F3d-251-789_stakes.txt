# ARGUMENT

## I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews the district court's factual findings for clear error, but reviews de novo whether those facts satisfy the legal standard for an affirmative defense under the Hague Convention. *Feder v. Evans-Feder*, 63 F.3d 217, 222 n.9 (3d Cir. 1995) (underlying facts reviewed for clear error, but "whether those facts constitute a wrongful removal" is subject to plenary review). A finding is clearly erroneous when, "although there is evidence to support it," the reviewing court "is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985) (quoting *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948)). That standard is met here.

## II. The District Court Clearly Erred in Finding That Gonzalez-Caballero Consented to Danelsy's Permanent Removal and Retention in the United States

### A. Mena bore the burden of proving consent, and the Convention's defenses are to be narrowly applied.

Under the International Child Abduction Remedies Act, once a petitioner establishes that a removal or retention was wrongful, the respondent must establish any Article 13(a) defense—including consent—"by a preponderance of the evidence." 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(2)(B). The Convention's remedial purpose is to restore the status quo ante and to prevent parents from unilaterally altering custody by crossing borders. *See* Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, Oct. 25, 1980, T.I.A.S. No. 11,670, 1343 U.N.T.S. 89; 42 U.S.C. § 11601. Because the defenses threaten to undermine that purpose, courts construe them narrowly. In addressing the closely related defense of acquiescence, the Sixth Circuit held that a parent's assent must be shown through "an act or statement with the requisite formality, such as testimony in a judicial proceeding[,] ... a convincing written renunciation of rights[,] or ... a consistent attitude of acquiescence over a significant period of time." *Friedrich v. Friedrich*, 78 F.3d 1060, 1070 (6th Cir. 1996). Consent, no less than acquiescence, must be clear and unequivocal, because a contrary rule would invite exactly the kind of ambiguous, after-the-fact justifications for retention that the Convention was designed to foreclose.

### B. The record does not establish, by a preponderance of the evidence, that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Danelsy's permanent removal and retention.

The district court's own recitation of the facts demonstrates the absence of the clear, unequivocal consent the Convention requires. Gonzalez-Caballero's account of the Panama City hotel conversation was, in the district court's words, "somewhat confused"—at times describing an agreement to a two-week visit, and at other times describing an arrangement conditioned on Mena's assistance with her own immigration, after which she would "reassume custody." Such conflicting and conditional statements cannot, as a matter of law, satisfy Mena's burden of proving unequivocal consent to permanent retention. *Cf. Friedrich*, 78 F.3d at 1070 (requiring formality and consistency, not an "isolated statement," before assent will defeat return).

Nor can consent be inferred merely because Gonzalez-Caballero surrendered Danelsy's documents and accompanied Mena to obtain exit paperwork. Those acts are entirely consistent with facilitating a temporary visit, not a permanent transfer of custody. Where the petitioning parent's conduct is equally explainable by a temporary arrangement, the respondent has not carried his burden of proving that the parent consented to permanent retention.

### C. Gonzalez-Caballero's contemporaneous conduct affirmatively negates any inference of consent.

Even if the hotel-room conversation were ambiguous, Gonzalez-Caballero's conduct immediately afterward removes any doubt. The very day after Danelsy's departure, when Mena failed to call as promised, Gonzalez-Caballero went to Panamanian child-custody authorities and the police. Days later she again sought help from local police. Over the following weeks she pursued every avenue available to her—contacting the Judge Advocate General Corps liaison, the Institute for Children, the office of the Panamanian President, Panamanian radio stations, and the Children's Commission—culminating in the President's office providing her an airline ticket so that she could travel to the United States to recover her daughter. She arrived in Texas within five months of Danelsy's departure.

This is not the conduct of a mother who consented to her child's permanent removal; it is the conduct of a mother actively resisting it from the outset. Courts confronting analogous facts have recognized that a parent's prompt resort to legal and governmental channels to recover a child is powerful evidence against a finding of consent or acquiescence. *See Rydder v. Rydder*, 49 F.3d 369, 373 (8th Cir. 1995) (petitioning parent's pursuit of legal remedies to recover the child undercut any claim of consent or acquiescence). The same logic controls here. Gonzalez-Caballero's uninterrupted, multi-front campaign to recover Danelsy—beginning the day after the child left Panama and continuing until she personally traveled to the United States—is fundamentally incompatible with the district court's finding that she consented to permanent retention.

## CONCLUSION

Because Mena failed to prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that Gonzalez-Caballero gave clear and unequivocal consent to Danelsy's permanent removal and retention, and because Gonzalez-Caballero's contemporaneous conduct affirmatively contradicts any such finding, the district court's determination was clearly erroneous. This Court should reverse and remand with instructions to order Danelsy's return to Panama.
