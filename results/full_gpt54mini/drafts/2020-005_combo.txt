Ford asks this Court to impose a rigid “causation-only” rule for specific jurisdiction that the Due Process Clause has never required. The question is not whether Ford’s in-state contacts caused these particular injuries in the first instance; it is whether the litigation is sufficiently connected to Ford’s purposeful forum conduct that Montana may fairly exercise adjudicatory authority. That answer is yes.

The Supreme Court has long held that due process permits jurisdiction when a defendant has purposefully availed itself of the forum and the lawsuit bears a meaningful relationship to those forum contacts. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316-19 (1945). Specific jurisdiction exists where the defendant’s activities in the forum make it reasonable to require it to answer there for claims tied to those activities. Id.; McGee v. International Life Insurance Co., 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957). And the Court has repeatedly emphasized that due process “rejects the notion that a State may exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant solely because the defendant’s business happens to reach there;” rather, the inquiry turns on the “quality and nature” of the defendant’s forum conduct. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945).

Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction here fits squarely within those principles. Ford deliberately cultivated the Montana market for the very product that injured respondent’s decedent. It advertised, marketed, and serviced vehicles and parts in Montana; it sought to profit from Montana sales; and it maintained a continuing business presence in the State. Those are not random, fortuitous, or isolated contacts. See Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). When a manufacturer “continuously and deliberately” serves a state’s market, it may be sued there for claims connected to that market presence. Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437, 445-48 (1952). Ford’s Montana business was of that kind.

Ford’s contrary rule would improperly collapse specific jurisdiction into proximate-cause doctrine. The Court has never adopted such a narrow test. To the contrary, it has treated the “arise out of” and “relate to” inquiry as flexible and grounded in fairness. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316-19 (1945); McGee v. International Life Insurance Co., 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957). The Supreme Court’s later decisions likewise confirm that jurisdiction may be proper when claims “relate to” forum contacts even if those contacts did not themselves physically cause the injury. In Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462, 472, 475-76 (1985), the Court held that a defendant who “purposefully directed” conduct at the forum may be required to defend there when the litigation stems from that course of conduct. And in Kulko v. Superior Court, 436 U.S. 84, 94-98 (1978), the Court focused on whether the defendant had purposefully created a substantial connection with the forum—not on a mechanical causation rule.

The Court’s older cases from the 1940s and 1950s are especially instructive. In International Shoe, the Court upheld jurisdiction where the suit “arose out of” the company’s systematic in-state activities, even though those activities were not the immediate cause of the liability at issue. 326 U.S. at 319. In McGee, a single insurance contract delivered into California supported jurisdiction because the suit was based on that deliberate forum transaction. 355 U.S. at 223. Those decisions reflect the constitutional core: a defendant that reaps the benefits of a state market may be called to account there for litigation sufficiently connected to that market.

Ford’s reliance on a strict causation requirement also conflicts with the Court’s recognition that specific jurisdiction serves reciprocal fairness. A state may require a defendant to answer where it has “invoked the benefits and protections” of the forum’s laws. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945); Travelers Health Assn. v. Virginia ex rel. State Corp. Commission, 339 U.S. 643, 647-49 (1950). Here, Ford did exactly that by systematically serving Montana consumers and supporting Montana sales of the Explorer and related parts. Having done so, it is not unfair to require Ford to defend a design-defect action in Montana when one of its vehicles failed on Montana roads and injured a Montana resident.

Nor does the Due Process Clause demand that the precise forum contact be the immediate “but for” cause of the injury. This Court has rejected such artificial narrowing in related contexts, recognizing that jurisdiction turns on the relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation. See Milliken v. Meyer, 311 U.S. 457, 463-64 (1940); Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352, 356-57 (1927). The respondent’s claims plainly arise from and relate to Ford’s ongoing Montana marketing and servicing of the very vehicles and components at issue. That is enough.

In short, Ford purposefully established a substantial Montana market for the Explorer and replacement parts, and respondent’s claims are connected to that forum conduct. Due process does not require Montana to pretend that Ford’s Montana business is irrelevant merely because the particular Explorer at issue was first sold elsewhere. Under International Shoe and its predecessors, jurisdiction is proper. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945); McGee v. International Life Insurance Co., 355 U.S. 220 (1957); Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235 (1958); Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437 (1952); Travelers Health Assn. v. Virginia ex rel. State Corp. Commission, 339 U.S. 643 (1950); Milliken v. Meyer, 311 U.S. 457 (1940); Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352 (1927). The judgment below should be affirmed.
