**ARGUMENT**

This Court has long held that a private party lacks standing to assert a Tenth Amendment challenge to a federal statute. The Tenth Amendment “is not a source of individual rights”; it is a structural limit on federal power vis-à-vis the States. *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 156 (1992). Because the Amendment protects only state sovereignty, only a State or its officers may invoke it. *See Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898, 930 (1997). Petitioner Carol Anne Bond, a private individual, therefore cannot challenge 18 U.S.C. § 229 on federalism grounds.

**I. Standing Requires a Personal Interest, Not a Structural Grievance**

The “irreducible constitutional minimum of standing” requires injury in fact, causation, and redressability. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992). The injury must be “personal” and “particularized.” *Id.* at 560 n.1. The Tenth Amendment, however, “does not confer any right on an individual” to complain about the distribution of federal power. *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284 (10th Cir. 2004). Instead, it “reserves to the States a realm of sovereign authority.” *New York*, 505 U.S. at 156. Bond’s prosecution under § 229 is a concrete injury, but that injury is not “legally cognizable as a basis for a Tenth Amendment claim.” *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 35 (1st Cir. 2005). Her grievance is that the federal government exceeded its enumerated powers—a structural objection that belongs to the States, not to individuals.

**II. *Tennessee Electric* Controls and Forecloses Private-Party Standing**

In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies argued that the TVA’s sale of electricity violated the Tenth Amendment. This Court rejected that claim, holding that “absent the states or their officers, [the utilities] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the amendment.” *Id.* at 144. That holding is not dicta; it is an alternative ground for the decision. *See Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 34. This Court has never overruled *Tennessee Electric*, and it remains binding. *See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 94–95 (1998) (courts must resolve jurisdictional issues before merits).

**III. The Majority of Circuits Correctly Deny Standing**

Every circuit to address the issue after *Tennessee Electric*—except two—has held that private parties lack Tenth Amendment standing. *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525–27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971–72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 33–36; *Parker*, 362 F.3d at 1284–85. These courts reason that the Tenth Amendment “is a structural provision that protects the states, not individuals.” *Parker*, 362 F.3d at 1284. A private party “cannot complain if the Federal Government exercises its delegated powers in a way that arguably infringes upon state sovereignty,” because the State is the proper party to object. *Hacker*, 565 F.3d at 526. The two circuits that have allowed standing—the Seventh and Eleventh—either misread *Tennessee Electric* or have since retreated. *See Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693, 703–04 (7th Cir. 1999); *Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. U.S. Dep’t of Energy*, 666 F.2d 1359, 1368 n.16 (11th Cir. 1982); *but see Dillard v. Chilton County Comm’n*, 495 F.3d 1324, 1335 (11th Cir. 2007) (denying standing). The majority view is correct.

**IV. Bond’s Injury Is Not Redressable by a Tenth Amendment Ruling**

Even if Bond had a personal stake, her injury would not be redressed by a decree that § 229 violates the Tenth Amendment. The Amendment does not confer a right to be free from federal prosecution; it merely reserves power to the States. *See New York*, 505 U.S. at 156. The proper remedy for a Tenth Amendment violation is not dismissal of charges against an individual, but an action by the State to enjoin the federal overreach. *See Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35 (noting that “the State represents the interests of its citizens”). A State “may have recourse to local political processes to effect change,” but a private party cannot “commandeer the Tenth Amendment for personal benefit.” *Parker*, 362 F.3d at 1284. Bond’s conviction is redressable only if the statute is ultra vires on other grounds, but she has not shown that.

**V. Conclusion**

Because Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Third Circuit. The question of whether § 229 exceeds Congress’s treaty power is not properly before the Court. *See Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 101–02. The Tenth Amendment reserves its protection to the States, and only a State may invoke it.
