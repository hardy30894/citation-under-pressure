The Bankruptcy Act of 1898 excepts from discharge only those debts “created by [the debtor’s] fraud, embezzlement, misappropriation, or defalcation while acting as an officer or in any fiduciary capacity.” Act of July 1, 1898, ch. 541, § 17, 30 Stat. 544, 550. This language confines the exception to the sum actually obtained by the prohibited conduct. Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877) (the exception reaches only “debts created by fraud” and does not extend to “implied or constructive fraud”).  

The same limitation appears in the 1903 amendment, which added “false pretenses or false representations” but retained the phrase “to the extent obtained by.” Act of Feb. 5, 1903, ch. 487, § 5, 32 Stat. 797, 798. Courts construing that phrase have held that it measures the nondischargeable debt by the value received, not by subsequent penalties. Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176, 193 (1904) (“the debt must be one which was obtained by the fraud”); Strang v. Bradner, 114 U.S. 555, 559 (1885) (liability “created by the fraud” is limited to the sum the creditor parted with).  

Punitive or treble damages awarded under state law fall outside this measure. They are not “obtained by” the debtor; they are imposed by statute after the fact. Wetmore v. Markoe, 196 U.S. 68, 77 (1904) (a judgment for alimony arrears was dischargeable because it did not represent “a debt created by fraud”); Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 236 U.S. 549, 555 (1915) (the Act “was intended to relieve the honest debtor” and “give him a new opportunity”).  

The policy of the statute reinforces this textual limit. Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (“One of the primary purposes of the bankruptcy act” is to permit “the honest but unfortunate debtor” to obtain “a new opportunity in life.”). Allowing discharge of statutory multiples preserves that purpose while still denying discharge of the compensatory sum actually procured by fraud. McIntyre v. Kavanaugh, 242 U.S. 138, 141 (1916) (the exception “should be confined to those cases which are within its letter”).  

Tinker v. Colwell, 193 U.S. 473 (1904), does not enlarge the exception. There the Court addressed only whether a judgment for criminal conversation constituted “willful and malicious injury”; it did not hold that punitive additions to a fraud debt survive discharge. Id. at 485. Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915), likewise treated the exception as reaching only “positive fraud, or fraud in fact.”  

Because the treble damages here were not money, property, or services obtained by Cohen, they remain dischargeable under the pre-1970 authorities. The judgment below should be reversed to that extent.
