# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION SATISFIES THE INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TEST AND DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER

Article I, Section 1 vests "all legislative powers" in Congress, but this Court has long recognized that this provision does not prevent Congress from enlisting the assistance of the Executive Branch in filling in statutory details. As Chief Justice Taft explained in *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394 (1928), the touchstone of permissible delegation is whether Congress has laid "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *Id.* at 409. That standard is satisfied here.

Section 811(h) constrains the Attorney General's discretion in multiple respects. He must find that scheduling is "necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety," and he must consider specific statutory factors relating to a substance's history of abuse, its scope and duration, and the risk it poses to public health. These constraints are no less exacting than those this Court has repeatedly upheld. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), this Court sustained a delegation to the Price Administrator to fix commodity prices that would be "generally fair and equitable" and would effectuate the purposes of the Emergency Price Control Act—a standard considerably more open-ended than the multi-factor test Congress prescribed here. The Court explained that Congress "is not confined to that method of executing its policy which involves the least possible delegation of discretion to administrative officers." *Id.* at 425.

Similarly, in *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245 (1947), this Court upheld a delegation authorizing the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to regulate the reorganization and conservation of savings and loan associations pursuant to regulations promulgated by the Board itself, reasoning that Congress may rely on "well-known and generally acceptable standards" already established in a regulated field. *Id.* at 250. Congress did the same here: the temporary scheduling factors are drawn directly from the permanent scheduling criteria in Section 811(c), reflecting decades of accumulated pharmacological and enforcement expertise. And in *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), this Court upheld a delegation to the Attorney General to determine which alien Communist Party members posed sufficient danger to warrant detention without bail pending deportation proceedings, emphasizing that the availability of some form of review, coupled with statutory standards, was sufficient to preserve accountability. *Id.* at 542–44.

Petitioners' reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In both cases, the delegating statute supplied no criteria whatsoever. *Panama Refining* invalidated a delegation because the statute "did not declare any policy as to the transportation of the excess production" and established no "criterion to govern the President's course." 293 U.S. at 415. *Schechter Poultry* invalidated a scheme permitting the President to approve industry-drafted codes of "fair competition" backed by criminal penalties, without any legislative standard cabining what could constitute such a code. 295 U.S. at 541-42. Section 811(h) bears no resemblance to those standardless delegations; it supplies precise substantive factors, a temporal limitation of one year (extendable by only six months), and mandatory procedural safeguards, including notice to the Secretary of Health and Human Services.

The fact that violation of a scheduling order carries criminal penalties does not demand a more exacting non-delegation standard. This Court sustained exactly such delegations in *Yakus*, where violation of price regulations was itself a federal crime, 321 U.S. at 423, and the discretion delegated to the Price Administrator was substantially broader than that delegated here. Nothing in this Court's precedents—including *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), which first recognized that Congress may condition the application of a statute on executive fact-finding, *id.* at 693—suggests that criminal consequences transform an otherwise valid delegation into an unconstitutional one.

## II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL WAS AUTHORIZED TO SUBDELEGATE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA

Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Controlled Substances Act. See 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This Court has consistently recognized that when Congress grants an executive official broad delegated authority, that official may in turn subdelegate implementation to subordinate officers absent a clear congressional prohibition. In *Fleming v. Mohawk Wrecking & Lumber Co.*, 331 U.S. 111 (1947), this Court held that an agency head's delegation of statutory authority to a subordinate division was proper where Congress had not withheld such power, explaining that administrative efficiency requires that heads of agencies be able to distribute their statutory responsibilities. *Id.* at 121.

The DEA is the component of the Department of Justice with specialized expertise in narcotics enforcement, and Congress's grant of general delegation authority under Section 871(a), read together with the specific scheduling provisions, reflects no intent to require the Attorney General personally to sign every temporary scheduling order. The subdelegation regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), assigning to the DEA Administrator the "[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General" under the Act, falls squarely within the authority Congress conferred.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
