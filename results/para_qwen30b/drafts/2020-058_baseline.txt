**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is one of statutory interpretation, and the answer must be grounded in the text, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of TPS satisfies the “inspected and admitted or paroled” requirement of § 1255(a). That interpretation distorts the meaning of “admission,” conflates status with entry, and undermines the carefully calibrated framework Congress established for adjusting immigration status. We respectfully submit that a grant of TPS does not constitute an admission, and that the District Court’s contrary conclusion must be reversed.

### I. THE TEXT OF THE INA CLEARLY DISTINGUISHES “ADMISSION” FROM “LAWFUL STATUS”

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely procedural—it is substantive and exclusive. It refers to a physical, factual event: the moment an alien crosses the border and is lawfully permitted to enter. As this Court has held, “admission” involves “physical entrance into the country,” and is “inapposite to adjustment of status in removal proceedings, a procedure that is structured to take place entirely within the United States.” *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012); see also *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013).

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” This language does not say, and cannot be read to mean, that the alien has been “admitted.” The distinction is not semantic—it is structural. Congress used two separate terms—“admission” and “status”—for a reason. To equate them is to ignore the clear line drawn in the INA between the *event* of entry and the *condition* of presence. As the Fifth Circuit has observed, “admission is an occurrence, defined in wholly factual and procedural terms,” while “status usually describes the type of permission to be present in the United States.” *Gomez v. Lynch*, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016). The grant of TPS does not alter the fact that Sanchez and Gonzalez entered without inspection or admission. Their status may now be lawful, but their entry was not.

### II. THE STRUCTURE OF THE INA REVEALS CONGRESS’S INTENTION TO PRESERVE THE DISTINCTION

The structure of the INA reinforces the conclusion that TPS does not constitute admission. Section 1255(a) requires that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled” to be eligible for adjustment of status. Congress did not exempt TPS recipients from this requirement. Instead, it created a separate, limited exception in § 1255(k), which permits adjustment for certain aliens who are “present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(1). The fact that Congress provided a specific, narrow exception for some categories of aliens—such as special immigrants under § 1255(h)—but not for TPS recipients, confirms that TPS does not satisfy the admission requirement.

Moreover, Congress included a supermajority requirement in § 1254a(h)(2) for any legislation that would adjust the status of TPS recipients. This provision exists precisely because Congress recognized that TPS is temporary and does not automatically lead to permanent residence. If a grant of TPS were equivalent to admission, then TPS recipients would already be eligible for adjustment under § 1255(a), rendering § 1254a(h)(2) superfluous. As this Court has repeatedly held, “a statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.” *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004). The interpretation urged by the Appellees would render § 1254a(h)(2) meaningless.

### III. THE PURPOSE OF TPS REQUIRES A LIMITED, TEMPORARY RELIEF, NOT A PATH TO PERMANENT RESIDENCE

The purpose of the TPS program is to provide temporary protection from removal during extraordinary conditions in an alien’s home country. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). It is not a mechanism for legalizing unlawful presence. The program is “designed to shield aliens already in the country from removal when a natural disaster or similar occurrence has rendered removal unsa[fe].” *Serrano v. Att’y Gen.*, 655 F.3d 1260, 1265 (11th Cir. 2011) (per curiam). To read § 1254a(f)(4) as conferring admission would transform TPS from a temporary reprieve into a de facto pathway to permanent residence—precisely the result Congress sought to avoid.

The Appellees argue that requiring TPS recipients to reenter lawfully would undermine the purpose of TPS, especially if their home countries remain unsafe. But that concern is misplaced. TPS recipients are not required to leave the country to seek adjustment. They may remain in the United States with lawful nonimmigrant status as long as TPS is extended. The fact that they cannot adjust status under § 1255(a) does not mean they are subject to removal. The TPS program itself provides a legal basis for their continued presence. To allow adjustment of status based on TPS alone would effectively bypass the admission requirement for all aliens who entered without inspection—regardless of their circumstances—undermining the integrity of the immigration system.
