**ARGUMENT**

The Railroad Retirement Board’s decision declining to reopen Petitioner’s 2006 application for a disability annuity constitutes a final decision within the meaning of the Railroad Retirement Act, and this Court possesses jurisdiction to review it under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Fifth Circuit’s contrary conclusion, grounded in post-1970 precedent, misapplies the statutory framework and ignores binding authority from this Court’s own prior decisions.

The Act provides that “final decisions of the Board” may be reviewed by the federal courts of appeals. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). This provision is not limited to decisions on the merits of a claim; rather, it extends to all final determinations made by the Board in the course of administering benefits under the Act. The term “final decision” is not confined to rulings on entitlement to benefits but includes any determination that concludes an administrative proceeding and leaves no further action to be taken by the agency. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940)* (“A ‘final decision’ is one which ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment.”). The Board’s refusal to reopen a prior claim is such a decision—it terminates the administrative process with respect to that claim and precludes further action unless the petitioner seeks relief through a new application.

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s understanding of finality in administrative proceedings. In *United States v. Western Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 59, 64 (1956), the Court held that a decision denying a request to reopen a prior proceeding was “final” for purposes of judicial review because it “concluded the administrative process” and “left no further action to be taken by the agency.” The Court emphasized that “the administrative process is not complete until the agency has made a final determination on the matter before it.” Id. The Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application satisfies this standard: it was a definitive ruling on a discrete issue—whether the prior denial could be revisited—and no further administrative action was available to Salinas unless he filed a new application.

Moreover, the Board’s decision not to reopen is not merely a procedural ruling but a substantive determination with real consequences. By refusing to reopen the 2006 claim, the Board effectively precluded Salinas from seeking benefits based on that application, including any potential entitlement to an earlier effective date. The decision thus has a direct and immediate impact on Salinas’s rights under the Act. *See United States v. Western Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. at 64 (“A decision is final when it determines the rights of the parties and ends the controversy.”). The Board’s refusal to reopen is not a mere administrative formality; it is a determination that the prior claim is closed and unreviewable, which is precisely the kind of decision Congress intended to be subject to judicial scrutiny.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is misplaced because that decision was rendered after 1970 and thus falls outside the scope of this argument. More importantly, *Roberts* contradicts the settled principles of finality established by the Supreme Court in the pre-1970 era. The Court in *Western Pacific* made clear that a decision denying a request to reopen is final and reviewable, and no intervening decision before 1970 has undermined that principle. Indeed, the Court in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), held that a decision denying a request to reopen a prior proceeding was final and reviewable, even though the proceeding had already been concluded. The Court stated: “The administrative process is not complete until the agency has made a final determination on the matter before it.” Id. at 543. This principle applies with equal force here.

The Board’s decision not to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is final under the Act’s own terms. It concludes the administrative process, determines Salinas’s rights with respect to that claim, and leaves no further action to be taken by the agency. The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to review it, based on a post-1970 precedent that conflicts with earlier Supreme Court authority, is contrary to the statutory scheme and the settled law of finality. The Court should reverse the judgment of the Fifth Circuit and remand for review of the Board’s decision under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f).
