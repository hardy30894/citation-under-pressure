# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT'S FMLA CLAIM BECAUSE HE PROVIDED HABITAT WITH SUFFICIENT NOTICE OF A SERIOUS HEALTH CONDITION.

An employee seeking FMLA leave need not invoke the statute by name or recite a magic formula. This Court has repeatedly held that "the employee need not mention the FMLA by name in order to trigger its protection." *Price v. City of Fort Wayne*, 117 F.3d 1022, 1026 (7th Cir. 1997). Rather, the employee's burden is only "to give the employer enough information to know that FMLA leave might be requested." *Stoops v. One Call Commc'ns, Inc.*, 141 F.3d 309, 312 (7th Cir. 1998). Once the employer has that information, "the burden then shifts to the employer to request such additional information from the employee's doctor as it deems necessary." *Price*, 117 F.3d at 1026. This standard reflects the reality that employees are frequently in the dark about the precise medical or legal significance of their symptoms; the statute does not demand medical sophistication as a precondition to protection. *See Byrne v. Avon Prods., Inc.*, 328 F.3d 379, 381 (7th Cir. 2003) (employer's notice obligation triggered by information sufficient to alert it that the FMLA may apply, not by a diagnosis).

The district court's ruling cannot be reconciled with this standard. Burnett's disclosures to Polo and other Habitat managers, viewed cumulatively and in the light most favorable to him, gave Habitat ample reason to suspect that his absences stemmed from a serious health condition. In October 2003, Burnett disclosed a "weak bladder" and told Polo he intended to see a doctor. By December 11, he had presented Polo with a doctor's order for blood testing and disclosed that testing had revealed a dangerously high PSA level, along with his need to see a urologist. On December 16, in a meeting with multiple Habitat managers and a union representative, Burnett stated that he had been "sick" and analogized his condition to his brother-in-law's prostate cancer. By January 7, 2004, Burnett had given a Habitat supervisor a document expressly identifying "an elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA)" as the reason for his upcoming biopsy.

Habitat cannot escape liability by asserting that Burnett never used the words "serious health condition" or explained the clinical significance of an elevated PSA. Under *Price* and *Stoops*, that explanation was Habitat's burden to seek, not Burnett's burden to volunteer. Indeed, this Court has held that once an employer receives information that reasonably suggests a serious health condition, it has a duty to investigate further rather than assume the worst about the employee's candor. *See Aubuchon v. Knauf Fiberglass, GmbH*, 359 F.3d 950, 953 (7th Cir. 2004) (employer could not rely on employee's failure to specify the nature of "chest pains" where the employer never asked follow-up questions). Habitat's own internal email on December 16—directing that Burnett be excused for doctor's appointments—confirms that Polo and Habitat's management understood Burnett was dealing with a legitimate medical issue requiring ongoing care. A reasonable jury could find that Habitat had all the notice the FMLA requires, and the district court erred in resolving that factual question against Burnett as a matter of law.

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT'S ADA CLAIM BECAUSE HABITAT HAD SUFFICIENT KNOWLEDGE OF HIS DISABILITY.

The same factual record defeats summary judgment on the ADA claim. To prevail on a failure-to-accommodate or discriminatory-discharge theory under the ADA, a plaintiff must show that the employer knew of his disability. *Hendricks-Robinson v. Excel Corp.*, 154 F.3d 685, 693 (7th Cir. 1998). But this Court has never required that an employee supply a formal diagnosis to satisfy that knowledge requirement. In *Hoffman v. MCA, Inc.*, 144 F.3d 1117 (7th Cir. 1998), this Court made clear that an employer may be charged with notice of a disability even absent an explicit medical label, so long as the employer possesses information from which the existence of a disability reasonably could be inferred. *Id.* at 1120. The relevant inquiry is what the employer actually knew or should have understood from the employee's disclosures—not whether the employee correctly diagnosed himself.

Here, Habitat's managers knew, well before Burnett's termination, that he had an elevated PSA level, was scheduled for a prostate biopsy, and had directly compared his symptoms to his brother-in-law's prostate cancer. They were handed a document stating that his procedure was indicated by "an elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA)." A reasonable jury could conclude that this constellation of facts gave Habitat sufficient knowledge that Burnett was contending with a substantially limiting impairment—or at minimum, a condition Habitat perceived as such—triggering the ADA's protections. *See Hendricks-Robinson*, 154 F.3d at 693. Whether Habitat's professed ignorance of the "significance" of Burnett's PSA levels was genuine, or whether it was willfully blind to information squarely before it, is a quintessential jury question, not one appropriately resolved on summary judgment.

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Burnett respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment on both his FMLA and ADA claims and remand for trial.
