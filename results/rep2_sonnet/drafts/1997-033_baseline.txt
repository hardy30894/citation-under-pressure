# ARGUMENT

## Section 1404(a) Does Not Authorize a Transferee Court to Assign an MDL Case to Itself for Trial; Section 1407(a) Requires Remand.

The text of § 1407(a) resolves this case. The statute provides that any action transferred for coordinated pretrial proceedings "shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). This Court has repeatedly recognized that "shall" is mandatory language admitting no exception absent clear contrary indication from Congress. *Lexington Ins. Co. v. Forrest*, 508 U.S. 200, 207 (1993). Nothing in § 1407(a) authorizes the transferee court, rather than the Panel, to determine whether and when remand occurs, and nothing permits the transferee court to circumvent remand by invoking a different statute altogether.

Section 1404(a), by contrast, permits a district court to transfer a civil action to another district "for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice." 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). By its terms, § 1404(a) contemplates transfer between two courts—it says nothing about, and provides no mechanism for, a court transferring a case to itself. A court cannot "transfer" an action to itself in any ordinary sense of that word. As Judge Kozinski recognized in dissent below, the practice of self-assignment "disregarded the statutory mandate" of § 1407(a) from its inception, beginning with the Second Circuit's "summary treatment" in *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 124–25 (2d Cir. 1971). *Lexecon Inc. v. United States Dist. Court*, 61 F.3d 911 (9th Cir. 1995) (Kozinski, J., dissenting). That court and its progeny openly acknowledged they were not following the statute's text, but instead relied on "grounds of policy or convenience." *Id.* Indeed, the District of New Hampshire candidly admitted that "there is nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial." *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971).

This Court's precedents confirm that statutory text controls over judicially perceived convenience. In *Connecticut National Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253–54 (1992), this Court held that "courts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there," and that when the statutory language is plain, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." The Ninth Circuit's own panel below acknowledged that "despite what appears to be a clear statutory mandate," it would defer to contrary practice—precisely the approach this Court has forbidden. *Lexecon*, 61 F.3d 911.

Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b), which purports to authorize transferee courts to invoke § 1404(a) to transfer cases to themselves, cannot override the statute. An agency or judicial-panel rule cannot expand a court's statutory jurisdiction beyond what Congress has granted. *See Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp.*, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988) (agencies possess only the authority Congress confers). Congress vested the power to remand exclusively in the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation, not in the transferee district court, and specified that remand "shall" occur "at or before the conclusion" of pretrial proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). A procedural rule cannot rewrite this allocation of authority.

The structural logic of the MDL statute reinforces this reading. Section 1407(a) reflects a deliberate compromise: cases may be temporarily consolidated in a distant forum for efficient pretrial coordination, but the plaintiff's chosen forum—and the defendant's right to be tried in an appropriate district—is preserved for trial itself. As Judge Kozinski explained, "[p]retrial proceedings under 1407 are obviously limited to those raising issues common to other cases, primarily discovery; that's the entire point of the multidistrict process." *Lexecon*, 61 F.3d 911 (Kozinski, J., dissenting). Permitting self-transfer under § 1404(a) allows transferee judges to "routinely glom onto" cases entrusted to them "for limited purposes," subverting the very policy § 1407(a) was designed to protect. *Id.*

Petitioner was transferred to Arizona solely for coordinated pretrial proceedings. It participated in no common discovery relevant to its individual claims against Respondents, and no efficiency rationale under § 1407(a) justified retaining the case for trial. The Arizona district court's invocation of § 1404(a) to assign the case to itself—rather than remanding to the Northern District of Illinois as § 1407(a) commands—exceeded its statutory authority.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that § 1404(a) does not empower a transferee court in MDL proceedings to assign a case to itself for trial, and that § 1407(a)'s remand mandate must be honored.
