**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Certifying a Class Under Rule 23(b)(3) Where Plaintiffs Failed to Present Admissible Evidence Demonstrating That Damages Could Be Measured on a Class-Wide Basis.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). To satisfy this predominance requirement in an antitrust case, plaintiffs must show that damages are capable of measurement on a class-wide basis through a common methodology. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 311 (3d Cir. 2008) (citing *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 34 (2013)). The Supreme Court has made clear that plaintiffs cannot rely on speculative or individualized damages models at the class certification stage. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011) (“[A] class cannot be certified on the premise that [the defendant] will not be entitled to litigate its statutory defenses to individual claims.”).

Here, the District Court erred by certifying the class based on Dr. McClave’s damages model, which the court itself acknowledged was “divorced from the antitrust impact theory” and “not tied to the overbuilder deterrence theory.” *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 172 (E.D. Pa. 2010). The Supreme Court has rejected such a disconnect between liability and damages. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 34 (“The model [plaintiffs] offered did not measure damages resulting from the particular antitrust violation they sought to prove.”). The Third Circuit’s decision in *Hydrogen Peroxide* reinforces this principle: “[T]he district court must conduct a ‘rigorous analysis’ to determine whether the plaintiff has established that damages are capable of class-wide proof.” 552 F.3d at 318 (quoting *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974)).

The District Court’s certification order rested on an impermissible merits inquiry into the merits of the damages model. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350 (“Focusing upon the merits of the claim is inconsistent with the proper standard for evaluating certification.”). The court improperly resolved factual disputes about the reliability of Dr. McClave’s methodology, a task reserved for trial. *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 574 (1985) (“[T]he court of appeals may not reverse [a district court’s] choice between two permissible views of the evidence.”). Because the damages model was not tied to the alleged antitrust violation and could not measure class-wide harm, the District Court abused its discretion in certifying the class.

II. The District Court Failed to Apply the Correct Legal Standard for Evaluating the Reliability of Plaintiffs’ Damages Model.

The Third Circuit has emphasized that district courts must conduct a “rigorous analysis” of expert testimony at the class certification stage. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318. This requires assessing whether the expert’s methodology is reliable under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993). *In re Blood Reagents Antitrust Litig.*, 783 F.3d 183, 187 (3d Cir. 2015) (applying *Daubert* at class certification).

Here, Dr. McClave’s model suffered from critical flaws that the District Court ignored. First, it relied on an arbitrary geographic market definition that varied by household, rendering the damages calculation speculative. *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 294, 337 (1962) (“The geographic market must be defined with reference to objective market realities.”). Second, the model assumed harm from “overbuilder deterrence” without empirical support. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 34 (“The model [plaintiffs] offered did not measure damages resulting from the particular antitrust violation they sought to prove.”). Third, the model failed to account for individualized factors such as customer switching behavior. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350 (“[A] class cannot be certified on the premise that [the defendant] will not be entitled to litigate its statutory defenses to individual claims.”).

The District Court’s decision to certify the class despite these deficiencies was an abuse of discretion. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318 (“The district court must conduct a ‘rigorous analysis’ to determine whether the plaintiff has established that damages are capable of class-wide proof.”).

III. The District Court’s Certification Order Violates the Seventh Amendment by Resolving Merits Issues at the Class Certification Stage.

The Supreme Court has cautioned against conflating class certification with merits adjudication. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350 (“Focusing upon the merits of the claim is inconsistent with the proper standard for evaluating certification.”). The Third Circuit has echoed this concern, emphasizing that district courts must not conduct a “mini-trial” on the merits at the certification stage. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 317 (quoting *Newton v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc.*, 259 F.3d 154, 166 (3d Cir. 2001)).

Here, the District Court resolved factual disputes about the reliability of Dr. McClave’s damages model, a task reserved for trial. *Anderson*, 470 U.S. at 574 (“[T]he court of appeals may not reverse [a district court’s] choice between two permissible views of the evidence.”). By doing so, the court improperly adjudicated the merits of the damages calculation, violating the Seventh Amendment. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350 (“The inquiry into whether an employer’s actions were motivated by a discriminatory intent is fact-specific and frequently turns on credibility determinations.”).

**Conclusion**

The District Court abused its discretion by certifying a class under Rule 23(b)(3) where plaintiffs failed to present admissible evidence demonstrating that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. The court’s decision rested on an unreliable damages model that was not tied to the alleged antitrust violation, violated the *Daubert* standard, and improperly resolved merits issues at the certification stage. This Court should reverse the judgment below and remand with instructions to decertify the class.
