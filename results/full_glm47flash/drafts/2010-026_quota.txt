**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit erred by adopting the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard for Exemption 2, a standard that conflicts with the plain text of the statute and Supreme Court precedent.**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision to adopt the “predominantly internal” standard for Exemption 2 is a misinterpretation of the statute and a distortion of the Supreme Court’s instructions. Exemption 2 exempts from disclosure matters “related *solely* to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has instructed that FOIA exemptions “must be narrowly construed” and are “explicitly exclusive.” *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Adm’r FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)). This Court has previously rejected the “predominantly internal” standard in favor of the “solely” test. In *Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service*, this Court held that information does not “relate ‘solely,’ or even predominantly, ‘to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.’” 108 F.3d 1082, 1085 (9th Cir. 1997) (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2)). The “predominantly internal” standard is a judicial invention that expands the scope of Exemption 2 beyond the clear limitations set by Congress.

**II. The documents at issue are not “internal personnel rules and practices” but technical data for facility design.**

The Navy’s ESQD data is technical data used to design and construct ammunition storage facilities, not internal personnel rules. The purpose of the requested documents is to measure the effects of an explosion and ensure safe storage distances, a function distinct from the management of agency personnel. This distinction is critical. In *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, this Court held that instructions contained in a manual were exempt because they “concern[ed] internal personnel practices.” 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980). However, the ESQD data is not a manual for personnel; it is a technical planning tool. Furthermore, the Navy has not demonstrated that the data is compiled for an “adjudicative or enforcement purpose.” *Church of Scientology v. IRS*, 611 F.2d 748, 748 (9th Cir. 1979). Because the data is not a rule governing agency personnel, it does not fall within the ambit of Exemption 2.

**III. The risk of circumvention, if real, is a matter for Exemption 1, not Exemption 2.**

If the Navy genuinely fears that the disclosure of ESQD data will risk circumvention of agency regulation, the proper remedy is classification under Exemption 1, not a claim under Exemption 2. The Supreme Court has emphasized that FOIA reflects a “general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976) (quoting S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 3 (1965)). The Navy has failed to classify the ESQD data, despite Commander Whitbred’s assertions that its release could endanger national security. *Dep’t of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 527 (1988). By withholding unclassified technical data under Exemption 2, the Navy distorts the careful balance between public access and national security. As the Supreme Court has noted, FOIA is a “balance between the interests of the public in greater access to information and the needs of the Government to protect certain kinds of information from disclosure.” *John Doe Agency v. United States*, 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1990). The Navy’s reliance on Exemption 2 to hide unclassified data undermines this balance and defies the Court’s instruction that exemptions are to be narrowly construed.
