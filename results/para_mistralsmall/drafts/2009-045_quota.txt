**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Enhancing the Lodestar Based on Exceptional Performance and Results.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s award of attorneys’ fees, including a $4.5 million enhancement, because the lodestar calculation did not fully account for the extraordinary quality of counsel’s representation and the exceptional results achieved. This Court has long recognized that fee-shifting statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988 are designed to ensure that plaintiffs can obtain competent counsel, *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433 (1983), and that enhancements may be appropriate in “rare” and “exceptional” cases where the lodestar fails to reflect the true value of the services rendered. *Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air v. Pennsylvania (“Delaware Valley I”),* 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986); *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 898-99 (1984).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the lodestar—calculated as the product of reasonable hours and a reasonable hourly rate—is presumptively sufficient. *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565. However, the Court has also acknowledged that in “rare” cases, an enhancement may be warranted where the lodestar does not adequately compensate for superior performance and exceptional results. *Id.* at 565-66; *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-99. The Court in *Blum* explicitly left open the possibility of enhancement where “the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged” and the results were “exceptional.” *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899. The Eleventh Circuit has faithfully applied this standard, affirming enhancements where supported by specific evidence in the record. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008).

Here, the district court’s detailed findings—based on its observation of counsel’s performance over a lengthy and complex litigation—demonstrated that the lodestar did not reflect the true value of the services rendered. The court noted that counsel’s “stellar performance” and the “exceptional” nature of the results justified the enhancement. *Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue*, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006). This was not an abuse of discretion. *See Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565 (upholding enhancements where supported by specific evidence); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000) (permitting enhancements where “quality of service was superior” and “success was exceptional”).

**II. The District Court’s Findings Were Sufficiently Specific and Supported by the Record.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly rejected the argument that the district court’s findings were insufficient. The Supreme Court has made clear that enhancements must be supported by “specific evidence” in the record, *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, but it has not required a rigid formula for such evidence. The district court’s findings—based on its observation of counsel’s performance, the complexity of the litigation, and the exceptional results achieved—were more than adequate. *See Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565-66 (upholding enhancements where supported by detailed findings); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999) (affirming enhancement “on account of the exceptional results obtained”).

The dissent’s suggestion that the district court improperly “testified” ex parte is meritless. The court’s observations were part of its judicial role in evaluating counsel’s performance, not impermissible testimony. *See Kenny A.*, 532 F.3d at 1242 (upholding enhancement based on district court’s findings). The Eleventh Circuit properly deferred to the district court’s discretion, as it is uniquely positioned to assess the quality of advocacy. *See id.* at 1242-43.

**III. The Weight of Circuit Precedent Supports the Enhancement.**

Every circuit to consider this issue—including the Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits—has recognized that enhancements may be appropriate in rare cases where the lodestar fails to reflect superior performance and exceptional results. *See, e.g., Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999) (“The lodestar may be adjusted based on several factors, including in particular the results obtained”); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999) (permitting enhancements where “quality of service rendered and the results obtained were superior”); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993) (upholding enhancements where supported by specific evidence). This overwhelming consensus confirms that the Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with controlling authority.

**Conclusion**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in enhancing the lodestar based on exceptional performance and results. The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the award, and this Court should do likewise.
