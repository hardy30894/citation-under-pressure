The equal footing doctrine, which "is the settled rule of law" that new states enter the Union "upon an equal footing with the original States in all respects whatsoever," vests title in the State to the beds of rivers that were navigable in fact at the moment of statehood. *Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 223 (1845). Title to such beds "is not a matter of implication, but of express grant." *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894). The constitutional test therefore turns on the river's condition "at the time of the admission of the State into the Union." *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 55 (1926).

Navigability for title is a factual question resolved by evidence of use or susceptibility to use "in [the river's] ordinary condition" as a highway for commerce at statehood. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870). A river segment satisfies the test if, at that historic moment, it was "navigable in fact" even if interrupted by occasional obstructions. *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 586 (1922). Later improvements or changes in use are irrelevant; the inquiry is fixed at the date the State acquired sovereignty. *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113, 118 (1921).

The trial court therefore applied the correct constitutional standard when it required proof that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time Montana entered the Union. Because the equal-footing grant is measured by conditions existing on that date, any other temporal focus would contradict the doctrine's foundational premise that the State received the same proprietary rights the original States possessed in 1789.
