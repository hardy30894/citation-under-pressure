**ARGUMENT**

Federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) extends only to cases “in which a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). A state-law claim triggers this “arising under” jurisdiction only when it “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).

Minton’s malpractice claim fails the third and fourth *Grable* factors. The experimental-use exception to the on-sale bar, 35 U.S.C. § 102(b), is not “substantial” in the sense required. *Grable* itself involved a federal tax question whose resolution would control the validity of a federal tax lien and affect the government’s ability to collect taxes nationwide. 545 U.S. at 315. By contrast, the validity of one private patent affects only the parties to that litigation; it “will not change the real-world result” for any other patent holder. *Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh*, 547 U.S. 677, 701 (2006). The Federal Circuit’s contrary view in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1272 (Fed. Cir. 2007), rests on a generalized interest in uniformity rather than the case-specific substantiality *Grable* demands.

Even if the patent issue were substantial, federal jurisdiction would “disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. Legal-malpractice actions have been the exclusive province of state courts for centuries. *See, e.g.*, *Krouse v. McKenna*, 26 Pa. 467, 468 (1856). Congress gave federal courts exclusive patent jurisdiction to ensure uniform construction of the patent laws, not to federalize garden-variety tort suits merely because they contain an embedded patent question. *See Merrell Dow Pharm. Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 814 (1986). Allowing federal jurisdiction here would open the federal courts to “a tremendous number of cases” that Congress never intended to remove from state dockets. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 319.

Because Minton’s claim does not satisfy *Grable*’s requirements, the Texas courts possessed subject-matter jurisdiction. The judgment of the Texas Supreme Court should be reversed.
