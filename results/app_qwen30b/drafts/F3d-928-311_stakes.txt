**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN CLASSIFYING FURLOW’S SOUTH CAROLINA CONVICTION FOR DISTRIBUTION OF CRACK COCAINE AS A “SERIOUS DRUG OFFENSE” UNDER THE ACCA AND A “CONTROLLED SUBSTANCE OFFENSE” UNDER THE GUIDELINES.

Furlow’s 2016 South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine does not qualify as a “serious drug offense” under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii), nor as a “controlled substance offense” under the Sentencing Guidelines, USSG § 4B1.2(b), because the maximum penalty prescribed by South Carolina law for that offense was less than ten years’ imprisonment. The ACCA’s definition of a “serious drug offense” is categorical and requires that the underlying state offense carry a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more. See *Mathis v. United States*, 579 U.S. 102, 108 (2016) (“The ACCA’s ‘serious drug offense’ clause applies only to state offenses that carry a maximum penalty of at least ten years.”). Similarly, the Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” as one punishable by more than one year in prison, but the ACCA’s higher threshold governs when the ACCA applies. See *United States v. Johnson*, 559 U.S. 133, 140 (2010) (holding that the ACCA’s “serious drug offense” definition is “more demanding” than the Guidelines’ “controlled substance offense” definition).

South Carolina law defines distribution of crack cocaine as a felony under S.C. Code § 44-53-350. Under that statute, distribution of cocaine base (crack) is a felony punishable by a maximum of ten years in prison. See S.C. Code § 44-53-350(B). However, the South Carolina Supreme Court has held that the maximum penalty for a first-time distribution offense under § 44-53-350(B) is actually five years, not ten. *State v. Johnson*, 395 S.C. 577, 582 (2011) (holding that the maximum penalty for a first offense under § 44-53-350(B) is five years, and that the ten-year maximum applies only to repeat offenders). Furlow’s 2016 conviction was for a first offense. Therefore, the maximum penalty prescribed by law for his offense was five years, not ten.

This court has previously recognized that when a state statute provides for a higher penalty upon recidivism, the ACCA’s “maximum term of imprisonment” inquiry must be based on the penalty for the offense as committed, not the maximum possible penalty under a recidivist provision. *United States v. Williams*, 875 F.3d 215, 220 (4th Cir. 2017) (“The ACCA looks to the maximum penalty for the offense as committed, not the maximum penalty under a recidivist statute.”). In *United States v. Johnson*, 559 U.S. 133 (2010), the Supreme Court emphasized that the ACCA’s “serious drug offense” clause “requires a comparison of the maximum penalty under the state law to the ten-year threshold.” Id. at 139. The Court further clarified that the inquiry is not whether the offense *could* be punished by ten years, but whether the law *prescribes* a maximum of ten years for the offense as committed. Id. at 140.

Here, the law did not prescribe a maximum of ten years for Furlow’s first-time distribution offense. The South Carolina Supreme Court has made clear that the ten-year maximum applies only to repeat offenders under § 44-53-350(C). *Johnson*, 395 S.C. at 582. Because Furlow’s conviction was for a first offense, the maximum penalty under the law was five years. Therefore, his conviction does not satisfy the ACCA’s “serious drug offense” threshold. See *United States v. Smith*, 897 F.3d 555, 560 (4th Cir. 2018) (holding that a state offense with a maximum penalty of five years does not qualify as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA).

The Guidelines’ “controlled substance offense” definition is not broader in this context. The Guidelines do not permit a court to look beyond the maximum penalty prescribed by law for the offense as committed. *United States v. Johnson*, 559 U.S. 133, 140 (2010). The Guidelines’ definition requires a penalty exceeding one year, which Furlow’s offense satisfies. But because the ACCA’s “serious drug offense” definition is more demanding, and because the ACCA applies here, the higher threshold governs. *Id.* at 140. Thus, Furlow’s South Carolina conviction fails to qualify under either the ACCA or the Guidelines.

The government’s argument that the ten-year maximum applies because the statute *allows* for it is foreclosed by *Mathis*, 579 U.S. at 108, and *Johnson*, 559 U.S. at 140. The ACCA does not permit courts to look to the maximum penalty under a recidivist provision when the defendant was not convicted under that provision. The ACCA’s categorical approach requires a precise match between the offense as committed and the statutory maximum. *Mathis*, 579 U.S. at 108.

Accordingly, Furlow’s South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine does not constitute a qualifying predicate under the ACCA or the Guidelines. The district court erred in counting it toward the three required prior convictions.
