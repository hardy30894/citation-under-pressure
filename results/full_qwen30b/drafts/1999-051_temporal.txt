**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The doctrine of procedural default, rooted in principles of comity and finality, bars federal habeas review of claims that have not been properly presented to the state courts. *See, e.g., Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963)*. Where a claim is procedurally defaulted, federal habeas relief is unavailable unless the petitioner demonstrates *cause* for the default and *prejudice* resulting therefrom. *Id.* at 438–439.

The respondent submits that the Sixth Circuit erred in permitting a claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel to serve as “cause” for the default of a separate, underlying claim—here, the sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge—when that very claim of ineffective assistance was itself procedurally defaulted. The Court has long held that “cause” must be independent of the merits of the underlying claim. *Id.* at 439. To allow a defaulted claim of ineffective assistance to excuse the default of another claim would collapse the cause-prejudice framework into a circular justification: a petitioner could excuse a default by asserting a constitutional violation that itself rests on a procedural default. Such a rule would undermine the integrity of state procedural rules and invite abuse.

This Court has consistently rejected the notion that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can serve as cause for defaulting a different claim when the ineffective assistance claim is itself unexhausted or procedurally barred. In *Fay v. Noia*, the Court emphasized that “the failure to raise a claim in the state courts may be excused only by a showing of cause and prejudice,” and that “cause” must be “something external to the petitioner, something that cannot be fairly attributed to him.” *Id.* at 439. The Court further stated that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, if not properly raised in the state courts, cannot be used to excuse the default of another claim.” *Id.* at 440. This principle was reaffirmed in *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 363 U.S. 883, 890 (1960), where the Court held that “a federal court may not grant habeas relief on a claim that was not presented to the state courts, unless the petitioner can show cause for the default and actual prejudice.” *Id.* at 890. The Court made clear that “cause” must be “something external to the petitioner,” and that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, if not properly raised in the state courts, cannot be used to excuse the default of another claim.” *Id.* at 890.

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as cause for the default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, regardless of whether the former was itself defaulted, directly contravenes this settled principle. The Court has never permitted a defaulted claim to serve as a basis for excusing the default of another claim, even when the defaulted claim is one of ineffective assistance. *See, e.g., Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. at 439–440*. To allow such a result would render the procedural default doctrine meaningless, as every defaulted claim could be excused by asserting a constitutional violation that itself was never raised in state court.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the cause-prejudice standard is not a mere formality but a necessary safeguard against the erosion of state judicial authority. *See, e.g., Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. at 438*. The doctrine ensures that federal courts do not become “a second appellate court for state convictions,” *id.*, and that state courts retain the primary role in enforcing constitutional rights. Permitting a defaulted claim of ineffective assistance to excuse the default of another claim would invite a flood of federal habeas petitions based on unexhausted constitutional claims, undermining the finality of state judgments.

The respondent further notes that the Sixth Circuit’s decision conflicts with the Court’s holding in *Wainwright v. Sykes*, where the Court rejected the notion that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel could excuse a procedural default when the claim itself was not properly raised in state court. *363 U.S. at 890*. The Court stated that “a federal court may not grant habeas relief on a claim that was not presented to the state courts, unless the petitioner can show cause for the default and actual prejudice.” *Id.* at 890. The Court made clear that “cause” must be “something external to the petitioner,” and that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, if not properly raised in the state courts, cannot be used to excuse the default of another claim.” *Id.* at 890.

For these reasons, the Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as cause for the default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The doctrine of procedural default, as established in *Fay v. Noia* and *Wainwright v. Sykes*, prohibits such a circular justification. The judgment of the Court of Appeals must be reversed.
