The petitioners challenge the constitutionality of the Massachusetts law creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics, arguing that it restricts their First Amendment rights. However, the law is a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time-place-manner regulation that protects the rights of prospective patients and clinic employees without offending the First Amendment rights of others.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government has a legitimate interest in maintaining public order and safety, particularly in areas surrounding healthcare facilities. In Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569 (1941), the Court upheld a statute requiring a permit for parades and processions, noting that the government has the authority to regulate the use of public streets and sidewalks. Similarly, in Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77 (1949), the Court upheld an ordinance prohibiting the use of sound trucks that emit loud and raucous noises, holding that the government has the power to regulate the use of public spaces to protect the public from nuisance.

The Massachusetts law at issue here is a reasonable exercise of this authority. By creating a buffer zone around abortion clinics, the law ensures that patients and employees can enter and exit the facilities without being subjected to harassment or intimidation. This is a legitimate government interest, as recognized by the Court in Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), which held that the government has a duty to protect the public from harm.

The law is also content-neutral, as it applies equally to all individuals who approach the clinic, regardless of their message or viewpoint. This is in line with the principles established in Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), where the Court held that a municipality's denial of a permit to a group of Jehovah's Witnesses to use a public park was unconstitutional because it was based on the group's message. In contrast, the Massachusetts law does not target any particular message or viewpoint.

Furthermore, the law leaves open adequate alternative channels of communication for the petitioners to express their views. As the Court noted in Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147 (1939), the government may regulate the use of public spaces to prevent littering and maintain public order, while still allowing individuals to express their views through other means. Here, the petitioners can still engage in oral speech, distribute literature, and display signs outside the buffer zone.

The petitioners' argument that the law is unconstitutional because it restricts their ability to engage in "sidewalk counseling" is unpersuasive. The Court has long recognized that the government has the authority to regulate the use of public spaces to protect the public from harm. In Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568 (1942), the Court upheld a statute prohibiting the use of "offensive, derisive or annoying" language in public, holding that such speech is not protected by the First Amendment.

Additionally, the Court's decision in Feiner v. New York, 340 U.S. 315 (1951), is relevant here. In Feiner, the Court upheld the conviction of a speaker who refused to stop speaking after being asked to do so by police, noting that the government has the authority to regulate speech that poses a threat to public order. Similarly, the Massachusetts law regulates the use of public spaces around abortion clinics to protect patients and employees from harassment and intimidation.

The law is also not overly broad, as it is narrowly tailored to achieve the government's legitimate interest in protecting public safety. In Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), the Court upheld a statute requiring a license to hold a meeting in a public park, noting that the government has the authority to regulate the use of public spaces to prevent disorder. The Massachusetts law is similarly tailored to prevent disorder and protect public safety around abortion clinics.

In conclusion, the Massachusetts law creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional exercise of the government's authority to regulate the use of public spaces. It is a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time-place-manner regulation that protects the rights of prospective patients and clinic employees without offending the First Amendment rights of others. The petitioners' challenge to the law should be rejected.
