The statute does not authorize indefinite detention beyond the 90-day removal period.

The starting point is the statutory text. Section 1231(a)(6) permits detention “beyond the removal period,” but it says nothing about perpetual confinement. In comparable settings, this Court has long required a clear statement before reading a statute to authorize extraordinary restraints on liberty. See The Abby Dodge, 223 U.S. 166 (1912); United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482 (1869). That canon is especially strong where a construction would raise grave constitutional doubts. See Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22 (1932); Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 297 U.S. 288 (1936).

Those doubts are substantial here. The Constitution disfavors indefinite executive detention absent the narrow circumstances historically recognized by law. In Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2 (1866), the Court rejected military detention where the civil courts were open, explaining that constitutional liberty does not yield to convenience. In Ex parte Bollman, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75 (1807), the Court held that imprisonment must rest on lawful authority, not executive will. And in Ex parte Merryman, 17 F. Cas. 144 (C.C.D. Md. 1861) (Taney, C.J.), the principle was stated with particular force that detention cannot be justified by necessity alone when law does not authorize it. See also Ex parte Randolph, 20 F. Cas. 242 (C.C.D. Va. 1833) (Marshall, C.J.); Ableman v. Booth, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 506 (1859).

The government’s reading would permit potentially permanent custody of a removable alien even after the removal period has expired and removal is not reasonably foreseeable. That reading is inconsistent with the longstanding rule that restraints on liberty must be closely tied to their lawful justification. In Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 421 (1855), the Court recognized that legal authority must be matched to the relief sought; where the basis for restraint fails, the restraint cannot continue. Likewise, in Johnson v. Eisentrager, 339 U.S. 763 (1950), the Court emphasized that liberty questions involving executive confinement require careful attention to the source and limits of governmental power.

The Court’s immigration precedents do not support the government’s position. It is true that the political branches have broad authority over admission and exclusion. But even in that field, the Court has not licensed limitless detention. In Yamataya v. Fisher, 189 U.S. 86 (1903), the Court held that an alien physically present in the United States is entitled to due process. In Japanese Immigrant Case, 189 U.S. 86 (1903), and United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537 (1950), the Court upheld exclusionary authority, but those cases involved admission decisions at the threshold, not ongoing imprisonment after a final order of removal and failed repatriation efforts. Likewise, Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), addressed an excluded alien stopped at the border; it does not control the continued detention of an admitted resident alien where removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. See also Bridges v. Wixon, 326 U.S. 135 (1945); Delgadillo v. Carmichael, 332 U.S. 388 (1947).

The traditional common-law conception of imprisonment also cuts against the government. Habeas corpus exists to test whether custody is lawful, and unlawful detention cannot be prolonged merely because the government has not succeeded in its objective. See Ex parte Watkins, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193 (1830); Ex parte Bollman, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75 (1807). If removal is no longer practically achievable, detention ceases to serve the statute’s purpose and becomes punishment by another name—something the Court has repeatedly treated with skepticism absent express authorization. See Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228 (1896); Wong Wing Hong v. United States, 163 U.S. 228 (1896) (same case).

The better construction is therefore the one adopted by this Court: detention under § 1231(a)(6) lasts only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal, and once removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued custody is unauthorized. That interpretation honors the statute while avoiding the serious constitutional questions that an indefinite-detention reading would create. See Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22 (1932); Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 297 U.S. 288 (1936).

Accordingly, the judgment should be affirmed.
