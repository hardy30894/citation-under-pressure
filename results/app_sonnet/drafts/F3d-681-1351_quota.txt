ARGUMENT

I. The Board Erred by Extending Harari I’s Narrow, Fact-Specific Standard to the Intervening ’838 and ’768 Applications

This Court’s decision in Harari v. Hollmer, 602 F.3d 1348 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (“Harari I”), announced a deliberately narrow rule applicable only “at th[e] stage” of an initial filing accompanied by a transmittal sheet and preliminary amendment that resolved an ambiguity in a copied incorporation statement. Id. at 1352–53. The Court held that “the identity of the incorporated reference” need only be “clear to a reasonable examiner in light of the documents presented”—documents that, critically, included the transmittal sheet and preliminary amendment filed contemporaneously with the ’880 application. Id. at 1353–54. No such curative documents exist in the record for the ’838 or ’768 applications. The Board’s judgment below erroneously imported Harari I’s reasonable-examiner gloss into an entirely different procedural posture, one governed instead by this Court’s longstanding general rule for incorporation by reference.

That general rule requires that the host document itself—without resort to external context supplied years later—“identify with detailed particularity what specific material it incorporates and clearly indicate where that material is found.” Advanced Display Systems, Inc. v. Kent State University, 212 F.3d 1272, 1282 (Fed. Cir. 2000); accord Zenon Environmental, Inc. v. U.S. Filter Corp., 506 F.3d 1370, 1378 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (incorporation by reference must be “sufficiently precise” to indicate the incorporated material). This Court has repeatedly held that “[m]ere reference to another application, or patent, or publication is not incorporation of anything therein” unless the reference is “clear and unequivocal.” In re De Seversky, 474 F.2d 671, 674 (C.C.P.A. 1973). See also Cook Biotech Inc. v. Acell, Inc., 460 F.3d 1365, 1376 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (incorporation by reference requires that the host document “identify with detailed particularity” the incorporated subject matter); Callicrate v. Wadsworth Mfg., Inc., 427 F.3d 1361, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (same); Transco Products Inc. v. Performance Contracting, Inc., 38 F.3d 551, 555 (Fed. Cir. 1994) (incorporation by reference is only effective if the host application makes clear what specific material is incorporated).

The ’838 and ’768 applications contain the same copied language stating that the referenced application was “filed on the same day as the present application”—a statement that is facially false as applied to those later-filed continuations, which were not filed on the same day as the ’579 application. Unlike the ’880 application, neither the ’838 application nor the ’768 application was accompanied by a transmittal sheet or preliminary amendment correcting this facial defect or otherwise identifying the ’579 application by serial number. Harari I’s holding was expressly limited to circumstances in which such curative filings existed “at th[e] stage of the proceedings.” 602 F.3d at 1352. The Board therefore erred as a matter of law in applying Harari I’s reasonable-examiner standard—crafted for a unique factual circumstance—to intervening applications that lack any comparable contemporaneous clarification. Under the general rule of Advanced Display Systems, Zenon Environmental, Cook Biotech, and Transco Products, the incorporation statements in the ’838 and ’768 applications were fatally ambiguous and did not incorporate the ’579 application’s disclosure.

II. Because the ’579 Application Was Not Properly Incorporated in the Intervening Applications, the Chain of Priority Under § 120 Was Broken

Section 120 requires that each application in a chain of continuity contain a disclosure that complies with 35 U.S.C. § 112, first paragraph, including adequate written description of the claimed subject matter. This Court has made clear that “the disclosure of each application in the chain must adequately describe the claimed subject matter” to preserve the benefit of an earlier filing date. Lockwood v. American Airlines, Inc., 107 F.3d 1565, 1571–72 (Fed. Cir. 1997). A break in written-description support at any link in the chain severs the applicant’s entitlement to the earlier filing date. Id. The written description requirement exists to ensure that the inventor “had possession” of the claimed invention as of the earlier filing date, Vas-Cath Inc. v. Mahurkar, 935 F.2d 1555, 1563–64 (Fed. Cir. 1991), and this Court sitting en banc reaffirmed that the test asks whether the disclosure “reasonably conveys to those skilled in the art that the inventor had possession” of the claimed subject matter as of the filing date in question, Ariad Pharmaceuticals, Inc. v. Eli Lilly & Co., 598 F.3d 1336, 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (en banc).

Because the ’838 and ’768 applications did not properly incorporate the ’579 application, they did not adequately disclose the subject matter later claimed in the ’880 application that depends on the ’579 application’s teachings. Under Lockwood, this break in the chain means the ’880 application cannot claim priority through the ’838 and ’768 applications back to the original ’566 application’s April 13, 1989 filing date. The earliest date to which the ’880 application is entitled is therefore December 20, 1996—the filing date of the ’708 application, which undisputedly discloses the ’579 application without ambiguity.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Board’s judgment awarding Harari the benefit of the ’566 application’s filing date should be reversed, and the case remanded for entry of judgment based on the ’708 application’s December 20, 1996 priority date.
