# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of the FDCPA Confirms That an Entity Which Collects Debts It Owns on Its Own Account Is a Creditor, Not a "Debt Collector"

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates "debt collectors," 15 U.S.C. § 1692e, a term Congress defined to encompass, as relevant here, "any person who . . . regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The critical word is "another." Congress did not define a debt collector as any entity that regularly collects debts; it defined the term to reach only entities that collect debts owed to *someone else*. When Santander purchased the Petitioners' defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto, the debts ceased to be owed to "another" and became debts owed to Santander itself. Santander's subsequent collection activity was therefore undertaken as a creditor collecting its own debt, not as a debt collector collecting the debt of another.

This Court has recognized that the FDCPA's debt-collector definition "includes a person 'who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] . . . another.'" *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (alteration in original) (quoting § 1692a(6)). The Eleventh Circuit's decision in *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309 (11th Cir. 2015), correctly explained that "our inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether [the purchaser] regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to [the purchaser]; our inquiry is whether [the purchaser] regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection." *Id.* at 1318. The statute asks a present-tense question—to whom is the debt owed *now*—not a historical one.

Petitioners' contrary reading, under which "owed or due another" could mean debts that were "originally owed or due another," finds no support in the text and has been squarely rejected. As the Ninth Circuit held, "[t]he statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs'] interpretation that 'owed or due another' means 'originally owed or due another.'" *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013). Nothing in § 1692a(6) supports freezing a debt's ownership status at origination for purposes of determining who "regularly collects" it years later.

## II. The Default Status of a Debt at the Time of Acquisition Does Not Determine Whether the Purchaser Is a "Debt Collector"

Petitioners' theory rests on an inference drawn from statutory exclusions rather than from the operative definition of "debt collector" itself. They point to the exclusion from the definition of "creditor" for any person who "receives an assignment or transfer of a debt in default solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another," 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4), and to the exclusion from "debt collector" for one collecting debt "which was not in default at the time it was obtained by such person," *id.* § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). From these exclusions, Petitioners reason that default status alone is the dispositive factor separating creditors from debt collectors.

This argument inverts the statute's structure. Section 1692a(6) first defines who *is* a debt collector; only after that threshold definition is satisfied does the analysis turn to whether one of the enumerated exclusions in subsections (A)-(F) removes that person from coverage. As the Eleventh Circuit explained, "where a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a 'debt collector' as a matter of course. Instead . . . he must satisfy the Act's substantive requirements" in the first instance. *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1314. Petitioners' argument skips this threshold step entirely, attempting to bootstrap Santander into debt-collector status through an exclusion from a definition it never satisfied.

The default-status exclusions serve a narrower purpose: protecting entities that service performing loans on behalf of others from being swept into debt-collector regulation, while withdrawing that protection once a serviced loan defaults. As the Senate Report accompanying the FDCPA makes clear, Congress did not intend the definition of debt collector "to cover the activities of . . . mortgage service companies and others who service outstanding debts for others, so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing." S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977), *reprinted in* 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. That provision addresses servicers acting *on behalf of others*; it says nothing about the separate scenario presented here—an entity that purchases a defaulted debt outright and thereafter collects for its own account.

## III. Petitioners' Rule Would Convert Ordinary Debt Purchasers Into Regulated Debt Collectors Contrary to the FDCPA's Structure and Purpose

Petitioners' position would mean that Santander—despite having purchased the loans for its own account—remains permanently tainted by its prior role as a third-party servicer, "simply by purchasing the defaulted debt it was collecting for another." This sweeping rule cannot be reconciled with the statute Congress wrote. Congress distinguished creditors from debt collectors based on the *capacity* in which a person acts—for itself or for another—not based on a debt's history before the current collector acquired it. See 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692a(4), 1692a(6). A consumer finance company like Santander may simultaneously service loans for others, lend money, and purchase and collect debts for its own portfolio; the FDCPA regulates only the conduct undertaken in the debt-collector capacity, not every activity of an entity that sometimes acts as a debt collector.

This limitation reflects Congress's stated purpose: "to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors." 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). Congress targeted the distinctive incentive problem posed by third-party collectors who have no ongoing relationship with the debtor and no stake beyond extracting payment. A purchaser who owns the debt outright, by contrast, stands in the same position as the original creditor, bearing the same incentives and — critically — the same exposure to state-law remedies for its own misconduct. Nothing in the statutory text suggests Congress meant to erase this creditor/debt-collector line simply because the creditor acquired the debt after default rather than at origination.

## Conclusion

The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander, having purchased the Petitioners' loans and thereafter collected on them for its own account, acted as a creditor and not as a "debt collector" under 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The judgment below should be affirmed.
