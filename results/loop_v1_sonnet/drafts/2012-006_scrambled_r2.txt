**ARGUMENT**

**Once a defendant produces evidence that he withdrew from a conspiracy before the limitations period began, the burden shifts to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he remained a member during that period.**

Conspiracy is a continuing partnership in crime, and the government's obligation to prove every element of that crime does not evaporate merely because the partnership has grown old. This Court explained the nature of the offense in *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601 (1910), describing conspiracy as an ongoing criminal enterprise that persists so long as its members continue to act in furtherance of its objects. It follows that where a defendant introduces evidence that his membership in that partnership ended before the statute of limitations began to run, the question whether the partnership—and his place in it—survived into the limitations period becomes an element of the charged offense, not an affirmative defense that the defendant must disprove the government's case to establish.

This Court's decision in *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), confirms that withdrawal is a legally significant act that terminates a defendant's criminal exposure for subsequent conduct of the conspiracy. Because withdrawal marks the end of the defendant's culpable participation, evidence of withdrawal directly negates the continuing-membership element that the government must otherwise prove. The government cannot rely on a presumption of continued membership to relieve itself of that burden. In *Fiswick v. United States*, 329 U.S. 211 (1946), this Court examined precisely this problem in the limitations context, holding that mere past association with a conspiracy's aims does not establish that a defendant remained a participant during the statutory period; the government must show continuing adherence to the conspiracy's illegal purposes within that period. And in *Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440 (1949), the Court recognized that a conspiracy terminates once its main objectives are accomplished or abandoned, so that later acts or statements by former co-conspirators cannot be attributed to a defendant who is no longer part of the venture.

Petitioners' position is not novel; it reflects the settled approach of the courts of appeals confronting this precise question before the decision below. In *United States v. Borelli*, 336 F.2d 376 (2d Cir. 1964), Judge Friendly held that once a defendant comes forward with evidence of withdrawal, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant's membership continued into the limitations period, because the alternative would permit conviction on proof of a conspiracy that, as to the defendant, may have ended years before the government could lawfully prosecute it. The D.C. Circuit's contrary rule—that the defendant must prove withdrawal—cannot be reconciled with the constitutional principle that the prosecution alone bears the burden of proving guilt.

That principle rests on this Court's due process jurisprudence governing the allocation of proof burdens in criminal cases. In *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the Court held that once a defendant introduces evidence raising a reasonable doubt as to an element of the crime—there, sanity—the government must overcome that doubt beyond a reasonable doubt; the burden does not shift to the accused simply because he was the one who introduced the evidence. This Court reaffirmed that framework in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), distinguishing between defenses that negate an element of the offense, where the *Davis* rule applies, and independent affirmative defenses, where a State may constitutionally require the defendant to bear the burden. Continued membership in a conspiracy within the limitations period is not an independent defense like insanity in the *Leland* sense; it is inseparable from the very existence of the offense charged against a particular defendant during the relevant period.

The same principle forecloses any reliance on a presumption of continued membership as a substitute for proof. In *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463 (1943), this Court held that a statutory presumption offered in place of direct proof of an element of a crime must rest on a rational connection between the fact proved and the fact presumed, and cannot be used to relieve the government of its burden to establish guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. A presumption that a defendant who was once a conspirator remains one indefinitely, notwithstanding uncontroverted evidence of withdrawal, has no such rational foundation and cannot substitute for the government's affirmative proof.

Finally, the constitutional stakes of misallocating this burden are not abstract. In *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513 (1958), the Court cautioned that "the risk of error" inherent in any allocation of a burden of proof must be weighed against the interest at stake, and held that when fundamental liberty interests are at issue, courts must be "reluctant to increase the risks of error borne by" the party whose liberty is threatened. Few risks are greater than requiring a defendant to disprove, by a preponderance of evidence, that he remained a criminal conspirator during years for which the government's own charging decision reflects doubt about its ability to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

Taken together, *Kissel*, *Hyde*, *Fiswick*, and *Krulewitch* establish that withdrawal terminates the continuing offense of conspiracy as to the withdrawing defendant, while *Davis*, *Leland*, *Tot*, and *Speiser* establish that once evidence of that termination is in the case, the Constitution places the burden of persuasion squarely on the government to prove membership within the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt. The judgment below, which inverted that burden, should be reversed.
