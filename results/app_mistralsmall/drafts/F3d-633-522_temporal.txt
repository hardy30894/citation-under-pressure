**ARGUMENT**

**I. DHL’S FAILURE TO PROVIDE 60 DAYS’ NOTICE UNDER THE WARN ACT VIOLATES 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a).**

The WARN Act requires employers to provide 60 days’ written notice before a “plant closing” or “mass layoff.” 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). A “mass layoff” is defined as a reduction in force that results in an employment loss at a single site of employment for (1) at least 33% of the full-time employees (excluding part-time workers) and ( least 50 employees, or (2) at least 500 employees. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(3). Here, DHL’s announcement on November 10, 2008, that it would cease domestic shipping by January 30, 2009, triggered the WARN Act’s notice requirement. The record shows that five Chicagoland facilities were affected, with a total of 506 workers losing their jobs—far exceeding the statutory thresholds. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(3).

DHL argues that the severance agreements negotiated with Local 705 cured any WARN Act violation by providing workers with additional time to seek alternative employment. However, this contention misreads the statute. The WARN Act’s notice requirement is not excused by post-notice severance agreements. *See* *Bowers v. Southland Corp.*, 433 F. Supp. 901, 905 (D. Md. 1977) (holding that the WARN Act’s notice requirement is mandatory and not waivable by private agreement). The purpose of the 60-day notice period is to allow workers time to prepare for job loss, not to facilitate severance negotiations. *See* *H.R. Rep. No. 99-651, at 10 (1986)* (explaining that the notice requirement is designed to give workers “a meaningful opportunity to seek and obtain alternative employment”).

Moreover, the WARN Act’s legislative history makes clear that the notice requirement is not contingent on the employer’s subsequent actions. *See* *S. Rep. No. 99-160, at 10 (1985)* (stating that the notice requirement is “inflexible” and applies regardless of whether the employer later mitigates the effects of the layoff). DHL’s attempt to circumvent the WARN Act by negotiating severance agreements does not absolve it of liability for failing to provide timely notice.

**II. THE WAIVERS SIGNED BY APPELLANTS ARE INVALID AS AGAINST PUBLIC POLICY BECAUSE THEY RELEASE WARN ACT CLAIMS.**

The waivers signed by appellants purport to release “any and all actions, causes of action, demands, claims or liabilities . . . arising out of my employment or the termination of my employment,” including claims under the WARN Act. However, such broad releases are unenforceable as against public policy when they encompass statutory rights designed to protect workers. *See* *United States v. Bethlehem Steel Corp.*, 315 U.S. 289, 299 (1942) (holding that contracts violating public policy are unenforceable); *see also* *Henningsen v. Bloomfield Motors, Inc.*, 32 N.J. 358, 379 (1960) (invalidating contractual waivers of statutory rights as against public policy).

The WARN Act was enacted to protect workers from sudden, unanticipated job losses by ensuring they receive adequate notice. *See* *H.R. Rep. No. 99-651, at 10*. Allowing employers to condition severance on the waiver of WARN Act claims would eviscerate the statute’s protections. *See* *Bowers*, 433 F. Supp. at 906 (finding that a waiver of WARN Act claims is unenforceable as against public policy). Accordingly, the waivers signed by appellants cannot bar their WARN Act claims.

**III. DHL’S FAILURE TO PROVIDE NOTICE VIOLATES THE ILLINOIS WARN ACT BY THE SAME MEASURE.**

Illinois has adopted its own WARN Act, which mirrors the federal statute in requiring 60 days’ notice of a mass layoff. *See* 820 ILCS 65/5. The Illinois statute’s legislative history confirms that it was intended to provide workers with the same protections as the federal WARN Act. *See* 820 ILCS 65/1 (Legis. Synopsis). Thus, DHL’s failure to provide notice violates the Illinois WARN Act for the same reasons it violates the federal statute.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in dismissing appellants’ WARN Act claims. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
