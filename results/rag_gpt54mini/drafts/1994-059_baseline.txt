**Argument**

The Arkansas Amendment cannot stand because the Constitution fixes the qualifications for Members of Congress and leaves no room for state supplementation. Article I provides the exclusive qualifications: a Representative must be at least 25 years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the State when elected; a Senator must be at least 30, a citizen for nine years, and an inhabitant of the State when elected. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, cl. 2, 3, cl. 3. The question is not whether Arkansas may regulate ballot access generally, but whether it may add disqualifications for federal office. It may not.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that the Constitution itself creates and controls federal congressional office. In *Wiley v. Sinkler*, 179 U.S. 58 (1900), the Court explained that the office of Representative “is created by that Constitution and by that alone,” and that the Constitution “adopts the qualification thus furnished” for congressional elections. Id. at 65. That principle is fatal to Amendment 73. A State may not transform an elected federal office into a state-created office subject to additional state-imposed eligibility rules.

The State’s power over elections is real, but it is a power over the “Times, Places and Manner” of holding elections, not over the substantive qualifications for office. U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1. *Oregon v. Mitchell*, 400 U.S. 112 (1970), recognized that the Constitution allocates to the States authority over election mechanics, but that authority exists in a system where Congress may alter regulations and where the Constitution itself sets federal qualifications. Id. at 117-18. The distinction between election procedure and officeholder qualifications is decisive here. Ballot-access rules can regulate how elections are conducted; they cannot nullify a constitutionally eligible candidate because of prior service.

Arkansas’s contrary theory cannot be squared with the structure of Article I or the Supreme Court’s treatment of congressional qualifications as nationally uniform. In *Hawke v. Smith (No. 1)*, 253 U.S. 221 (1920), the Court held that when the Constitution assigns a function to a designated constitutional actor, state law may not interpose an alternative condition. Id. at 230-31. Likewise, in *Newberry v. United States*, 256 U.S. 232 (1921), the Court emphasized that congressional elections and the federal constitutional scheme are governed by federal, not state, control. Id. at 250-51. Amendment 73 would do what those cases forbid: add a state condition to the federal constitutional design.

The lower court’s reliance on the people’s reserved powers cannot overcome the Constitution’s specific text. A State may structure its own offices, and it may even establish term limits for those offices. But when it comes to Members of Congress, the Constitution speaks directly, and that direct command controls. *Wiley*, 179 U.S. at 65; *Oregon v. Mitchell*, 400 U.S. at 117-18. The State’s sovereignty does not include authority to revise the qualifications for federal legislators that the national Constitution has already fixed.

That is also why the State’s ballot-access formulation fails. A state may not evade the Qualifications Clauses by relabeling a disqualification as a ballot restriction. The practical effect is the same: a person otherwise qualified under the Constitution is barred from seeking office. This Court has never allowed form to defeat constitutional substance. See *Rodriguez v. Popular Democratic Party*, 457 U.S. 1, 8 (1982) (recognizing the Constitution’s specific provisions governing federal vacancies and electoral mechanics); *Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham*, 279 U.S. 597, 615 (1929) (treating constitutional judgments about qualifications and elections as matters committed to the constitutional structure). Arkansas cannot do indirectly what it cannot do directly.

Because Section 3 is unconstitutional, it follows that the remainder of Amendment 73 must fall as well. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that the proposal was presented to voters as a single package, combining state term limits with federal disqualification rules. Where the valid and invalid provisions were “all or nothing” in practical effect, severance is improper if the measure would not have passed in the invalid form. See *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). The record here shows no separate popular vote on state term limits alone. The State chose to couple its unquestionably valid state-objective with an invalid federal-objective; it must bear the consequences. See *Hoban v. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 316 S.W.2d 185 (1958).

The judgment should therefore be affirmed. The Constitution’s Qualifications Clauses are exclusive, and Arkansas may not alter them by term-limit amendment, ballot-access rule, or otherwise. *Wiley*, 179 U.S. 58; *Hawke*, 253 U.S. 221; *Oregon v. Mitchell*, 400 U.S. 112.
