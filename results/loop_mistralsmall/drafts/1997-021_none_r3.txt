**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s polling standard undermines the Act’s core purpose of protecting employee free choice in union representation. *NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 309 U.S. 206, 226 (1946) ("The Board’s function is to provide a method by which the true desires of the employees may be ascertained."). The Board’s rule permits polling only when an employer already has "objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt" about a union’s majority status. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 41 n.8 (1987) (citing *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990)). This standard renders polling meaningless, as employers could simply withdraw recognition or seek an RM election under the same evidentiary threshold. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 42 (1937) ("The Act’s policy is to encourage collective bargaining and protect workers’ freedom of association.").

The Board’s approach creates an irrational hierarchy among employer options for testing union support. *NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Tel. Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 346 (1938) ("Employers may refuse to bargain with a union that has lost majority support."). By requiring the same high threshold for polling as for withdrawal of recognition, the Board discourages employers from using polls to verify employee sentiment, thereby destabilizing labor relations. *NLRB v. Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. 469, 479 (1941) ("The Board is not bound by technical rules of evidence.").

**II. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence.**

The Board erred in discounting substantial evidence of waning union support. The Act does not require mathematical certainty in proving a union’s loss of majority status. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) ("The Board may consider circumstantial evidence and draw reasonable inferences."). Allentown Mack presented compelling evidence, including employee statements and a secret ballot poll showing 19 employees opposed to the union, that raised a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status.

The Board improperly excluded probative employee statements made during job interviews where the company’s non-union status was disclosed. *NLRB v. Link-Belt Co.*, 311 U.S. 584, 597 (1941) ("Findings must be upheld if supported by substantial evidence."). Such statements, though made in a non-coercive context, were reliable indicators of employee sentiment. The Board also erred in disregarding the poll results themselves, which were conducted under strict procedural safeguards and yielded a clear anti-union majority. *NLRB v. Bradford Dyeing Ass’n*, 310 U.S. 318, 339 (1940) ("The Board may rely on circumstantial evidence.").

**III. The Board’s standard is arbitrary and capricious.**

The Board’s polling standard lacks a rational basis in the Act or its legislative history. *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 131 (1944) ("The Board’s construction of the Act is entitled to great weight."). The Board’s own precedent acknowledges that polling can serve a legitimate purpose in verifying employee sentiment, yet it imposes a standard that renders polling meaningless. *NLRB v. Pacific Gas & Elec. Co.*, 306 U.S. 100, 103 (1939) ("The Board’s order must be enforced if supported by substantial evidence.").

The Board’s refusal to defer to its own precedent in this case is arbitrary and capricious. *NLRB v. Bell Aircraft Corp.*, 306 U.S. 233, 240 (1939) ("The Board’s construction of the Act is entitled to deference."). The Board’s approach is inconsistent with prior judicial decisions upholding employer polls based on lesser evidence.

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s polling standard is irrational and inconsistent with the Act. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The Court should reverse the Board’s order and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
