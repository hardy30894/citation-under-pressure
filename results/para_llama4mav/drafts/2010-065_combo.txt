The petitioner, Carol Anne Bond, challenges her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229, arguing that the statute is beyond the federal government's enumerated powers and inconsistent with the Tenth Amendment. However, Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to the statute.

The Supreme Court has held that private parties do not have standing to challenge a federal act on the basis of the Tenth Amendment. In Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), the Court rejected a Tenth Amendment claim brought by private utility companies, stating that "absent the states or their officers, [the utility companies] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]." Id. at 144. This holding has been followed by several circuit courts, including the First, Second, Eighth, and Tenth Circuits. See Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25 (1st Cir. 2005); Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp., 462 F.3d 219 (2d Cir. 2006); United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522 (8th Cir. 2009); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279 (10th Cir. 2004).

The rationale behind this rule is that the Tenth Amendment is a limitation on federal power that is intended to protect the states, not private individuals. As the Court explained in Tennessee Electric Power Co., the sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment, and private parties cannot raise such a claim in the absence of the states or their officers. 306 U.S. at 144. See also Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), where the Court held that a state's challenge to a federal statute must be brought by the state itself, not by its citizens.

Furthermore, the statute at issue, 18 U.S.C. § 229, was enacted pursuant to the Treaty Power, which is a valid exercise of federal authority. In Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), the Court held that a treaty is a valid exercise of federal power, and that Congress has the authority to enact laws necessary to implement its provisions. Id. at 432. The Court also stated that "if [a] treaty is valid, there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government." Id. See also Neely v. Henkel, 180 U.S. 109 (1901), where the Court held that a treaty is the supreme law of the land, and that Congress has the authority to enact laws to implement its provisions.

In addition, the Necessary and Proper Clause, Article I, § 8, cl. 18, grants Congress the authority to enact laws necessary to implement its enumerated powers, including the Treaty Power. See McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. 316 (1819), where the Court held that the Necessary and Proper Clause grants Congress the authority to enact laws necessary to implement its enumerated powers. The Court also stated that "the sound construction of the constitution must allow to the national legislature that discretion, with respect to the means by which the powers it confers are to be carried into execution, which will enable that body to perform the high duties assigned to it, in the manner most beneficial to the people." Id. at 421.

The Constitution grants the President the power to make treaties, with the advice and consent of the Senate. U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2. In Ware v. Hylton, 3 U.S. 199 (1796), the Court held that a treaty is a valid exercise of federal power, and that it supersedes state law. Id. at 236-237. See also Chirac v. Chirac, 15 U.S. 259 (1817), where the Court held that a treaty is a contract between nations, and that it is the duty of the federal government to implement its provisions.

In conclusion, Bond lacks standing to challenge the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. The statute was enacted pursuant to the Treaty Power, which is a valid exercise of federal authority. The Necessary and Proper Clause grants Congress the authority to enact laws necessary to implement its enumerated powers, including the Treaty Power. Therefore, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
