**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATES RETAIN BROAD AUTHORITY TO SHAPE THE ADMISSIBILITY AND EFFECT OF EXPERT MENTAL-STATE EVIDENCE WITHOUT VIOLATING DUE PROCESS.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require Arizona to permit a defendant to use expert evidence of his mental disease or defect to negate *mens rea* when the State has chosen to channel such evidence exclusively to the insanity defense. This Court has long recognized that the definition of the elements of a criminal offense is a matter of state law. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952). Because Arizona may define the mental element of first-degree murder as a cognitive capacity distinct from the abnormal mental condition that gives rise to an insanity plea, the State may correspondingly limit the evidence admissible on each issue.

In *Leland*, this Court upheld an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant and restricted the effect of insanity evidence to that affirmative defense. The Court held that the defense of insanity is separate from the issue of specific intent. *Id.* at 795. So too here: Arizona law separates the insanity defense—an affirmative excuse—from the State’s burden to prove *mens rea*. The evidence Clark sought to introduce went not to whether he intended to kill the officer, but to whether his delusional beliefs rendered him blameless. The State may constitutionally confine such evidence to the insanity inquiry.

**II. ARIZONA’S RULE DOES NOT IMPERMISSIBLY SHIFT THE PROSECUTION’S BURDEN OF PROOF.**

The cornerstone of criminal due process is that the State must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943). But Arizona’s rule does not relieve the State of this burden. The State must still prove that Clark acted intentionally—a mental state that, under Arizona law, is entirely compatible with a severe mental illness. The rule merely excludes irrelevant or misleading evidence from the *mens rea* determination.

When a defendant claims that his mental condition prevented him from forming intent, the State may cabin that evidence to the insanity defense because the two inquiries are logically distinct. As the Court explained in *Leland*, the question of sanity is a separate inquiry from whether the defendant formed the requisite *mens rea*. 343 U.S. at 795. Nothing in *Leland* requires that the same evidence be allowed to contest *mens rea*.

This Court has consistently rejected claims that due process compels the unbounded admission of expert psychiatric testimony. In *Tot v. United States*, the Court recognized that the Constitution does not forbid all evidentiary limitations; rather, "the due process clause is not to be used as a device to test the validity of state laws or to restrict the states in the exercise of their power." 319 U.S. at 466. And in *Leland*, the Court held that "the due process clause does not require that the state adopt any particular method of procedure." 343 U.S. at 799. That principle extends to the State’s ability to determine how evidence supporting the insanity defense may be used.

**III. MULTIPLE PRECEDENTS UPHOLD STATE CONTROL OVER THE INTERPLAY OF INSANITY AND MENS REA.**

Since the earliest days of this Court, the States have been permitted to define the relationship between mental illness and criminal responsibility. In *Leland*, the Court reiterated that a State may allocate the burden of proving an affirmative defense to the defendant without offending due process. 343 U.S. at 798-99. The Court explained that "the state is not required to adopt any particular form of procedure." *Id.* at 799. Arizona has done precisely that: the State retains the burden of proving intent; the defendant bears the burden on insanity. Evidence supporting insanity is not automatically evidence negating intent.

Long before *Leland*, this Court recognized that the defense of insanity is separate from the issue of criminal intent. In *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 484 (1895), the Court stated that "the burden of proof as to the sanity of the accused is upon the government when the defense of insanity is set up." The Court further explained that the jury must be satisfied that the defendant was sane at the time of the offense. *Id.* at 485-86. This reflects the distinct nature of the insanity defense and does not require that evidence of mental disease be admitted to negate *mens rea*.

Furthermore, in *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), the Court observed that "the trial court has a large discretion in the management of the trial." This discretion includes the authority to determine how evidence of mental condition may be used. And in *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 582 (1884), the Court recognized that the State may treat the defendant's mental condition at the time of the offense as a matter of criminal responsibility rather than a negation of intent.

Finally, in *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937), this Court held that due process requires only those rights "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty." The right to use expert mental-state evidence to negate *mens rea* in the manner Clark proposes is not such a right. It is a procedural choice that the State may make or reject.

**IV. CLARK’S PROPOSED RIGHT WOULD DISRUPT THE STATE’S CAREFULLY CRAFTED INSANITY SCHEME.**

Arizona has chosen to treat insanity as an affirmative defense requiring proof by clear and convincing evidence. Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-502(C). Allowing the same evidence to refute *mens rea* would permit a defendant to circumvent the higher burden and the specialized adjudication of insanity. This Court has never held that due process requires such a result. To the contrary, in *Leland*, the Court approved Oregon’s scheme requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt—a higher standard than Arizona’s—without any separate channel for the evidence in the *mens rea* context. 343 U.S. at 798-99.

Because Arizona’s rule respects the distinct roles of *mens rea* and insanity, does not relieve the State of its burden, and reflects a balanced legislative judgment, it does not violate the Due Process Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
