**ARGUMENT**

The Massachusetts Act is a textbook content-neutral time-place-manner regulation, and this Court’s decision in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), governs this appeal. Petitioners’ invitation to strike down the statute rests on a misreading of both the record and this Court’s public-forum jurisprudence.

**I. The Act Is a Content-Neutral, Narrowly Tailored Time-Place-Manner Restriction That Leaves Ample Alternatives.**

The framework is settled: the government may impose reasonable restrictions on the time, place, or manner of protected speech “provided the restrictions are content-neutral, are narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leave open ample alternative channels for communication.” *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989); *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984).

**A. The Act is content-neutral.**

“The principal inquiry in determining content neutrality, in speech cases generally and in time, place, or manner cases in particular, is whether the government has adopted a regulation of speech because of disagreement with the message it conveys. The government’s purpose is the controlling consideration.” *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719. By its plain terms, the Act bars “any person” from remaining on a public way or sidewalk within a clearly marked 35-foot radius of a reproductive health care facility. It references neither abortion, nor counseling, nor any viewpoint. It is justified without reference to speech at all: the legislature acted to ensure safe access to medical facilities. That is a purpose “unrelated to the content of expression.” *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The exemptions for patients, employees, public safety officers, and passersby advancing to other destinations serve the same access purpose; they are not speaker-based carve-outs for preferred messages. Because the statute regulates the places where speech may occur, not its message, it is a “regulation of the places where some speech may occur.” *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), and *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), is unavailing. Those cases involved regulations aimed at the content of particular messages or at particular speakers. The Act here applies to everyone—pro-life advocates, abortion-rights advocates, and indifferent pedestrians alike—regardless of viewpoint.

**B. The Act serves a significant governmental interest.**

This Court has repeatedly recognized “the State’s interest in protecting access to medical facilities” as significant. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715; see also *Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 767–68 (1994) (recognizing the State’s strong interest in public safety, order, and the free flow of traffic near healthcare facilities). The Commonwealth’s interest in ensuring that patients, staff, and passersby can enter healthcare facilities safely is not ancillary to the police power; it is at its core.

**C. The Act is narrowly tailored.**

Narrow tailoring does not require the least restrictive means. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798–99. The Act is materially narrower than the Colorado statute this Court upheld in *Hill*: it covers only 35 feet, not 100; it is limited to marked areas adjacent to entrances, exits, and driveways; and it burdens no speech other than remaining within those areas. It does not prohibit “approaching” any person, does not ban particular communicative activities, and dispenses with the 8-foot “floating bubble” upheld in *Hill*. Massachusetts also calibrated this statute after experience with the earlier floating-zone regime, demonstrating legislative tailoring rather than overbreadth. Cf. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 729–30 (noting the incremental approach states may take).

**D. Ample alternative channels remain.**

Petitioners remain free to speak at any volume, display signs, distribute literature, pray, and engage in sidewalk counseling from the perimeter of the zones—precisely the type of “ample alternative channels” that sufficed in *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 727–28. The record confirms they continue to do so and to succeed: McCullen alone testified that she persuaded approximately eighty women to forgo abortions during the relevant period. The First Circuit’s assessment of the evidence was faithful to the proposition that the First Amendment “does not guarantee the right to communicate one’s views at all times and places or in any manner that may be desired.” *Heffron v. Int’l Soc’y for Krishna Consciousness, Inc.*, 452 U.S. 640, 647 (1981).

**II. *Hill v. Colorado* Remains Good Law and Compels Affirmance.**

Petitioners ask this Court to discard *Hill*. That request fails under settled stare decisis principles, for “the very concept of the rule of law underlying our own Constitution requires such continuity over time that a respect for precedent is, by definition, indispensable.” *Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pa. v. Casey*, 505 U.S. 833, 854 (1992). *Hill* was decided over a decade ago, has guided legislatures and courts across the country, and no subsequent decision of this Court has questioned its reasoning. Petitioners identify no “special justification” for overruling it, particularly where the statute before the Court is materially less restrictive than the one *Hill* upheld.

The decisions petitioners invoke are not inconsistent with *Hill*. *Sorrell* struck down a law that discriminated based on the content of regulated speech. *Citizens United* struck down a speaker-based ban on corporate political speech. *Snyder* rejected tort liability for offensive protest speech in a residential setting. Nothing in those cases purported to alter the time-place-manner framework or to reject the proposition that public sidewalks near healthcare facilities may be subject to reasonable, content-neutral buffer zones. See *Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474, 480–81 (1988) (upholding residential picketing ban). *Hill*’s cornerstone—that a buffer zone protecting safe access to a medical facility is a permissible exercise of the State’s police power—remains untouched by this Court’s recent First Amendment decisions.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
