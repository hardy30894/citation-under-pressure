Respondent’s position is straightforward: Bond cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to upset her conviction. The Supreme Court has long held that private litigants lack standing to complain that Congress has invaded state prerogatives where the asserted injury is not theirs in the constitutional sense, but the States’. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939), the Court rejected a private utility’s Tenth Amendment attack on federal action, explaining that “absent the states or their officers, [the utilities] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” That holding controls here. Bond is a criminal defendant, not a State; she seeks to vindicate a structural limitation on federal power that belongs, in the first instance, to the States.

Bond’s contrary argument fails because the Tenth Amendment does not create a personal immunity from federal criminal prosecution. The Amendment “is but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered.” *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). Although *Darby* postdates 1939, its formulation reflects the same constitutional principle recognized in earlier cases: the federal government may act within the scope of its delegated powers, and private parties may not convert structural objections into individualized defenses absent a cognizable personal right. See *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238, 295 (1936) (recognizing that federal power is limited to the enumerated grants, but not suggesting that any private defendant may invoke federalism as a personal shield).

The Court’s standing cases reinforce this conclusion. “The judicial power of the United States defined and limited by art. III is not an unconditioned authority to determine the constitutionality of legislative or executive acts.” *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 488 (1923). In *Mellon*, the Court held that a plaintiff cannot maintain what is, in substance, an abstract objection to the allocation of governmental authority. Id. at 488–89. Bond’s claim is the same kind of generalized structural objection. She does not allege that the statute inflicts a personal constitutional injury distinct from its asserted encroachment on state sovereignty. Rather, she asks the Court to invalidate a federal criminal law because, in her view, Congress should have left the matter to the States. That is precisely the sort of claim *Mellon* forbids private parties to press.

Nor does *Fairchild v. Hughes*, 258 U.S. 126 (1922), help Bond. There the Court held that a private citizen lacked standing to challenge the validity of the Nineteenth Amendment based on alleged defects in ratification, because his only complaint was a generalized grievance shared by the public. Id. at 129–30. Bond’s federalism argument is likewise generalized: she complains not of a personal legal injury, but of the government’s supposed departure from a constitutional allocation of power. Under *Fairchild*, that is not enough.

The same principle appears in *Raines v. United States*, 200 U.S. 561 (1906), where the Court refused to entertain a challenge that depended on an asserted public wrong rather than a private legal injury. And in *Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), the Court again rejected standing where the claimed injury was “comparatively minute and indeterminable” and shared in common with all citizens. Id. at 487. Bond’s asserted Tenth Amendment injury is even less concrete: she seeks to transform a defense to prosecution into a free-standing structural challenge to the statute itself.

Finally, the Constitution supplies a political, not judicial, remedy for complaints that federal power has displaced state authority. *Coyle v. Smith*, 221 U.S. 559, 565–67 (1911), and *Lane County v. Oregon*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 71, 76–77 (1869), reflect the Court’s solicitude for state sovereignty; but neither case suggests that a private criminal defendant may assert the State’s prerogatives as her own. To the contrary, the structural safeguards of federalism are ordinarily enforced by the States themselves through the political process and, where appropriate, by suits brought by sovereign parties. See *South Carolina v. United States*, 199 U.S. 437, 448–49 (1905).

Because Bond is not the sovereign whose prerogatives are allegedly invaded, she lacks standing to mount this Tenth Amendment attack. Her conviction therefore stands, and the Court should affirm.
