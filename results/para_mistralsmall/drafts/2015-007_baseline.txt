**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee as interpreted in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). In *Ring*, the Supreme Court held that Arizona’s sentencing scheme violated the Sixth Amendment because it permitted a judge, rather than a jury, to find the existence of an aggravating factor necessary for imposition of the death penalty. *Id.* at 609. The Court reasoned that the aggravating factors in Arizona’s scheme were “the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense,” and thus must be found by a jury. *Id.* at 609.

Florida’s scheme is fundamentally different. Unlike Arizona, Florida does not authorize a judge to find an aggravating factor. Instead, the jury is tasked with weighing aggravating and mitigating factors and recommending a sentence of death or life imprisonment. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2) (2015). The trial judge then independently weighs the aggravating and mitigating factors and makes the final sentencing determination. *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640-41 (1989) (per curiam). The Sixth Amendment does not require that the jury impose the sentence; it only requires that the jury find the facts necessary for imposition of the death penalty. *Sparf v. United States*, 156 U.S. 51, 102 (1895) (holding that the Sixth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in sentencing).

Moreover, Florida’s scheme does not permit a judge to find an aggravating factor that was not found by the jury. The trial judge may only impose the death penalty if the jury unanimously finds at least one aggravating factor. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 589 (distinguishing Florida’s scheme from Arizona’s). Thus, the jury’s role in Florida is not merely advisory; it is the sole finder of fact for aggravating factors. *See Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 647-48 (1990) (upholding Arizona’s scheme where the jury found aggravating factors and the judge made the ultimate sentencing decision).

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme also does not violate the Eighth Amendment. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that states have broad discretion in designing their capital sentencing schemes. *See, e.g., Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 195-96 (1976) (plurality opinion) (upholding Georgia’s sentencing scheme that permitted the jury to consider aggravating and mitigating factors). The Eighth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in sentencing, nor does it require that the jury make the final sentencing determination. *See Pulley v. Harris*, 465 U.S. 37, 44 (1984) (upholding California’s scheme that permitted proportionality review by the state supreme court rather than the jury).

Furthermore, Florida’s scheme does not create an unacceptable risk of arbitrary or capricious imposition of the death penalty. The trial judge’s independent weighing of aggravating and mitigating factors ensures that the death penalty is imposed only in cases where the aggravating factors outweigh the mitigating factors. *See, e.g., Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242, 258 (1976) (upholding Florida’s scheme because the trial judge’s role in sentencing provided an additional safeguard against arbitrary imposition of the death penalty).

**III. FLORIDA’S SCHEME FOR DETERMINING MENTAL RETARDATION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT.**

Florida’s scheme for determining mental retardation also does not violate the Eighth Amendment. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Supreme Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of mentally retarded defendants. However, the Court left it to the states to determine the manner in which this constitutional restriction would be enforced. *Id.* at 317.

Florida’s scheme for determining mental retardation is consistent with *Atkins*. Florida law sets forth a three-pronged test for mental retardation: (1) significantly subaverage general intellectual functioning (IQ of 70 or below); (2) concurrent deficits in adaptive behavior; and (3) manifestation of the condition before age eighteen. Fla. Stat. § 921.137(1) (2015). The burden is on the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence. *Nixon v. State*, 2 So. 3d 137, 145 (Fla. 2009). This scheme ensures that mentally retarded defendants are not executed, while also providing a clear and objective standard for determining mental retardation.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
