The reinstatement statute, Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date. Even if it did, its application to Fernandez-Vargas would be impermissibly retroactive.

First, the plain language of the statute and the relevant legislative history do not clearly indicate that Congress intended for the reinstatement statute to apply retroactively to pre-enactment re-entries. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), the Supreme Court established a two-step test for determining whether a statute applies retroactively. The first step is to determine whether Congress has clearly indicated the statute's temporal reach. Id. at 280. If not, the court must then consider whether applying the statute retroactively would have an impermissible retroactive effect. Id.

The government bears the burden of showing that Congress clearly intended the reinstatement statute to apply retroactively. See INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 320 (2001) (holding that "a statute may not be applied retroactively ... absent clear congressional intent"). The government has not met this burden. The statute's language is ambiguous, and the legislative history does not provide clear guidance on this issue. See Fernandez-Vargas v. Ashcroft, 394 F.3d 881, 884-85 (10th Cir. 2005) (noting the ambiguity in the statute's language and legislative history).

Furthermore, even if the statute were deemed to apply to pre-enactment re-entries, its application to Fernandez-Vargas would be impermissibly retroactive. The Supreme Court has held that a statute has an impermissible retroactive effect when it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 270. In this case, Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States in 1982, 15 years before the reinstatement statute became effective. Applying the statute to him would deny him the opportunity to adjust his status, a consequence that did not exist at the time of his re-entry.

The Tenth Circuit's holding that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" is not supported by the case law. See, e.g., St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 321-23 (holding that aliens who pleaded guilty to deportable offenses had a reasonable reliance on the availability of discretionary relief from deportation). Fernandez-Vargas, like the aliens in St. Cyr, had a reasonable expectation that he could adjust his status when he re-entered the country and began building his life here.

Additionally, the court should consider the principles enunciated in cases such as Republic of Austria v. Altmann, 541 U.S. 677 (2004), and Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States, 520 U.S. 939 (1997), which emphasize the importance of clear congressional intent and the potential for unfairness in retroactive applications of statutes. See also, e.g., Pinho v. Gonzales, 432 F.3d 193, 209-10 (3d Cir. 2005) (holding that the reinstatement statute does not apply retroactively to pre-enactment re-entries).

The government's interpretation of the reinstatement statute would also raise serious constitutional concerns. See, e.g., Calderon v. Reno, 163 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (holding that retroactive application of a statute can raise due process concerns). In light of these concerns and the lack of clear congressional intent, the court should interpret the statute to avoid retroactive application.

In conclusion, the reinstatement statute does not apply to Fernandez-Vargas because he re-entered the United States before its effective date. Even if it did, its application to him would be impermissibly retroactive. The court should reverse the Tenth Circuit's decision and remand the case for further proceedings.
