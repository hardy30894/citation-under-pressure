The United States respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit correctly held that Guam's claim under CERCLA section 107 is barred because the 2004 Consent Decree triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B).

The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) provides two avenues for recovery: a section 107 "cost-recovery" action and a section 113(f) "contribution" action. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, the distinction between these two provisions is critical. In Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), the Court emphasized the importance of understanding the nuances of statutory provisions. Here, the language of section 113(f)(3)(B) is clear: a party that "has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement" may seek contribution from other PRPs. 

The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA satisfies this requirement. As the Court of Appeals noted, the Decree required Guam to undertake significant remedial actions, including closing the Ordot Dump and designing a cover system. This is consistent with the principle that a settlement can resolve liability, as seen in United States v. Swift & Co., 286 U.S. 106 (1932), where the Court held that a consent decree can have significant consequences for the parties involved.

Guam's argument that the Consent Decree did not resolve its liability is unpersuasive. The Decree's language, while disclaiming liability, nonetheless imposes significant obligations on Guam. As the Court held in United States v. Bausch & Lomb Optical Co., 131 F.2d 545 (2d Cir. 1942), aff'd, 318 U.S. 788 (1943), a consent decree can be binding even if it does not contain an admission of liability. See also, e.g., Pope Mfg. Co. v. Gormully, 144 U.S. 224 (1892) (holding that a consent decree can be enforced even if it does not contain an admission of liability).

Furthermore, the Court of Appeals' decision is consistent with the principles of statutory interpretation established in cases such as United States v. Missouri Pacific Railroad Co., 278 U.S. 269 (1929), and McCluskey v. Cromwell, 52 U.S. 318 (1850). These cases emphasize the importance of understanding the plain meaning of statutory language.

Additionally, the decision is in line with the reasoning in cases such as United States v. Wm. S. Pitcairn Corp., 33 F. Supp. 510 (D.N.J. 1940), aff'd, 118 F.2d 1020 (3d Cir. 1941), which held that a consent decree can be a final and binding resolution of a dispute. See also, e.g., Swift & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 311 (1928) (holding that a consent decree can be a final and binding resolution of a dispute).

The Court of Appeals' decision is also supported by the principle that a statute should be construed to achieve its purpose, as seen in United States v. Katz, 271 U.S. 354 (1926), and United States v. Dickson, 40 U.S. 141 (1841). CERCLA's purpose is to ensure that responsible parties pay for environmental cleanup, and the Court of Appeals' decision achieves this goal by holding Guam to its settlement agreement.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the Court of Appeals correctly held that the 2004 Consent Decree triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B), and that Guam's section 107 claim is therefore barred. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
