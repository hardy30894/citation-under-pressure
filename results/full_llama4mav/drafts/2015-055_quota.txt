The rule announced in Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 258 (2015), regarding what constitutes a violent offense under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. The Supreme Court's decision in Johnson held that the residual clause of the ACCA, which defined a violent felony as one that "otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another," was unconstitutionally vague. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). This holding was a substantive change in the law, as it narrowed the scope of the ACCA's residual clause and thereby altered the range of conduct punishable under the statute.

The retroactive application of Johnson is supported by the Supreme Court's decision in Welch v. United States, which was decided after the Eleventh Circuit's opinion in this case. However, the reasoning in Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), and subsequent cases, provides a framework for determining whether a new rule of constitutional law applies retroactively. Under Teague, a new rule applies retroactively if it is a substantive rule, rather than a procedural one. Id. at 311. The rule announced in Johnson is substantive because it "alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes." Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004).

The Eleventh Circuit's decision in this case, which held that Welch's Florida strong arm robbery conviction was a violent felony under the ACCA, was based on the residual clause. United States v. Welch, (Excerpted opinion). However, in light of Johnson, it is clear that the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague, and therefore, Welch's conviction can no longer be considered a violent felony under the ACCA.

The Supreme Court's decision in McNeill v. United States, 131 S.Ct. 2218 (2011), is instructive on the importance of applying the correct version of state law when determining whether a prior conviction qualifies as a violent felony. Id. at 2222. In this case, the Eleventh Circuit should have applied the version of Florida law in effect at the time of Welch's conviction in 1996. Under Florida law at that time, the degree of force required to commit robbery was not clearly established, and some courts held that mere snatching was sufficient. See, e.g., Goldsmith v. State, 573 So.2d 445, 445 (Fla. 2d Dist.Ct.App.1991).

The Court's decision in Begay v. United States, 553 U.S. 137 (2008), also supports the retroactive application of Johnson. In Begay, the Court held that the ACCA's residual clause required a showing that the prior offense was "roughly similar, in kind as well as in degree of risk posed, to the examples themselves." Id. at 143. The Court's subsequent decision in Johnson, which held that the residual clause was unconstitutionally vague, is consistent with this reasoning.

Furthermore, the Court's decision in Sykes v. United States, 131 S.Ct. 2267 (2011), demonstrates that the ACCA's residual clause was subject to varying interpretations, which further supports the conclusion that Johnson announced a substantive change in the law. Id. at 2277.

In United States v. Chitwood, 676 F.3d 971 (11th Cir. 2012), the Eleventh Circuit held that a prior conviction for burglary under Georgia law was a violent felony under the ACCA's residual clause. Id. at 979. However, in light of Johnson, it is clear that this holding is no longer good law.

The retroactive application of Johnson is also consistent with the Supreme Court's decision in James v. United States, 550 U.S. 192 (2007), which held that the ACCA's residual clause required a showing that the prior offense presented a "serious potential risk of physical injury to another." Id. at 208. The Court's subsequent decision in Johnson, which held that the residual clause was unconstitutionally vague, is a natural extension of this reasoning.

In conclusion, the rule announced in Johnson v. United States applies retroactively to cases on collateral review, and therefore, Welch's sentence under the ACCA should be vacated. The Eleventh Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for resentencing.
