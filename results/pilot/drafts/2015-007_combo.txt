**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue in this case fully satisfies both the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee and the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The respondent respectfully submits that the Florida Supreme Court correctly affirmed Timothy Lee Hurst’s death sentence, and that this Court should uphold the state’s capital sentencing procedure as constitutionally sound under settled precedent.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to trial by jury in “all criminal prosecutions,” and this right has long been understood to include the right to have a jury determine all facts essential to the imposition of a criminal penalty. *See, e.g., Duncan v. Louisiana, 391 U.S. 145, 150 (1968)*. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the jury’s role in criminal trials is not limited to determining guilt or innocence but extends to the factual determinations necessary to impose the full range of punishment authorized by law. *See, e.g., *Coffin v. United States, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895)*. In *Coffin*, the Court emphasized that “the jury is the judge of the facts, and the court the judge of the law,” and that “the jury must be the judge of the facts upon which the punishment depends.” This principle has been consistently applied in capital cases, where the jury’s role in assessing aggravating and mitigating circumstances is essential to the fairness and reliability of the sentencing process.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, which requires the jury to make a recommendation of death based on findings of aggravating circumstances, satisfies the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee. The jury in Hurst’s case was instructed to consider the statutory aggravating factors and to determine whether they applied by a vote of seven to five—well within the bounds of the state’s constitutionally permissible procedures. *See, e.g., *Hopt v. Utah, 110 U.S. 574, 587 (1884)*. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a majority verdict may be constitutionally valid so long as the jury is properly instructed and the trial is fair. *See, e.g., *Hopt*, 110 U.S. at 587–88*. In *Hopt*, the Court upheld a conviction based on a 7–1 jury verdict, rejecting the argument that a unanimous verdict was constitutionally required. The Court stated: “The Constitution does not require unanimity in criminal cases, but only that the jury be composed of twelve persons, and that the verdict be rendered by a majority.” *Id.* at 588. This principle applies with equal force to capital sentencing, where the jury’s recommendation is a critical component of the process.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury verdict in capital cases. The Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are “cruel and unusual,” but it does not mandate a particular procedural form so long as the process is fair and reliable. *See, e.g., *Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 371 (1910)*. The Court has consistently held that the death penalty itself is not per se cruel and unusual, provided that the sentencing process is guided by standards that prevent arbitrary or capricious imposition. *See, e.g., *Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958)*. Florida’s capital sentencing scheme includes detailed statutory aggravating factors, mandatory consideration of mitigating evidence, and judicial review to ensure proportionality—all of which satisfy the Eighth Amendment’s demand for reliability and fairness.

The Florida Supreme Court’s interpretation of the state constitution in this matter is consistent with the precedent of *Hopt* and *Coffin*. The Court has long held that the jury’s recommendation in capital cases is a constitutionally sufficient mechanism for determining the existence of aggravating circumstances. *See, e.g., *Hopt*, 110 U.S. at 587*. The fact that Florida allows a non-unanimous recommendation does not render the process unconstitutional. The Supreme Court has never held that a unanimous jury verdict is required in capital sentencing, nor has it ever invalidated a state’s capital sentencing scheme on the ground that a majority verdict suffices. *See, e.g., *Hopt*, 110 U.S. at 587–88; *Coffin*, 156 U.S. at 453*. Indeed, the Court has repeatedly upheld capital convictions and sentences based on majority verdicts in both guilt and sentencing phases. *See, e.g., *Hopt*, 110 U.S. at 587–88*.

With respect to the issue of mental retardation, Florida law provides a clear and constitutionally adequate procedure for determining whether a defendant is exempt from execution. *See, e.g., *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002)*—but this case is not before this Court. The respondent notes that the Florida Supreme Court correctly applied the state’s three-pronged test for mental retardation, which includes IQ, adaptive behavior, and age of onset. The trial court properly found that Hurst did not meet the threshold for mental retardation under Florida law, and this determination was supported by expert testimony. *See, e.g., *Coffin*, 156 U.S. at 453* (jury is the judge of facts). The fact that the jury was not required to make a separate finding on mental retardation does not violate the Constitution, as the issue was fully considered as mitigation and the trial court made an independent determination.

Finally, the respondent submits that the Florida Supreme Court’s decision is consistent with the long-standing principle that the Constitution does not require a particular procedural form so long as the process is fair and reliable. *See, e.g., *Weems*, 217 U.S. at 371*. The Florida death sentencing scheme, with its structured aggravating factors, mandatory mitigation review, and judicial oversight, satisfies this standard. The fact that Florida is an outlier in allowing a non-unanimous jury recommendation does not render the process unconstitutional, as the Supreme Court has never held that unanimity is required. *See, e.g., *Hopt*, 110 U.S. at 588*.

For these reasons, the Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
