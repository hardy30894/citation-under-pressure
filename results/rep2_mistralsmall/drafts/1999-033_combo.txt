**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE RECOGNIZES A "CLASS OF ONE" THEORY OF LIABILITY**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a state from denying any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886). This Court has long recognized that the Clause is not limited to invidious discrimination against discrete and insular minorities. *See Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 112 (1949) (Jackson, J., concurring) ("The equal protection clause is not a guarantee of equality in the abstract but a guarantee against inequality resulting from legislative action."). Rather, the Clause also reaches arbitrary and irrational state action that targets a single individual without rational justification. *See Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441, 445 (1923) ("The equal protection clause does not forbid classifications; it forbids unreasonable, arbitrary, and invidious discriminations.").

This principle undergirds the "class of one" theory of equal protection liability, which permits a plaintiff to challenge state action that, without any rational basis, treats the plaintiff differently from others similarly situated. *See Nordlinger v. Hahn*, 505 U.S. 1, 10 (1992) (citing *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562 (2000), for the proposition that "a class-of-one theory is cognizable under the Equal Protection Clause"). The theory is rooted in the fundamental requirement that the government must treat like cases alike. *See Gulf, C. & S.F. Ry. Co. v. Ellis*, 165 U.S. 150, 155 (1897) ("The inhibition of the Fourteenth Amendment against class legislation was directed not only against discrimination in favor of particular classes, but equally against hostile and oppressive legislation affecting individuals as such.").

II. **A PLAINTIFF NEED NOT ALLEGE MEMBERSHIP IN A PROTECTED CLASS TO STATE AN EQUAL PROTECTION CLAIM**

This Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that the Equal Protection Clause is limited to claims brought by members of suspect or protected classes. *See Rinaldi v. Yeager*, 384 U.S. 305, 308-09 (1966) ("The Fourteenth Amendment does not impose on the States any affirmative duty to provide appellate counsel for indigents. But when a State does afford such a right, it may not discriminate between classes of indigents in the provision of that right."); *McLaughlin v. Florida*, 379 U.S. 184, 191 (1964) ("The Fourteenth Amendment reaches not only arbitrary class-based discrimination but also irrational and arbitrary official action."). The Clause protects individuals from arbitrary and irrational state action, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a discrete and insular minority. *See Railway Express Agency*, 336 U.S. at 112 (Jackson, J., concurring).

The Court has also recognized that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits state action that is motivated by animus or ill will toward a particular individual. *See Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) ("The unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application, to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination."). This principle applies with equal force to a plaintiff who alleges that state action was taken for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate governmental objective. *See id.* at 8-9.

III. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY RECOGNIZED THAT A PLAINTIFF MAY STATE AN EQUAL PROTECTION CLAIM BY ALLEGING THAT STATE ACTION WAS MOTIVATED BY SPITE OR ILL WILL**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that state action was motivated by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective." *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 160 F.3d 367, 370 (7th Cir. 1998) (quoting *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373-74). This principle is consistent with this Court's recognition that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary and irrational state action, even when the plaintiff is the sole object of the state's animus. *See Sioux City Bridge Co.*, 260 U.S. at 445.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is also supported by this Court's longstanding recognition that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits state action that is motivated by animus or ill will. *See Snowden*, 321 U.S. at 8. The Court has repeatedly held that the Clause prohibits state action that is taken for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate governmental objective. *See Railway Express Agency*, 336 U.S. at 112 (Jackson, J., concurring). The Seventh Circuit's decision is therefore consistent with this Court's precedents and should be affirmed.
