**ARGUMENT**

The Massachusetts buffer zone statute is a valid time, place, and manner regulation that satisfies the First Amendment as applied through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve significant governmental interests, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication.

**I. The Statute Is Content-Neutral and Viewpoint-Neutral**

A regulation is content-neutral if it is justified without reference to the content of regulated speech. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 558 (1965). The Massachusetts Act applies to all persons equally, regardless of message. It prohibits *anyone* from knowingly entering a 35-foot buffer zone, exempting only those entering or leaving the facility, employees, emergency personnel, and persons passing through to other destinations. Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E 1/2(b), (c). The law does not single out pro-life or pro-choice speech; it regulates conduct—physical presence—not expression. *See Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 88-89 (1949) (upholding content-neutral noise ordinance). Because the statute’s purpose is to ensure safe access to healthcare facilities, not to suppress any viewpoint, it is content-neutral. *See Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 162 (1939) (upholding regulation aimed at safety, not speech).

**II. The Statute Serves Significant Governmental Interests**

The Commonwealth has compelling interests in protecting public safety, ensuring unimpeded access to healthcare, and preserving public order. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (upholding parade license requirement to promote safety and order). The record demonstrates that prior to the 2007 Act, patients and staff faced harassment, obstruction, and physical intimidation outside clinics. The buffer zone is a reasonable response to these documented harms. *See Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 306-07 (1940) (recognizing that states may regulate conduct that threatens public safety, even when intertwined with speech). The government’s interest in protecting citizens’ access to lawful medical services is beyond dispute. *See Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88, 105 (1940) (upholding state power to regulate conduct that obstructs lawful business operations).

**III. The Statute Is Narrowly Tailored**

A time, place, and manner regulation need not be the least restrictive means; it must merely be narrowly tailored to serve the government’s legitimate interests. *Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 88-89. The 35-foot zone is far smaller than the 100-foot zone upheld in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), and it applies only to entrances, exits, and driveways—not entire sidewalks. The Act exempts persons merely passing through, ensuring that the regulation does not burden more speech than necessary. *See Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 163-64 (upholding anti-littering ordinance that left alternative means of communication). Petitioners remain free to speak, pray, display signs, and distribute literature from outside the buffer zone. The law does not ban speech; it merely relocates it by 35 feet. *See Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. at 559-60 (upholding law that moved demonstrators away from courthouse entrances).

**IV. The Statute Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels**

The First Amendment does not guarantee speakers the most effective or preferred forum. *Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 88-89. Petitioners may still engage in sidewalk counseling, display signs, pray aloud, and distribute literature from the buffer zone’s perimeter. They may also use amplified sound, as the record shows. The Act does not prevent petitioners from communicating with patients; it merely prevents them from standing directly at clinic entrances. *See Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 163 (upholding ordinance that restricted leafleting but left other means of communication). Because petitioners retain numerous avenues to convey their message, the regulation satisfies the First Amendment. *See Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 304 (holding that the First Amendment protects speech, not a right to reach every listener in every place).

**V. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls and Should Not Be Overruled**

*Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), upheld a nearly identical Colorado statute creating a 100-foot buffer zone around healthcare facilities. The Court held that the law was content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and left open ample alternative channels. *Id.* at 725-30. The Massachusetts Act is even more restrained, applying only to a 35-foot zone. *Hill* is directly on point and should govern this case. Petitioners’ request to overrule *Hill* is meritless. *Hill* was correctly decided and has not been undermined by subsequent precedent. The doctrine of *stare decisis* demands respect for settled law, particularly where, as here, the Commonwealth has relied on *Hill* in enacting its statute. *See* *Helvering v. Hallock*, 309 U.S. 106, 119 (1940) (emphasizing that *stare decisis* applies with special force to statutory and constitutional rulings that have shaped legislative and executive action).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
