The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act provides that state law applies on the outer Continental Shelf only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This Court has long recognized that the statute assimilates state law solely to supply rules where federal law is silent. Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (“the purpose of the [OCSLA] was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf”).

The Fair Labor Standards Act supplies a complete scheme governing wages and hours for employees engaged in commerce. Overnight Motor Transp. Co. v. Missel, 316 U.S. 572, 577 (1942) (Act “restricted only by the requirement that the wages paid should comply with the minimum wage schedule … with overtime pay at time and a half that minimum”); Warren-Bradshaw Drilling Co. v. Hall, 317 U.S. 88, 90 (1942) (coverage turns on whether employees are “engaged in the production of goods for commerce”); Walling v. Helmerich & Payne, Inc., 323 U.S. 37, 40 (1944) (split-day contracts cannot evade the statutory overtime requirement); Walling v. A. H. Belo Corp., 316 U.S. 624, 631 (1942) (guaranteed weekly wage must still satisfy the Act’s overtime formula); Walling v. Halliburton Oil Well Cementing Co., 331 U.S. 17, 21 (1947) (same contractual device examined under the Act’s “usual workweek” standard); Walling v. Youngerman-Reynolds Hardwood Co., 325 U.S. 419, 423 (1945) (piece-rate arrangements must yield the statutory minimum and overtime); 149 Madison Ave. Corp. v. Asselta, 331 U.S. 199, 204 (1947) (formulaic hourly rates remain subject to the Act’s actual-hours calculation); Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co., 375 U.S. 34, 35 (1963) (Longshoremen’s Act, as extended by OCSLA, is “explicitly inapplicable to a ‘member of a crew of any vessel’”).

Because the FLSA already prescribes minimum wages, overtime, and remedies for the precise conduct alleged, California law is neither needed nor consistent with the federal regime. The district court therefore correctly held that no gap exists for state law to fill.
