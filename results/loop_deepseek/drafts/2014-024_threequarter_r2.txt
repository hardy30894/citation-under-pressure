**ARGUMENT**

The judgment below must be reversed. Section 875(c) does not criminalize communications that a reasonable speaker would foresee as threatening; it criminalizes “any threat.” A threat is a communication made with the subjective intent to intimidate or to communicate a serious intent to harm. The district court’s objective standard stripped the statute of that intent requirement and, in doing so, allowed Elonis to be convicted for expressive rap-style postings he did not intend as threats.

**I. Section 875(c) Incorporates a Subjective Intent Requirement**

This Court has long presumed that criminal statutes require a culpable mental state. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (recognizing that the requirement of intent is deeply rooted in the criminal law). That presumption is especially strong for statutes regulating speech and carrying significant penalties. *See id.* at 255. Public-welfare offenses are a narrow exception, not a model for §875(c). *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 252 (1922). By using the word “threat,” Congress required the speaker to intend his words to be understood as a threat. A contrary construction would criminalize accidental, jocular, or artistic statements based entirely on how a hypothetical listener reacts.

**II. The First Amendment Requires Subjective Intent for True Threats**

The First Amendment shields speech unless it falls within a narrowly defined exception. The true-threat exception covers only statements the speaker intends as threats. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), reversed a conviction under a similar threat statute because the statement, viewed in context, was not a “true threat.”

This Court’s incitement jurisprudence is to the same effect. *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), held that speech may be punished only when it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” The intent requirement reflects a fundamental principle: speech cannot be punished merely because it provokes or unsettles. *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (“Speech is often provocative and challenging. It may strike at prejudices and preconceptions and have profound unsettling effects as it presses for acceptance of an idea.”). Similarly, *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940), reversed a breach-of-peace conviction where the defendant’s speech was not directed to inciting violence. An objective standard—asking what a reasonable speaker would foresee—punishes the provocative and the unclear just as readily as the intentional threat.

Context and intent have always been central to the speech inquiry. *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), asked whether words are used “in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger.” The speaker’s purpose is part of those circumstances. Removing subjective intent from the threat inquiry discards the very safeguard that distinguishes protected expression from punishable intimidation.

**III. The Objective Standard Is Void for Vagueness**

A statute that criminalizes speech without requiring subjective intent leaves the speaker with no ascertainable standard of conduct. *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921), struck down a statute that left it to courts and juries to determine the prohibited act. A statute “which either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application” violates due process. *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926).

Under the objective test, a speaker cannot know whether his words are criminal until a jury decides how a reasonable person would interpret them. That unpredictability is especially unconstitutional in the realm of protected expression. *L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. at 89. The Constitution requires more before a person may be sent to prison for words alone.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury be required to find that Elonis subjectively intended his communications to be threats.
