ARGUMENT

I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews the district court's legal conclusion that the pat-frisk of Soares satisfied the Fourth Amendment de novo, while reviewing the underlying factual findings for clear error. Ornelas v. United States, 517 U.S. 690, 699 (1996) (determinations of reasonable suspicion "should be reviewed de novo"); United States v. Chhien, 266 F.3d 1, 5-6 (1st Cir. 2001). Because the ultimate question—whether the officers possessed reasonable, particularized suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous—is a mixed question of law and fact, plenary review applies to the application of the Terry standard to the facts found below.

II. The Pat-Frisk of Soares Violated the Fourth Amendment Because the Officers Lacked Reasonable Suspicion That He Was Armed and Dangerous

A. A frisk requires an independent, particularized showing of danger.

An investigatory stop and a frisk are analytically distinct events, each requiring its own justification. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). While an officer may briefly detain a person on reasonable suspicion of criminal activity, "the officer must be able to point to specific and articulable facts" and the additional frisk is justified only "where he has reason to believe that he is dealing with an armed and dangerous individual." Id. at 21, 27. The Supreme Court has been emphatic that the two inquiries do not collapse into one: "The Terry case created an exception to the requirement of probable cause ... [but] [n]othing is more clear than that the Fourth Amendment was meant to prevent wholesale intrusions upon the personal security of our citizenry." Sibron v. New York, 392 U.S. 40, 61-62 (1968). In Sibron, the Court held that an officer's authority to approach and question a suspect "affords no reason whatever to suppose that [the officer] was armed and dangerous," and thus could not, standing alone, support a frisk. Id. at 64.

This Court and the Supreme Court have likewise recognized that lawful authority to order occupants from a vehicle during a traffic stop, see Pennsylvania v. Mimms, 434 U.S. 106, 111 (1977), and to do the same to passengers, see Maryland v. Wilson, 519 U.S. 408, 414-15 (1997), does not itself authorize a frisk. Mimms upheld only the order to exit the vehicle as a de minimis intrusion justified by officer safety concerns generic to every stop; the separate frisk in that case was upheld only because the officer observed "a large bulge under [the driver's] jacket." 434 U.S. at 112. No comparable observation exists here.

B. The facts known to the officers before the frisk did not amount to reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed.

Reasonable suspicion demands specific, articulable facts particular to the individual searched—not a generalized unease. Florida v. J.L., 529 U.S. 266, 271-72 (2000) (rejecting reliance on "an accurate description of a subject's readily observable location and appearance" untethered to any indication of a weapon). Here, the government's justification for frisking Soares rests on three categories of fact: (1) pre-stop "furtive movements" by the vehicle's occupants; (2) the late hour and high-crime location; and (3) Soares's agitation, profanity, and hand movements after the stop began. None, alone or together, supplies the individualized suspicion of danger that Terry and Sibron require.

First, the "furtive movements" the officers observed before the stop—Soares motioning toward the console, Lee toward his door—are exactly the kind of ambiguous, innocent gestures this Court has cautioned against treating as automatically suspicious. Movement toward a console is at least as consistent with retrieving a registration or simply adjusting one's seat as it is with concealing a weapon. Illinois v. Wardlow, 528 U.S. 119, 125 (2000), permits flight in a high-crime area to contribute to reasonable suspicion of criminal activity, but even Wardlow requires conduct distinctively suggestive of wrongdoing; it does not sanction treating any movement inside a stopped car, in any neighborhood, as evidence of a weapon. See Sibron, 392 U.S. at 64 (an officer's generalized hunch is not enough).

Second, the high-crime, late-night setting adds context but cannot substitute for individualized suspicion directed at Soares himself. Wardlow, 528 U.S. at 124. Boston police testimony established only that the neighborhood had a reputation for crime—not that Soares had done anything, at that point, to suggest he personally was armed.

Third, and most critically, Soares's agitation, cursing, and rocking after the stop began reflect emotional distress and protest at the encounter, not indicators of a concealed weapon. Nervousness and hostility toward police are common and constitutionally insufficient bases for a frisk when unaccompanied by any observation of a weapon, bulge, or reaching motion toward the body. Officer Reid himself testified only that he felt "uneasy" about Soares's "behaving"—a subjective discomfort, not an articulable fact pointing to a firearm. That subjective unease is precisely the kind of "inarticulate hunch" Terry forbids as a basis for the intrusive frisk that followed. 392 U.S. at 22.

Notably, Soares's act of swatting away Officer Lapolis's hand occurred only after the frisk was already initiated and cannot retroactively justify the decision to frisk him in the first place; the constitutional inquiry is confined to what the officers knew before they laid hands on him. See Sibron, 392 U.S. at 62-63 (evaluating justification for a search based on facts available at its inception).

Because the officers lacked reasonable, articulable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous before subjecting him to a pat-frisk, the frisk violated the Fourth Amendment, and the handgun recovered as its direct product must be suppressed.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court denying the motion to suppress should be reversed.
