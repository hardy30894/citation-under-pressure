**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends workers’ compensation coverage only to employees who sustain injuries on the Outer Continental Shelf itself. The plain text, structure, and purpose of the statute compel this conclusion, and the contrary holding of the court below conflicts with the only Supreme Court guidance on point and with the considered judgment of the Fifth Circuit.

**I. The Text of OCSLA Requires a Situs-of-Injury**

Section 1333(b) of OCSLA provides that the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act “shall apply with respect to disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” unambiguously modifies the entire clause, requiring that the injury itself bear a geographic connection to the Shelf. The statute does not say “any injury resulting from operations,” which would focus solely on causation; it says “any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The phrase “occurring as the result of” incorporates both a causal and a locational element. *See Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356, 361 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc) (holding that OCSLA “requires that the injury occur on the Outer Continental Shelf”).

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test reads the locational requirement out of the statute. Under that test, an employee injured anywhere—even thousands of miles inland—could recover so long as his work bore some relationship to offshore operations. That interpretation cannot be squared with Congress’s deliberate choice to limit coverage to injuries “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” *See Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805, 810 (3d Cir. 1988) (adopting a “but for” test but acknowledging that the statute’s language “could be read to require that the injury occur on the Shelf”).

**II. Supreme Court Precedent Supports a Situs-of-Injury Requirement**

In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Supreme Court considered whether OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision applied to a helicopter crash on the waters above the Shelf. The Court held that “the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Id.* at 219. In a footnote, the Court expressly distinguished § 1333(b), noting that it “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement.” *Id.* at 219 n.2. The Court explained that § 1333(b) “makes compensation for the death or injury of an ‘employee’ resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act.” *Id.* The Court’s use of the phrase “situs requirement” confirms that § 1333(b) incorporates a geographic limitation.

The Ninth Circuit dismissed this footnote as “unconsidered dictum.” *Valladolid v. Pacific Operations Offshore, LLP*, 604 F.3d 1126, 1135 (9th Cir. 2010). But the Supreme Court’s considered dictum is entitled to “great weight” and should not be “blandly shrugged off.” *United States v. Montero-Camargo*, 208 F.3d 1122, 1132 n.17 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc). The Court’s statement was not an aside; it was a deliberate explanation of why § 1333(b) did not affect the case before it. The Court recognized that § 1333(b) contains both a status requirement (employee status) and a situs requirement (operations on the Shelf). *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219 n.2.

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s Situs-of-Injury Test Is Correct**

The Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, has held that OCSLA requires the injury to occur on the Shelf. *Mills*, 877 F.2d at 361-62. The court reasoned that “the plain language of § 1333(b) requires that the injury occur on the Outer Continental Shelf” because the statute “does not extend coverage to all injuries that are causally related to Shelf operations.” *Id.* at 361. The Fifth Circuit has consistently applied this rule. *See Becker v. Tidewater, Inc.*, 586 F.3d 358, 366-67 (5th Cir. 2009); *Pickett v. Petroleum Helicopters, Inc.*, 266 F.3d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 2001); *Sisson v. Davis & Sons, Inc.*, 131 F.3d 555, 558 (5th Cir. 1998).

The Third Circuit’s contrary decision in *Curtis* is unpersuasive. The *Curtis* court acknowledged that the statute’s language “could be read to require that the injury occur on the Shelf,” but it chose to adopt a “but for” test based on its view of the statute’s purpose. *Curtis*, 849 F.2d at 810. That approach improperly substitutes judicial policy preferences for the text Congress enacted. *See Mills*, 877 F.2d at 361 (rejecting *Curtis* as “contrary to the plain language of the statute”).

**IV. The Purpose of OCSLA Confirms a Geographic Limitation**

OCSLA was enacted to extend federal law to the Outer Continental Shelf, an area beyond state jurisdiction. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1332(1) (declaring it “the policy of the United States” to assert jurisdiction over the Shelf). The workers’ compensation provision was designed to fill a gap: employees working on the Shelf had no state workers’ compensation coverage because the Shelf is outside state territory. *See Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985) (noting that OCSLA “extend[s] the LHWCA to cover workers on the Outer Continental Shelf”). There is no similar gap for onshore injuries, which are covered by state workers’ compensation laws. Indeed, Valladolid’s widow received California workers’ compensation benefits. The Ninth Circuit’s test would create duplicative federal coverage for onshore injuries, contrary to Congress’s intent to provide a federal remedy only where state law is unavailable.

**V. The Ninth Circuit’s “Substantial Nexus” Test Is Unworkable**

The court below held that an injury is covered if there is “a substantial nexus between the injury and extractive operations on the shelf.” *Valladolid*, 604 F.3d at 1137. This standard is vague and invites litigation over every onshore injury that has any connection to offshore work. The Fifth Circuit’s bright-line situs rule, by contrast, provides clear guidance and avoids case-by-case adjudication of the “nexus” between an injury and offshore operations. *See Mills*, 877 F.2d at 362 (adopting a situs requirement to “avoid the very problems of line-drawing that the ‘but for’ test would create”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and hold that OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to workers injured on the Outer Continental Shelf itself.
