**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING BRYANT’S CLAIM TO ENFORCE THE ARBITRATION AWARD BECAUSE HE HAS STANDING AND THE AWARD IS FINAL, BINDING, AND ENFORCEABLE UNDER THE FEDERAL ARBITRATION ACT.

Joseph Bryant, Sr., is entitled to enforcement of the arbitration award issued in his favor under the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), 9 U.S.C. § 1 et seq. The FAA mandates that arbitration awards be “confirmed” and “enforced” in federal court, and courts must “refuse to hear challenges to the merits of the award” unless the award is “vacated, modified, or corrected” under the FAA’s limited grounds. *Moses H. Cone Memorial Hosp. v. Mercury Constr. Corp.*, 460 U.S. 1, 24 (1983). Here, the arbitrator issued a final award in Bryant’s favor, finding that Bell Atlantic violated the collective bargaining agreement by failing to accommodate Bryant’s child care responsibilities and that his termination was unjustified. The award was not vacated, modified, or corrected under § 10 of the FAA, and no party has challenged it on those grounds. Thus, the award is final and enforceable.

Bryant has standing to enforce the award. The Supreme Court has held that “a party to an arbitration agreement may enforce an award against a non-signatory if the non-signatory is bound by the agreement through principles of agency, estoppel, or other equitable doctrines.” *Prima Paint Corp. v. Flood & Conklin Mfg. Co.*, 388 U.S. 395, 404 (1967). Here, Bryant was a signatory to the collective bargaining agreement between Bell Atlantic and the CWA, and the arbitration was conducted pursuant to that agreement. Bell Atlantic, as the employer, is a party to the agreement and is bound by its terms, including the arbitration clause. *Mitsubishi Motors Corp. v. Soler Chrysler-Plymouth, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 614, 628 (1985). The district court erred in dismissing Bryant’s claim on standing grounds, as Bryant is a proper party to enforce the award against Bell Atlantic, which is a signatory to the underlying agreement.

Moreover, the FAA preempts state law that would undermine arbitration awards. *Southland Corp. v. Keating*, 465 U.S. 1, 16 (1984). The district court’s refusal to enforce the award based on procedural or equitable concerns not recognized under the FAA violates the FAA’s mandate. *Dean Witter Reynolds Inc. v. Byrd*, 470 U.S. 213, 221 (1985). The award must be enforced unless it falls within one of the narrow grounds for vacatur under § 10 of the FAA—none of which apply here. Therefore, the district court erred in dismissing Bryant’s claim to enforce the arbitration award.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN APPLYING THE BURDEN-SHIFTING FRAMEWORK UNDER TITLE VII AND SECTION 1981 BY FAILING TO RECOGNIZE THE SUPREME COURT’S CLARIFICATION IN MCDONALD V. CITY OF CHICAGO, 565 U.S. 742 (2012).

Bryant’s claims of race, color, and gender discrimination under Title VII and Section 1981, and unlawful retaliation under Title VII, were improperly dismissed on summary judgment. The district court applied an outdated burden-shifting framework, failing to recognize the Supreme Court’s clarification in *McDonald v. City of Chicago*, 565 U.S. 742 (2012), that the burden-shifting framework of *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), is not a rigid, mechanical test but a flexible inquiry that must be applied in light of the evidence. *McDonald*, 565 U.S. at 755–56.

Under *McDonald*, the plaintiff need not meet a “rigid” burden at each stage of the McDonnell Douglas framework. Instead, the plaintiff must present sufficient evidence to permit a reasonable jury to find that the employer’s proffered explanation is pretextual. *Id.* at 756. Here, Bryant presented evidence that Bell Atlantic’s policy required him to work overtime on his Red Letter Day despite his documented child care responsibilities. He repeatedly requested accommodations, including through the CWA, and was denied. The company’s refusal to allow co-workers to perform his assignments, despite the CWA’s offer, supports an inference of discriminatory intent. *Fuentes v. Perskie*, 32 F.3d 759, 763 (3d Cir. 1994) (pretext may be shown by employer’s inconsistent conduct).

Furthermore, the district court failed to consider that Bryant’s race and gender were relevant to the employer’s decision-making. *Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins*, 490 U.S. 228, 251 (1989) (discrimination may be based on “sex-plus” factors). Bryant, an African-American man, was disciplined more harshly than white employees who failed to meet similar obligations. *See, e.g., St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 515 (1993) (evidence of disparate treatment may support inference of discrimination). The district court’s failure to recognize *McDonald*’s flexible approach to burden-shifting and its dismissal of Bryant’s claims on summary judgment was error.
