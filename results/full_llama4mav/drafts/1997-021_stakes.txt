The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). The NLRB has primary responsibility for developing and applying national labor policy, and its expertise in this area is entitled to deference. NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990).

The NLRB's standard for polling is consistent with the NLRA's goal of promoting stable collective-bargaining relationships. Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1062 (1989). By requiring an employer to have objective indications raising a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status before conducting a poll, the NLRB balances the employer's interest in testing employee support for the union against the potential disruption to the collective-bargaining relationship. Id. at 1061-62.

Although some courts of appeals have rejected the NLRB's standard, arguing that it renders employer polls useless, the NLRB's reasoning in Texas Petrochemicals is persuasive. The NLRB noted that an employer with a good-faith reasonable doubt about a union's majority status may still wish to poll its employees to obtain more certain information about the union's support before withdrawing recognition. Id. at 1063. This reasoning is consistent with the NLRA's emphasis on promoting stable collective-bargaining relationships.

The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is supported by substantial evidence in the record. The NLRB's finding that only 7 of the 32 employees in the bargaining unit made statements repudiating the union is a reasonable assessment of the evidence. Allentown Mack's challenge to this conclusion is unpersuasive, as the NLRB had good reason to discount the statements of certain employees, such as those made during job interviews after being told that the new company would be non-union. Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc., 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978).

The NLRB's decision to uphold the Administrative Law Judge's (ALJ) findings regarding the statements of employees Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh is supported by precedent. The ALJ reasonably found Ridgick's statement during his job interview to be "at best equivocal," and Marsh's expression of dissatisfaction with the union did not necessarily mean he wanted the union to stop representing him and the other employees. Destileria Serralles, Inc., 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988).

The NLRB's treatment of the statements of employees Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr is also consistent with its precedent. The NLRB has consistently questioned the reliability of reports by one employee of the antipathy of other employees toward their union. Westbrook Bowl, 293 N.L.R.B. 1000 (1989).

In conclusion, the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is rational and consistent with the NLRA. The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is supported by substantial evidence in the record. Therefore, the Court should uphold the NLRB's decision and grant its cross-application for enforcement of its cease and desist order and bargaining order.
