# ARGUMENT

## I. The Statutory Scheme Requires a Single, Coherent Forum for Review of Mixed Cases, Regardless of the Label the Board Attaches to Its Disposition

Congress created a bifurcated review scheme for Merit Systems Protection Board decisions, sending "cases of discrimination" to district court while routing other Board decisions to the Federal Circuit. That structure only functions if courts look to the substance of what the Board actually decided, not to the procedural label it affixes to its order. This Court has long recognized that jurisdictional grants must be construed according to what was actually "the issue made and decided," rather than by surface characterization. Beadles v. Smyser, 209 U.S. 393, 394 (1908) (jurisdiction turns on "the issue made and decided," not on how a party frames the appeal). Where, as here, the Board's "jurisdictional" ruling and its assessment of Perry's discrimination and retaliation allegations rest on the identical factual predicate—whether Perry's retirement and suspension were the product of coercion tainted by discriminatory and retaliatory animus—there is no principled basis for treating the ruling as something other than a disposition of a discrimination claim.

This Court's decision in Local No. 207, International Ass'n of Bridge, Structural & Ornamental Iron Workers Union v. Perko, 373 U.S. 701 (1963), confirms that the proper forum for review should follow the substance of the controversy rather than a formal classification assigned at the threshold. There, the Court looked past the nominal characterization of the discharged employee's status to what was "cognizable" by the agency in substance, holding that the underlying reality of the dispute—not a formal label—determined which body retained authority over it. Id. at 701–02. The same logic controls here: Perry's case is, in substance, a discrimination case. The MSPB's voluntariness ruling was not a freestanding jurisdictional inquiry independent of the merits; it required the ALJ to weigh precisely the same allegations of discriminatory coercion that would define Perry's discrimination claim on the merits.

## II. Jurisdictional and Merits Determinations in Cases Like Perry's Are Factually Inseparable, and Review Should Not Be Split Between Two Courts

This Court has recognized that questions nominally labeled "jurisdictional" or "preliminary" are frequently inseparable from the ultimate merits, requiring a unified "factual evaluation of the entire situation." Elgin, Joliet & Eastern Railway Co. v. Burley, 325 U.S. 711, 723 (1945) (noting that determinations of authority to act are "questions also to be determined from a factual evaluation of the entire situation, essentially preliminary to determination of legal effects"). Just as the Court in Burley refused to resolve an agency's authority to act without confronting the factual merits bound up in that authority, the voluntariness of Perry's settlement cannot be untangled from his substantive claim that the settlement was coerced through discriminatory and retaliatory conduct. Splitting review of these overlapping questions between the Federal Circuit and the district courts—one court passing on "jurisdiction," the other on "discrimination"—produces exactly the fragmentation this Court's precedents caution against.

Nor is there any sound reason to create an artificial procedural boundary between dismissals resting on jurisdictional grounds and those resting on other threshold grounds. In Glaspell v. Northern Pacific Railroad, 144 U.S. 211 (1892), this Court held that a motion could not be treated as having carried over from one tribunal to another where doing so would rest on a technical distinction untethered from the practical realities of the proceeding. Id. at 224–25. So too here: there is no practical distinction between a Board ruling that dismisses a discrimination claim as "untimely" and one that dismisses the same claim as "involuntary" and hence outside its jurisdiction. Both dispose of the discrimination claim without ever reaching its merits, and both should be reviewed in the same forum.

## III. A Single Forum for Discrimination-Related Claims Best Serves the Statute's Remedial Purpose and Avoids Confusion for Litigants

The practical costs of a bifurcated scheme are severe, particularly for pro se litigants like Perry navigating a technical statutory appeals structure. See Levers v. Anderson, 326 U.S. 219, 220–22 (1945) (describing rigid statutory appeal procedures and time limits governing agency review). Congress structured the Civil Service Reform Act's appellate provisions to allow employees a clear and predictable route to court; forcing an employee to guess, in advance, which forum will ultimately prove correct based on how the Board later characterizes its own ruling undermines that predictability. Cf. Douglas v. Green, 363 U.S. 192, 193 (1960) (recognizing that fairness in appellate procedure requires courts to ensure that litigants are not deprived of a meaningful and coherent avenue for review). Similarly, this Court has recognized the value of maintaining consistent avenues of relief for employees challenging adverse actions tainted by allegations of unconstitutional or discriminatory motive, rather than permitting the forum to hinge on incidental procedural rulings below. See Lerner v. Casey, 357 U.S. 468, 476–77 (1958) (examining employment dismissal through the lens of the actual basis for discharge, not the formal characterization given by the reviewing authority).

Finally, as this Court has emphasized in analogous jurisdictional contexts, a reviewing court's jurisdiction must be assessed according to "the final judgment" actually rendered and its true legal effect, not according to the procedural vehicle by which it arrived. NLRB v. MacKay Radio & Telegraph Co., 304 U.S. 333, 338 (1938). The Board's order here, whatever its label, finally disposed of Perry's discrimination and retaliation claims. Review therefore belongs in district court.
