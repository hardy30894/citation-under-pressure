## Argument

The district court erred in admitting the expert testimony of FBI Special Agent Charles Jones, which impermissibly invaded the province of the jury and prejudiced Stanley Hill's right to a fair trial.

Agent Jones's testimony was not limited to providing factual information or explaining the results of his investigation. Rather, he offered his opinion on the veracity of Stanley's statements, stating that many of Stanley's answers "did not make sense" and were "not worthy of credence." This type of testimony is impermissible under Federal Rule of Evidence 704, as it "tells the jury what result to reach." United States v. Richter, 826 F.3d 1320, 1328 (10th Cir. 2016) (quoting United States v. Dazey, 403 F.3d 1147, 1171 (10th Cir. 2005)). By testifying that Stanley's statements were deceptive, Agent Jones effectively told the jury that Stanley was guilty. See United States v. Toledo, 985 F.2d 1462, 1470 (10th Cir. 1993) ("An expert witness may not supplant the jury's fact-finding role.").

Moreover, Agent Jones's testimony was not based on his expertise in a particular field, but rather on his experience in interviewing suspects. He stated that "never in my career have I seen" an innocent person make a statement like Stanley's, and that "when people start bringing faith into validating their statements, they're deceptive." This type of testimony is not admissible under Federal Rule of Evidence 702, as it is not based on "sufficient facts or data" and is not the product of "reliable principles and methods." United States v. Nacchio, 555 F.3d 1234, 1241 (10th Cir. 2009) (en banc). The reliability of Agent Jones's methods is further undermined by the fact that his testimony was based on a subjective interpretation of Stanley's behavior, rather than on any objective evidence. See United States v. Benally, 541 F.3d 990, 994-95 (10th Cir. 2008).

The admission of Agent Jones's testimony was plain error, as it affected Stanley's substantial rights and seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The error was not harmless, as the government relied heavily on Agent Jones's testimony to establish Stanley's guilt. See United States v. Meienberg, 263 F.3d 1177, 1180 (10th Cir. 2001). The testimony was particularly prejudicial, as it was presented as expert opinion, which can be given significant weight by jurors. See United States v. Rodriguez-Felix, 450 F.3d 1117, 1126 (10th Cir. 2006).

The admission of Agent Jones's testimony also violated Stanley's right to a fair trial under the Fifth Amendment. See United States v. Scheffer, 523 U.S. 303, 313 (1998) ("A fundamental premise of our criminal trial system is that the jury is the lie detector.") (internal quotation marks omitted). By allowing Agent Jones to testify on the ultimate issue of Stanley's guilt, the district court undermined the jury's role as the sole arbiter of credibility. See United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 18 (1985) ("The jury is the sole arbiter of the credibility of witnesses.").

In light of the foregoing, this Court should reverse Stanley Hill's conviction and remand for a new trial, free from the taint of Agent Jones's improper testimony. United States v. Gabaldon, 389 F.3d 1090, 1098 (10th Cir. 2004).
