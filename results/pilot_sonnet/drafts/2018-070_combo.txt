ARGUMENT

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) declares that "the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State ... are declared to be the law of the United States" for structures on the Shelf only "[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That provision does not create a freestanding state-law cause of action wherever state law "pertains" to the same subject as federal law; it borrows state law as surrogate federal law only where federal law does not already occupy the field. The text, history, and the analogous body of federal-enclave doctrine that Congress imported into OCSLA confirm that a gap in federal coverage is a precondition to the statute's borrowing mechanism.

I. OCSLA's Text and History Confirm that State Law Applies Only as a Gap-Filler

Congress modeled OCSLA's choice-of-law provision on the law governing federal enclaves, under which state law is assimilated into federal law only where federal law is silent. See Fort Leavenworth R.R. Co. v. Lowe, 114 U.S. 525 (1885) (state law existing at the time of cession continues in force within a federal enclave only until superseded by congressional legislation); Chicago, Rock Island & Pac. Ry. Co. v. McGlinn, 114 U.S. 542 (1885) (applying the same principle to civil law within a federal enclave). This Court has repeatedly recognized that Congress may assimilate state law into federal enclaves precisely because federal law otherwise leaves interstitial gaps that Congress has not chosen to fill through independent federal legislation. United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958) (upholding Congress's prospective adoption of state criminal law to supply gaps in federal criminal law governing enclaves); James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94 (1940) (state safety regulations continued to operate on a federal enclave because no federal law addressed the same subject). But where Congress has enacted a comprehensive federal scheme addressing the same subject matter, state law recedes; it is not additionally available merely because it touches the same general topic. See Pacific Coast Dairy, Inc. v. Department of Agriculture, 318 U.S. 285 (1943) (state price-control law inapplicable within a federal enclave once Congress had legislated on the subject); Johnson v. Yellow Cab Transit Co., 321 U.S. 383 (1944) (state liquor law could not reach transactions on a federal enclave in the face of comprehensive federal regulation).

OCSLA borrows this enclave framework by its own terms: § 1333(a)(1) treats the Shelf's fixed structures "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). Congress's choice of the enclave analogy was deliberate, and it carries with it the enclave rule that state law supplies federal law only interstitially, where Congress's own law leaves a "significant void or gap." Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969).

II. This Court's Decision in Rodrigue Confirms the Gap-Filling Function of § 1333(a)(2)(A)

In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), this Court held that Louisiana wrongful-death law governed deaths occurring on fixed drilling platforms because no federal remedy—maritime or otherwise—covered the loss. The Court explained that "the [federal] admiralty action under the [High] Seas Act no more applies to these accidents actually occurring on the islands than it would to accidents occurring in an upland federal enclave," id. at 366, and that because no federal remedy existed, adoption of state law "remove[d]" the "obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]." Id. Rodrigue thus confirms that state law becomes available under OCSLA precisely because, and only because, federal law left a void. The Court's analysis proceeded from the premise that OCSLA's purpose was "to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures ... on the outer Continental Shelf," id. at 355—a single, unified body of law, not a dual system permitting a state overlay whenever state law happens to address the same general subject as an existing federal statute.

III. The Gap Requirement Reflects the Correct Reading of § 1333(a)(2)(A)

Applying Rodrigue, the Fifth Circuit has required a showing that federal law fails to address the relevant subject before state law may be borrowed under OCSLA. Continental Oil, 417 F.2d at 1036 (state law applies only where there is "necessity to fill a significant void or gap"). That rule follows directly from the enclave analogy Congress selected and from Rodrigue's own reasoning. It also serves OCSLA's purpose of ensuring a single, coherent body of law offshore, rather than a patchwork in which any of several States' regimes could be grafted onto comprehensive federal statutes such as the Fair Labor Standards Act.

The FLSA is precisely such a comprehensive scheme: it regulates minimum wages, overtime, and recordkeeping, and it contains its own savings clause permitting more protective state law to operate concurrently within the States. Where Congress wished state law to supplement federal wage law, it said so expressly within the FLSA itself; it had no need of OCSLA to accomplish that purpose offshore. Because federal law already comprehensively addresses wage-and-hour standards for Shelf workers, no "significant void or gap" remains for California law to fill. Continental Oil, 417 F.2d at 1036. The decision below, permitting state law to apply merely because it is not "inconsistent" with, and touches the same subject matter as, federal law, cannot be reconciled with the enclave doctrine Congress imported into § 1333(a)(2)(A) or with this Court's decision in Rodrigue. This Court should adopt the gap-filling standard and reverse.
