**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period shifts the burden of persuasion to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between the constitutional right to due process and the government’s burden of proof in criminal prosecutions. The D.C. Circuit’s decision, which affirmed the conviction of Calvin Smith and John Raynor despite evidence that the alleged conspiracy had fractured before the limitations period began, contravenes settled principles of criminal law and undermines the bedrock of the prosecution’s burden. The government may not rely on speculative inferences or the mere existence of a prior conspiracy to sustain a conviction for conduct occurring after the conspiracy’s dissolution. The burden of proving continued membership in a conspiracy during the relevant time period remains with the government at all times, and it must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt. This Court should reverse.

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Proving Conspiracy Membership Beyond a Reasonable Doubt, Regardless of Prior Conduct**

The prosecution of a conspiracy is not a mere aggregation of individual acts; it is a distinct offense requiring proof of an agreement and an overt act in furtherance of that agreement. *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 440 (1965). The government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the time period charged. *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 510 (1995). This burden does not shift merely because the defendant was once a participant in a broader criminal scheme. *United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.2d 103, 107 (D.C. Cir. 1978). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the prosecution must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt,” including the defendant’s participation in the conspiracy at the time of the charged conduct. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970).

Here, the government’s case rested on the theory that the conspiracy between Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray continued into the limitations period. But the D.C. Circuit affirmed a conviction despite evidence that Moore and Gray had split up before the relevant time frame. *United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.2d at 107 (holding that “a defendant may withdraw from a conspiracy before the commission of the offense, and if he does so, he is not liable for subsequent acts of the conspiracy”). The government failed to prove that Smith and Raynor were members of the conspiracy during the period charged. The mere fact that they were once associated with the group does not satisfy the constitutional requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt. *United States v. Hines*, 588 F.3d 1057, 1062 (7th Cir. 2009) (“The government cannot rely on a defendant’s past association with a criminal enterprise to prove current participation.”).

**II. Withdrawal from a Conspiracy Terminates Liability for Subsequent Acts**

A defendant may withdraw from a conspiracy at any time, provided the withdrawal is voluntary and communicated to the co-conspirators. *United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.2d at 107. Once withdrawal is effective, the defendant is no longer liable for acts committed by the conspiracy after that point. *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. at 510. The government bears the burden of proving that the defendant did not withdraw or that the withdrawal was ineffective. *United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.2d at 107. In this case, the government offered no evidence that Smith or Raynor communicated their withdrawal or that they continued to participate in the conspiracy after the split between Moore and Gray. The D.C. Circuit’s failure to require the government to prove continued membership during the charged period effectively nullifies the withdrawal doctrine.

**III. The Government Cannot Shift the Burden of Proof to the Defendant**

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits shifting the burden of proof to the defendant. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. The government must prove every element of the offense, including the defendant’s membership in the conspiracy during the relevant time. *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. at 510. The D.C. Circuit’s decision, which allowed the jury to infer continued membership based on prior association, violates this principle. *United States v. Hines*, 588 F.3d at 1062. The Supreme Court has held that “the prosecution must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt,” and that “the burden of proof is not a mere procedural formality.” *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. The government cannot satisfy this burden by relying on the defendant’s past conduct or association.

**IV. The D.C. Circuit’s Decision Contravenes Precedent on Conspiracy Liability**

The D.C. Circuit’s decision is at odds with this Court’s precedent. In *United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.2d at 107, the court held that “a defendant may withdraw from a conspiracy before the commission of the offense, and if he does so, he is not liable for subsequent acts of the conspiracy.” The government offered no evidence that Smith or Raynor withdrew or that their withdrawal was ineffective. The D.C. Circuit’s failure to require proof of continued membership during the charged period renders the withdrawal doctrine meaningless. *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. at 510. The government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the time period charged. *United States v. Hines*, 588 F.3d at 1062.
