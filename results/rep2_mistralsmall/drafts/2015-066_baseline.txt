**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE SIXTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT APPLY TO TRIBAL COURT PROCEEDINGS, AND THEREFORE UNCOUNSELED TRIBAL CONVICTIONS DO NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT.**

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies only in federal and state criminal prosecutions. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 315 (1978) ("[T]he Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply to tribal courts."). Tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment because they are not federal or state courts. *Id.* at 323-24. Instead, tribal courts operate under the Indian Civil Rights Act ("ICRA"), which provides a right to counsel only when a defendant faces imprisonment. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)(6). Because the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, uncounseled tribal convictions do not violate the Sixth Amendment. *See Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 746-47 (1994) (holding that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply to uncounseled state convictions where no imprisonment was imposed).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is inapposite. *Ant* predates *Nichols* and incorrectly assumed that tribal courts must provide Sixth Amendment protections. *Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1394-95. This Court has since clarified that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts. *Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 315. The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Ant* is misplaced and undermines tribal sovereignty. *See Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (holding that the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes).

II. **EVEN IF THE SIXTH AMENDMENT APPLIED, UNCOUNSELED TRIBAL CONVICTIONS ARE VALID FOR USE AS PREDICATE OFFENSES UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).**

In *Nichols*, the Supreme Court held that uncounseled misdemeanor convictions may be used for sentencing enhancements, even where the prior conviction would have violated the Sixth Amendment if it had occurred in federal or state court. 511 U.S. at 746-47. The Court reasoned that sentencing courts may consider such convictions because the Sixth Amendment does not prohibit their use in sentencing. *Id.* at 748. The same logic applies here: uncounseled tribal convictions are valid for use as predicate offenses under § 117(a), which enhances punishment based on prior convictions.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision conflicts with *Nichols* by imposing a higher standard for tribal convictions than for state or federal convictions. This is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s holding that the Sixth Amendment does not bar the use of uncounseled convictions for sentencing purposes. *Id.* at 746-47. The Ninth Circuit’s rule also disregards the unique sovereignty of tribal courts. *See Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 323-24.

III. **THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S RULE UNDERMINES CONGRESS’S EFFORTS TO COMBAT DOMESTIC VIOLENCE IN INDIAN COUNTRY.**

Congress enacted § 117(a) to address the epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country. *See* Violence Against Women and Department of Justice Reauthorization Act of 2005, Pub. L. No. 109-162, tit. IX, § 909, 119 Stat. 2960, 3084. The Ninth Circuit’s decision frustrates this purpose by invalidating valid tribal convictions as predicate offenses. This creates a circuit split, as the Eighth and Tenth Circuits have held that uncounseled tribal convictions may be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a). *See United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011).

The Ninth Circuit’s rule also disregards tribal sovereignty. Tribal courts are sovereign entities with their own legal systems, and federal courts must respect their judgments. *See Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987) (extending comity to tribal courts). The Ninth Circuit’s decision imposes federal constitutional standards on tribal courts, which is inconsistent with tribal sovereignty. *See Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) (holding that federal courts should not intrude on tribal self-government).

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision is incorrect and conflicts with Supreme Court precedent. Uncounseled tribal convictions do not violate the Sixth Amendment because the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts. Even if the Sixth Amendment applied, *Nichols* permits the use of uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses. The Ninth Circuit’s rule undermines Congress’s efforts to combat domestic violence in Indian country and disregards tribal sovereignty. This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the circuit split and clarify the law.
