**ARGUMENT**

**I. Selam Did Not Waive His Confrontation Clause Claim By Omitting It From His Tribal Appellate Brief.**

The district court held that Selam forfeited review of his confrontation claim under 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6) because he did not present that claim to the Warm Springs Tribal Court of Appeals. That holding rests on too rigid a conception of waiver in the habeas context. In Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Supreme Court held that a habeas petitioner's failure to pursue a state appellate remedy bars federal review only where the petitioner has made a "deliberate bypass of the orderly procedure of the state courts." Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. at 438. Mere failure to raise a claim, without more, does not establish the considered, strategic abandonment that Fay requires.

Nothing in this record shows that Selam deliberately bypassed presentation of his confrontation claim. Selam was a layman advised only by a lay tribal spokesperson; his appeal was drafted without the benefit of trained counsel. Waiver of a constitutional right cannot be presumed from such circumstances. As this Court's forebear teaching in Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), instructs, "[a] waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. at 464, and courts must "indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver of fundamental constitutional rights." Id. at 464. Selam's inexperienced, unadvised omission of one legal theory from a multi-ground appeal is not the intentional relinquishment that Zerbst demands, nor the tactical bypass that Fay condemns. The district court therefore erred in refusing to reach the merits of Selam's confrontation claim.

**II. The Admission of the Five-Year-Old Victim's Out-of-Court Statements Through Four Surrogate Witnesses Violated Selam's Right of Confrontation.**

The ICRA guarantees an accused tribal defendant "the right ... to confront the witnesses against him." 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). That guarantee mirrors the protection the Supreme Court has recognized as essential to a fair trial. In Pointer v. Texas, 380 U.S. 400 (1965), the Court held that the right of confrontation is "a fundamental right" essential to a fair trial, Pointer v. Texas, 380 U.S. at 403-04, and it condemned exactly the practice used against Selam: the introduction of a witness's out-of-court statement without any opportunity to cross-examine the source. See also Douglas v. Alabama, 380 U.S. 415, 419 (1965) (confrontation violated where prosecution introduced a non-testifying declarant's statement implicating the defendant, denying "the benefit of full cross-examination").

The purpose of the confrontation guarantee has long been understood to prevent precisely what occurred in Selam's second trial. As the Supreme Court explained in Mattox v. United States, 156 U.S. 237 (1895), "the primary object" of the confrontation right "was to prevent depositions or ex parte affidavits ... being used against the prisoner in lieu of a personal examination and cross-examination of the witness." Mattox v. United States, 156 U.S. at 242. Here, the five-year-old victim never testified. Instead, her accusation reached the tribal court exclusively through the parents' account of what they were told, the physician's account of what he was told during examination, and the investigator's account of what she was told secondhand. Selam was denied any opportunity to test the reliability of the child's original accusation through cross-examination — the very evil Mattox and Pointer condemn.

**III. Selam Was Denied His Right of Compulsory Process.**

The right to compulsory process, secured to state defendants through the Fourteenth Amendment and equally fundamental to ICRA proceedings, guarantees a defendant meaningful ability to present witnesses in his defense. In Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), the Supreme Court held that the right to compel the attendance of witnesses is "in plain terms the right to present a defense, the right to present the defendant's version of the facts ... so [the tribunal] may decide where the truth lies." Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. at 19. The right is not satisfied by a passive recitation of its existence; it requires that the accused, or someone acting effectively on his behalf, be afforded a genuine opportunity to secure favorable testimony. See In re Oliver, 333 U.S. 257, 273 (1948) (due process requires "a fair opportunity to defend against the State's accusations").

Selam's appointed representative was a lay spokesperson without legal training, tasked with locating five potential witnesses largely on her own initiative, in a system that offered Selam no meaningful mechanism to compel their attendance once located. The mere advisement that Selam theoretically possessed a right to subpoena witnesses, without any functioning process to exercise that right through effective assistance, does not satisfy the guarantee recognized in Washington v. Texas. A right to compulsory process that exists only on paper, unaccompanied by any practical means of enforcement, is no right at all.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the district court and remand with instructions to grant the writ of habeas corpus.
