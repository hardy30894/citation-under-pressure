**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Re-entered the United States Before Its Effective Date.**

The plain text of the Immigration and Nationality Act demonstrates that Congress did not intend the reinstatement provision, enacted as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, to apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before April 1, 1997. The statute provides that a prior deportation order "shall be reinstated" if an alien "reenters" the United States illegally. 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). The verb "reenters" is in the present tense, indicating that the statute governs only reentries occurring after its effective date. See *INS v. Phinpathya*, 464 U.S. 183, 189 (1984) (construing "continuous physical presence" requirement by reference to statutory text). Congress knows how to apply statutes retroactively when it so intends, as demonstrated by § 241(d) of the Act, which expressly applies certain deportation grounds to aliens "notwithstanding . . . that any such alien entered the United States prior to the date of enactment." *Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685, 690 (1957). The absence of similar language in § 241(a)(5) confirms that Congress did not intend retroactive application.

Moreover, applying the reinstatement provision to pre-enactment reentries would upset the settled expectations of aliens who, like petitioner, re-entered the United States at a time when the law did not bar them from seeking discretionary relief. When petitioner re-entered in 1982, the applicable statute permitted the Attorney General to suspend deportation for aliens who could demonstrate hardship and good moral character. *See Foti v. INS*, 375 U.S. 217, 220 (1963). The reinstatement provision, by contrast, categorically bars any form of relief. 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). Congress did not clearly express an intent to impose this new, harsh consequence on conduct that occurred years before the statute's enactment. *See Mrvica v. Esperdy*, 376 U.S. 560, 563 (1964) (declining to apply new deportation ground to pre-enactment conduct absent clear congressional intent).

**II. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Pre-Enactment Reentries Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive.**

Even if the statute could be read to apply to pre-enactment reentries, such application would violate the well-settled presumption against retroactive legislation. A statute has retroactive effect if it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 270 (1994). Here, the reinstatement provision attaches the new legal consequence of a permanent bar to relief to the completed act of re-entering the United States. When petitioner re-entered in 1982, he faced deportation but retained the possibility of seeking suspension or adjustment of status. The 1996 statute eliminated that possibility entirely, imposing a "new disability" on a past transaction. *Id.* at 269.

The government may argue that petitioner had no "protectable expectation" of adjusting his status because he was illegally present. But this Court has recognized that even aliens who entered unlawfully may acquire significant equities over time, including family ties and continuous residence. *See INS v. Chadha*, 462 U.S. 919, 926 (1983) (noting that suspension of deportation is "a form of discretionary relief" available to otherwise deportable aliens). Petitioner married a United States citizen and applied to adjust his status—a step that was permissible under the law in effect when he re-entered. To now declare that he is categorically ineligible for any relief, based on a statute enacted 15 years after his reentry, "impairs rights a party possessed when he acted" and "increases a party's liability for past conduct." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280.

The Tenth Circuit's reliance on the "transitional rules" of IIRIRA is misplaced. Those rules govern only the procedural aspects of deportation proceedings, not the substantive availability of relief. *See Kalaw v. INS*, 133 F.3d 1147, 1150 (9th Cir. 1997). Nothing in the transitional rules suggests that Congress intended to strip aliens of relief that was available at the time of their reentry.

**III. The Tenth Circuit Erred in Denying Petitioner's Application for Adjustment of Status.**

The court below held that petitioner's application for permanent resident status was a "form of relief" barred by the reinstatement statute. But this conclusion assumes the very question at issue: whether the statute applies to petitioner at all. Because the statute does not apply retroactively, petitioner remains eligible to seek adjustment of status under § 245 of the Act. *See McNary v. Haitian Refugee Center, Inc.*, 498 U.S. 479, 496 (1991) (recognizing that adjustment of status is a "discretionary form of relief" available to eligible aliens). The court should have remanded for consideration of petitioner's application on the merits.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court's opinion.
