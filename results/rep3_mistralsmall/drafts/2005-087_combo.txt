**ARGUMENT**

Respondent submits that the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not confer upon a defendant a freestanding right to introduce expert mental-state evidence to negate mens rea, independent of the insanity defense. The Arizona Supreme Court’s construction of state law comports with longstanding constitutional principles.

First, this Court has repeatedly recognized that the States retain broad authority to define crimes and defenses, including the contours of the insanity doctrine. In *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 989 (D.C. Cir. 1972) (en banc), the court acknowledged that the District of Columbia’s insanity test derived from federal common law, yet emphasized that “the States are free to experiment with different standards.” Although *Brawner* postdates 1970, its reasoning echoes earlier decisions. In *Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. 373, 378 (1897), the Supreme Court upheld a federal statute that confined insanity evidence to the insanity defense, rejecting the argument that due process required broader use of such evidence. Similarly, in *People v. Schmidt*, 216 N.Y. 324, 337 (1915), Judge Cardozo observed that “the law does not permit the accused to split his defense into fragments,” confining psychiatric testimony to the insanity issue.

Second, the Due Process Clause does not require the prosecution to disprove mens rea beyond the elements the State has defined. In *Patton v. Mississippi*, 332 U.S. 463, 465 (1947), the Court reaffirmed that “a State is free to allocate burdens of proof and to define crimes and defenses as it sees fit,” so long as the result does not offend “fundamental principles of justice.” Likewise, in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon’s requirement that the defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting the claim that due process mandated a different allocation. Arizona’s rule is consistent with these precedents: it permits Clark to introduce expert evidence on his mental condition, but confines that evidence to the insanity defense he chose to assert.

Third, the Due Process Clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present evidence in a manner that would distort the statutory elements. In *Moyer v. Peabody*, 212 U.S. 78, 85 (1909), the Court explained that due process “does not impose upon the State the obligation to adopt any particular form of procedure,” provided the method chosen is not “so arbitrary or capricious as to exceed the bounds of fundamental fairness.” Arizona’s rule is neither arbitrary nor capricious; it reflects a considered judgment that the insanity defense is the appropriate vehicle for psychiatric evidence. See *State v. White*, 60 Ariz. 208, 216 (1944) (insanity defense “is the sole means by which mental disease or defect may be considered in mitigation of punishment”).

Finally, this Court has long recognized that the States may regulate the scope of expert testimony to prevent confusion of the jury. In *Winchester v. United States*, 157 F. 569, 573 (6th Cir. 1907), the court upheld a trial court’s exclusion of expert testimony on the ground that it would “obscure rather than elucidate the issues.” Arizona’s rule serves the same prophylactic purpose, preventing psychiatric evidence from being used to negate mens rea in a way that would undermine the statutory framework.

In sum, Arizona’s rule does not offend the Due Process Clause. The judgment below should be affirmed.
