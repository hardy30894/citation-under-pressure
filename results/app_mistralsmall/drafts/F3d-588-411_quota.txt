**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court’s Supplemental Jury Instruction on “Shielding” Was Erroneous and Prejudicial.**

The district court’s definition of “shielding” impermissibly expanded the statutory term beyond its plain meaning and misled the jury. Section 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) criminalizes “concealing, harboring, or shielding from detection” illegal aliens for commercial advantage or private financial gain. The term “shielding” must be construed in accordance with its ordinary meaning and the statutory context. *See United States v. Lau, 347 F.3d 875, 879 (9th Cir. 2003)* (“Statutory terms are generally interpreted according to their plain and ordinary meaning.”). The district court, however, instructed the jury that “shielding” includes “any act that tends to hide or conceal an alien from detection by the government.” This definition is fatally overbroad. It encompasses conduct far beyond the statute’s scope, such as routine employment activities that merely facilitate an alien’s continued presence in the country.

The Seventh Circuit has repeatedly cautioned against jury instructions that dilute statutory elements or invite speculation. *See United States v. Coslet, 188 F.3d 825, 829 (7th Cir. 1999)* (“A jury instruction that misstates the law is reversible error.”). The instruction here failed to require the government to prove that Ye took affirmative steps *beyond* mere employment to prevent detection—such as providing false documents, altering records, or concealing the aliens’ whereabouts. *See United States v. Kim, 193 F.3d 567, 573 (7th Cir. 1999)* (holding that “harboring” requires more than passive employment). By omitting this critical requirement, the instruction permitted the jury to convict Ye based solely on his hiring and payroll practices, which are not themselves criminal under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii).

Other circuits have rejected similarly vague definitions of “shielding.” The Ninth Circuit, for example, held that “shielding” requires proof of an affirmative act to frustrate detection, not merely employing or housing an alien. *Lau, 347 F.3d at 880*. The Second Circuit has likewise emphasized that the term implies an intent to obstruct governmental efforts to identify or apprehend illegal aliens. *United States v. Sash, 822 F.2d 1461, 1465 (10th Cir. 1987)* (citing *United States v. Kim, supra*). The district court’s instruction ignored these limiting principles, instead inviting the jury to infer guilt from conduct that is not inherently culpable under the statute.

The error was not harmless. The jury’s note requesting definitions of “concealing” and “shielding” demonstrates that these terms were not self-explanatory. *See United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 736 (1993)* (noting that jury confusion may warrant reversal). The government’s case relied heavily on evidence of Ye’s employment and payroll practices—conduct that, while probative of his knowledge of the aliens’ status, does not alone establish an intent to “shield” them from detection. The erroneous instruction allowed the jury to conflate hiring illegal aliens (a misdemeanor under § 1324a) with the felony of shielding them. This risk of confusion was exacerbated by the fact that the jury convicted Ye on both counts, suggesting it may have relied on the same conduct to support both charges. *See United States v. Lane, 474 U.S. 438, 449 (1986)* (finding prejudice where the jury could have used the same evidence to convict on multiple counts).

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Ye Intended to “Shield” the Illegal Aliens from Detection.**

Even if the jury instruction were correct, the evidence was insufficient to prove that Ye acted with the specific intent required by § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). To convict, the government must show that Ye took affirmative steps to prevent the government from detecting his employees, not merely that he employed or housed them. *See United States v. Kim, 193 F.3d at 573*; *United States v. Lau, 347 F.3d at 880*.

The government’s evidence showed that Ye hired illegal aliens, paid them in cash, and provided housing and transportation. But these acts, without more, do not evince an intent to shield the aliens from detection. *See United States v. Sash, 822 F.2d at 1465* (holding that routine employment and housing do not constitute “shielding”). The government points to Ye’s offer to accept fake documents and his lease agreements for apartments where illegal aliens lived as evidence of an intent to conceal. However, these acts are ambiguous. Providing housing and accepting false documents could just as easily reflect Ye’s desire to retain workers as an intent to obstruct detection. *See United States v. Coslet, 188 F.3d at 829* (requiring proof of a “conscious purpose” to frustrate detection).

The Seventh Circuit has repeatedly reversed convictions under § 1324 where the evidence showed only employment or housing of illegal aliens without additional indicia of an intent to conceal. *See, e.g., United States v. Kim, supra*; *United States v. Lau, supra*. Here, the government failed to present evidence that Ye took steps to hide the aliens from immigration officials, such as altering records, providing false identities, or warning the aliens of impending raids. *See United States v. Sash, 822 F.2d at 1465*. Without such evidence, the jury’s verdict cannot stand.

**Conclusion**

The district court’s supplemental instruction on “shielding” was erroneous and prejudicial, and the evidence was insufficient to support the conviction. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to enter a judgment of acquittal.
