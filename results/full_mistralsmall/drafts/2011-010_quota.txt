**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF.**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that § 1333(b) of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act ("LHWCA") coverage to injuries occurring on land, so long as there is a "substantial nexus" between the injury and extractive operations on the shelf. *Valladolid v. Pacific Operations Offshore, LLP*, 604 F.3d 1134, 1143 (9th Cir. 2010). This interpretation contravenes the plain text and structure of the OCSLA, as well as controlling Supreme Court precedent.

Section 1333(b) of the OCSLA provides that LHWCA coverage extends to "the disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf" unambiguously requires that the *operations* themselves be conducted on the shelf. *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 424 (1985) ("The OCSLA’s coverage is tied to the place where the injury occurs."). The Ninth Circuit’s "substantial nexus" test improperly divorces the injury from the situs of the operations, effectively reading the word "on" out of the statute.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the OCSLA’s coverage is geographically limited to the outer Continental Shelf. In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Court noted that "Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed." *Id.* at 219. The Court further observed that § 1333(b) is the only provision of the OCSLA that superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative situs requirement, but it explicitly declined to extend § 1333(b) to injuries occurring on the waters above the shelf. *Id.* at 219 n.2. The Court’s dictum in *Tallentire* thus supports the conclusion that § 1333(b) requires both that the operations be conducted on the shelf *and* that the injury occur on the shelf. *See also Director, OWCP v. Perini North River Assocs.*, 459 U.S. 297, 306 (1983) ("The LHWCA applies to injuries occurring on the ‘navigable waters of the United States.’").

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), is consistent with this interpretation. The Fifth Circuit held that an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it, in addition to satisfying the "but for" test. *Id.* at 362. The court reasoned that the OCSLA’s coverage is "geographically limited to the outer Continental Shelf," and that § 1333(b) does not extend coverage to injuries occurring on land. *Id.* at 361-62. The Third Circuit’s contrary decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is unpersuasive, as it improperly conflates the "but for" causation standard with the situs requirement. *Id.* at 810-11.

The Ninth Circuit’s "substantial nexus" test also conflicts with the LHWCA’s situs requirement. The LHWCA applies only to injuries occurring on "navigable waters" or on an "adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel." 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Supreme Court has held that the LHWCA’s situs requirement is strictly construed. *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69, 83 (1979) ("The coverage of the Act is limited to injuries sustained on the navigable waters of the United States or on an adjoining area customarily used for maritime activity."). The Ninth Circuit’s decision effectively nullifies this requirement for OCSLA claims, as it allows coverage for injuries occurring on land, so long as there is a "substantial nexus" to shelf operations. This interpretation is inconsistent with the statutory text and the Supreme Court’s precedent.

**II. THE BRB CORRECTLY HELD THAT LA CONCHITA IS NOT A MARITIME SITUS UNDER THE LHWCA.**

The Ninth Circuit also erred in reversing the Benefits Review Board’s ("BRB") denial of LHWCA benefits on the ground that La Conchita is not a maritime situs. The BRB correctly applied the "functional relationship" test set forth in *Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978), to determine whether La Conchita qualifies as an "adjoining area" under § 903(a) of the LHWCA.

Under *Herron*, courts consider several factors to determine whether a facility is an "adjoining area," including: (1) the particular suitability of the site for maritime uses; (2) whether adjoining properties are devoted primarily to maritime commerce; (3) the proximity of the site to the waterway; and (4) whether the site is as close to the waterway as feasible. *Id.* The BRB correctly found that La Conchita does not satisfy these factors. The facility is separated from the ocean by a highway and railroad tracks, has no direct access to any pier or dock, and is three miles from the nearest maritime facility. *Valladolid*, 604 F.3d at 1159. The BRB also correctly rejected the petitioner’s argument that La Conchita is a "transshipment" facility, as the scrap metal handling there did not involve moving cargo "directly from ship to land transportation." *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. at 82-83.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Pfeiffer* does not support the petitioner’s position, as the injuries in that case occurred on a dock and a pier, which are indisputably maritime situs. *Id.* at 71. The Court’s holding that the warehousemen were engaged in maritime employment because they handled cargo within a port does not address the situs requirement. *Id.* at 83. The Ninth Circuit’s decision thus improperly expands the LHWCA’s situs requirement beyond the Supreme Court’s precedent.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision and hold that the OCSLA extends LHWCA coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The Court should also affirm the BRB’s denial of LHWCA benefits, as La Conchita is not a maritime situs under § 903(a) of the LHWCA.
