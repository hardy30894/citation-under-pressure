# ARGUMENT

## I. The Plain Text of §§ 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) Exempts Only Funds Held for the Debtor's Own Retirement

The Bankruptcy Code exempts from the estate "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under" specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. 11 U.S.C. §§ 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). This Court has recognized that the exemption inquiry under § 522 turns on the objective characteristics of the asset the debtor holds at the time of filing. *Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320 (2005). In *Rousey*, the Court held that traditional IRAs qualify because they bear the "characteristics of the enumerated" retirement categories—namely, they are funds "held for the purpose of retirement." *Id.* at 331. The touchstone is not the tax label attached to an account, but whether the money is actually reserved for retirement.

An inherited IRA fails that test. The funds at issue here cannot be held until Heidi Heffron-Clark's retirement; federal tax law compels their withdrawal on an accelerated schedule regardless of the beneficiary's age or retirement status. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); *id.* § 402(c)(11)(A) (incorporating § 401(a)(9)(B)). As the Seventh Circuit correctly explained, "an inherited IRA is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner's retirement." *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013). Tax deferral alone does not convert an asset into "retirement funds"; the statute requires both tax-exempt status *and* the character of retirement savings. Because the second element is absent, the exemption cannot apply.

## II. The Legal Attributes of an Inherited IRA Confirm That It Is Not a Retirement Vehicle

Every feature that distinguishes an inherited IRA from an ordinary IRA points to the same conclusion. The beneficiary may not make new contributions. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). The beneficiary may not roll the funds into her own retirement account. *Id.* And most critically, the beneficiary must begin—and in as little as five years, complete—distribution of the entire balance, without any penalty for early withdrawal. *Id.* § 401(a)(9)(B). A traditional IRA holder who withdraws funds before age 59½ pays a penalty precisely because premature withdrawal defeats the retirement purpose of the account. *See Rousey*, 544 U.S. at 325. No such penalty attaches to withdrawals from an inherited IRA at any age, because there is no retirement purpose left to protect. The inherited IRA is, in economic substance, an inheritance that happens to retain a temporary tax shelter—not a nest egg for the beneficiary's own old age.

This Court has cautioned against reading bankruptcy exemptions more broadly than their text and function permit. In *Schwab v. Reilly*, 560 U.S. 770 (2010), the Court emphasized that exemptions must be assessed according to the "value" and functional character of the interest claimed, not the debtor's characterization of it. *Id.* at 791–92. Here, the character of the interest—an accelerating, non-rollover, penalty-free distribution requirement—is simply incompatible with the retirement-savings function that Congress sought to protect.

## III. Petitioners' Reliance on the Word "Retirement" in "Inherited IRA" Improperly Elevates Label Over Substance

Petitioners, like the Fifth Circuit in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), argue that because the Internal Revenue Code calls the asset an "inherited individual retirement account," it must retain its retirement character in the beneficiary's hands. That reasoning mistakes nomenclature for function. As the Seventh Circuit observed, the "IRA" in "inherited IRA" "designates the funds' source, not the assets' current status." *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564. A dollar does not remain "retirement funds" forever merely because it once resided in a retirement account. If Ruth Heffron had withdrawn the $300,000 during her lifetime and then given it to her daughter, no one would suggest the gift remained exempt "retirement funds" in Heidi's hands. The result should not differ merely because the transfer occurred by inheritance rather than lifetime gift; in both cases, by the time of the Clarks' bankruptcy filing, the money was "no one's retirement funds." *Id.* at 563.

Nor does the omission of the word "debtor's" from §§ 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), compared to neighboring provisions, support a contrary reading. That drafting choice reflects Congress's intent to permit ongoing employer contributions during bankruptcy without penalty—not to extend the exemption to assets that, by their nature, can never again function as retirement savings. *See Clark*, 714 F.3d at 565–66; *cf. Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) ("[N]o legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.").

## IV. Bankruptcy's Fresh-Start Policy Does Not Require Shielding Inheritances from Creditors

Petitioners' policy argument—that treating inherited IRAs as part of the estate discourages retirement savings—proves too much. Nearly any inherited asset could be characterized as encouraging saving if shielded from creditors, yet Congress did not exempt inheritances generally. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(d) (enumerating specific, limited exemptions). This Court should not stretch a targeted retirement exemption into a general asset-protection trust for inheritances. As the Seventh Circuit held, and as this Court should affirm, "inherited IRAs represent an opportunity for current consumption, not a fund of retirement savings." *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 559, 567.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
