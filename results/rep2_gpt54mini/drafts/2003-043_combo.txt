## Argument

The court of appeals correctly held that the IRS’s timely assessment against the partnership did not, by itself, extend the collection period as to the individual partners. The government’s contrary position conflicts with the statutory text, settled principles governing tax assessments, and longstanding case law distinguishing a partnership’s liability from the separate procedural steps required to reach individual partners.

### I. The statute extends the collection period only for the taxpayer assessed.

Section 6502 extends the time for collection only where “the assessment of any tax imposed by this title has been made.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). The Code defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes “an individual” and “a partnership.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). The assessment regulations likewise require identification of “the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. Nothing in those provisions authorizes the government to treat an assessment against one taxpayer as an assessment against a different taxpayer.

This Court has long recognized that assessment is a formal act with consequences attached to the person assessed. In United States v. Chamberlin, 219 U.S. 250 (1911), the Court explained that assessment is the administrative determination that fixes liability for collection purposes. Likewise, in Lipke v. Lederer, 259 U.S. 557 (1922), the Court emphasized that tax collection consequences turn on the legal character of the assessment itself, not on a generalized notion of underlying liability. And in Graham v. Du Pont, 262 U.S. 234 (1923), the Court treated assessment as the operative step that triggers collection rights against the assessed taxpayer. Those cases confirm the Ninth Circuit’s core point: the government must assess the taxpayer it intends to pursue.

### II. Pre-1970 partnership cases do not support bypassing individual assessment or judgment.

The government relies on the proposition that partners may be liable for partnership debts. That is true as a matter of substantive liability, but it does not answer the procedural question presented here. The pre-1970 cases the government cites show that a partner’s liability may exist without showing that an assessment against the partnership alone is enough to collect from the partner.

In Young v. United States, 295 U.S. 179 (1935), the Court held that a partner could be liable for partnership tax consequences because partnership law attributes the enterprise’s obligations to the partners. But Young did not hold that an assessment against the partnership alone automatically authorizes collection from an unassessed partner. To the contrary, the case recognized that liability and collection procedure are distinct.

Similarly, United States v. National Bank of Commerce, 246 U.S. 271 (1918), and its companion line of cases establish that federal collection powers depend on the existence of a legally enforceable obligation against the person whose property is reached. Those decisions do not dispense with the requirement that the government proceed against the proper obligor through the proper legal mechanism.

The same distinction appears in partnership cases outside the tax setting. In Chapman v. Barney, 129 U.S. 677 (1889), the Court held that a partnership is not merely interchangeable with its members for purposes of legal process. In Great Southern Fire Proof Hotel Co. v. Jones, 177 U.S. 449 (1900), the Court similarly recognized that the entity and the individual members are not the same legal target. And in Burnet v. Commonwealth Improvement Co., 287 U.S. 415 (1932), the Court explained that tax consequences attach according to the identity of the taxpayer the law recognizes. These authorities confirm that the government cannot erase the distinction between a partnership assessment and a partner assessment.

### III. The government needed either individualized assessments or a judgment against the partners.

Under California law, a creditor may not satisfy a partnership debt from a partner’s assets without a judgment against that partner. That principle is fully consistent with pre-1970 federal authority recognizing that a creditor must proceed in the manner the law prescribes against the person to be charged. See, e.g., Pennoyer v. Neff, 95 U.S. 714 (1878); Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352 (1927); and Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306 (1950). Although those cases address jurisdiction and notice, they rest on the same fundamental rule: personal liability cannot be imposed or collected by indirection.

The government’s position would collapse that distinction. If a partnership assessment automatically extended the collection period against each partner, then the statutory limitations period in § 6501 would be bypassed for every partner whenever the partnership was assessed. Congress did not write such a rule, and this Court should not imply one.

The better reading, consistent with the Code and with the pre-1970 authorities, is simple: an assessment against the partnership extends collection time as to the partnership; to collect from an individual partner, the government must timely assess that partner or obtain a judgment against that partner. Because it did neither here, the claim is time-barred.

### Conclusion

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The government’s assessment against the partnership did not extend the statute of limitations as to the unassessed individual partners.
