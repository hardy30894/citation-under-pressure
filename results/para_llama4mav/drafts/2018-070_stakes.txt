The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law. Instead, the OCSLA allows state law to apply only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the outer Continental Shelf "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. The Supreme Court has not directly addressed the meaning of "applicable" in this context, but the Fifth Circuit has interpreted it to require a gap in federal law before state law can be applied. See Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969).

This interpretation is consistent with the purpose of the OCSLA, which was to provide a comprehensive body of law for the outer Continental Shelf. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). The OCSLA was enacted to address the jurisdictional dispute between the federal government and adjacent states over the outer Continental Shelf. See Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep't of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19, 26 (1988).

In this case, the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour claims. The FLSA's savings clause expressly allows for more protective state minimum wage and overtime laws, see 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), but this does not create a role for state law to supplement the FLSA on the outer Continental Shelf. Instead, the FLSA's comprehensive scheme precludes the application of state wage and hour laws.

The Ninth Circuit's decision to the contrary is inconsistent with the Fifth Circuit's interpretation of the OCSLA and undermines the uniformity of federal law on the outer Continental Shelf. See Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043 (5th Cir. 1990). The court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that the OCSLA does not permit the application of state law unless there is a gap in federal law.

The FLSA is a comprehensive statute that governs wage and hour claims, and it does not leave room for state law to address these claims on the outer Continental Shelf. See 29 U.S.C. § 201, et seq. The Department of Labor regulations elaborating the FLSA further support this conclusion. See Brigham v. Eugene Water & Elec. Bd., 357 F.3d 931, 940 (9th Cir. 2004).

In conclusion, the OCSLA permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law. The FLSA provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour claims, and it precludes the application of state wage and hour laws on the outer Continental Shelf. The court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that Newton's claims are governed exclusively by the FLSA.
