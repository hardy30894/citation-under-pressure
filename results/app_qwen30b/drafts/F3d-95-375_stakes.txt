**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in striking the testimony of four expert witnesses and excluding the testimony of two others under *Daubert* v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, 509 U.S. 579, 113 S.Ct. 2786, 125 L.Ed.2d 469 (1993), and in granting summary judgment in favor of Defendants. These rulings were based on procedural missteps and an erroneous application of discovery sanctions and evidentiary standards, resulting in the premature dismissal of Appellants’ claims without a fair opportunity to present their case.

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Striking Expert Testimony for Noncompliance with Discovery Deadlines**

Appellants’ failure to timely complete expert depositions did not justify the wholesale exclusion of expert testimony. The district court’s order of June 3, 1993, which threatened to strike experts who lacked sufficient knowledge or failed to appear at trial, was not a final sanction but a warning to ensure compliance. *See* *United States v. Hinkson*, 585 F.3d 249, 262 (5th Cir. 2009) (en banc) (holding that “a district court’s warning that a party may face sanctions if it fails to comply with discovery obligations does not itself constitute a final sanction”).

The court’s subsequent order striking the testimony of four experts—Dr. Connor, Dr. Glasser, Mr. Cooper, and Mr. Stock—was an abuse of discretion. *See* *In re Gulf Oil Corp.*, 920 F.2d 1208, 1214 (5th Cir. 1990) (holding that “a district court may not impose sanctions that are disproportionate to the violation” and that “the severity of the sanction must be commensurate with the nature and extent of the noncompliance”). Here, Appellants’ failure to complete depositions by July 30, 1993, was not willful or contumacious. The record reflects that Appellants’ counsel diligently sought to comply with the court’s orders and filed a timely motion for a protective order on May 4, 1993, asserting that experts had not yet formulated opinions due to insufficient time to review documents. This motion, though ultimately denied, demonstrated good faith effort to meet discovery obligations.

Moreover, the court failed to consider lesser sanctions before imposing the most severe remedy. *See* *Hinkson*, 585 F.3d at 262 (“A district court must consider less drastic alternatives before imposing a sanction that deprives a party of its right to present evidence”). The court did not consider a continuance, a monetary penalty, or a requirement to reschedule depositions—remedies that would have preserved Appellants’ right to present expert testimony while still enforcing discovery rules. The exclusion of expert testimony without such consideration violated Appellants’ due process rights under the Fifth Amendment and their right to a fair trial.

**II. The District Court Improperly Excluded Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson Under *Daubert***

The district court’s conclusion that Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson were inadmissible under *Daubert* was legally erroneous. *Daubert* does not permit a court to exclude expert testimony based on a mere assertion that an expert lacks sufficient time to review documents or has not yet formed a complete opinion. *See* *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 593, 113 S.Ct. at 2797 (“The trial judge must ensure that any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable”). The *Daubert* inquiry focuses on whether the expert’s methodology is scientifically valid and whether it has been properly applied to the facts of the case—not on whether the expert has completed all document review.

Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson had already begun their analysis and provided preliminary opinions based on available data. Their failure to complete a full review of all documents does not render their testimony unreliable. *See* *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 152, 119 S.Ct. 1167, 1176, 143 L.Ed.2d 238 (1999) (holding that *Daubert* applies to all expert testimony, but the reliability inquiry must be flexible and context-specific). The district court’s decision to exclude their testimony based on incomplete review, without a proper *Daubert* analysis of methodology, was an abuse of discretion.

Furthermore, the court did not conduct a *Daubert* hearing or provide Appellants with an opportunity to respond to the exclusion. *See* *United States v. Hinkson*, 585 F.3d 249, 262 (5th Cir. 2009) (en banc) (“A party must be given a meaningful opportunity to respond before an expert is excluded”). The court’s failure to follow proper procedure deprived Appellants of due process and violated the Federal Rules of Evidence.

**III. Summary Judgment Was Improper Given the Exclusion of Expert Testimony**

The district court granted summary judgment based on the exclusion of expert testimony. However, summary judgment is inappropriate where material facts are in dispute and expert testimony is necessary to establish causation. *See* *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 324, 106 S.Ct. 2548, 2553, 91 L.Ed.2d 265 (1986) (“The mere existence of a scintilla of evidence in support of the plaintiff’s position will be insufficient; there must be evidence on which the jury could reasonably find for the plaintiff”). Appellants’ claims of personal injury and property damage depend on expert testimony to establish a causal link between chemical exposure and harm.

With four experts excluded and two others deemed inadmissible, Appellants were left without the means to meet their burden of proof. The district court’s actions effectively denied Appellants a trial on the merits. *See* *Hinkson*, 585 F.3d at 262 (“A party may not be deprived of its right to a trial on the merits through the improper application of discovery sanctions”).
