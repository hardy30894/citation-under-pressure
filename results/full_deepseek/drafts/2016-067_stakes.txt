**ARGUMENT**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act imposes liability only on “debt collectors,” not on creditors collecting their own debts. The statute’s plain text, structure, and purpose compel the conclusion that Santander Consumer USA, Inc. was a creditor when it attempted to collect the defaulted automobile loans it had purchased—and thus is not subject to the FDCPA.

**I. The FDCPA’s Definition of “Debt Collector” Turns on Whether the Person Collects Debts for Another, Not on the Debt’s Default Status**

Section 1692a(6) defines a “debt collector” as any person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due *another*.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). This language is “entirely transparent,” *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1315–16 (11th Cir. 2015): a person must collect debts “for others” in order to qualify. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (quoting § 1692a(6)). The material distinction between a creditor and a debt collector is “whether a person’s regular collection activity is only for itself” or “whether it regularly collects for others.” *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA, Inc.*, 817 F.3d 131, 137 (4th Cir. 2016) (quoting the opinion below). The default status of the debt when acquired is “no bearing on whether a person qualifies as a debt collector under the threshold definition.” *Id.* at 136.

The statute’s structure confirms this. The definition of “debt collector” in § 1692a(6) contains two parts: an inclusion clause (who is a debt collector) and an exclusion clause (who is not). The exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii)—which removes from the definition persons collecting debts “which was not in default at the time it was obtained”—applies only to those who already satisfy the inclusion clause. *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1314. Petitioners’ argument that default status alone determines debt-collector status improperly skips the threshold inquiry. As the Fourth Circuit correctly held, “if a person does not satisfy one of the definitions in the main text, the exclusions in subsections § 1692a(6)(A)–(F) do not come into play.” *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 137.

**II. Santander Was a Creditor Collecting Its Own Debts, Not a Debt Collector**

The complaint alleges that Santander purchased the petitioners’ defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto on December 1, 2011, “for a few cents on the dollar.” *Id.* at 135. After that purchase, Santander owned the debts. It was collecting for its own account, not for another. The FDCPA defines a “creditor” as “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). Because Santander became the owner of the debts, it was “a person to whom the debt is owed” and thus a creditor. *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 136. The Act “generally does not regulate creditors, when they collect debt on their own account.” *Id.* at 134.

Petitioners argue that the FDCPA’s definition of “creditor” excludes “any person to the extent that he receives an assignment or transfer of a debt in default solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). But Santander did not receive the debts “solely for the purpose of facilitating collection ... for another.” It purchased the debts for its own investment portfolio. That exclusion therefore does not apply. *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 136.

**III. Petitioners’ Interpretation Would Distort the FDCPA’s Purpose**

Congress enacted the FDCPA to “eliminate abusive debt collection practices by *debt collectors*.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e) (emphasis added). The Act targets third-party collectors who act on behalf of creditors, not creditors who collect their own debts. *See Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 293. Petitioners’ reading would sweep every purchaser of defaulted debt into the FDCPA’s regulatory net, even when that purchaser is collecting solely for itself. That would “create a loophole” that Congress did not intend. *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 139. Moreover, the FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” must be assessed “at the time of the conduct that is subject to regulation.” *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1318; *see also Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (the phrase “owed or due another” refers to “the time of collection,” not origination). Because Santander owned the debts when it engaged in the challenged collection practices, it was a creditor, not a debt collector.

The judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
