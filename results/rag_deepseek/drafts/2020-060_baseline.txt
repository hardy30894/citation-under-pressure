**ARGUMENT**

The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that Guam’s 2004 Clean Water Act consent decree triggered CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby barring Guam’s timely § 107 cost-recovery claim. A § 107 action remains available to any party that has incurred response costs unless the party has been adjudicated liable in a CERCLA action, which Guam has not.

**I. A PRP That Incurred Response Costs May Bring a § 107 Cost-Recovery Action Even If It Has Settled with the Government Under a Non-CERCLA Statute.**

Under *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 168 (2004), “a private party that has not been sued under § 106 or § 107 may nonetheless recover costs under § 107(a)(4)(B).” Guam was never sued under CERCLA; EPA’s 2002 complaint alleged only Clean Water Act violations. The 2004 consent decree resolved those violations, not any CERCLA liability. Nothing in CERCLA’s text excludes a § 107 claim simply because the plaintiff has entered a non-CERCLA settlement. *Cooper* made clear that § 113(f) “provide[d] two express avenues for contribution” but did not “affect the availability of § 107(a)(4)(B) to a party that has incurred response costs.” *Id.* at 168 n.5. Guam incurred millions in closure and remediation costs; it may seek full cost recovery under § 107 from the Navy, a PRP that deposited hazardous substances for decades.

**II. The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not “Resolve” Guam’s Liability for a CERCLA Response Action.**

Section 113(f)(3)(B) applies only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action.” CERCLA defines “response action” as “remove, removal, remedy, and remedial action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24). The consent decree here targeted Clean Water Act violations—discharges of untreated leachate—not a CERCLA removal or remediation. Although CERCLA may encompass leachate collection, the decree’s label as a Clean Water Act settlement, coupled with its express disclaimer of “any finding or admission of liability,” demonstrates that Guam did not resolve CERCLA liability. *See* J.A. 92. A disclaimer of liability should be given effect consistent with the parties’ intent. *Ngiraingas v. Sanchez*, 495 U.S. 182, 187 (1990) (recognizing Guam’s status under the Organic Act, 48 U.S.C. § 1421a, but not altering principles of statutory interpretation). The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on cases discounting disclaimers conflicts with this Court’s directive to construe statutes “according to their terms.” *Hancock v. Train*, 426 U.S. 167, 174 (1976).

**III. The Consent Decree Did Not Trigger § 113(f)(3)(B) Because It Did Not Involve CERCLA Liability.**

*Cooper* instructs that § 113(f)(3)(B) is triggered only when a party has resolved its liability in a “CERCLA-based settlement.” 543 U.S. at 167. Here, EPA sued solely under the Clean Water Act; the consent decree required a cover system to stop pollutant discharges, not to remediate hazardous substances under CERCLA. The D.C. Circuit erred by treating the decree as a “CERCLA settlement” simply because it imposed cleanup-like obligations. But the Clean Water Act and CERCLA serve different purposes. *Compare Meghrig v. KFC Western, Inc.*, 516 U.S. 479, 485 (1996) (CERCLA provides recovery of cleanup costs) *with PUD No. 1 of Jefferson County v. Washington Department of Ecology*, 511 U.S. 700, 707 (1994) (Clean Water Act protects water quality). Guam’s performance under the decree did not constitute a resolution of CERCLA liability, and therefore it retains its § 107 claim.

**IV. Holding That Any Settlement Triggers § 113 Would Undermine CERCLA’s Remedial Purpose.**

If a non-CERCLA settlement automatically forces a PRP into a § 113 contribution action with a three-year statute of limitations, then PRPs would be discouraged from settling quickly—contrary to Congress’s goal of encouraging prompt cleanup. *See Hallstrom v. Tillamook County*, 493 U.S. 20, 27 (1990) (strict compliance with statutory requirements may frustrate remedial purposes). The D.C. Circuit’s rule would penalize Guam, which acted at EPA’s insistence, while the Navy—the true polluter—escapes liability. CERCLA was designed to “ensure that responsible parties foot the bill.” *City of Chicago v. Environmental Defense Fund*, 511 U.S. 328, 332 (1994). Guam is not the responsible party; the Navy is. The court’s decision would allow the Navy to avoid its share of the cleanup costs, contrary to the statute’s fundamental objective.

**V. Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and Guam should be permitted to proceed with its § 107 cost-recovery claim.
