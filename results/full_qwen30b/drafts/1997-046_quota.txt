**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency process creates a constitutionally protected liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects not only life and liberty in their natural, pre-statutory sense, but also those interests “created by state law” when the state has “made a person’s continued confinement dependent upon the outcome of a particular administrative proceeding.” *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 484 (1995). In *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539, 557 (1974), the Court recognized that “a prisoner may have a liberty interest in avoiding disciplinary sanctions,” even though such interests are not inherent in the concept of liberty itself. The Court emphasized that “the Due Process Clause protects the individual against arbitrary deprivation of liberty by the State,” and that “a liberty interest may be created by state law even in the absence of a constitutional guarantee.” *Id.* at 557–58.

Here, Ohio has created a structured, mandatory clemency process that directly affects the duration of Mr. Woodard’s confinement. Under Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07, the Ohio Adult Parole Authority (APA) is required to initiate a clemency review 45 days before a scheduled execution unless the date is stayed. This procedural obligation is not discretionary—it is mandatory and triggered by a fixed timeline. The APA’s own regulation, the “Death Penalty Clemency Procedure,” further establishes that the review includes a personal interview option, which, if exercised, will be considered at the clemency hearing. This is not a mere formality; it is a state-created mechanism that can result in the commutation of a death sentence. As the Supreme Court held in *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 12 (1979), “the liberty interest at stake is not the right to be free from confinement, but the right to be free from prolonged confinement.” When a state creates a process that can alter the length of confinement, it creates a liberty interest protected by the Due Process Clause.

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that no such interest exists is inconsistent with *Dumschat*, which the Court itself distinguished in *Sandin*. In *Dumschat*, the Court held that a prisoner had no protected liberty interest in a pardon or commutation process because the state had not created a “substantive entitlement” to relief. *452 U.S. at 465*. But *Sandin* clarified that the presence of a liberty interest depends not on whether the state has promised relief, but on whether the state has “created a liberty interest by making the outcome of a particular administrative proceeding determinative of the length of confinement.” *515 U.S. at 484*. Ohio’s clemency process satisfies this standard: the outcome of the APA’s review directly determines whether Mr. Woodard remains on death row or is granted commutation. The state’s own regulation confirms that the interview and review are not advisory but are “considered at the clemency hearing, along with all other relevant information.” Thus, the APA’s process is not a mere “grace” but a mechanism that can alter the duration of confinement—precisely the kind of process that triggers due process protection.

II. The APA’s clemency interview procedure violates the Fifth Amendment by coercing a waiver of the right against self-incrimination.

The Fifth Amendment protects individuals from being compelled to be witnesses against themselves. *U.S. Const. amend. V*. This protection extends to administrative proceedings where the individual faces a real and substantial risk of self-incrimination. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 318 (1976). In *Baxter*, the Court held that a prisoner could not be punished for refusing to answer questions in a prison disciplinary hearing if the answers might incriminate him in a criminal prosecution. *Id.* at 324. The Court emphasized that “the risk of self-incrimination is not speculative” when the answers could be used in a future criminal trial. *Id.* at 323.

Here, the APA’s clemency interview is not a neutral forum. The regulation explicitly states that any statement made during the interview “shall be considered at the clemency hearing.” This creates a direct incentive to speak—because silence may be interpreted as a lack of remorse or cooperation—while simultaneously exposing the inmate to the risk that his statements could be used against him in a future criminal prosecution. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a person may not be compelled to incriminate himself,” and that “the privilege against self-incrimination is not limited to criminal trials.” *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609, 614 (1965). The Court has also held that “the Fifth Amendment privilege is not forfeited by participation in a state administrative proceeding.” *Cleveland v. United States*, 329 U.S. 14, 18 (1946).

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that the regulation does not compel a waiver is legally flawed. While the regulation does not *require* a prisoner to speak, it creates an unconstitutional condition by making participation in the interview a de facto prerequisite for clemency. As the Court held in *Cleveland*, “the privilege against self-incrimination is not lost because the proceeding is administrative.” *329 U.S. at 18*. The APA’s process, by linking the interview to the clemency decision, effectively forces a choice: speak and risk self-incrimination, or remain silent and forfeit any chance of commutation. This is precisely the kind of “Hobson’s choice” the Fifth Amendment was designed to prevent. *See also* *Lefkowitz v. Turley*, 414 U.S. 70, 81 (1973) (holding that a person cannot be compelled to waive constitutional rights as a condition of receiving a benefit).
