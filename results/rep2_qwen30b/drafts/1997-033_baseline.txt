**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires this Court to interpret a clear statutory command and to correct a long-standing judicial departure from it. The Ninth Circuit’s decision upholding the District of Arizona’s transfer of the case to itself for trial under § 1404(a) is fundamentally at odds with the text, structure, and purpose of § 1407(a), and must be reversed.

**I. Section 1407(a) Unambiguously Mandates Remand for Trial**

The plain language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) leaves no room for judicial discretion: “Each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” to the original district court “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” (Emphasis added.) This is not a permissive provision; it is a mandatory directive. The statute does not authorize the transferee court to retain jurisdiction for trial, nor does it grant the district court the power to transfer the case to itself for trial under § 1404(a). The phrase “shall be remanded” is a command, not a suggestion. As this Court has repeatedly held, “when the words of a statute are unambiguous, that is the end of the matter.” *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842 (1984). The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that the statute “appears to be a clear statutory mandate” yet “lacks merit” in practice is a judicial abdication of statutory duty.

The legislative history confirms that Congress intended § 1407 to be a temporary, limited transfer mechanism. The statute was designed to consolidate pretrial proceedings—primarily discovery and motion practice—where common issues of fact exist, but to preserve the plaintiff’s right to trial in the original forum. *See H.R. Rep. No. 89-1453, at 1 (1966)* (“The purpose of the bill is to provide for the transfer of cases to a single district for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings, but not for trial.”). To allow a transferee court to retain jurisdiction for trial undermines this foundational policy. It transforms a temporary, coordinated pretrial mechanism into a permanent forum-shifting device, contrary to Congress’s intent.

**II. Section 1404(a) Cannot Be Used to Subvert § 1407(a)**

The District of Arizona’s invocation of § 1404(a) to transfer the case to itself for trial is legally impermissible because it conflicts with the exclusive remand mandate of § 1407(a). Section 1404(a) permits a court to transfer a case “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only where the transfer is to a district “where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). The case in question was originally filed in the Northern District of Illinois. Under § 1407(a), it was transferred to Arizona *only* for pretrial proceedings. Once those proceedings conclude, the case must be remanded to Illinois—its original forum—regardless of convenience.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), is misplaced. Those cases, and the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation’s own Rule 14(b), have created a de facto exception to § 1407(a) that this Court has never endorsed. The fact that courts have “repeatedly” erred in this manner does not make the error lawful. *See United States v. Hays*, 515 U.S. 735, 743 (1995) (“A long-standing practice does not justify a departure from the clear meaning of a statute.”). The judiciary may not rewrite statutes through judicial precedent, especially when the text is unambiguous.

Moreover, the notion that a transferee court may “sua sponte” transfer a case to itself for trial under § 1404(a) is a legal fiction. The transfer is not “for the convenience of parties and witnesses” in any meaningful sense. The parties have already been litigating in Arizona for over a year. The “convenience” argument collapses into a self-fulfilling prophecy: the court retains jurisdiction because it has already been litigating there, and it can retain jurisdiction because it has already been litigating there. This circular logic cannot justify a violation of a clear statutory mandate.

**III. The Prejudice to Petitioner Is Not Correctable on Appeal**

The Ninth Circuit held that petitioners could “attain the relief they request on direct appeal,” but this ignores the practical reality of the case. The trial is scheduled to begin in less than a month. The cost and burden of litigating in Arizona—where Lexecon has no local counsel, no familiarity with the district, and no connection to the case—constitute irreparable harm. The prejudice is not merely “inconvenience”; it is a fundamental deprivation of the right to trial in the forum originally chosen by the plaintiff under the Seventh Amendment and the common law tradition of venue.

The Ninth Circuit cited *Sunshine Beauty Supplies v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 872 F.2d 310 (9th Cir. 1989), to reject the claim of prejudice, but that case is distinguishable. There, the plaintiff was forced to travel from California to Texas to litigate an arbitration confirmation. Here, the prejudice is not just travel—it is the loss of the right to trial in the original forum, a right Congress explicitly preserved in § 1407(a). The prejudice is not merely “inconvenient”; it is constitutional in nature.
