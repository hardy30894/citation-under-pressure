**ARGUMENT**

Respondent submits that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim. The Sixth Circuit’s contrary holding conflicts with settled principles of federal habeas corpus law and this Court’s precedents.

I. A Procedurally Defaulted Claim Cannot Excuse Another Procedural Default.

This Court has long held that a federal habeas corpus petitioner may not raise a claim that was procedurally defaulted in state court unless the petitioner can demonstrate cause and prejudice. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). The Court has further explained that the "cause" requirement is satisfied only where the petitioner shows that "some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule." *Id.* at 84. The Court has never suggested that a procedurally defaulted claim—such as a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel—can itself serve as cause to excuse another procedural default.

The Sixth Circuit’s decision in this case is irreconcilable with *Sykes*. In *Sykes*, the Court rejected the argument that a petitioner’s ignorance of the law could excuse a procedural default, explaining that "a deliberate bypass is not excused merely because the defendant lacked a full and perfect understanding of the law." *Id.* at 86. The same principle applies here: a petitioner cannot use a procedurally defaulted claim to excuse another default, regardless of whether the defaulted claim is meritorious.

II. The Sixth Circuit’s Holding Conflicts with This Court’s Precedents.

The Sixth Circuit’s decision also conflicts with *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), and *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293 (1963), which this Court later clarified in *Sykes*. In *Fay*, the Court held that a federal habeas corpus petitioner could raise a claim that was procedurally defaulted in state court if the petitioner could show "cause" for the default. *Fay*, 372 U.S. at 419. However, the Court later explained in *Sykes* that *Fay*’s "cause" standard was not intended to allow petitioners to use one defaulted claim to excuse another. *Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 88-89.

The Sixth Circuit’s decision also conflicts with *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443 (1965), in which the Court held that a petitioner could not use a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse a procedural default unless the petitioner could show that the ineffective assistance was "sufficient to justify a decision on the merits." *Id.* at 452. The Court explained that "the mere assertion of a constitutional violation does not suffice to excuse a procedural default." *Id.* The Sixth Circuit’s decision in this case effectively allows a petitioner to use a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse another procedural default, a holding that is squarely at odds with *Henry*.

III. The Sixth Circuit’s Holding Undermines the Integrity of State Procedural Rules.

The Sixth Circuit’s decision also undermines the integrity of state procedural rules and the finality of state court judgments. As this Court has explained, "procedural rules are not to be disregarded lightly, for they serve important interests in the administration of justice." *Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 88. Allowing a petitioner to use a procedurally defaulted claim to excuse another procedural default would encourage petitioners to default claims strategically, knowing that they could later use a defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse the default. This would frustrate the state courts’ efforts to enforce their procedural rules and would undermine the finality of state court judgments.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s decision and hold that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim.
