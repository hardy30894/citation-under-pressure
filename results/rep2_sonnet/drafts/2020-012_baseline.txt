# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Section 355(f) Compels the Conclusion That Board Decisions Denying Reopening Are Reviewable "Final Decisions"

Section 5(f) of the Railroad Unemployment Insurance Act, incorporated by the Railroad Retirement Act, provides that "[a]ny claimant, or any railway labor organization organized in accordance with the provisions of the Railway Labor Act, national in scope, which has in its membership employees of an employer as defined in this Act, or any other party aggrieved by a final decision under subsection (c) of this section, may . . . obtain a review of any final decision of the Board." 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). Congress used the word "any"—not "some," not "decisions on the merits," but "any final decision." This Court has repeatedly instructed that such expansive statutory language "carr[ies] . . . an expansive meaning." *United States v. Gonzales*, 520 U.S. 1, 5 (1997) (the word "any" has "an expansive meaning" and courts should not add restrictions Congress did not include).

A Board order denying a request to reopen is unquestionably a "decision." And it is "final" in every relevant sense: it terminates the administrative proceeding, leaves the claimant with no further avenue for relief on that request, and is treated by the Board's own regulations as a conclusive disposition. *See* 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. Nothing in the text confines "final decision" to only those decisions resolving the merits of an original benefits application. The Fifth Circuit's rule—and the rule of the circuits it followed—engrafts a merits limitation onto statutory text that contains none.

## II. This Court's Analogous Social Security Precedent Confirms That Reopening Denials Are Reviewable Unless a Constitutional Claim Is Absent—Not Categorically Unreviewable

The courts that have denied jurisdiction, including the Fifth Circuit below, rely on this Court's decision in *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977). But *Sanders* does not support blanket non-reviewability; it supports only a narrower proposition that must be read in context.

In *Sanders*, this Court held that the Social Security Act's judicial review provision, 42 U.S.C. § 405(g), does not authorize review of the Secretary's decision not to reopen a prior claim, because "the opportunity to reopen final decisions and any hearing convened to determine the propriety of such action are afforded by the Secretary's regulations and not by the Social Security Act." 430 U.S. at 108. The Court reasoned that reopening is a matter of grace under agency regulation, not a matter of statutory right, and thus "an interpretation that would allow a claimant judicial review simply by filing—and being denied—a petition to reopen his claim would frustrate the congressional purpose" of imposing a 60-day limitations period on review of the Secretary's decisions. *Id.* at 108.

The Railroad Retirement Act's structure is materially different. Unlike the Social Security Act, the RRA's reopening procedure is not merely a matter of administrative grace layered atop a statute silent on the subject—the RRA's own review provision uses the unqualified term "any final decision," without the limiting structure that concerned the *Sanders* Court. Moreover, even under *Sanders*, this Court left open judicial review where the agency's refusal to reopen is challenged on grounds that go beyond mere abuse of discretion—the *Sanders* Court itself noted the petitioner "st[ood] on no better footing" because her challenge raised no colorable constitutional claim. *Id.* at 109. The reasoning of *Sanders* thus counsels a claim-specific inquiry, not the categorical jurisdictional bar the Fifth Circuit imposed.

## III. The D.C. Circuit's Decision in Stovic Correctly Interprets Section 355(f) and Should Guide This Court

The D.C. Circuit in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), correctly held that "the Railroad Retirement Act grants the [circuit] Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations." *Id.* at 502. That court recognized what the majority-rule circuits missed: Section 355(f)'s text draws no distinction between an initial merits denial and a denial of reopening. Both are "final decision[s]" of the Board disposing of a claimant's request. The Second Circuit's earlier decision in *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), and the Eighth Circuit's decision in *Sones v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 933 F.2d 636 (8th Cir. 1991), likewise recognized reviewability, creating the very circuit split the Fifth Circuit acknowledged below. *See* Pet. App. 3 (citing *Sones*, 933 F.2d at 638; *Szostak*, 370 F.2d at 254–55).

This Court should resolve that split by adopting the textually faithful approach. The competing rule adopted in *Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), and followed by the Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, and Tenth Circuits, imports a merits-only limitation absent from the statutory text based on an over-extension of *Sanders*'s Social-Security-specific reasoning to a differently worded and differently structured statute.

## IV. Denying Review Would Leave Claimants Like Petitioner Without Any Meaningful Check on Arbitrary Agency Action

Petitioner's case illustrates the stakes. The hearing officer determined that Petitioner's request fell outside the four-year regulatory window for reopening. *See* 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. If such determinations are wholly unreviewable, the Board's application of its own regulations—including questions of whether a claimant's request was correctly categorized, whether tolling principles apply, or whether the agency correctly calculated the relevant time period—escapes all judicial scrutiny, no matter how arbitrary. This Court has long recognized a "strong presumption" favoring judicial review of administrative action. *Bowen v. Michigan Academy of Family Physicians*, 476 U.S. 667, 670 (1986) (recognizing "the strong presumption that Congress intends judicial review of administrative action"). Nothing in Section 355(f) rebuts that presumption; to the contrary, its text confirms it.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
