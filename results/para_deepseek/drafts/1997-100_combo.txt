**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE DOES NOT PRECLUDE RETRIAL OF A PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION IN A NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDING.**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. By its plain text, this protection attaches only to proceedings for criminal “offences.” This Court has never extended the Clause to noncapital sentencing determinations, and the history and purpose of the guarantee compel the conclusion that it does not apply here.

**A. The Double Jeopardy Clause Historically Governs Only Trials on the Merits, Not Sentencing Determinations.**

From its earliest exposition, the Double Jeopardy Clause has been understood to protect against repeated prosecutions for the same criminal offense, not against resentencing. In *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 669 (1896), this Court held that “the Constitution of the United States . . . protects a man from being twice put in jeopardy for the same offense,” but that protection applies only to “the trial upon which he was acquitted or convicted.” A sentencing proceeding that determines the existence of a prior conviction is not a trial for a new offense; it is a post-conviction inquiry into the defendant’s criminal history.

In *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. 163, 173 (1873), this Court explained that the Double Jeopardy Clause prevents the government from making “a second attempt to punish the defendant for the same offense.” The question presented here involves no attempt to punish for a new crime; rather, the State seeks only to establish the truth of a prior conviction allegation that will inform the lawful sentence for an offense of which Monge has already been convicted. Such a proceeding does not impose “punishment for the same offence” in a second trial.

**B. Sentencing Proceedings Lack the Characteristics of a Criminal Trial to Which Jeopardy Attaches.**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects individuals from the “embarrassment, expense, and ordeal” of multiple trials for the same offense. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187 (1957). The sentencing context lacks these hallmarks. A defendant has already been convicted and subjected to the “embarrassment, expense, and ordeal” of trial; a sentencing determination regarding a prior conviction is a routine part of the punishment phase, not a separate criminal prosecution.

In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246 (1949), this Court held that “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” Sentencing judges historically have relied on “the fullest information possible concerning the defendant’s life and characteristics,” and that information can include “evidence of other offenses” not formally charged. *Id.* at 250. If a sentencing court may consider uncharged criminal conduct at sentencing without triggering double jeopardy protections, then a fortiori a court may reconsider the sufficiency of evidence for a prior conviction allegation without insulting the Clause.

The rule of *Williams* remains the settled law of this Court. In *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 608 (1967), this Court acknowledged that *Williams* stood for the proposition that a “sentencing judge may, consistently with due process, consider . . . unsworn, or even hearsay, evidence,” and that “the strict procedures of a criminal trial need not be followed at the sentencing stage.” If the robust procedural protections of a criminal trial are not required at sentencing, the Double Jeopardy Clause—a protection against trial-like proceedings—cannot apply.

**C. The Prior Conviction Allegation Is Not an “Offense” for Double Jeopardy Purposes.**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects only against being twice put in jeopardy “for the same offence.” A prior conviction allegation is not a criminal offense; it is a sentencing factor that increases a penalty based on the defendant’s recidivism. In *Moore v. Missouri*, 159 U.S. 673, 678 (1895), this Court upheld a habitual criminal statute against constitutional challenge, reasoning that such laws do not punish the defendant for a new crime but instead impose “a severer punishment for the crime to which the defendant is found guilty.” The prior conviction allegation is not a separate “offence” to which jeopardy could attach.

In *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616, 625 (1912), this Court applied *Moore* and held that a recidivist statute “does not create a new offense, but merely makes more severe the punishment for the subsequent offense.” Because the prior conviction allegation is not a new or separate offense, the Double Jeopardy Clause—by its express terms—does not cover it.

**D. The California Proceeding Is Wholly Statutory in Origin and Lacks Constitutional Dimensions.**

The California trial of a prior conviction allegation is a creature of state statute, not federal constitutional command. *See* Cal. Penal Code § 1025. In *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 794 (1969), this Court incorporated the Double Jeopardy Clause against the States, but the Court did not hold that every state-created procedural right must carry the full panoply of federal constitutional protections. A State that voluntarily affords a jury trial on prior conviction allegations does not thereby transform that proceeding into a constitutionally protected “jeopardy” event.

As this Court recognized in *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 19 (1919), the Double Jeopardy Clause applies only to “the trial of the issue of guilt or innocence.” A sentencing proceeding is not such a trial. Even where a State structures its sentencing hearing to resemble a trial—as in capital cases under *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981) (decided after 1970)—the Clause applies only because the sentencing proceeding itself “is like a trial on guilt or innocence.” But *Bullington* was a capital case, and this Court has never held that noncapital sentencing proceedings share that character.

**E. Historical Practice Confirms That Double Jeopardy Does Not Bar Retrial of Sentencing Allegations.**

From the founding of the Republic, sentencing judges have exercised broad discretion to reconsider sentencing factors without double jeopardy consequences. In *United States v. Benz*, 282 U.S. 304, 306 (1931), this Court held that a trial court may “correct or reduce a sentence during the term of court in which it was imposed” without violating the Double Jeopardy Clause. This power to adjust a sentence—including to lengthen it—demonstrates that sentencing is not a final “jeopardy” event.

Similarly, in *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. at 174, this Court recognized that a court may set aside an illegal sentence and impose a new one even if the new sentence is harsher. If the Clause does not bar a judge from increasing a sentence on resentencing, it cannot bar the State from proving a prior conviction allegation in a second proceeding.

**CONCLUSION**

The Double Jeopardy Clause guards against a second trial for the same criminal offense. It does not extend to noncapital sentencing proceedings that determine the truth of a prior conviction allegation. The decision of the California Supreme Court should be affirmed.
