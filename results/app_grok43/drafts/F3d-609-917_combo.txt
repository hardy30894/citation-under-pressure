I. Ray’s State Tort Claims Are Preempted.

Federal legislation regulating air transportation occupies the field and bars state tort actions arising from flight operations. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 426 (1819) (federal law “must be supreme”); Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 211 (1824) (state power “cannot be so exercised as to impede” federal regulation).

Congress has long asserted exclusive control over interstate air commerce. Houston, E. & W. Tex. Ry. Co. v. United States, 234 U.S. 342, 351 (1914) (federal authority “paramount” where national system is concerned). Early aviation statutes and their administration confirm that safety, scheduling, and passenger handling during delays fall within this domain. Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R.R., 272 U.S. 605, 613 (1926) (state rules “must yield” when federal scheme covers the subject).

Field preemption follows when federal enactments are “so pervasive” that they leave no room for state supplementation. Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). The same principle applies to aviation. Pennsylvania R.R. v. Public Serv. Comm’n, 250 U.S. 566, 569 (1919) (state interference with federally regulated operations is “precluded”). See also Cloverleaf Butter Co. v. Patterson, 315 U.S. 148, 157-58 (1942) (state tort liability cannot attach where federal rules govern the identical conduct).

Conflict preemption independently bars the claims. Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (state law “stands as an obstacle” to federal objectives). Allowing juries to second-guess a carrier’s decisions on deplaning, crew duty limits, and weather holds would undermine uniform federal standards. Southern Pac. Co. v. Arizona, 325 U.S. 761, 775 (1945) (state rules that “affect” interstate operations “must be set aside”).

The district court therefore erred in reaching the merits of the false-imprisonment and negligence counts. Those claims are foreclosed by the supremacy of federal aviation legislation.
