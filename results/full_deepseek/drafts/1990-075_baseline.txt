**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT CONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATES LEGISLATIVE POWER TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL**

The Constitution does not prohibit Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches so long as Congress lays down “by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), satisfies this requirement.

Section 811(h) directs the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance only when necessary “to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). The statute specifies three factors the Attorney General must consider: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of abuse, and the risk it poses to the public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3). Congress also required notice in the Federal Register and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). These standards meaningfully confine discretion. *See Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374-75 (upholding delegation where Congress specified goals, factors, and limits on the delegatee’s authority).

The temporary period is limited to one year, with a possible six-month extension. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). This temporal limit provides “an intelligible principle” because the Attorney General’s power is narrow, bounded, and temporary. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944) (upholding delegation where the administrative action was “temporary and emergency” in nature). The rapid evolution of designer drugs further justifies the need for executive flexibility. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372 (“Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives”).

Petitioners rely on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). But those cases invalidated delegations that “contained no standards,” *Panama*, 293 U.S. at 415, or that gave “virtually unfettered” discretion, *Schechter*, 295 U.S. at 538. Section 811(h), by contrast, provides specific criteria, deadlines, and inter-agency consultation. Unlike the National Industrial Recovery Act, which delegated authority to create codes of fair competition without guidance, the CSA cabins the Attorney General’s power with “meaningful limits.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374.

The preclusion of judicial review under 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6) does not render the delegation unconstitutional. Judicial review is not a constitutional predicate for lawful delegation. *See Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952). Moreover, section 811(h)(6) does not bar constitutional challenges to the delegation itself, as the court below correctly recognized. *See* App. 759-60; *United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988).

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL PROPERLY DELEGATED TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA**

Congress authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the CSA. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) provides that the Attorney General may “authorize the performance of any of the functions vested in him by this subchapter by any officer, employee, or agency of the Department of Justice.” This broad grant of subdelegation authority reflects Congress’s understanding that effective drug enforcement requires operational flexibility.

The Attorney General exercised this authority through 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which assigns the DEA Administrator the “[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.” This regulation encompasses the temporary scheduling power. Nothing in section 811(h) prohibits subdelegation, and Congress expressly contemplated it. Cf. *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 514 (1974) (subdelegation valid where Congress explicitly authorizes it).

Accordingly, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
