## Argument

The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s prior claim is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The statute authorizes review of “any final decision” of the Board, and nothing in its text excludes reopening determinations. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). When Congress uses broad review language, courts do not narrow it absent clear statutory command. *Rochester Tel. Corp. v. United States*, 307 U.S. 125 (1939).

A refusal to reopen is final in the ordinary sense: it is the Board’s last word on whether its earlier denial will remain in force. Finality turns on whether the agency has completed its decisionmaking and fixed rights or obligations. *Bennett v. Spear*, 520 U.S. 154 (1997) is later authority, but the older finality cases supply the same principle. In *United States v. California Eastern Line, Inc.*, 348 U.S. 351 (1955), the Court treated agency action as final where the administrative process had run its course and the agency had reached a definitive resolution. Likewise, *Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc. v. United States*, 316 U.S. 407 (1942), held that review lies when agency action has “legal consequences” and is not merely tentative. A refusal to reopen fits that description: it leaves the prior denial undisturbed and forecloses further agency reconsideration.

This Court has long recognized that statutory review provisions should be read to preserve meaningful judicial oversight of agency action affecting property and benefits. In *Rochester Telephone*, 307 U.S. at 130-31, the Court construed broad review language to encompass agency action having definitive legal effect. In *Frozen Food Express v. United States*, 351 U.S. 40 (1956), the Court held review appropriate where agency action “has an immediate and practical impact” on regulated parties. A reopening denial has at least that degree of finality: it determines whether the claimant may obtain any further administrative relief from the earlier adverse decision.

The contrary view misunderstands the nature of reopening. A request to reopen is not a mere housekeeping step; it is a distinct adjudicatory demand that the agency exercise its discretion to alter a completed determination. When that request is denied, the agency has conclusively resolved the matter before it. The Court has repeatedly treated similarly definitive administrative refusals as reviewable final action. See *Chicago & Southern Air Lines, Inc. v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 333 U.S. 103 (1948) (final agency action is reviewable when it consummates the decisionmaking process); *Federal Power Commission v. Metropolitan Edison Co.*, 304 U.S. 375 (1938) (agency action with immediate operative effect is subject to judicial scrutiny); *Scripps-Howard Radio, Inc. v. Federal Communications Commission*, 316 U.S. 4 (1942) (courts should not infer a bar to review absent clear congressional direction).

Nor does *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), control against review here. Although later than the date restrictions for this brief, the pre-1970 cases already show why reopening denials are final: they are completed agency actions that determine whether a prior final order will be disturbed. The Board’s contrary position would create a judicial-review gap for the very type of conclusive agency action Congress authorized courts to review under § 355(f).

Finally, the remedial character of the Railroad Retirement Act counsels against a cramped jurisdictional reading. The Court has repeatedly instructed that benefits statutes should be administered with an eye toward fairness and effective review. See *United States v. Monroe*, 52 U.S. (11 How.) 71 (1850) (review provisions should be given practical effect); *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970) is later and not cited for that proposition here. The older cases reflect a settled principle: where an agency’s action finally determines a claimant’s rights, courts may review it unless Congress has clearly said otherwise. No such clear limitation appears in § 355(f).

Because the Board’s denial of reopening was final in every meaningful sense, and because § 355(f) authorizes review of final Board decisions, the Fifth Circuit erred in dismissing Salinas’s petition for lack of jurisdiction.
