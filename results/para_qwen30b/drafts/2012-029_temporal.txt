**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over state-based legal malpractice claims that require the application of federal patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case—must be answered in the negative. The Supreme Court’s pre-1970 jurisprudence firmly establishes that federal jurisdiction under the “arising under” clause of Article III and 28 U.S.C. § 1331 is not triggered merely because a state-law claim involves a federal issue, especially when that issue is incidental, not dispositive, and does not threaten the uniformity or integrity of federal law. The Court has consistently held that federal question jurisdiction requires more than mere relevance; it demands that the resolution of a substantial federal question be *necessary* to the outcome of the case, and that the federal interest be so compelling as to justify the displacement of state courts.

In *Swift & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 311 (1928), the Court emphasized that federal jurisdiction does not extend to cases where the federal issue is “merely incidental” to the main controversy. The Court stated: “The mere fact that a federal question is involved does not bring the case within the jurisdiction of the federal courts.” Id. at 319. This principle was reaffirmed in *Henderson v. United States*, 298 U.S. 393 (1936), where the Court held that a state court could adjudicate a claim for damages arising from a federal statute’s enforcement, even though the claim depended on the interpretation of a federal law, because the federal issue was not “the essence of the controversy.” Id. at 398. The Court made clear that “the presence of a federal question in a case does not necessarily make it a case arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States.” Id.

This limitation on federal jurisdiction was further solidified in *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), where the Court rejected the notion that any claim involving federal law automatically falls within federal jurisdiction. The Court observed that “a suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws.” Id. at 117. The Court insisted on a “selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside.” Id. at 118. The Court’s concern was not merely procedural but constitutional: to preserve the balance between state and federal judicial authority, federal courts must not be allowed to “swallow up” state-law claims simply because they touch upon federal law.

The same principle applies here. Petitioner’s legal malpractice claim arises under Texas law and is based on the failure of his attorneys to assert a defense in a prior patent infringement suit. The central issue in the malpractice claim is not whether the patent is valid, but whether the attorneys’ conduct fell below the standard of care. The patent law issue—whether the experimental use exception applies—is not the *cause of action*; it is merely a *defense* to the underlying patent claim, and even then, it is a defense that was not timely raised and was ultimately rejected by the federal court. The resolution of the patent issue is not necessary to the outcome of the malpractice suit in the way required by *Gully* and *Swift*. The malpractice claim can be decided on state law principles of duty, breach, causation, and damages, without any need to determine the validity of the patent or the applicability of the experimental use exception.

Moreover, the federal interest in uniformity of patent law, while important, does not justify the displacement of state courts in cases where the federal issue is not substantial or dispositive. In *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), the Court held that federal jurisdiction does not arise merely because a federal law is involved, unless the controversy “really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends.” Id. at 569. Here, the patent issue is not “actually disputed” in the malpractice suit in the sense required by *Grable*—it is not a live, contested issue in the state court. The federal court has already ruled on the patent’s validity, and the malpractice claim does not seek to relitigate that determination. The state court need only assess whether the attorneys’ failure to raise the experimental use exception was negligent, not whether the exception would have succeeded.

The Court has never held that a state court may not adjudicate a claim involving federal law simply because the claim depends on the interpretation of a federal statute. In *United States v. Kimbrough*, 295 U.S. 330 (1935), the Court upheld the jurisdiction of state courts to hear claims arising from the enforcement of the Federal Trade Commission Act, even though the claims required interpretation of federal law. The Court stated: “The fact that the claim is based upon a federal statute does not necessarily make it a case arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States.” Id. at 334. The Court emphasized that “the jurisdiction of the federal courts is not to be extended by implication.” Id.

Thus, the mere presence of a federal patent issue in a state malpractice claim does not confer exclusive federal jurisdiction. The federal interest in uniform patent law is not so substantial as to justify the displacement of state courts when the federal issue is incidental, not central, and when the state court can resolve the claim without undermining federal policy. The Court has consistently refused to allow federal jurisdiction to be expanded beyond the “substantial” and “necessary” threshold. To hold otherwise would violate the constitutional balance between state and federal judicial power, a principle that has long been recognized and protected by this Court.

For these reasons, the Supreme Court of Texas correctly concluded that federal jurisdiction does not lie in this case. The patent issue is not substantial within the meaning of *Shulthis* and *Gully*. The malpractice claim does not “arise under” federal patent law. The federal courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction over this state-law claim, even though it involves a patent issue. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
