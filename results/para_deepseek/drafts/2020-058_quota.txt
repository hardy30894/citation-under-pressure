**ARGUMENT**

The Third Circuit erred by holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a does not constitute an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The text, context, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act compel the opposite conclusion.

**I. The Plain Text of the INA Supports Petitioners’ Interpretation**

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). A grant of TPS satisfies this definition. When an alien applies for TPS, the government inspects the application and authorizes the alien to remain. The alien thereby obtains lawful entry through that authorization. The Board of Immigration Appeals has recognized that “an adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission.” *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). By parity of reasoning, a grant of TPS—which places the alien in lawful nonimmigrant status—should also constitute an admission.

The Sixth and Ninth Circuits have correctly so held. *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548, 553 (6th Cir. 2013) (“[A] grant of TPS constitutes an ‘admission’ for purposes of § 1255.”); *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017) (“[T]he process of obtaining TPS constitutes an admission.”). These decisions respect the statutory definition: the alien undergoes inspection and receives authorization, effectuating a lawful entry.

**II. Statutory Context and Structure Confirm That TPS Constitutes Admission**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” This deeming provision is not merely a status designation; it equates TPS recipients with nonimmigrants who were lawfully admitted. Congress knows how to distinguish between “admission” and “status” when it wishes. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(2)(A) (referring to “lawful admission” and “lawful status” as separate requirements). By deeming TPS recipients to be in lawful nonimmigrant status, Congress intended that they be treated as admitted for adjustment purposes. *See I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (“[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another . . . it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally.”). The omission of an explicit “admission” language in § 1254a(f)(4) is not dispositive because the deeming provision itself accomplishes that result.

The Third Circuit’s interpretation would render § 1254a(f)(4) superfluous for TPS recipients who entered illegally. If TPS does not constitute admission, then those recipients can never satisfy § 1255(a)’s threshold requirement, and the deeming provision provides no benefit. Statutes should be construed to give effect to all provisions. *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004).

**III. The Purpose of TPS and Adjustment of Status Supports Petitioners**

TPS is a humanitarian program designed to shield aliens from removal when their home countries are unsafe. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). The Third Circuit’s holding forces TPS recipients who entered illegally to either depart and reenter lawfully—often impossible due to dangerous conditions—or remain indefinitely without a path to permanent residence. This absurd result undermines the humanitarian purpose of TPS. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (statutes should be interpreted to avoid absurd results). Moreover, ambiguities in immigration statutes should be resolved in favor of the alien. *I.N.S. v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 320 (2001); *Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. at 449.

**IV. The Third Circuit’s Distinction Between Admission and Status Is Erroneous**

The Third Circuit drew a sharp line between “admission” as a physical event and “status” as a legal condition. But the statutory definition of “admission” focuses on “lawful entry . . . after inspection and authorization,” not on the moment of physical crossing. A grant of TPS provides that authorization. The court’s formalistic approach ignores that the INA itself treats status and admission as intertwined. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(m)(1) (referring to “an alien admitted into the United States (or otherwise provided nonimmigrant status)”). Because the statute is clear, no deference is owed to the agency’s contrary interpretation. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984) (if the intent of Congress is clear, that is the end of the matter).

For these reasons, this Court should hold that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” under § 1255 and reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit.
