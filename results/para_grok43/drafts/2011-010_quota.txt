The OCSLA extends LHWCA coverage only where the injury occurs on the outer Continental Shelf. Section 1333(b) provides benefits for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The statute’s text thus imposes both a geographic and a causal limitation. Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 n.2 (1986) (“Only one provision of OCSLA superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement.”).

Congress deliberately tethered coverage to the Shelf’s physical boundaries. The OCSLA defines the Shelf as submerged lands “lying seaward and outside of the area of lands beneath navigable waters.” 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). This territorial definition controls the scope of the compensation provision. Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 361 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). The Fifth Circuit therefore requires that the injury occur “on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above” the Shelf. Id. at 362; see also Becker v. Tidewater, Inc., 586 F.3d 358, 366-67 (5th Cir. 2009); Pickett v. Petroleum Helicopters, Inc., 266 F.3d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 2001).

The Supreme Court has confirmed that OCSLA coverage is “determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Tallentire, 477 U.S. at 219. That principle precludes extending benefits to injuries sustained on land. Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414, 422-24 (1985) (emphasizing the “explicit geographic limitation” in OCSLA’s incorporation of the LHWCA). An injury occurring hundreds of feet inland, separated by a highway and railroad tracks, falls outside that limitation. Motoviloff v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 692 F.2d 87, 89 (9th Cir. 1982).

The Third Circuit’s contrary “but for” approach in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), cannot be reconciled with the statutory text or with Tallentire. The Fifth Circuit correctly rejected that view, holding that a welder injured during onshore construction of a platform destined for the Shelf was ineligible. Mills, 877 F.2d at 357, 362. The same result follows here: Valladolid’s death occurred at an onshore processing facility whose primary function—receiving and separating crude oil slurry—is non-maritime. Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray (Herb’s Welding II), 766 F.2d 898, 900 (5th Cir. 1985).

The LHWCA’s own situs requirement reinforces this conclusion. Benefits are available only for injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States” or adjoining areas “customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). La Conchita satisfies none of these criteria. Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978). It is three miles from the nearest pier, separated from the water by a highway and railroad, and used principally for oil processing rather than vessel-related activities. P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford, 444 U.S. 69, 82-83 (1979), is inapposite because the maritime cargo movement ended at the pier.

Because Valladolid’s injury did not occur on the Shelf, OCSLA coverage is unavailable. The Benefits Review Board’s denial of benefits should be affirmed.
