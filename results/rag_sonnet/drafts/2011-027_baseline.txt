# ARGUMENT

## THE CONSTITUTIONAL TEST FOR TITLE NAVIGABILITY REQUIRES A SEGMENT-BY-SEGMENT DETERMINATION OF NAVIGABILITY AS OF THE DATE OF STATEHOOD

### A. Title to the Beds of Navigable Rivers Passed to Montana at Statehood Under the Equal Footing Doctrine

Under the equal footing doctrine, each State entering the Union acquires title to the beds of navigable waters within its borders as an incident of sovereignty. As this Court explained in *Utah Division of State Lands v. United States*, 482 U.S. 193 (1987), "[u]nder English common law the English Crown held sovereign title to all lands underlying navigable waters," and because "all subsequently admitted States enter the Union on an 'equal footing' with the original 13 States, they too hold title to the land under navigable waters within their boundaries upon entry into the Union." *Id.* at 195-96 (citing *Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan*, 3 How. 212 (1845)). This title vests in the State at the precise moment of statehood. As Justice Kennedy observed in dissent in *Idaho v. United States*, 533 U.S. 262 (2001), "[a]t the very moment that Idaho entered the Union 'on an equal footing with the original States,' . . . Congress and the President vested in Idaho the accoutrements of sovereignty, including title to submerged lands." *Id.* at 296 (Kennedy, J., dissenting). The relevant inquiry is therefore fixed in time: it asks what the character of the waterway was when the State was admitted—not before, and not after.

This temporal fixity is not incidental to the doctrine; it is essential to it. Because title vested instantaneously and irrevocably at statehood, courts applying the equal footing doctrine must look to conditions existing at that moment. The dissent in *Idaho* emphasized that this Court has never applied the doctrine "to determine title to submerged lands" by looking "beyond the moment of statehood for evidence of federal intent." 533 U.S. at 296 (Kennedy, J., dissenting). The corollary is equally true: courts cannot determine the *scope* of what passed at that moment without examining the condition of the specific waterway at that same historical juncture.

### B. Navigability for Title Purposes Must Be Assessed Segment by Segment as of Statehood, Not on a Generalized or Present-Day Basis

The segment-specific, statehood-era character of the navigability-for-title inquiry is confirmed by this Court's practice in adjudicating riverbed title disputes. In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 801 (1931), this Court did not issue a single undifferentiated navigability ruling for the Colorado and San Juan Rivers; instead, it parceled the inquiry by discrete river segments, holding that one "stretch of river" was navigable while a different, adjacent "stretch of river" was "not . . . navigable," with title to the latter "vested in the United States." *Id.* This segment-by-segment methodology reflects the reality that a single river may possess different characteristics—rapids, falls, shallows—along its course, such that navigability for title purposes cannot be assumed uniformly for an entire watercourse based on evidence drawn from a different reach or a different era.

Montana's dams span multiple segments of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers, constructed between 1891 and 1958—long after statehood in 1889. The historical record concerning navigability must therefore be assessed as it stood in 1889, for each segment of river on which PPL's facilities sit, not by reference to modern recreational use, post-statehood commercial development, or the river's character at locations distant from the specific segments at issue. This is consistent with the analytic framework this Court applied in *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377 (1941), which required a "decision as to the navigability" of the specific watercourse—there, the New River—as a "preliminar[y]" step before reaching the federal licensing question. *Id.* at 404. The inquiry into navigability was tied to the particular river segment presented, not to a generalized assessment of interstate waterways.

### C. Evidence of Historical Conditions at Statehood Is Both Necessary and Properly Admitted to Establish Title Navigability

Because the constitutional test fixes the relevant date as the moment of statehood, courts must necessarily rely on historical evidence reconstructing river conditions from that era. The Montana Supreme Court correctly recognized that "there is no one alive today who personally witnessed the condition of Montana's rivers in 1889," yet properly admitted historical records, survey reports, and archival materials under recognized exceptions to hearsay rules, including the ancient-documents exception and public-records exception, precisely because such evidence is indispensable to reconstructing statehood-era navigability. Any other rule would render the equal footing doctrine unenforceable, since direct testimonial evidence of 1889 river conditions is categorically unavailable.

### D. State Law Governs the Scope and Consequences of Title Once Vested, But Federal Law Fixes the Navigability Test Itself

While the *initial* question of title vesting is governed by federal common law under the equal footing doctrine, this Court has long recognized that once title vests, "the rights of the state and of riparian owners in navigable waters within the state and to the soil beneath are matters of state law." *Fox River Paper Co. v. Railroad Commission*, 274 U.S. 651, 655 (1927). This bifurcation does not permit Montana to redefine the navigability-for-title test itself; rather, it confirms that the federal constitutional test—asking whether the specific river segment was navigable in fact at the date of statehood—must be satisfied first, after which state law may govern the beneficial use and disposition of the riverbed, including through mechanisms like Montana's Hydroelectric Resources Act.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires courts to determine, on a segment-specific basis, whether the relevant stretch of river was navigable at the time of statehood. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
