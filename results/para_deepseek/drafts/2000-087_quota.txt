**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. Rather, consistent with this Court’s holding, the statute contains an implicit “reasonable time” limitation, and once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is unauthorized. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 689 (2001).

**I. The Statute, Read in Light of Constitutional Demands, Authorizes Only Reasonable Detention**

Congress enacted § 1231(a)(6) to ensure an alien’s presence at the moment of removal. *Id.* at 699. The statute’s basic purpose is removal, not punishment. *Id.* at 690. Where removal is not reasonably foreseeable, detention ceases to serve that purpose and becomes impermissibly punitive. *Id.* at 690-91. This Court has long recognized that “freedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty that the Due Process Clause protects.” *Id.* at 690 (citing *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992)).

Indefinite detention of admitted aliens raises serious constitutional concerns. *Id.* at 691. Unlike excludable aliens who have not effected entry, see *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 212 (1953), admitted aliens are within the territorial jurisdiction of the United States and “entitled to due process protection.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 693; see also *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 237-38 (1896). The Constitution forbids the government from “subjecting an alien to an indefinite civil detention that has no relationship to the removal purpose.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 692.

Because § 1231(a)(6) applies broadly to aliens ordered removed for many reasons, including minor violations, *id.* at 694, indefinite detention cannot be justified as a categorical rule. Civil detention must be limited to periods reasonably necessary to achieve its purpose. *Foucha*, 504 U.S. at 80 (holding that a state may not “confine a person without more” when no punitive or remedial purpose is served). Where “removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the court should hold continued detention unreasonable and no longer authorized by statute.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 699-700.

**II. The Six-Month Presumption Ensures That Detention Remains Reasonable**

This Court established a presumptively reasonable six-month period for post-removal-order detention. *Id.* at 701. After six months, “once the alien provides good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, the Government must respond with evidence sufficient to rebut that showing.” *Id.* This presumption ensures that detention does not become indefinite, consistent with this Court’s holding in *Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. 715, 738 (1972), that indefinite commitment violates due process where there is no “reasonable probability” of achieving its purpose.

The Government’s argument that detention may continue as long as “good faith efforts” persist would require an alien to prove that removal is “impossible,” a standard far more demanding than what the statute can bear. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 702. Such a standard would authorize detention “for months or years” with no end in sight, *id.* at 691, effectively permitting the very indefinite detention the Constitution prohibits. *See* *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 747 (1987) (recognizing that preventive detention is permissible only when “narrowly focused” and limited in duration).

**III. The Lower Court’s Decision Correctly Applied This Court’s Holding**

The Fifth Circuit, on remand, properly held that Zadvydas had “provided good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future” and that the INS had not rebutted that showing. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 285 F.3d 398, 402 (5th Cir. 2002) (per curiam). The court recognized that Zadvydas had been in INS custody for over three years after expiration of the removal period, and as “the period of prior post-removal confinement grows, what counts as the ‘reasonably foreseeable future’ conversely would have to shrink.” *Id.* (quoting *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 701).

The Fifth Circuit’s approach faithfully implements this Court’s holding. It does not require proof of absolute impossibility, but instead asks whether removal is reasonably foreseeable. *Id.* That standard respects the statute’s purpose while avoiding the constitutional concerns that would arise from indefinite detention. *See* *Clark v. Martinez*, 543 U.S. 371, 380-81 (2005) (applying *Zadvydas* to inadmissible aliens and reaffirming that “the statute does not authorize indefinite detention”).

**IV. The Statute Does Not Authorize Detention for Purposes of Punishment or Deterrence**

The Government may not justify indefinite detention as a means of preventing future crimes or punishing past misconduct. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690-91. The Due Process Clause prohibits “punitive” detention without a criminal trial. *Foucha*, 504 U.S. at 80. Civil detention is permissible only when it serves a legitimate regulatory purpose, such as ensuring removal, and is limited to the period reasonably necessary to achieve that purpose. *Id.* at 78-79. The supervision provisions of § 1231(a)(3) provide an alternative that protects the public without indefinite imprisonment. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 696.

**V. The Conditional Release Ordered by the District Court Was Appropriate**

The district court imposed conditions on Zadvydas’s release, including regular reporting, a bond, and restrictions on residence and employment. *Zadvydas*, 285 F.3d at 400 n.2. Such conditions ensure compliance with removal orders and protect public safety. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 696. The statute expressly authorizes supervised release, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(3), and nothing prevents the INS from later seeking to revoke release if a substantial likelihood of removal arises. *Zadvydas*, 285 F.3d at 402.

**CONCLUSION**

The post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize indefinite detention. The decision below correctly held that Zadvydas’s continued detention was unreasonable and ordered his release subject to appropriate conditions. This Court should affirm.
