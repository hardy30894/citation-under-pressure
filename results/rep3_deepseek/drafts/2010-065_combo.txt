**ARGUMENT**

The Tenth Amendment is a structural provision that reserves powers to the States; it does not confer individual rights. A private party therefore lacks standing to invoke the Tenth Amendment as a defense to a federal criminal prosecution. This Court has consistently held that only a State—not a private individual—may challenge a federal statute on federalism grounds.

In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939), private utility companies argued that the TVA’s sale of electricity violated the Tenth Amendment. This Court rejected the claim, holding that “absent the states or their officers, [the companies] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” That holding is binding. The Court did not treat the standing question as dictum; it resolved the jurisdictional issue before reaching the merits. *See id.* at 144.

The same principle applies here. Carol Anne Bond is a private individual, not a State. She cannot assert the Tenth Amendment because that provision “is not a source of individual rights.” *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17, 20–22 (1960). In *Raines*, the Court held that a defendant charged with violating a federal statute lacked standing to challenge the statute as exceeding Congress’s enumerated powers under the Tenth Amendment. *Id.* at 22. The Court explained that “the Tenth Amendment is not a source of individual rights” and that a defendant “cannot raise a claim that the statute invades the reserved powers of the States.” *Id.* at 20–22.

This Court has long recognized that the Tenth Amendment “states but a truism” about the distribution of power between the federal government and the States. *Steward Machine Co. v. Davis*, 301 U.S. 548, 596–97 (1937). It does not create a personal right enforceable by a private litigant. *See New York v. United States*, 326 U.S. 572, 576–77 (1946) (holding that a State may raise a Tenth Amendment claim, but a private party may not). Bond’s attempt to use the Tenth Amendment as a shield against prosecution is therefore foreclosed.

The requirement of standing demands that a litigant allege a “personal stake in the outcome of the controversy.” *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962). Bond cannot show that her own rights have been invaded. The Tenth Amendment protects the sovereignty of the States, not the interests of individuals. *See Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 488–89 (1923) (dismissing a taxpayer’s challenge to a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds because the plaintiff “has no such interest” in the federal-state balance). In *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 479–80 (1923), the Court held that a State may sue to protect its own powers, but an individual “cannot be heard to complain” of an alleged invasion of state sovereignty.

Similarly, in *Doremus v. Board of Education*, 342 U.S. 429, 434 (1952), the Court denied standing to a taxpayer who challenged a state law on federalism grounds, explaining that “the interests of the taxpayer are, to be sure, remote and indeterminable.” Bond’s interest is no less remote. She is not a State, and she cannot borrow the State’s prerogative to challenge federal authority.

The lower court correctly concluded that Bond lacks standing. The Third Circuit relied on *Tennessee Electric* and *Raines*, and its decision aligns with the uniform weight of authority. *See also Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 155–56 (1908) (permitting an individual to challenge a state statute under the Fourteenth Amendment, but not under the Tenth). The Tenth Amendment claim belongs to the States alone. Bond has no standing to raise it.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
