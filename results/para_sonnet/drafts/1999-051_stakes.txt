**ARGUMENT**

**I. Murray v. Carrier's exhaustion principle requires only that a prisoner present his ineffective-assistance claim to the state courts, not that the state courts adjudicate it on the merits.**

This Court's procedural-default doctrine rests on the "cause and prejudice" standard first articulated in *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72 (1977). In *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), the Court held that "the exhaustion doctrine, which is 'principally designed to protect the state courts' role in the enforcement of federal law,' . . . generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default." *Id.* at 488-489 (quoting *Rose v. Lundy*, 455 U.S. 509, 518 (1982)). The operative word is *presented*. Carrier's concern was comity—that a federal habeas court not adjudicate an ineffective-assistance theory the state courts never had a chance to consider. It was not concerned with the *outcome* the state court reached once the claim was presented.

Carpenter satisfied that comity interest in full. He raised his ineffective-assistance-of-appellate-counsel claim in an application to reopen his direct appeal in the Ohio Court of Appeals, and the Ohio Supreme Court reviewed and affirmed the denial of that application. The Ohio courts thus had—and exercised—the first opportunity to pass on the claim. That the Ohio Court of Appeals dismissed the application as untimely does not mean the claim was never "presented"; it means the state court considered and rejected it on a threshold ground. Nothing in *Carrier*'s exhaustion rationale suggests that a state court's procedural ruling erases the fact of presentation. Requiring more would convert a rule about comity into a trap that punishes prisoners for procedural missteps unrelated to the values *Carrier* sought to protect.

**II. Treating a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim as itself requiring a separate showing of cause and prejudice creates an infinite regress inconsistent with the purposes underlying the default doctrine.**

The procedural-default rules exist to encourage prisoners to raise claims properly and to respect the finality of state-court judgments. See *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 126-129 (1982). Once a prisoner has, as Carpenter did, actually brought his ineffective-assistance claim before the state's highest court, those interests are vindicated. To then demand that the prisoner show independent "cause" for the state court's procedural ruling on that very claim—and, if that cause is itself defaulted, cause for *that* default, and so on—produces an infinite regress that serves no purpose *Sykes* or *Carrier* identified. This Court has never suggested that the cause-and-prejudice inquiry should multiply endlessly; rather, the inquiry is meant to identify a legitimate excuse for noncompliance with state procedure, not to erect successive procedural barriers with no logical stopping point. The Sixth Circuit's approach—looking to whether the ineffective-assistance claim has merit as cause for the underlying default, without requiring a separate default analysis of the cause claim itself—more faithfully serves the equitable, discretionary character of the cause-and-prejudice standard that this Court described in *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87-88 (1977).

**III. In any event, Carpenter can satisfy cause and prejudice for any default of his ineffective-assistance claim, and the fundamental-miscarriage-of-justice exception independently permits review.**

Even if a separate showing of cause were required, Carpenter has made it. His postconviction counsel was appointed only after the time to seek reopening under Ohio's rule had begun to run, and the delay in raising appellate counsel's ineffectiveness stemmed from the same structural gap in representation that produced the underlying default—precisely the kind of external impediment this Court recognized as cause in *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986) (cause exists where "some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel's efforts to comply with the State's procedural rule").

Prejudice is equally clear. Appellate counsel's failure to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence supporting Carpenter's conviction fell below the standard set out in *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687-688 (1984), and but for that omission there is a reasonable probability the outcome of the appeal would have differed. *Id.* at 694.

Finally, even absent cause, the writ may issue because "a constitutional violation has probably resulted in the conviction of one who is actually innocent." *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 496 (1986). The uncontested weakness of the evidence supporting Carpenter's conviction—evidence the District Court and the Court of Appeals both found insufficient to sustain the judgment—brings this case within that narrow but vital safeguard against miscarriages of justice. See *McCleskey v. Zant*, 499 U.S. 467, 494 (1991) (reaffirming the miscarriage-of-justice exception as a "safety valve" against fundamentally unjust incarceration).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
