**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY FALL WITHIN THE STATUTORY EXCEPTION FOR “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL.”**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s state judges are “appointees on the policymaking level” and thus excluded from the ADEA’s definition of “employee.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f); *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 603–06 (8th Cir. 1990), *aff’d*, 499 U.S. 916 (1991). This conclusion is compelled by the text and structure of the ADEA, as well as the weight of judicial authority.

The ADEA’s “policymaking level” exception excludes “any appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). As the First Circuit recognized in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), judges exercise policymaking authority when they interpret statutes, develop common law, and resolve unsettled legal questions. This is not mere mechanical application of law but the exercise of discretion that shapes future legal standards. *Id.* (quoting *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 680 F. Supp. 455, 462 (D. Mass. 1988)). The Supreme Court has long recognized that judges perform a quintessentially policymaking function. *See Sweatt v. Painter*, 339 U.S. 629, 632 (1950) (holding that judicial decisions interpreting constitutional rights are inherently policymaking).

The Eighth Circuit’s analysis in *Gregory* is consistent with this principle. 898 F.2d at 604–06. It emphasized that Missouri judges exercise supervisory authority over court administration, adopt rules governing attorney conduct, and resolve disputes within the judicial hierarchy—functions that are undeniably policymaking. *Id.* at 605–06. Other courts have reached the same conclusion. *See EEOC v. Illinois*, 721 F. Supp. 156, 160 (N.D. Ill. 1989) (state judges are policymakers); *EEOC v. Vermont*, 717 F. Supp. 261, 264 (D. Vt. 1989) (same); *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 145 (Mass. 1988) (same). The contrary view, adopted in *Schlitz v. Virginia*, 681 F. Supp. 330 (E.D. Va. 1988), *rev’d on other grounds*, 854 F.2d 43 (4th Cir. 1988), and *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 680 F. Supp. at 265, is inconsistent with the ADEA’s text and purpose.

Moreover, the legislative history cited by petitioners does not support their narrow reading of the exception. The Conference Report explains that the exception was intended to exclude “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments, such as cabinet officers.” 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179–80. The term “agencies” includes the judicial branch, which is a co-equal branch of government. *See Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 606. The EEOC’s own interpretive guidance confirms this: in an amicus brief, it argued that appointed judges are policymakers because their “powers and responsibilities are identical” to those of elected judges. Brief for EEOC as Amicus Curiae at 9, *Gregory*, 898 F.2d 598 (No. 89-2360).

**II. EVEN IF THE ADEA APPLIED, IT DOES NOT PREEMPT MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR JUDGES.**

The ADEA does not expressly preempt state constitutional provisions governing judicial tenure. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 602–03. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that federal statutes do not preempt state law unless Congress has clearly manifested its intent to do so. *See, e.g., Will v. Michigan Dept. of State Police*, 491 U.S. 58, 65 (1989) (“It is a fundamental principle of our constitutional system that federal law may not supersede state law absent a clear statement by Congress.”). This clear-statement rule is particularly stringent in areas implicating state sovereignty, such as the structure of state courts. *Gregory*, 499 U.S. at 918–19 (plurality opinion) (citing *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 602–03).

The ADEA contains no such clear statement. The statute’s preemption clause applies only to state laws that “purport to require or permit the involuntary retirement of any individual” based on age. 29 U.S.C. § 623(f)(2). It does not address state constitutional provisions or judicial tenure rules. The Eighth Circuit correctly concluded that this silence cannot be read as an implicit repeal of state authority over judicial qualifications. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 602–03. The Supreme Court has refused to infer preemption in similar contexts. *See, e.g., Atascadero State Hosp. v. Scanlon*, 473 U.S. 234, 242–43 (1985) (state sovereign immunity not preempted by Rehabilitation Act absent clear statement).

**III. MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

The Eighth Circuit properly upheld Missouri’s mandatory retirement age for judges under rational-basis review. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 607–08. The Fourteenth Amendment permits age-based classifications so long as they are rationally related to a legitimate state interest. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). Missouri’s rule easily satisfies this standard.

The state has a legitimate interest in ensuring judicial competence and efficiency. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 607. Mandatory retirement reduces the risk of cognitive decline impairing judicial performance, minimizes delays caused by death or disability, and promotes public confidence in the judiciary. *Id.* (citing *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627–29 (7th Cir. 1979), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979)). These interests are not speculative; they are empirically grounded in the realities of aging and judicial workload. *See also Apkin*, 517 N.E.2d at 146 (listing rationales for judicial retirement ages).

Petitioners’ reliance on *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980), is misplaced. That case involved a federal statute, not a state constitutional provision, and did not address the unique concerns of judicial tenure. The Third Circuit’s observation that mandatory retirement “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged ... judges” does not undermine Missouri’s legitimate interests. *Id.* Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly deferred to state judgments in this area. *See, e.g., Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 314 (upholding mandatory retirement for state troopers at 50).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed. The ADEA does not apply to Missouri’s state judges, and even if it did, it does not preempt the state’s mandatory retirement provision. The provision also satisfies the Equal Protection Clause.
