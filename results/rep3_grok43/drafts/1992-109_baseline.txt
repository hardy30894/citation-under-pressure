ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause requires that a defendant be competent to stand trial, to plead guilty, and to waive counsel under a single standard: whether he has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam); see Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966). The Ninth Circuit’s imposition of a distinct, heightened “reasoned choice” standard for waivers of counsel and guilty pleas finds no support in this Court’s precedents.

This Court has never held that the competency inquiry changes with the stage of the proceeding. In Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), the Court remanded for a competency determination before a self-representation hearing but applied the Dusky formulation without elaboration. Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975), likewise required only that a waiver of counsel be “knowing and intelligent,” a determination that presupposes the defendant’s basic capacity to understand the rights being relinquished. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), used identical language for waivers of the right to counsel. None of these decisions announced or applied a more demanding mental-functioning test.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” formulation, derived from Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and its progeny, conflates the distinct inquiries of capacity and voluntariness. Competency concerns the defendant’s functional ability to participate; the knowing-and-intelligent character of a waiver concerns whether the defendant was adequately informed and acted without coercion. See Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389, 400-01 (1993) (holding the Dusky standard governs both). A defendant who meets the Dusky threshold may still enter an unknowing plea or an unintelligent waiver, but that possibility does not alter the constitutional minimum for capacity.

Requiring trial courts to apply two different competency standards would impose an unworkable and unwarranted burden. Courts already must determine whether a defendant understands the nature of the proceedings and can assist counsel. Adding a second, more stringent test for every waiver or plea would necessitate repeated hearings and expert examinations without advancing any constitutional interest identified by this Court. The record here shows that Moran was examined, found competent under Dusky, and thereafter made a series of knowing waivers after being advised of his rights. Due process requires nothing more.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
