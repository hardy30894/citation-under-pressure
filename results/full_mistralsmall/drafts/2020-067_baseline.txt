**ARGUMENT**

I. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Limited Its Review to the Trial Record When Assessing Plain Error.

A federal appellate court reviewing for plain error under Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b) must confine its prejudice analysis to the trial record. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 733–34 (1993) (“[T]he court of appeals can consider… only the trial record… when determining whether the error was prejudicial.”). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that appellate courts may not rely on extra-record materials to assess prejudice. *See id.* at 734 (“[T]he court of appeals’ consideration of evidence dehors the record is not part of the Rule 52(b) analysis.”); *United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258, 264 (2010) (noting that plain-error review “is confined to the record”).

The Eleventh Circuit faithfully applied this principle. It examined the trial record—including the jury instructions, verdict form, and evidence presented at trial—to determine whether the government’s failure to prove Greer’s knowledge of his felon status affected his substantial rights. *Greer v. United States*, 994 F.3d 1279, 1285–86 (11th Cir. 2021). The court did not consider Greer’s prior convictions or other post-trial conduct, as those were not part of the record before the jury. *Id.* This approach comports with settled precedent.

II. The Eleventh Circuit Correctly Concluded That Greer Failed to Demonstrate Prejudice.

To establish prejudice under plain-error review, a defendant must show that the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. The error must be “prejudicial in the sense that it affected the outcome of the proceeding.” *Id.* (quoting *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 16 (1985)). The Eleventh Circuit properly held that Greer could not meet this burden.

The government’s failure to prove Greer’s knowledge of his felon status was harmless because the jury necessarily found that Greer possessed a firearm. *Greer*, 994 F.3d at 1286. The jury’s verdict on the § 922(g) count established that Greer knowingly possessed the firearm; the only missing element was his knowledge of his felon status. *Id.* Given the overwhelming evidence of possession, the error did not affect the verdict. *See Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 17 (1999) (holding that failure to submit a material element to the jury is harmless if the evidence was overwhelming and the error did not affect the verdict).

The Eleventh Circuit also correctly rejected Greer’s argument that the error undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. *Greer*, 994 F.3d at 1286. The Supreme Court has cautioned that this fourth *Olano* factor is “meant to be applied in those rare cases in which the defendant’s actual guilt is so clear that the error can truly be said to have affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the judicial proceedings.” *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 470 (1997) (internal quotation marks omitted). Greer’s case does not meet that exacting standard. The jury’s verdict on the possession count, combined with the overwhelming evidence of his prior felony convictions, demonstrates that his guilt was clear. *See Greer*, 994 F.3d at 1286.

III. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Err in Declining to Consider Extra-Record Evidence.

Greer argues that the Eleventh Circuit improperly confined its prejudice analysis to the trial record. This contention misapprehends the law. As the Supreme Court has made clear, appellate courts may not rely on extra-record materials to assess prejudice under Rule 52(b). *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 733–34. The Eleventh Circuit’s adherence to this principle was correct.

Greer’s reliance on *United States v. Resendiz-Patino*, 423 F.3d 1197 (11th Cir. 2005), is misplaced. In *Resendiz-Patino*, the court considered extra-record evidence to determine whether a defendant’s substantial rights were affected, but the case did not involve plain-error review. *Id.* at 1201. By contrast, the Eleventh Circuit here applied the correct standard under *Olano* and *Marcus*, limiting its prejudice analysis to the trial record. *Greer*, 994 F.3d at 1285–86.

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit properly confined its plain-error analysis to the trial record and correctly concluded that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice. The judgment below should be affirmed.
