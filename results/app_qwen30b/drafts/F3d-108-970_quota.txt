**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON THE TITLE VII SEX DISCRIMINATION CLAIM BECAUSE FELTMANN FAILED TO ESTABLISH A PRIMA FACIE CASE OF DISCRIMINATION.

To survive summary judgment on a Title VII sex discrimination claim, a plaintiff must establish a prima facie case under McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). That requires showing: (1) she is a member of a protected class; (2) she was qualified for her position; (3) she suffered an adverse employment action; and (4) the adverse action occurred under circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination. See id. Here, Feltmann fails on the fourth prong—she cannot establish that the adverse actions occurred under circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination.

The alleged adverse actions—Rich’s personal memos, probation, exclusion from the Chicago sales competition, and restrictions on demo car use—were all based on legitimate, non-discriminatory reasons. Rich’s memo of January 4, 1993, criticized Feltmann’s “work ethic,” specifically her frequent use of guest drives and lack of teamwork. These concerns were not isolated; they were part of a broader performance evaluation process. As the Supreme Court held in Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981), “the employer’s reasons need not be wise, fair, or even correct, so long as they are legitimate and non-discriminatory.” The same applies to the probation decision in March 1993, which was based on documented low sales performance in January and February—objective metrics that were not disputed. See id. at 254.

Feltmann’s exclusion from the Chicago sales competition was not an adverse action at all. The opportunity was offered to the two top-performing consultants, who declined. Rich then did not extend it to others, including Feltmann, because the competition was not open to all. See St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993) (an employer’s decision not to promote is not an adverse action if it is based on legitimate performance criteria). Feltmann’s sales in March 1993 were strong—she sold more than all but one other consultant—but she was not among the top two. Thus, the decision was based on performance, not gender.

The restriction on demo car use was similarly justified. After sales declined, Rich implemented a cost-control measure restricting demo car use to the St. Louis area. Feltmann continued to drive 300 miles weekly to Marion, Illinois, despite the restriction. No other consultant received a memo, but that does not establish discrimination. As the Eighth Circuit held in Henson v. City of Dundee, 682 F.2d 897, 904 (11th Cir. 1982), “the fact that others were not disciplined does not prove discrimination if the employer had a legitimate reason for the differential treatment.” Here, the restriction was based on cost, not gender. Feltmann’s continued violation of the policy—after receiving a written warning—justified the disciplinary focus on her.

Moreover, Feltmann’s claim of discriminatory treatment is undermined by the fact that she was the only female consultant, yet she received the Pinnacle Club recognition in 1992, an elite honor based on sales and customer satisfaction. See 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1). The fact that she was recognized for high performance undermines any inference of discrimination. See Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins, 490 U.S. 228, 251 (1989) (a plaintiff’s success in a job can rebut an inference of discrimination).

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON THE RETALIATION CLAIM BECAUSE FELTMANN FAILED TO ESTABLISH A CAUSAL CONNECTION BETWEEN HER COMPLAINT AND THE ALLEGED ADVERSE ACTIONS.

To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under Title VII, a plaintiff must show: (1) she engaged in protected activity; (2) the employer took an adverse action; and (3) a causal connection exists between the two. See Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). Feltmann’s protected activity was reporting Anzalone’s vulgar comment in November 1992. But the adverse actions—memo in January, probation in March, restriction on demo use—occurred months later, and were based on performance, not retaliation.

The Eighth Circuit has held that “a temporal gap of several months between protected activity and adverse action undermines a claim of retaliation.” See Hensley v. City of St. Paul, 122 F.3d 551, 555 (8th Cir. 1997). Here, the gap was over two months between the complaint and the first memo, and over four months before probation. Such a delay weakens any inference of causation. See id. at 556 (a gap of more than two months “undermines the inference of retaliation”).

Further, the employer took corrective action after the complaint: Anzalone was reprimanded, and Feltmann was told to report future incidents. This demonstrates a good-faith effort to address the complaint, not retaliation. See Faragher v. City of Boca Raton, 524 U.S. 775, 790 (1998) (employers who respond appropriately to harassment complaints are not liable for retaliation). The fact that Feltmann later believed she was “polarized” from the department does not create a triable issue—such a belief is speculative and not supported by evidence of retaliatory intent.
