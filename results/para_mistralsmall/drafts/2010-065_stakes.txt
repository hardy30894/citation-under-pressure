**ARGUMENT**

**I. CAROL ANNE BOND HAS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO 18 U.S.C. § 229**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to § 229. This Court has long recognized that a criminal defendant may raise constitutional challenges to the statute under which she is prosecuted, including structural constitutional claims. *See United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 510–11 (1995) (defendant may raise Fifth Amendment due process challenge to statute); *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570, 580–81 (1968) (defendant may raise Sixth Amendment challenge to federal statute).

The Third Circuit relied on *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), for the proposition that private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims. *See* 668 F.3d at 200–01. However, *Tennessee Electric Power* did not address whether a criminal defendant may raise such a claim. The Court in *Tennessee Electric Power* rejected the plaintiffs’ Tenth Amendment challenge because the plaintiffs were not proper parties to assert the rights of the State of Tennessee. *Id.* at 144. The Court did not hold that a criminal defendant lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to a federal statute under which she is prosecuted. *See also United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284 (10th Cir. 2004) (distinguishing *Tennessee Electric Power* and recognizing that criminal defendants may raise Tenth Amendment challenges).

Moreover, this Court has repeatedly permitted criminal defendants to raise structural constitutional challenges to the statutes under which they are prosecuted. *See, e.g., United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 607–08 (2000) (permitting challenge to federal statute under Commerce Clause); *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 552–53 (1995) (same). There is no principled reason to distinguish Tenth Amendment challenges from other structural constitutional challenges in this context. *See Bond v. United States*, 564 U.S. 211, 221–22 (2011) (recognizing that Bond’s Tenth Amendment challenge is properly before the Court).

**II. 18 U.S.C. § 229 IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER UNDER THE NECESSARY AND PROPER CLAUSE**

A. The Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act of 1998, including § 229, is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to implement treaties under the Necessary and Proper Clause.

This Court has long recognized that Congress may enact legislation to implement valid treaties under the Necessary and Proper Clause. *See Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920) (“If the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.”); *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004) (recognizing the validity of treaty-implementing legislation under the Necessary and Proper Clause). The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a necessary and proper means of implementing the United States’ obligations under the Convention. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 223–24.

B. Section 229 does not violate the Tenth Amendment because it is a valid exercise of federal power under the Treaty Clause.

The Tenth Amendment provides that “[t]he powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.” U.S. Const. amend. X. However, the Treaty Clause grants the federal government plenary authority to enter into treaties, and the Necessary and Proper Clause authorizes Congress to enact legislation to implement those treaties. *See Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432–33. This Court has repeatedly recognized that the Treaty Clause is an independent source of federal power that is not subject to the same limitations as other enumerated powers. *See id.* at 432–33; *Lara*, 541 U.S. at 201.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144 (1992), and *Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898 (1997), is misplaced. Those cases addressed the scope of Congress’s power under the Commerce Clause and the Necessary and Proper Clause in the context of federal legislation that commandeered state governments. *See New York*, 505 U.S. at 177; *Printz*, 521 U.S. at 928. They do not limit Congress’s power to implement valid treaties under the Treaty Clause. *See Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432–33.

C. Section 229 does not violate the principle of dual sovereignty because it does not intrude on a traditional state function.

The Tenth Amendment protects the sovereignty of the States in areas traditionally reserved to them. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 224. However, the regulation of chemical weapons is not a traditional state function. The Chemical Weapons Convention was entered into to address a matter of national and international concern, and § 229 implements the United States’ obligations under that Convention. *See Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432–33. The regulation of chemical weapons is not an area traditionally reserved to the States, and § 229 does not intrude on state sovereignty. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 224–25.

**III. 18 U.S.C. § 229 IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE**

A statute is unconstitutionally vague if it “fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited” or if it “authorizes or encourages arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement.” *Hoffman Estates v. Flipside, Hoffman Estates, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 489, 498 (1982). Section 229 provides clear notice of the conduct it prohibits. It criminalizes the knowing possession or use of a “toxic chemical” or “chemical weapon” for non-peaceful purposes. 18 U.S.C. § 229(a)(1), (f)(1)(A). The statute defines these terms with specificity, and the prohibited conduct is readily understandable. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 225–26.

Bond’s argument that § 229 is unconstitutionally vague because it reaches a broad range of conduct is unavailing. The statute’s broad reach does not render it vague; rather, it reflects Congress’s intent to criminalize a wide range of harmful conduct involving chemical weapons. *See Hoffman Estates*, 455 U.S. at 498–99. The statute provides fair notice of the conduct it prohibits, and it does not authorize arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
