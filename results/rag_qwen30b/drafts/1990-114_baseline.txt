**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE EXEMPT AS “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL”

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. § 621 et seq., prohibits employment discrimination on the basis of age against individuals who are 40 years of age or older. However, Congress explicitly excluded certain categories of public officials from the Act’s coverage, including “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The petitioners—appointed state court judges under Missouri’s Non-Partisan Court Plan—are squarely within this exemption. Their role is not merely to apply settled law but to exercise the kind of judgment, discretion, and policy-making authority that defines the “policymaking level” under the ADEA.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that judicial decisionmaking involves policymaking. In *Western Air Lines, Inc. v. Criswell*, 472 U.S. 400, 408 (1985), the Court observed that “classifications based on age, like classifications based on sex or race, are subject to heightened scrutiny under the Constitution.” Yet the Court also acknowledged that Congress may permit age-based distinctions when they serve a legitimate governmental interest. The ADEA’s exception for “appointees on the policymaking level” reflects Congress’s understanding that judges, like other high-level officials, are entrusted with responsibilities that require the exercise of judgment and discretion. As the First Circuit held in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), “policymaking” is “indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” This is not a theoretical abstraction. Missouri judges, including the petitioners, exercise substantial policymaking authority in administering the state’s court system. They serve as chief justices, chief judges, and presiding judges with general supervisory powers over court operations, the adoption of procedural rules, and the resolution of internal judicial disputes. See *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985); *In re Rules of Circuit Court of Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 757 S.W.2d 590 (Mo. 1988). These responsibilities are not administrative minutiae; they are core functions of governance.

The petitioners argue that judges are not “policymakers” because their role is to “decide cases between litigants involving questions of law.” But this view is inconsistent with the reality of judicial work. As the Eighth Circuit correctly noted, “the reality of judging is that it is not always— and perhaps not even usually—merely a matter of mechanically applying settled, unambiguous principles of law.” The Court has repeatedly affirmed that judicial decisions often create new law. In *Cruzan v. Harmon*, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988), the Missouri Supreme Court confronted a question of profound ethical and public policy significance—whether a comatose patient could be deprived of artificial nutrition and hydration. The Court’s decision, which was later reviewed by the U.S. Supreme Court, had far-reaching implications for medical ethics and end-of-life care. Such decisions are not mere applications of existing rules; they are exercises of policymaking authority.

Moreover, the legislative history of the ADEA confirms that Congress intended the “policymaking level” exception to apply broadly. The Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers for the 1972 amendments stated that the exemption was meant to apply to “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments.” 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180. The term “agencies” includes the judiciary. As the Eighth Circuit observed, it is “highly unlikely that Congress intended” to exclude all appointed judges from the exemption. To read the exception narrowly would render it meaningless in the context of state courts, where judges are appointed and perform policymaking functions. The Court has long recognized that “in traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved.” *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). Here, Congress did not clearly intend to override state constitutional provisions governing judicial tenure. The ADEA’s exception for “appointees on the policymaking level” is not a mere technicality—it is a deliberate recognition that judges are not ordinary employees.

II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR STATE JUDGES SURVIVES EQUAL PROTECTION REVIEW

The petitioners also claim that Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. This claim fails because the provision is rationally related to a legitimate state interest.

The Supreme Court has consistently upheld mandatory retirement laws for public officials when they are based on rational grounds. In *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976), the Court held that “the Constitution does not require that a State’s classification be perfect, only that it be reasonable.” The Court has recognized that age-based classifications are permissible when they are “reasonably related to the achievement of a legitimate governmental objective.” *United Air Lines, Inc. v. McMann*, 434 U.S. 192, 200 (1977). Missouri’s mandatory retirement at age 70 is rationally related to several legitimate state interests.
