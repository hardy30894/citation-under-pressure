The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident corporate defendant whose in-state activities relate to the claims asserted. Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352, 356 (1927) (service on nonresident motorists upheld because “the process of a court of one State cannot run into another” absent adequate contacts). Ford’s extensive Montana operations—advertising, selling vehicles and replacement parts, and maintaining dealerships—supply the requisite connection. Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437, 445 (1952) (“The amount and kind of activities which must be carried on by the foreign corporation in the state of the forum, so as to make it reasonable and just to subject the corporation to the jurisdiction of that state are to be determined in each case.”).

These contacts satisfy the traditional test that jurisdiction may rest on activities that “make it necessary for it to secure a license and to designate a statutory agent upon whom process may be served.” Id. at 445. Montana’s long-arm statute, applied here, mirrors statutes sustained in Wuchter v. Pizzutti, 276 U.S. 13, 15-16 (1928), and Hess, 274 U.S. at 354, which conditioned the privilege of using state highways on consent to service. Ford availed itself of the same privilege by systematically placing its products in Montana commerce.

The requirement that the claim “arise out of or relate to” those contacts is met when the defendant’s general course of dealing in the forum creates the risk that materialized. Metzger Motor Car Co. v. Parrott, 233 U.S. 36, 37 (1914) (owner liability for negligent operation of vehicle within the state upheld as consistent with due process). Olberding v. Illinois Central Railroad, 346 U.S. 338, 341 (1953), likewise recognized that a nonresident causing injury through use of the forum’s roads may be required to answer there. Ford’s Montana sales and service network bear the same relation to a product-liability claim arising from a vehicle it manufactured and placed in national commerce.

No stricter causation requirement is compelled. Clark v. Wells, 203 U.S. 164, 172 (1906), permitted jurisdiction over attached property even after removal, emphasizing that the forum’s prior lawful seizure preserved authority. Western Loan & Savings Co. v. Butte & Boston Consolidated Mining Co., 210 U.S. 368, 372 (1908), confirmed that a defendant’s voluntary submission through litigation conduct waives objections. Provident Savings Life Assurance Society v. Ford, 114 U.S. 635, 640 (1885), and Minnesota Commercial Men’s Assn. v. Benn, 261 U.S. 140, 145 (1923), likewise upheld service on out-of-state insurers that had designated agents or conducted business within the forum.

Wells Fargo & Co. Express v. Ford, 238 U.S. 503, 508 (1915), further illustrates that a defendant’s failure to give notice of proceedings affecting its interests in the forum can itself support liability. Collectively these authorities establish that Ford’s deliberate, continuous Montana presence renders the exercise of jurisdiction neither arbitrary nor fundamentally unfair. The Montana Supreme Court’s judgment should be affirmed.
