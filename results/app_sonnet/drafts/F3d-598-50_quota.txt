# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING TESTIMONY FROM TWO ASSISTANT DISTRICT ATTORNEYS AND A POLICE LIEUTENANT THAT VOUCHED FOR OFFICERS RAMOS AND RIVERA, OFFERED LEGAL CONCLUSIONS ON PROBABLE CAUSE, AND USURPED THE JURY'S FACT-FINDING FUNCTION.

This Court has repeatedly held that a witness—lay or expert—may not offer an opinion on the credibility of another witness. *See Nimely v. City of New York*, 414 F.3d 381, 398 (2d Cir. 2005) (excluding expert testimony that "amounted to nothing more than advice to the jury on how to decide the case," including opinions bearing on witness truthfulness). Determinations of credibility are "within the sole province of the jury," and testimony that trespasses on that domain is inadmissible regardless of the witness's title or expertise. *Id.* Here, the ADAs and the police lieutenant were permitted to tell the jury, in substance, that they believed Ramos and Rivera. That testimony did precisely what *Nimely* forbids.

The same witnesses were also permitted to testify, in effect, that probable cause existed for Cameron's and Higgenbottom's arrests and for Cameron's prosecution. This Court has long held that testimony phrased as a legal conclusion is inadmissible because it "invade[s] the province of the court to determine the applicable law and to instruct the jury as to that law." *United States v. Duncan*, 42 F.3d 97, 101 (2d Cir. 1994) (internal quotation marks omitted). Probable cause is precisely such a legal conclusion, entrusted to the jury under instructions from the court—not to be supplied by a government witness's ipse dixit. *See United States v. Scop*, 846 F.2d 135, 139–40 (2d Cir. 1988) (holding that testimony phrased in terms of legal conclusions is properly excluded because it "invades the province of the court to instruct on the law"). Allowing the ADAs to testify that probable cause existed did exactly what *Scop* and *Duncan* forbid: it substituted a witness's legal judgment for the jury's factual one.

Finally, the witnesses were permitted to opine on whether particular pieces of evidence strengthened or weakened Appellants' case—commentary that did not require any specialized expertise and that jurors were fully equipped to perform themselves. This Court has held that such testimony is improper where the jury is equally capable of drawing the relevant inferences from the evidence. *See Hygh v. Jacobs*, 961 F.2d 359, 364 (2d Cir. 1992) (excluding opinion testimony where the "opinion is not helpful" because it addresses matters within the jury's own competence, and instead risks that the jury will "surrender its own common sense" to the witness). Likewise, in *United States v. Rea*, 958 F.2d 1206, 1216 (2d Cir. 1992), this Court cautioned against opinion testimony that effectively tells the jury which side to believe. The testimony here—delivered by ostensibly neutral government officials—was even more dangerous than ordinary vouching, because jurors could reasonably assume that ADAs and police supervisors possess special insight into whether Ramos and Rivera told the truth.

## II. THE ERRONEOUS ADMISSION OF THIS TESTIMONY WAS NOT HARMLESS.

An evidentiary error requires a new trial unless the appellate court finds it "harmless"—that is, that the error did not affect the outcome. *See Perry v. Ethan Allen, Inc.*, 115 F.3d 143, 150–51 (2d Cir. 1997) (reversing where erroneously admitted evidence went to the heart of a contested issue). This case turned entirely on two questions: whether Ramos and Rivera were credible, and whether they had probable cause. The erroneously admitted testimony spoke directly—and repeatedly—to both. Given the closeness of the case and the centrality of the tainted testimony, the error cannot be deemed harmless. *Id.*

## III. APPELLANTS WERE NOT ENTITLED TO JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW BASED ON THE SECURITY PHOTOGRAPHS, BUT THE REMAINING INSTRUCTIONAL ERRORS REQUIRE CORRECTION ON REMAND.

Appellants recognize that judgment as a matter of law is warranted only where the record so "blatantly contradict[s]" one party's account "that no reasonable jury could believe it." *Scott v. Harris*, 550 U.S. 372, 380 (2007). Because the photographs here do not foreclose Appellees' version of events, and because a court must "draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party," *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150 (2000), the District Court did not err in declining to grant judgment as a matter of law on this record.

The jury instructions, however, require correction on remand. New York law defines the crime of obstructing governmental administration to require that the officer being obstructed was engaged in an authorized, i.e., lawful, function. *See People v. Lupinacci*, 191 A.D.2d 589, 590 (2d Dep't 1993). The District Court's failure to so instruct the jury regarding Higgenbottom's arrest was error. Likewise, because Appellants' version of events—if credited—would permit a finding that Ramos and Rivera acted with "evil motive or intent" or "reckless or callous indifference" to Appellants' rights, Appellants were entitled to a punitive damages instruction. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial consistent with the evidentiary and instructional principles set forth above.
