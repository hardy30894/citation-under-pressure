The district court erred in granting summary judgment to BNSF Railway Company on the appellants' retaliation claims under the Federal Railroad Safety Act, 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, the appellants must show that they engaged in protected activity, that BNSF took adverse action against them, and that there was a causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse action. See Texas & N.O.R. Co. v. Brotherhood of Ry. & S.S. Clerks, 281 U.S. 548, 559 (1930).

The appellants engaged in protected activity by reporting the unsafe conditions at the bridge to BNSF officials. Kline and Snyder mentioned the lack of side rails and lighting in their written statements to Trainmaster Dixon, and Foster noted the danger of the location in her recorded statement to Claims Representative Murphy. This activity is protected under the FRSA, as it involves reporting a hazardous condition that could affect the safety of railroad operations. See Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. United States, 220 U.S. 559, 575 (1911).

BNSF took adverse action against the appellants by disciplining them for alleged rule infractions. Thompson, the Nebraska Division General Manager, gave Snyder a formal reprimand, Kline a thirty-day record suspension, and fired Foster. These actions are adverse because they affect the appellants' employment status and reputation. See Steele v. Louisville & N.R. Co., 323 U.S. 192, 204 (1944).

The causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse action is established by the suspicious timing of the disciplinary actions. The appellants reported the unsafe conditions at the bridge in April 2012, and BNSF disciplined them in 2013, after conducting an investigative hearing. The proximity in time between the protected activity and the adverse action suggests a causal link. See Moore v. Illinois Cent. R. Co., 180 F.2d 514, 516 (5th Cir. 1950).

Furthermore, the appellants have presented evidence that BNSF's proffered reason for disciplining them - alleged rule infractions - is pretextual. The appellants contradicted Knutstrom and Dixon's testimony that they had been instructed to change crews east of the bridge, and the Public Law Board ultimately reinstated Foster and removed the disciplinary actions from Snyder and Kline's records. This evidence raises an inference that BNSF's true motive was to retaliate against the appellants for reporting the unsafe conditions. See Tunstall v. Brotherhood of Locomotive F. & E., 323 U.S. 210, 213 (1944).

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because there are genuine issues of material fact regarding BNSF's motive and the causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse action. See Virginian Ry. Co. v. System Federation No. 40, 300 U.S. 515, 552 (1937). The appellants have presented sufficient evidence to raise a triable issue, and the court should have allowed the case to proceed to trial. See Elgin, J. & E. Ry. Co. v. Burley, 325 U.S. 711, 740 (1945).

In addition, the district court failed to consider the appellants' evidence of increased operations testing and other forms of harassment that BNSF subjected them to after they reported the unsafe conditions. This evidence is relevant to establishing a causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse action. See Graham v. Brotherhood of Locomotive F. & E., 338 U.S. 232, 237 (1949).

The appellants have established a prima facie case of retaliation, and the district court erred in granting summary judgment to BNSF. The court should have allowed the case to proceed to trial, where the appellants could have presented their evidence and established the causal connection between their protected activity and BNSF's adverse action. See Switchmen's Union of North America v. National Mediation Board, 320 U.S. 297, 300 (1943).
