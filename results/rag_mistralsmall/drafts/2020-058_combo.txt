**ARGUMENT**

**I. The text of § 1255(a) does not require a prior “admission” where Congress has expressly provided that a grant of TPS places the alien in “lawful status as a nonimmigrant.”**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that “[f]or purposes of adjustment of status under section 1255 . . . an alien who has been granted [TPS] shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) (emphasis added). Section 1255(a) permits adjustment of status for “[t]he status of an alien who was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). The plain text of § 1254a(f)(4) does not require a separate “admission” where Congress has already deemed TPS recipients to be in lawful nonimmigrant status. This construction is consistent with the canon that “[w]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.” *I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987). The Third Circuit’s contrary reading ignores the express statutory command of § 1254a(f)(4) and elevates a definitional term—“admission”—over the substantive grant of status Congress provided.

**II. The INA’s definition of “admission” does not foreclose treating TPS as an admission for purposes of § 1255.**

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition does not preclude Congress from deeming a grant of TPS to constitute an admission for purposes of § 1255. In *Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185, 188 (1958), the Supreme Court recognized that parole does not alter an alien’s status as an excluded alien, yet Congress may provide otherwise by statute. Here, Congress has done precisely that: it has provided that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for adjustment-of-status purposes. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This statutory fiction is consistent with the Court’s recognition that Congress may alter the legal consequences of an alien’s presence through specific statutory provisions. *See id.* at 188–89.

**III. The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), is misplaced because *Hanif* addressed a different statutory scheme and did not involve TPS.**

In *Hanif*, the Third Circuit held that “admission” in § 1255(b) must be interpreted in accordance with § 1101(a)(13)(A). 694 F.3d at 485. However, *Hanif* did not involve TPS, nor did it address the effect of § 1254a(f)(4). The Court’s reasoning in *Hanif* was limited to the specific context of adjustment of status for certain criminal aliens, and it did not consider the statutory fiction created by § 1254a(f)(4). *See id.* at 485–86. The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Hanif* in this case is therefore unpersuasive.

**IV. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that Congress may provide for adjustment of status without requiring a prior admission where the alien is otherwise in lawful status.**

In *United States ex rel. Stapf v. Corsi*, 287 U.S. 129, 138 (1932), the Supreme Court recognized that Congress may provide for the adjustment of status of aliens who are otherwise in lawful status, even if they were not initially admitted. The Court held that Congress’s power to regulate immigration is plenary, and it may provide for adjustment of status where the alien is otherwise eligible. *Id.* at 138–39. Similarly, in *Cheung Sum Shee v. Nagle*, 268 U.S. 336, 342 (1925), the Court recognized that Congress may provide for the admission of aliens who are otherwise ineligible, where the alien is otherwise in lawful status. These cases support the conclusion that Congress may provide for adjustment of status under § 1255 where the alien is in lawful status under § 1254a(f)(4), even if the alien was not initially admitted.

**V. The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281 (3d Cir. 2013), is misplaced because *Taveras* addressed a different statutory scheme and did not involve TPS.**

In *Taveras*, the Third Circuit held that “admission” and “status” are distinct concepts, and that adjustment of status does not constitute an admission. 731 F.3d at 290. However, *Taveras* did not involve TPS, nor did it address the effect of § 1254a(f)(4). The Court’s reasoning in *Taveras* was limited to the specific context of adjustment of status in removal proceedings, and it did not consider the statutory fiction created by § 1254a(f)(4). *See id.* at 289–90. The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Taveras* in this case is therefore unpersuasive.

**VI. The Supreme Court has recognized that Congress may provide for the adjustment of status of aliens who are otherwise in lawful status, even if they were not initially admitted.**

In *Ceballos v. Shaughnessy*, 352 U.S. 599, 600 (1957), the Supreme Court recognized that Congress may provide for the adjustment of status of aliens who are otherwise in lawful status, even if they were not initially admitted. The Court held that the Attorney General’s discretion to suspend deportation under § 19(c) of the Immigration Act of 1917 was not limited by the alien’s initial entry. *Id.* at 600–01. Similarly, in *Rogers v. Quan*, 357 U.S. 193, 194 (1958), the Supreme Court recognized that Congress may provide for the adjustment of status of aliens who are otherwise in lawful status, even if they were not initially admitted. These cases support the conclusion that Congress may provide for adjustment of status under § 1255 where the alien is in lawful status under § 1254a(f)(4), even if the alien was not initially admitted.
