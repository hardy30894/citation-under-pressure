**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NLRB’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS RATIONAL, CONSISTENT WITH THE ACT, AND PROPERLY DEFERRED TO UNDER ADMINISTRATIVE LAW.

The National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) establishes a strong presumption in favor of the incumbent union’s continued majority status. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41, 107 S.Ct. 2225, 2232–35, 96 L.Ed.2d 22 (1987). This presumption is not absolute, but it is rebuttable only upon the employer’s production of “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status.” *Id.* at 41 n.8, 107 S.Ct. at 2235 n.8. The NLRB’s rule—permitting an employer to conduct a poll only when it has such objective indications—does not render polling “useless,” as the dissent suggests, but rather ensures that polling serves its proper function: not as a substitute for the Board’s election process, but as a *supplemental* tool for an employer who already has a good-faith basis to doubt the union’s support.

The dissent’s claim that the Board’s standard is “anomalous” because it makes polling less accessible than an RM election is based on a false premise. The Board has consistently emphasized that RM elections are the preferred method for determining employee sentiment. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989). The Board’s decision to allow polling only when the employer has already met the evidentiary threshold for withdrawal of recognition is not arbitrary—it reflects a judgment that polling, while potentially useful, is inherently more susceptible to manipulation and coercion than a Board-supervised election. *Id.* at 1061–62. The Board’s concern is not with the employer’s interest in avoiding bargaining with a minority union—*that risk is nonexistent*—but with preserving the stability of collective bargaining relationships and preventing employer-driven efforts to undermine union representation. *Id.* at 1062.

The dissent’s proposed “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support” standard—lower than the standard for withdrawal of recognition—would create a dangerous loophole. It would allow employers to conduct unsupervised polls based on minimal or equivocal evidence, thereby circumventing the procedural safeguards of an RM election. The Board’s choice to maintain a higher standard for polling is not irrational; it is a reasonable accommodation of competing interests. As this Court has held, “the Board, not the courts, has the primary responsibility for developing and applying national labor policy.” *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786, 110 S.Ct. 1542, 1549, 108 L.Ed.2d 801 (1990). The Board’s decision to require “objective indications” sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt is a rational exercise of its expertise and is entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 844, 104 S.Ct. 2778, 2782, 81 L.Ed.2d 694 (1984).

II. THE NLRB’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT IS SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

The Board’s determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status is supported by substantial evidence in the record. The Board correctly found that only seven of the 32 employees in the bargaining unit made statements repudiating the union, and that these statements were insufficient to raise a reasonable doubt. *See* *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir.1984) (employer must have “objective indications” of loss of majority support).

The dissent’s reliance on statements by employees such as Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh is misplaced. Ridgick’s comment—that he would support the company “as long as it treated them right”—was “at best equivocal,” and the ALJ properly found it insufficient to demonstrate opposition to union representation. *See* *Destileria Serralles, Inc.*, 289 N.L.R.B. 51, 1988 WL 214114, at *3 (1988), enforced, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir.1989) (dissatisfaction with union services does not equate to opposition to representation). Similarly, Marsh’s complaint about paying dues without receiving value was not an expression of desire to decertify the union, but a grievance about union performance—precisely the kind of statement the Board has long held insufficient to justify polling. *Id.* at *4*.

The dissent also mischaracterizes the statements of Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr. Bloch’s report that “the entire night shift” did not want the union was uncorroborated and unverified. The ALJ correctly rejected it as hearsay and lacking in probative value. *See* *Westbrook Bowl*, 293 N.L.R.B. 1000, 1002 (1989) (employer may not rely on unverified reports of employee sentiment). Mohr’s statement—made to a manager during a casual conversation—was “almost off-the-cuff” and lacked any basis in fact. The ALJ properly discounted it as an “unverified assertion.” *See* *Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB*, 47 F.3d 332, 335–36 (9th Cir.1995) (employee’s dissatisfaction with union does not constitute objective evidence of loss of majority support).
