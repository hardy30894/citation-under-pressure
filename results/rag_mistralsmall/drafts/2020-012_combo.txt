**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is a final, reviewable decision under § 355(f) of the Railroad Retirement Act.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is not controlling. That case addressed only whether a merits denial of benefits could be reviewed, not whether a refusal to reopen a prior claim is reviewable. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a refusal to reopen is itself a final, reviewable decision when it definitively resolves a claimant’s rights. *SEC v. Louisiana Pub. Serv. Comm’n*, 353 U.S. 368, 371 (1957) (“[A]n order denying a petition to reopen is a final, reviewable order.”). The Court has repeatedly treated refusals to reopen as final decisions subject to judicial review, even when issued in the course of ongoing administrative proceedings. *Cheng Fan Kwok v. Immigration & Naturalization Serv.*, 392 U.S. 206, 212 (1968) (holding that a denial of a motion to reopen is reviewable as a final order under the applicable statute).

The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is no different. It is not a mere interlocutory ruling; it is a definitive determination that Salinas’s prior claim remains closed and cannot be revived. As such, it is a final decision under § 355(f). *Pennsylvania R.R. v. Day*, 360 U.S. 548, 551 (1959) (holding that a refusal to reopen a prior proceeding is a final order when it conclusively determines the rights of the parties).

II. The Fifth Circuit’s rule conflicts with the text and structure of the Railroad Retirement Act.

Section 355(f) grants the courts of appeals jurisdiction over “any order” of the Board that meets the statutory criteria. The Fifth Circuit’s rule—limiting review to only those orders that grant or deny benefits on the merits—reads the word “any” out of the statute. This is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s instruction that statutes conferring jurisdiction must be construed broadly to ensure meaningful review. *Railroad Retirement Bd. v. Alton R.R.*, 295 U.S. 330, 346 (1935) (holding that the Railroad Retirement Act must be construed to ensure judicial review of Board decisions).

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit’s rule creates an arbitrary distinction between a refusal to reopen and a merits denial, even though both determinations have the same practical effect: foreclosing a claimant’s entitlement to benefits. The Supreme Court has rejected such formalistic distinctions in other contexts, emphasizing that the substance of the agency’s action—not its label—determines whether it is final and reviewable. *Wragg v. Federal Land Bank of New Orleans*, 317 U.S. 325, 331 (1943) (holding that the substance of an agency’s action, not its form, determines finality).

III. The Fifth Circuit’s rule is inconsistent with the approach of other circuits and undermines uniformity in Railroad Retirement Act jurisprudence.

The Fifth Circuit’s rule conflicts with the D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), which held that refusals to reopen are reviewable under § 355(f). The Supreme Court has long favored uniformity in the interpretation of federal statutes, particularly where, as here, the statute governs a specialized area of administrative law. *International Union of Mine, Mill & Smelter Workers v. Eagle-Picher Mining & Smelting Co.*, 325 U.S. 335, 342 (1945) (emphasizing the importance of uniformity in the interpretation of federal labor statutes).

The Fifth Circuit’s rule also conflicts with the approach of the Second and Eighth Circuits, which have held that refusals to reopen are reviewable under analogous statutes. *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Bd.*, 370 F.2d 253, 254 (2d Cir. 1966); *Sones v. Railroad Retirement Bd.*, 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991). The Supreme Court has recognized that such uniformity is essential to the fair and efficient administration of federal benefit programs. *Dodge v. Board of Ed. of Chicago*, 302 U.S. 74, 79 (1937) (holding that uniformity in the interpretation of federal statutes is essential to their fair administration).

IV. The Fifth Circuit’s rule frustrates the purpose of the Railroad Retirement Act by insulating arbitrary or erroneous refusals to reopen from judicial review.

The Railroad Retirement Act was enacted to provide a comprehensive system of benefits for railroad workers and their families. *Railroad Retirement Bd. v. Alton R.R.*, 295 U.S. at 346. The Fifth Circuit’s rule undermines this purpose by allowing the Board to foreclose review of refusals to reopen, even when those refusals are arbitrary or contrary to law. This is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s instruction that statutes conferring benefits must be construed to ensure meaningful review. *Dismuke v. United States*, 297 U.S. 167, 172 (1936) (holding that statutes conferring benefits must be construed to ensure meaningful review).

The Fifth Circuit’s rule also conflicts with the Supreme Court’s instruction that agency refusals to reopen must be subject to judicial review when they have the practical effect of foreclosing a claimant’s rights. *Robertson v. Chambers*, 341 U.S. 37, 41 (1951) (holding that agency refusals to reopen must be subject to judicial review when they have the practical effect of foreclosing a claimant’s rights).

**CONCLUSION**

The Fifth Circuit’s rule is inconsistent with the text and structure of the Railroad Retirement Act, conflicts with the approach of other circuits, and frustrates the purpose of the Act by insulating arbitrary or erroneous refusals to reopen from judicial review. This Court should reverse the judgment below and hold that the Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is a final, reviewable decision under § 355(f).
