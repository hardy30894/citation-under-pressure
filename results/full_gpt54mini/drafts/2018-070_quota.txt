The OCSLA permits adjacent state law to become federal law on the Outer Continental Shelf only when that law is “applicable” and “not inconsistent with” other federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That text, read in context and in light of this Court’s OCSLA precedents, establishes a gap-filling regime—not a free-standing invitation to borrow any state rule that happens to regulate the same subject matter. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with the statute or with this Court’s decisions.

OCSLA was enacted to create “a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf.” Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). Congress understood that federal law alone would not always supply a complete code for activities on the Shelf; it therefore directed courts to borrow adjacent state law only “to the extent” it is “applicable” and not inconsistent with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That structure matters. As this Court explained in Rodrigue, when federal law does not itself furnish the governing rule, state law is incorporated as surrogate federal law to fill the void. 395 U.S. at 355, 366. The point is not to create a parallel state-law regime, but to supply federal law where federal law is absent.

The Court reinforced that understanding in Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473 (1981). There, the Court described OCSLA as making “the law of the adjacent State applicable as surrogate federal law” only “to the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with other federal law.” Id. at 479, 481. The Court did not suggest that state law independently applies whenever it addresses the same subject. Rather, the borrowing is conditional and subsidiary to federal law.

That conditional framework is exactly how the Fifth Circuit has long read the statute. In Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), the court explained that incorporation of state law is appropriate where “a significant void or gap” exists in federal law. Id. at 1036. And in Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043 (5th Cir. 1990), the court held that state law applies only if it is “applicable” and “not inconsistent” with federal law, and only where federal law does not otherwise provide the rule of decision. Id. at 1047. Later Fifth Circuit cases have continued to treat OCSLA as a gap-filling provision. See, e.g., E. P. Operating, Ltd. P’ship v. Placid Oil Co., 26 F.3d 563, 569 (5th Cir. 1994); In re Deepwater Horizon, 745 F.3d 157, 163-64 (5th Cir. 2014).

Newton’s position, adopted by the Ninth Circuit, collapses OCSLA’s distinct “applicable” and “not inconsistent” requirements into a single preemption inquiry. That is not what the statute says. If any state law governing a subject matter could be borrowed whenever it is not affirmatively preempted, then “applicable” would do no work, and OCSLA would become a general incorporation statute for any state regulation touching Shelf activity. This Court has rejected similarly expansive readings in analogous federal enclave contexts. In James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94 (1940), the Court held that state law applies on federal enclaves only as assimilated federal law, not as autonomous state law. Id. at 99-100. In Howard v. Commissioners of the Sinking Fund of Louisville, 344 U.S. 624 (1953), the Court likewise treated borrowed state law as filling the federal gap. Id. at 626-27. OCSLA follows that same model.

Nor does the FLSA’s savings clause alter the analysis. The FLSA preserves more protective state wage laws in their ordinary territorial sphere, but that says nothing about whether those laws are “applicable” under OCSLA on fixed offshore structures. See 29 U.S.C. § 218(a); see also Wyeth v. Levine, 555 U.S. 555, 576-77 (2009) (preemption analysis turns on the relevant federal statutory scheme). The question here is not ordinary preemption within a state’s borders. It is whether Congress, through OCSLA, made adjacent state law part of federal law on the Shelf. Under Rodrigue and Gulf Offshore, it did so only to fill gaps in federal law. 395 U.S. at 355, 366; 453 U.S. at 479, 481.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach also conflicts with this Court’s recognition that OCSLA extends federal law horizontally to fixed structures on the Shelf. See Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414, 425 (1985); Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 218-19 (1986). Those cases emphasize that the Shelf is treated like an area of exclusive federal jurisdiction and that state law enters only through the statute’s incorporation mechanism. Id. That mechanism is not satisfied merely because a state wage law speaks to hours and meal periods. It is satisfied only when federal law leaves a genuine gap.

Because the FLSA supplies the governing federal rules for wage and hour claims, there is no gap for California law to fill here. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
