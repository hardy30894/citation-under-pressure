**Argument**

*Johnson v. United States* announced a substantive rule that applies retroactively on collateral review. Under long-settled retroactivity principles, a decision that narrows the scope of a criminal punishment applies to cases already final because it alters “the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in judgments in part and dissenting in part). That is what *Johnson* did: it held that ACCA’s residual clause is void for vagueness, so the government may no longer use that clause to impose the Act’s fifteen-year mandatory minimum. A rule that removes a category of offenses from a sentencing statute’s reach is substantive, not procedural. See *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971).

The Court has long recognized that an unconstitutional criminal law cannot support punishment. In *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), the Court explained that a conviction under an unconstitutional statute is void. In *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874), the Court held that a court exceeds its lawful authority when it imposes punishment not authorized by law. *Johnson* rests on the same foundational principle: if ACCA’s residual clause is unconstitutional, then a sentence enhanced solely by that clause is unauthorized. That is a substantive defect, and substantive defects apply retroactively. See *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880); *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874).

The government may contend that *Johnson* merely altered the way courts identify ACCA predicates. But that characterization misstates the decision. *Johnson* did not prescribe a new sentencing procedure; it invalidated one of the statute’s operative definitions. Before *Johnson*, a prior conviction could qualify under the residual clause if it “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” After *Johnson*, it cannot. That change narrows ACCA’s scope and “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971). The Court’s older retroactivity cases likewise turn on whether a decision changes the substantive consequences of past conduct, not merely the mechanics of adjudication. See *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965); *Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. 719 (1966); *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967); *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244 (1969).

Retroactive application is especially appropriate here because Welch’s ACCA sentence was enhanced by a clause the Supreme Court has now declared void for vagueness. Due process forbids punishment under a law so indeterminate that it fails to give fair notice and invites arbitrary enforcement. A sentence resting on that kind of invalid definition cannot be insulated from collateral review simply because it became final before *Johnson* was decided. See *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880); *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874).

The point is straightforward: if *Johnson* means what it says, then prisoners sentenced under the residual clause were punished under an unconstitutional provision. A rule that takes away the government’s power to impose a particular punishment is substantive, and substantive rules apply retroactively. See *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971). Welch therefore is entitled to collateral relief.

Accordingly, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for resentencing without the ACCA enhancement.
