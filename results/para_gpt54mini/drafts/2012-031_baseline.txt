## Argument

### I. The District Court properly required admissible proof that damages could be measured on a classwide basis.

Rule 23 does not permit certification on speculation. A district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” and resolve factual disputes relevant to certification by a preponderance of the evidence. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 351-52 (2011); *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 320 (3d Cir. 2008). The Supreme Court has likewise held that Rule 23 “does not set forth a mere pleading standard.” *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 564 U.S. at 350. That principle is fully consistent with the Third Circuit’s instruction that class certification must be supported by actual proof, not allegations. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 321.

Comcast asks for a rule that would require plaintiffs to prove the merits of their damages case at certification, but that is not the law. The relevant question is whether the evidence showed a common method of proof capable of measuring damages for the class. Rule 23(b)(3) asks whether common questions “predominate,” and damages questions may be addressed classwide when they flow from a common theory of injury. *Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 623-24 (1997); *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 34-35 (2013). The district court found, after extensive expert testimony and evidentiary hearings, that plaintiffs had shown a classwide methodology through Dr. McClave’s analysis. That finding was not clearly erroneous. See *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573-74 (1985).

### II. A class may be certified where common proof can establish both antitrust impact and damages.

In antitrust class actions, courts routinely recognize that impact and damages may be proven with common evidence where the defendant’s conduct is uniform across the class. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 311-12; *In re Flat Glass Antitrust Litig.*, 385 F.3d 350, 366-67 (3d Cir. 2004). The Third Circuit has emphasized that Rule 23(b)(3) does not require the district court to decide whether plaintiffs will ultimately prevail; it requires a showing that the elements are capable of proof by common evidence. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 311, 320-21.

The district court found exactly that here. Comcast’s clustering strategy allegedly affected the entire Philadelphia market in the same way: by reducing competition, deterring overbuilders, and enabling supra-competitive pricing across the class period. Where plaintiffs’ theory of injury is marketwide, a classwide model of damages is appropriate if it measures the incremental harm from the challenged conduct. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 323-24; *Newton v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc.*, 259 F.3d 154, 189-90 (3d Cir. 2001). The district court did not certify on the basis of conjecture; it relied on expert analysis tying the claimed overcharge to the same anticompetitive conduct alleged to have injured all class members.

### III. Comcast’s attack on the damages model goes to merits and weight, not certification.

Comcast’s principal complaint is that plaintiffs’ damages model purportedly did not isolate only the antitrust injury the district court accepted. But that is a merits argument dressed as a certification objection. The class-certification inquiry may overlap with the merits, yet the court need only decide whether Rule 23’s requirements are met, not whether plaintiffs will ultimately establish damages at trial. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 564 U.S. at 351; *In re Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 317-18. The district court properly evaluated expert evidence and concluded that a common methodology existed; appellate courts may not displace that assessment simply because the defendant disputes the model’s assumptions. *Anderson*, 470 U.S. at 573-74.

Nor does *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27 (2013), require reversal. There, the Court rejected a model that measured damages for theories the district court had excluded. *Id.* at 36-38. Here, by contrast, the district court limited the class’s impact theory to the clustering-to-deter-overbuilders theory and found that the damages model matched that theory. That is the critical distinction. A model that tracks the certified theory of injury may support predominance. *Id.* at 35-36.

### IV. The Court of Appeals should affirm because the district court applied the correct legal standard and supported certification with record evidence.

Rule 23 entrusts district courts with careful gatekeeping, not categorical hostility to class treatment. *Amchem Prods.*, 521 U.S. at 613-14; *In re Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318. The district court here held a multi-day evidentiary hearing, heard experts, and made detailed findings that common evidence could establish both impact and damages. That is precisely the kind of record-based determination Rule 23 requires. Because Comcast identifies no legal error in the district court’s standard and no clear error in its factual findings, certification should be affirmed. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 320-21; *Anderson*, 470 U.S. at 573-74.
