**ARGUMENT**

I. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE RECOGNIZES A "CLASS OF ONE" CLAIM WHERE STATE ACTION IS MOTIVATED BY ILL WILL OR ARBITRARINESS TOWARD A PARTICULAR INDIVIDUAL.

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a state from denying any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws. U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has long recognized that the Clause targets arbitrary and invidious classifications, *see, e.g., Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620, 631 (1996), but it also reaches instances where government action is not based on any classification at all, but instead is driven by animus or ill will toward a particular individual. *See Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000) (per curiam) (recognizing that a plaintiff may bring an equal protection claim as a "class of one" where the plaintiff alleges that state action was taken "for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate governmental purpose").

This Court’s decision in *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), first articulated the principle that the Equal Protection Clause forbids arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement of laws, even absent a suspect classification. In *Yick Wo*, the Court struck down a facially neutral ordinance regulating laundry businesses because it was applied in a discriminatory manner against Chinese laundry operators. *Id.* at 373-74. The Court held that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits "a denial of equal justice" where "the law is applied and administered by public authority with an evil eye and an unequal hand." *Id.* at 373.

Subsequent decisions have reaffirmed this principle. In *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center*, 473 U.S. 432 (1985), the Court invalidated a zoning ordinance that required a special permit for a group home for the mentally disabled, while similar facilities were permitted without such a requirement. *Id.* at 440-41. The Court explained that the ordinance violated the Equal Protection Clause because it was "based on an irrational prejudice" against the mentally disabled. *Id.* at 450.

Most directly on point, this Court’s decision in *Olech* recognized that a plaintiff may bring an equal protection claim as a "class of one" where the plaintiff alleges that state action was taken "for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate governmental purpose." 528 U.S. at 564. In *Olech*, the plaintiff alleged that a village conditioned her water connection on an easement that was not required of similarly situated property owners, and that the village acted out of spite. *Id.* at 563. The Court held that such a claim states a cognizable equal protection claim. *Id.* at 565.

The Village’s reliance on *Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252 (1977), is misplaced. In *Arlington Heights*, the Court held that a plaintiff must prove that the official action was motivated by a discriminatory purpose to state an equal protection claim. *Id.* at 265. However, *Arlington Heights* addressed a claim brought by a group seeking to challenge a facially neutral zoning ordinance, not a "class of one" claim where the plaintiff alleges that the government acted out of animus toward a particular individual. *See id.* at 264-65. The Court in *Olech* distinguished *Arlington Heights* on precisely this ground, noting that *Arlington Heights* did not involve a "class of one" claim. 528 U.S. at 564.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING PETITIONER’S COMPLAINT BECAUSE IT ALLEGED THAT THE VILLAGE’S ACTION WAS MOTIVATED BY ILL WILL AND WAS ARBITRARY.

Petitioner’s complaint alleged that the Village conditioned her water connection on an easement that was not required of similarly situated property owners, and that the Village acted out of spite. The District Court dismissed the complaint for failure to state a cognizable claim under the Equal Protection Clause. However, as this Court recognized in *Olech*, such a claim states a cognizable equal protection claim. 528 U.S. at 564.

The Village’s reliance on *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*, 553 U.S. 591 (2008), is misplaced. In *Engquist*, the Court held that the "class of one" theory does not apply in the public employment context where the plaintiff alleges that she was treated differently from other employees. *Id.* at 604. The Court explained that the "class of one" theory is inapplicable in such cases because "the government does not act with the kind of arbitrary and irrational animus that the Equal Protection Clause targets when it treats one employee differently from others." *Id.* at 603. However, *Engquist* did not address the application of the "class of one" theory in a case like this one, where the plaintiff alleges that the government acted out of animus toward a particular individual. *See id.* at 598-99.

III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The Equal Protection Clause recognizes a "class of one" claim where state action is motivated by ill will or arbitrariness toward a particular individual. Petitioner’s complaint alleged that the Village’s action was motivated by ill will and was arbitrary, and thus stated a cognizable equal protection claim. The District Court erred in dismissing the complaint.
