# ARGUMENT

The court of appeals held that the Coast Guard's latent authority over uninspected vessels—authority never actually exercised over the occupational hazards of oil and gas drilling—suffices to strip OSHA of jurisdiction under Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act. That holding cannot be reconciled with the statute's text, this Court's preemption precedents, or the settled principle that remedial legislation should not be read to leave the very hazards it targets unregulated by any agency. This Court should reverse.

## I. Section 4(b)(1) Displaces OSHA Only Where Another Agency Has Actually Exercised Authority Over the Specific Working Conditions at Issue

Section 4(b)(1) exempts from OSHA coverage only "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies … exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards." Congress's choice of the active verb "exercise" demands more than the latent existence of regulatory power; it requires that the power actually have been brought to bear on the working conditions at issue. This Court has consistently measured the displacement of an otherwise applicable federal scheme by what an agency has actually done, not by what it might someday do. See Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (displacement requires "the clear and manifest purpose of Congress," discernible from the actual scope of the competing regulatory scheme).

Measured against that standard, the Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels—covering fire extinguishers, life preservers, backfire arrestors, and signaling equipment, 46 U.S.C. § 4102—govern navigational and life-saving equipment. They say nothing about well-control and blowout-prevention procedures, the absence of which caused the deaths and injuries here. Because the Coast Guard has never exercised authority over these working conditions, Section 4(b)(1) simply does not apply, and OSHA's citation rests on solid statutory ground.

## II. The Comprehensive Field-Occupation Doctrine of *Napier* Does Not Extend to Minimal, Unexercised Coast Guard Authority

Petitioner's field-occupation theory finds no support in Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co., 272 U.S. 605 (1926). There, state regulation of locomotive equipment was displaced because the Interstate Commerce Commission had promulgated a pervasive, detailed body of rules addressing that very equipment, leaving no room for supplementation. No comparable occupation of the field exists here. The Coast Guard's uninspected-vessel regulations are narrow and equipment-specific; they leave untouched the drilling and well-control practices at issue in this case. Absent the kind of pervasive regulatory occupation present in Napier, there is no basis to infer that Congress intended silent, largely unexercised Coast Guard authority to oust OSHA from an entire field of hazards the Coast Guard has never addressed.

## III. Concurrent Regulation of Vessel Safety Is Permissible Absent Actual Conflict

This Court has repeatedly tolerated overlapping regulation of vessel-related safety by different regulators where no genuine conflict exists between the schemes. In Kelly v. Washington ex rel. Foss Co., 302 U.S. 1 (1937), the Court upheld state regulation of tug-and-barge towing operations notwithstanding federal inspection laws, holding that Congress had not manifested an intent to exclude such regulation merely because federal authority touched the same general subject. Likewise, in Huron Portland Cement Co. v. City of Detroit, 362 U.S. 440 (1960), the Court sustained a municipal smoke-abatement ordinance as applied to federally licensed vessels, reasoning that regulation addressing a "legitimate local concern," id. at 445, survives absent an actual, operative federal rule addressing the identical conduct. The same principle governs here: the Coast Guard's failure to regulate drilling-related occupational hazards leaves ample room for OSHA's complementary regulation, without producing the sort of conflict Section 4(b)(1) was designed to prevent.

## IV. OSHA's Remedial Purpose and the Secretary's Reasoned Interpretation Confirm That the MR. BELDON Was a "Workplace"

Should the Court reach the question, Section 4(a) plainly covers the MR. BELDON. The Act is remedial legislation, and this Court has long construed such statutes to serve their protective purposes rather than to create artificial gaps in coverage. See NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 129 (1944) (construing "employee" broadly where a narrow construction would frustrate the statute's remedial design). And where statutory language is plain, courts need not resort to policy considerations to narrow its reach. United States v. American Trucking Assns., 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940). Section 4(a)'s broad reference to "employment performed in a workplace" plainly reaches a barge actively drilling an oil well and staffed by employees performing industrial tasks. The Secretary's interpretation of her own statute, reached through reasoned application of these principles to the facts of this case, warrants substantial weight. See Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944) (an agency's interpretation merits deference commensurate with "the thoroughness evident in its consideration").

## V. The Workers' Actual Function, Not the Vessel's Documentation Status, Determines Coverage

Petitioner's suggestion that mere presence aboard a vessel automatically triggers exclusive maritime regulation cannot be squared with this Court's seaman-status precedents, which look to the function actually performed rather than to a vessel's Coast Guard classification. See Desper v. Starved Rock Ferry Co., 342 U.S. 187, 190 (1952) (seaman status requires a substantial employment-related connection to a vessel "in navigation," not mere presence aboard); Norton v. Warner Co., 321 U.S. 565, 571–72 (1944) (assessing status by reference to the employee's actual duties). Employees engaged in oil and gas extraction from a stationary drilling barge perform industrial, not seafaring, functions. This confirms both that the barge was a "workplace" under Section 4(a) and that the Coast Guard's unexercised authority over uninspected vessels does not divest OSHA of jurisdiction under Section 4(b)(1).

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
