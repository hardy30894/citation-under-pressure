**Argument**

The National Park Service (“NPS”) properly applied its regulation prohibiting hovercrafts to the Nation River within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. The plain text of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (“ANILCA”) § 103(c) does not bar this application. Because the hovercraft ban applies to all lands and waters within Alaska’s conservation system units (“CSUs”)—not solely to federally owned “public lands”—the regulation falls outside § 103(c)’s limited carve-out. The Ninth Circuit correctly affirmed.

**I. The Statutory Text Unambiguously Supports NPS’s Authority**

ANILCA § 103(c) provides: “No … regulation … applicable solely to public lands within [CSUs] shall be effective … with respect to … lands … conveyed to the State … or to any Native Corporation.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The critical phrase is “applicable solely to public lands.” By its plain terms, this limitation governs only those regulations whose *sole* application is to federal public lands. If a regulation applies broadly—to all lands within a CSU—§ 103(c) does not restrict it.

The hovercraft regulation at issue, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), bans hovercraft “within [NPS-administered] lands and waters.” It is not confined to “public lands”; it reaches any location within the boundaries of a park unit, regardless of ownership. Because the regulation applies to state lands, Native lands, and private lands alike, it is not “applicable solely to public lands.” *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 812 F.3d 1195, 1207 (9th Cir. 2016). Section 103(c) therefore does not preclude its enforcement on the Nation River, even assuming the riverbed was “conveyed to the State.”

This conclusion flows from the “fundamental canon of statutory construction” that courts “must give effect to the plain meaning of the language used.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989). Congress chose the word “solely” deliberately. Had it intended to strip NPS of all regulatory authority over non-federal lands, it would have said “any regulation *that applies to* public lands shall not be effective on non-federal lands.” Instead, it created a narrow exception: only regulations that *operate exclusively* on public lands are limited. The broader the regulation—the more uniformly it governs the entire CSU—the *more* it escapes § 103(c). That result aligns with Congress’s overarching goal in ANILCA: to preserve the “natural and cultural integrity” of Alaska’s magnificent park system. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3101(d).

**II. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Is Faithful to ANILCA’s Framework**

The Ninth Circuit correctly rejected Sturgeon’s “erroneous reading of § 103(c).” *Sturgeon*, 812 F.3d at 1207. Where a regulation, like the hovercraft ban, “applies to state and private lands as well as public lands,” it is not “solely” applicable to public lands. *Id.* at 1208. The court further observed that NPS has long regulated non-federal lands within park boundaries—for example, requiring permits for scientific research on state-owned waters. *Id.* at 1209. Congress did not disturb that practice in ANILCA; it merely ensured that federal land management would not impose burdens on non-federal property that had no connection to broader park-wide rules.

This reading comports with the “common sense” understanding of the statute. *Sturgeon*, 812 F.3d at 1210. Allowing individual landowners to opt out of uniform park regulations would “frustrate the very purpose of creating a national preserve.” *Id.* As the Supreme Court has instructed, interpretations that “produce a result demonstrably at odds with the intentions of the drafters” must yield. *Griffin v. Oceanic Contractors, Inc.*, 458 U.S. 564, 571 (1982). Sturgeon’s interpretation would splinter management of the Yukon-Charley Preserve into a checkerboard of federal and state authority, undermining ANILCA’s conservation mandate.

**III. The Hovercraft Ban Is a Valid Exercise of NPS’s General Regulatory Authority**

Even if § 103(c) did not exist, NPS would retain authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within CSUs. The Property Clause empowers Congress to “make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States.” U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2. That power extends to the “regulation of conduct on non-federal lands when reasonably necessary to protect federal property.” *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 547 (1976). The hovercraft ban protects the Nation River’s wilderness character—a federal interest—and is a reasonable means of doing so.

Moreover, NPS’s interpretation of its own regulation is entitled to *Chevron* deference. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Nat. Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843 (1984). The agency has consistently read the hovercraft ban to apply throughout park units, and that reading is reasonable. *See Sturgeon*, 812 F.3d at 1209 (noting that NPS “has long interpreted its regulatory authority to extend to state-owned lands and waters”).

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit’s judgment should be affirmed. ANILCA § 103(c) does not strip NPS of authority to enforce uniform regulations that apply to all lands within a CSU. The hovercraft ban is such a regulation.
