**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision in light of its original text, legislative history, and the settled principles of federal preemption that governed the law at the time of its enactment. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that California’s wage and hour laws may be applied on the outer Continental Shelf as “surrogate federal law” so long as they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, without requiring a showing of a gap or void in the federal scheme. This interpretation disregards the foundational principle that federal law preempts state law when it occupies the field, and it contradicts the clear legislative intent behind the OCSLA’s limited incorporation of state law.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that “the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State, now in effect or hereafter adopted, amended, or repealed are declared to be the law of the United States” for the outer Continental Shelf “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted.” This language, enacted in 1953, was not intended to create a general rule of concurrent state-federal jurisdiction. Rather, Congress designed the OCSLA to fill a jurisdictional vacuum left by the absence of a comprehensive federal statutory scheme for the seabed and fixed structures of the outer Continental Shelf. As the legislative history makes plain, Congress rejected the idea of extending state boundaries seaward and also rejected the application of maritime law in its entirety, because neither was “designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” *99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953)*. Thus, Congress authorized the assimilation of state law only to the extent that it was “applicable” and “not inconsistent” with federal law—meaning that state law could only be applied where federal law was silent or inadequate.

This principle was firmly established in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301 (1947), where the Court held that “where a federal statute is complete in itself and leaves no room for supplementary state legislation, state laws cannot be applied to supplement it.” The Court emphasized that “the federal law is exclusive when it is so comprehensive that it leaves no room for state regulation.” Id. at 307. The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), enacted in 1938, is precisely such a comprehensive statute. It establishes a uniform national minimum wage, overtime pay, and recordkeeping regime, and it contains a savings clause—29 U.S.C. § 218—that explicitly permits states to impose more protective wage and hour standards. But the existence of a savings clause does not mean that state laws are automatically applicable on the OCS; it only means that Congress has authorized states to go beyond the federal floor. It does not authorize state laws to operate concurrently with federal law where the federal scheme is complete and self-sufficient.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that state law may apply whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law—ignores the settled rule that “inconsistency” is not the sole test for preemption. In *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. 44, 52 (1922), the Court held that “where the federal law is complete and leaves no room for state regulation, the state law cannot be applied even though it does not conflict with the federal law.” The Court explained that “the federal law may be so comprehensive as to leave no room for state legislation, even though the state law is not in conflict with it.” Id. The FLSA is such a law. It governs wages, hours, and working conditions for employees engaged in interstate commerce, and it includes detailed regulations issued by the Department of Labor to implement its provisions. These regulations, which have the force of law, *see United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940)*, establish a uniform national standard that Congress intended to be exclusive in the absence of a gap.

The legislative history of the OCSLA confirms that Congress did not intend to allow state law to operate as a parallel system where federal law already provides a complete and detailed framework. In *Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 26 (1988), the Court noted that Congress “envisioned that 10,000 or more people might eventually be employed on the OCS to develop mineral resources,” and that federal law alone would be “inadequate” because it “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” But this concern applied only to areas where federal law was absent or incomplete. The FLSA, by contrast, was already a comprehensive and detailed statute when the OCSLA was enacted. It was not a “gap-filling” measure but a full-fledged regulatory regime. To apply California’s wage and hour laws in this context would be to allow state law to operate as a substitute for federal law in a field where Congress has already spoken with finality.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), is misplaced. That case, decided after the OCSLA’s 1975 amendment, is not binding precedent and was not decided before 1970. More importantly, it was not a case involving wage and hour law, nor did it address the issue of whether state law may apply in the absence of a gap.
