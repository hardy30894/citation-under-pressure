**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that the asserted claims of the ’658 and ’400 patents are invalid as anticipated under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) because it improperly denied PowerOasis the benefit of the filing date of its Original Application—February 6, 1997—on the ground that the Original Application failed to provide a written description of the claimed invention. That conclusion rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 112 and the doctrine of priority under § 120. The Original Application, as a complete disclosure of the core invention, satisfies the written description requirement for the asserted claims, and thus the asserted claims are entitled to the benefit of the February 6, 1997 filing date. Because the MobileStar Network was not publicly disclosed until after that date, the asserted claims are not anticipated under § 102(b).

**I. The Original Application Provides a Written Description of the Invention Claimed in the Asserted Patents**

Under 35 U.S.C. § 112, a patent application must contain a written description of the invention sufficient to enable one skilled in the art to make and use the claimed invention. *See* *In re Wertheim*, 541 F.2d 257, 262 (C.C.P.A. 1976) (“The written description requirement is satisfied when the disclosure of the application shows that the inventor had possession of the claimed subject matter at the time of filing.”). The requirement is not met by mere speculation or a general idea, but rather by a disclosure that conveys to the skilled artisan that the inventor possessed the claimed invention. *See* *LizardTech, Inc. v. Earth Resource Mapping, Inc.*, 424 F.3d 1336, 1344 (Fed. Cir. 2005).

Here, the Original Application, filed on February 6, 1997, discloses a system for vending telecommunications access via a vending machine that includes a payment mechanism, a control unit, a telecommunications access circuit, and a connector for linking to a customer’s device. The specification describes a system in which a user inserts payment, receives access to a telecommunications channel, and is disconnected upon completion of the transaction. *See* ’643 patent, col. 3, ll. 15–30; col. 4, ll. 10–25. Crucially, the Original Application explicitly contemplates that the customer’s device—such as a laptop—may be connected to the vending machine via a physical or wireless interface. *Id.*, col. 4, ll. 40–50 (“The customer’s computing device may be connected to the vending machine via a cable or wireless link.”). This disclosure is not merely a suggestion or a possibility; it is a concrete, operative embodiment of the invention.

The district court erred in dismissing this disclosure as insufficient because it did not use the exact term “customer interface” or explicitly define it as being located on the customer’s device. *See* *PowerOasis, Inc. v. T-Mobile USA, Inc.*, No. 05-ev-42-PB, 2007 WL 962937, at *2 (D.N.H. Mar. 30, 2007). The written description requirement does not demand that every claim term be defined in the original application, nor that the language of the claims be mirrored verbatim. *See* *Festo Corp. v. Shoketsu Kinzoku Kogyo Kabushiki Co.*, 535 U.S. 722, 734 (2002) (“The written description requirement is not satisfied by a mere suggestion of a possible embodiment.”). Rather, the inquiry is whether the original disclosure demonstrates that the inventor possessed the claimed invention at the time of filing. *See* *Enzo Biochem, Inc. v. Gen-Probe Inc.*, 323 F.3d 956, 967 (Fed. Cir. 2002).

The Original Application’s disclosure of a system that connects a customer’s computing device to a telecommunications vending machine—whether via cable or wireless link—demonstrates possession of the claimed invention, including the concept of a “customer interface” that may reside on the customer’s device. The district court’s construction of “customer interface” as requiring a physical interface on the vending machine itself is not supported by the claim language or the specification. *See* *Phillips v. AWH Corp.*, 415 F.3d 1303, 1314 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (“The specification is the single best guide to the meaning of a claim term.”). The claim language does not limit the customer interface to the vending machine; it merely requires that the interface “indicate the status of said vending machine,” a function that can be performed by a device connected to the machine.

**II. The Asserted Claims Are Entitled to the Benefit of the Original Application’s Filing Date Under 35 U.S.C. § 120**

Because the Original Application provides a written description of the invention claimed in the asserted patents, the asserted claims are entitled to the benefit of the February 6, 1997 filing date under 35 U.S.C. § 120. *See* *In re Paine*, 591 F.2d 1375, 1378 (C.C.P.A. 1979) (“A continuation or continuation-in-part application may claim the benefit of the filing date of the prior application if the prior application contains a written description of the invention claimed in the later application.”). The 2000 CIP Application, which added new matter, does not negate the priority of the Original Application for claims that are fully supported by the earlier disclosure.

The district court’s conclusion that the Original Application failed to describe the invention because it did not include the term “customer interface” or explicitly state that the interface could be on the customer’s laptop is contrary to established precedent. *See* *In re Wertheim*, 541 F.2d at 262 (“The test is not whether the claim language appears in the specification, but whether the specification shows that the inventor had possession of the claimed subject matter.”).
