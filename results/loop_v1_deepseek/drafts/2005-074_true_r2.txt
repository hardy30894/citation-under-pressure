## ARGUMENT

### I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered Before Its Effective Date

The Immigration and Nationality Act's reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), became effective on April 1, 1997. Petitioner Fernandez-Vargas reentered the United States illegally in 1982—fifteen years before the statute existed. This Court has long held that statutes should be construed to operate prospectively unless the language unmistakably requires otherwise. *United States v. Heth*, 3 Cranch 399, 413 (1806) (stating that courts "ought not to give a retrospective operation to a statute, unless the words are so clear, strong, and imperative, that no other meaning can be annexed to them"). Nothing in Section 1231(a)(5) manifests such a clear intent to reach prior reentries. The statute speaks in the present tense, providing that the Attorney General may reinstate a prior removal order if the Attorney General finds that an alien has reentered the United States illegally. This language most naturally applies to reentries occurring after the statute's effective date. This ambiguity must be resolved in favor of the alien, for deportation is a drastic measure and the laws imposing it should be narrowly construed. *Mahler v. Eby*, 264 U.S. 32, 40 (1924). See also *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948) (stating that "the statute should be construed in favor of the alien and against the construction which would forfeit his right to remain in this country"). Had Congress intended the statute to apply to reentries occurring at any time, it could have said so. It did not. The statute therefore applies only to reentries occurring after its effective date.

### II. Applying the Reinstatement Statute Retroactively Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statute could be read to encompass pre-1997 reentries, such application would attach new legal consequences to past conduct, violating the bedrock "presumption against retroactive legislation." *Union Pacific R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913). A statute should not be given retroactive effect unless that purpose is clearly expressed by Congress. *United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926). Here, no such clear expression exists.

When petitioner reentered in 1982, the law permitted him to later apply for adjustment of status if he married a United States citizen. *Barber v. Gonzales*, 347 U.S. 637, 642 (1954) (recognizing that the law accords the alien a fair opportunity to present his claim for relief). The reinstatement statute eliminated that possibility entirely, barring any form of relief. This new bar alters the legal consequences of a reentry that was completed long before the statute's enactment. Such a change is impermissibly retroactive because it "impairs rights a party possessed when he acted." *Greene v. United States*, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964) (quoting *Union Pacific*, 231 U.S. at 199). Petitioner's reentry in 1982 carried with it the prospect of eventual adjustment. The 1997 statute stripped that prospect away.

This Court has consistently held that deportation statutes must not be applied retroactively to attach new penalties to past conduct. In *Delgadillo v. Carmichael*, 332 U.S. 388, 391 (1947), the Court refused to apply a deportation ground to an alien who had returned to the United States before the ground's enactment, stating that "the law must be construed to be prospective only unless a contrary intent is clearly expressed by Congress." The same principle governs here. The reinstatement statute imposes a new, harsh consequence—foreclosing all relief—on petitioner's pre-1997 reentry. As *Shwab v. Doyle*, 258 U.S. 529, 534 (1922), teaches, statutes should be construed to have only prospective operation unless the legislative intent to the contrary is clearly expressed.

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that petitioner lacked a "protectable expectation" of adjusting his status. An alien who reenters illegally does not forfeit all future claims to relief. *See Fong Haw Tan*, 333 U.S. at 10 (the "grave nature of deportation" requires that "the statute be construed in favor of the alien"). The expectation of applying for adjustment under then-existing law was a legitimate right that the government could not retroactively extinguish without clear congressional command. *Heth*, 3 Cranch at 413. No such command appears in Section 1231(a)(5).

Retroactive application also offends due process by upsetting settled reliance interests. When petitioner returned in 1982, the legal landscape permitted him to later seek adjustment if he married a citizen. The 1997 statute retroactively destroyed that avenue, making his earlier conduct the basis for permanent exclusion from relief. This is precisely the kind of retrospective change the presumption against retroactivity is designed to prevent. *St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. at 3.

For these reasons, Section 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date. Even if it did, applying it retroactively would be impermissible. The petition for review should be granted.
