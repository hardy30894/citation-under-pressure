**ARGUMENT**

I. THE MERIT SYSTEMS PROTECTION BOARD’S DISMISSAL OF PETITIONER’S APPEAL FOR LACK OF JURISDICTION IS A “CASE[ ] OF DISCRIMINATION” SUBJECT TO REVIEW IN DISTRICT COURT UNDER 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2).

The jurisdictional statute governing review of MSPB decisions draws a clear line between “cases of discrimination” and all other Board decisions. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2) provides that “[n]otwithstanding any other provision of law, any case of discrimination brought pursuant to the provisions of any law, rule, or regulation shall be filed in [a federal district court].” This Court has long held that the phrase “cases of discrimination” is to be construed broadly to effectuate the remedial purposes of the anti-discrimination laws. *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 800 (1973) (citing *Bowe v. Colgate, Palmolive Co.*, 416 F.2d 711, 719 (7th Cir. 1969)). Here, Petitioner’s appeal to the MSPB alleged that his suspension and retirement were the product of unlawful discrimination based on race, age, and disability, as well as retaliation for protected activity. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A) (defining “cases of discrimination” to include appeals alleging discrimination under enumerated statutes). The Board’s dismissal for lack of jurisdiction did not alter the nature of Petitioner’s claim; it merely determined that the Board lacked authority to adjudicate the merits. *See Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597, 599 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (recognizing that a jurisdictional dismissal does not strip a claim of its substantive character). Thus, Petitioner’s appeal falls squarely within the district court’s review jurisdiction under § 7703(b)(2).

This construction is consistent with the Supreme Court’s instruction that jurisdictional statutes must be read in light of the policies they were intended to effectuate. *Heckler v. Edwards*, 465 U.S. 870, 873 (1984) (citing *United States v. Stewart*, 311 U.S. 60, 70 (1940)). The Civil Service Reform Act’s mixed-case provisions were enacted to provide federal employees with a meaningful remedy for employment discrimination. *See S. Rep. No. 95-969*, at 31 (1978), *reprinted in* 1978 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2723, 2753. Denying district court review to Petitioner—who has alleged discrimination in connection with an appealable adverse action—would frustrate this remedial purpose. *See Brown v. General Servs. Admin.*, 425 U.S. 820, 832 (1976) (holding that remedial statutes must be liberally construed to achieve their objectives).

II. THE D.C. CIRCUIT’S DECISION IN *POWELL* IS INCONSISTENT WITH THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT AND SHOULD NOT BE FOLLOWED.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Powell* rests on the erroneous premise that a jurisdictional dismissal removes a case from the ambit of § 7703(b)(2). *Powell*, 158 F.3d at 599. This conclusion directly conflicts with the Supreme Court’s holding in *Bowen v. Massachusetts*, 487 U.S. 879, 900 (1988) (citing *United States v. Emholt*, 105 U.S. 442, 446 (1882)), that a jurisdictional defect does not deprive a claim of its substantive nature. *See also* *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506, 514 (1868) (holding that jurisdiction over the subject matter is not the same as jurisdiction over the particular claim). Moreover, *Powell* ignored this Court’s longstanding rule that the scope of § 7703(b)(2) turns on the nature of the claim asserted, not the Board’s determination of its own jurisdiction. *See* *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. at 800 (construing anti-discrimination provisions broadly to effectuate their remedial purpose).

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is further undermined by the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012). Although *Kloeckner* addressed a procedural dismissal rather than a jurisdictional one, the Court’s reasoning applies with equal force here. The Court held that when an employee alleges discrimination in connection with an appealable adverse action, the nature of the claim—not the Board’s disposition—determines the appropriate forum for review. *Id.* at 604 (citing *Brown v. General Servs. Admin.*, 425 U.S. at 832). The same principle governs Petitioner’s appeal, which alleges discrimination in connection with an appealable adverse action (his suspension and retirement). *See* 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A).

III. UNIFORM REVIEW IN DISTRICT COURT PROMOTES JUDICIAL ECONOMY AND CONSISTENCY.

Permitting district court review of jurisdictional dismissals ensures that Petitioner’s discrimination claim is adjudicated in a single forum, avoiding piecemeal litigation and inconsistent results. *See* *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs*, 383 U.S. 715, 726 (1966) (recognizing the benefits of litigating related claims in a single forum). The Federal Circuit, by contrast, lacks expertise in employment discrimination law and has no institutional role in interpreting the anti-discrimination statutes at issue here. *See* *Brown v. General Servs. Admin.*, 425 U.S. at 832 (emphasizing the need for uniform interpretation of anti-discrimination laws). District court review also aligns with Congress’s intent to provide aggrieved employees with a meaningful remedy. *See S. Rep. No. 95-969*, at 31.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s decision and hold that Petitioner’s appeal is a “case[ ] of discrimination” subject to review in district court under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2).
