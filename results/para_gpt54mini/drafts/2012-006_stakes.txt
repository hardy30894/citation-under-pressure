**Argument**

The question presented is not whether the Government must prove a defendant’s membership in a conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt at trial; it must. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). The question here is different: when a defendant claims he withdrew before the limitations period, does that defense shift the burden to the Government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he remained a conspirator during the limitations period? The answer is no. Withdrawal is a defense that defeats timeliness, and the defendant bears the burden of producing and proving it. Smith v. United States, 568 U.S. 106, 110-11 (2013).

A conspiracy offense is complete when the unlawful agreement is formed, and the statute of limitations runs from the last overt act, or from the conspiracy’s continuation, depending on the statute. United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 608-10 (1910); Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 216-17 (1946). Because a conspirator is liable for the acts of the conspiracy until he withdraws, withdrawal is an affirmative defense. Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-71 (1912). As this Court later explained, withdrawal “terminates” a defendant’s liability for future acts, but only if the defendant “affirmatively act[s]” to disavow or defeat the conspiracy. Smith, 568 U.S. at 111-13. Mere cessation of activity is not enough. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70; United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 438 U.S. 422, 464-65 (1978).

That framework answers the burden question. In Smith, this Court held that because “withdrawal is an affirmative defense,” the defendant “bears the burden of proving it.” 568 U.S. at 110. The Court rejected the argument that due process requires the Government to disprove withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt as part of its case-in-chief. Id. at 113-14. The reason is doctrinally straightforward: the Government’s burden is to prove the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation in it; withdrawal is a separate fact that excuses or limits liability. Id. at 110-12. Nothing in the Due Process Clause requires the prosecution to negate every possible excuse or tolling defense beyond a reasonable doubt. See Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 210-16 (1977); Martin v. Ohio, 480 U.S. 228, 233-36 (1987).

Petitioners’ rule would invert the conspiracy doctrine. If the Government had to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was still a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period, then every withdrawal claim would become an element of the offense. That is not how conspiracy law works. A defendant “who claims he withdrew from the conspiracy has the burden of proving withdrawal.” Smith, 568 U.S. at 110. The Government need only prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and that the defendant remained a participant absent withdrawal. Id. at 111-12. Once the Government establishes participation in the conspiracy, the law presumes continued membership until withdrawal is shown. See Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-71; United States v. Jimenez Recio, 537 U.S. 270, 274-75 (2003).

Petitioners may invoke the general rule that the prosecution must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt. In re Winship, 397 U.S. at 364. But withdrawal is not an element. This Court has repeatedly distinguished elements from affirmative defenses, holding that States and the federal government may place on defendants the burden of proving defenses that do not negate an element of the offense. Patterson, 432 U.S. at 210-17; Dixon v. United States, 548 U.S. 1, 8-12 (2006). Withdrawal fits comfortably within that rule because it does not dispute the existence of the conspiracy or the defendant’s original participation; it asserts that liability ended before the relevant period. Smith, 568 U.S. at 110-13.

Nor is there any special statute-of-limitations principle that changes the analysis. A limitations defense ordinarily bars prosecution only if the charged offense is untimely, and the defendant may bear the burden of establishing facts that make the prosecution untimely. See United States v. Cook, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 168, 179-81 (1872); Musacchio v. United States, 577 U.S. 237, 245-46 (2016). Withdrawal is precisely such a fact: it does not define the crime, but it determines whether the defendant can still be held responsible for the ongoing conspiracy. Smith, 568 U.S. at 111-12.

The court of appeals’ contrary approach cannot be reconciled with Smith, Hyde, Patterson, or Dixon. The Constitution required the Government to prove the conspiracy and petitioner’s membership beyond a reasonable doubt; it did not require the Government to disprove petitioner’s asserted withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt. Smith, 568 U.S. at 110-14. The judgment should be affirmed.
