**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess each taxpayer individually before it may collect from that taxpayer. A partnership and its partners are separate taxpayers. Because the IRS never assessed the partners within the three-year period, the ten-year extension of § 6502(a) never began to run as to them.

**I. The assessment against the partnership did not assess the partners.**

Section 6203 provides that an assessment is made by recording the liability of the taxpayer. The Code defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes both “an individual” and “a partnership.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Thus, a partnership and its partners are distinct taxpayers. The regulation under § 6203 requires that the assessment “provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. An assessment naming only the partnership does not identify the partners. This Court has long held that an assessment against a partnership is not an assessment against the partners. *United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. 489, 495 (1930) (“The assessment of the tax against the partnership was not an assessment against the partners.”). *See also United States v. Garbutt*, 289 U.S. 71, 75 (1933) (holding that a partner’s property is not subject to a lien unless the tax is assessed against him). The Ninth Circuit applied the same rule in *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961), holding that a tax lien cannot attach to a partner’s property absent an assessment against the partner.

**II. The statute of limitations for collection from the partners ran from the date of assessment against the partners, not the partnership.**

Section 6501(a) requires the IRS to assess a tax within three years after the return is filed. Section 6502(a) extends the collection period to ten years after assessment, but only for “the tax” that has been assessed. Because the partners were never assessed, the ten-year period never began to run as to them. This Court’s decisions in *Updike* and *Garbutt* are directly on point. In *Updike*, the IRS assessed a partnership and later sued the partners after the limitations period had run. The Court held that the suit was barred because the partners had not been assessed. 281 U.S. at 495. In *Garbutt*, the Court similarly held that the statute of limitations for collection from a partner runs from the assessment against the partner, not from the assessment against the partnership. 289 U.S. at 75. The Second Circuit reached the same conclusion in *United States v. Chabot*, 193 F.2d 584, 586 (2d Cir. 1952), holding that an assessment against a partnership does not extend the limitations period for collection from the partners.

The IRS’s contrary reading would render the statutory definition of “taxpayer” meaningless. If an assessment against a partnership were automatically an assessment against each partner, then the IRS could collect from any partner at any time, without regard to the three-year assessment period. Congress did not intend such a result. The separate assessment requirement ensures that each taxpayer knows when the limitations period begins to run.

**III. The IRS cannot rely on state law to avoid the federal limitations period.**

The IRS argues that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, and that this state-law liability permits collection without an individual assessment. But the federal statute of limitations governs the time for collection. As this Court held in *Updike*, the IRS must bring an action against a partner within the period prescribed by federal law. 281 U.S. at 495. Moreover, California law does not permit a creditor to collect from a partner without first obtaining a judgment against the partner. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The IRS obtained no such judgment. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS’s state-law claim was unenforceable because the time for obtaining a judgment had expired.

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In that case, the partner had voluntarily paid the tax and sought a refund. The court held only that a person who pays a tax cannot recover it merely because the assessment did not name him. *Id.* at 910. The court did not hold that the IRS could have collected the tax from the partner had he refused to pay. Indeed, the district court in *Young* expressly acknowledged that, absent an individual assessment, the IRS would have to resort to court action to collect from a partner. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). That is precisely what the IRS failed to do here.

**CONCLUSION**

The assessment against the Partnership extended the statute of limitations only for the Partnership. The IRS did not assess the partners, and it did not bring an action against them within the three-year period. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
