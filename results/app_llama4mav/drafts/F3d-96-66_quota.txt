## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Bora Corp., Ltd. ("the ship") because there are genuine issues of material fact regarding the ship's liability under section 5(b) of the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C. § 905(b). Specifically, the ship breached its "active operations duty" to exercise reasonable care to avoid injuries to longshoremen like Serbin.

The "active operations duty" is a well-established principle in maritime law, as set forth in Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos, 451 U.S. 156 (1981). Under this duty, a shipowner has a responsibility to exercise reasonable care to avoid injuries to longshoremen when the ship is actively involved in the cargo operations. Id. at 167. The duty is not limited to situations where the shipowner is directly supervising the stevedore's work, but also applies when the shipowner is actively participating in the cargo operations. See Davis v. Partenreederei M.S. Normannia, 657 F.2d 1048, 1053 (9th Cir. 1981).

In this case, the ship's crew was responsible for opening and closing the hatch covers using a block and pulley system, which included the snatch blocks. The crew's failure to return the snatch block to the up position after opening the hatch cover created a hazardous condition that Serbin encountered during his work. The ship's crew had a duty to ensure that the snatch block was properly secured in the up position, and their failure to do so constitutes a breach of the active operations duty. See Howlett v. Birkdale Shipping Co., S.A., 512 U.S. 92, 98 (1994) (holding that a shipowner's duty to exercise reasonable care includes "the duty to warn of hidden dangers or to remedy dangerous conditions that the shipowner should have anticipated").

The fact that Serbin decided to move the snatch block himself does not absolve the ship of its liability. Under the caselaw, a longshoreman's decision to intervene in a hazardous condition created by the shipowner does not necessarily constitute an assumption of risk or a superseding cause. See Gravatt v. City of New York, 226 F.3d 108, 122 (2d Cir. 2000) (holding that a longshoreman's decision to work in a hazardous area did not bar recovery under § 905(b) where the shipowner had created the hazardous condition). Moreover, the ship's argument that Serbin was contributorily negligent is a question of fact that should be determined by the jury, not on summary judgment. See Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn, 346 U.S. 406, 408-09 (1953) (holding that contributory negligence is a question of fact for the jury in a § 905(b) case).

Furthermore, the ship's reliance on cases such as Hill v. Texaco, Inc., 674 F.2d 447 (5th Cir. 1982), is misplaced. In Hill, the court held that a shipowner was not liable for a longshoreman's injury where the stevedore had assumed the risk of the hazardous condition. Id. at 451. However, in this case, Serbin did not assume the risk of the hazardous condition created by the ship's crew; rather, he attempted to mitigate the hazard by moving the snatch block. See Kirsch v. Plovidba, 971 F.2d 1026, 1031 (3d Cir. 1992) (distinguishing between assumption of risk and mitigation of harm).

The caselaw also establishes that a shipowner's duty to exercise reasonable care is not limited to situations where the shipowner has actual knowledge of the hazardous condition. See Turner v. Costa Line Cargo Services, Inc., 744 F.2d 505, 508 (5th Cir. 1984) (holding that a shipowner's duty to exercise reasonable care includes "the duty to inspect and to discover dangerous conditions"). In this case, the ship's crew had a duty to inspect the snatch block and ensure that it was properly secured, and their failure to do so constitutes a breach of their duty.

Additionally, the ship's argument that it is not liable because the stevedore was responsible for the cargo operations is not supported by the caselaw. See Derr v. Kawasaki Kisen K.K., 835 F.2d 490, 493 (3d Cir. 1987) (holding that a shipowner's duty to exercise reasonable care is not limited to situations where the shipowner is directly supervising the stevedore's work).

In conclusion, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to the ship because there are genuine issues of material fact regarding the ship's liability under § 905(b). The ship breached its active operations duty to exercise reasonable care to avoid injuries to Serbin, and the caselaw supports the conclusion that the ship's liability is not absolved by Serbin's decision to move the snatch block or by the stevedore's responsibility for the cargo operations. See, e.g., Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos, 451 U.S. 156 (1981); Howlett v. Birkdale Shipping Co., S.A., 512 U.S. 92 (1994); Gravatt v. City of New York, 226 F.3d 108 (2d Cir. 2000); Kirsch v. Plovidba, 971 F.2d 1026 (3d Cir. 1992); Turner v. Costa Line Cargo Services, Inc., 744 F.2d 505 (5th Cir. 1984); Derr v. Kawasaki Kisen K.K., 835 F.2d 490 (3d Cir. 1987); Davis v. Partenreederei M.S. Normannia, 657 F.2d 1048 (9th Cir. 1981); Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn, 346 U.S. 406 (1953). The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.
