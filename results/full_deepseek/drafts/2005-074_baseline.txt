**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered the United States Before Its Effective Date.**

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), provides that a prior removal order against an alien “who has reentered” the United States illegally “shall be reinstated.” The provision is silent as to whether it governs reentries that occurred before its April 1, 1997 effective date. Under the framework of *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), a statute may not be applied retroactively unless Congress clearly expressed that intent; the mere fact that the statute’s operative language is phrased in the present perfect tense does not constitute such clear intent. Indeed, *Landgraf* requires that we “apply the default rule” that “a statute will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless Congress expressly stated its intention.” *Id.* at 264 (citing *Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp.*, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988)).

The conduct that triggers reinstatement is the illegal reentry. Fernandez-Vargas reentered in 1982—fifteen years before the statute existed. Applying the 1997 statute to him would “attach new legal consequences” to that completed event. *Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States*, 520 U.S. 939, 947 (1997). The Tenth Circuit erred in locating the relevant event in his 2001 application for adjustment of status. But the statute operates automatically upon the reentry itself, not upon any later application. The Attorney General’s own regulations confirm that reinstatement is imposed “regardless of whether the alien … has since filed an application for relief.” 8 C.F.R. § 241.8(a)(1). The triggering act—reentry—was fully completed long before the statute arose.

Where a statute is ambiguous about temporal scope, this Court has repeatedly declined to read it to reach pre-enactment conduct. *See Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 354 (1999) (refusing to apply statute to pre-enactment services); *Lindh v. Murphy*, 521 U.S. 320, 328-29 (1997) (rejecting retroactive application of AEDPA where Congress did not “clearly” say otherwise). Section 241(a)(5) contains no such clarity. Congress knew how to express temporal reach when it wished; the neighboring provision in the same statute explicitly applies to offenses occurring “before, on, or after” the effective date. 8 U.S.C. § 1229b(c)(6). The absence of comparable language here is telling. *See* *Gozlon-Peretz v. United States*, 498 U.S. 395, 404 (1991) (Congress’s use of explicit temporal language elsewhere evinces deliberate choice).

**II. Retroactive Application of the Statute Deprives Fernandez-Vargas of a Protectable Interest and Violates Due Process.**

Even if the statute could be read to apply, such an application is impermissibly retroactive. When Fernandez-Vargas reentered in 1982, he lawfully could seek to adjust his status to that of a lawful permanent resident if he married a citizen and was otherwise admissible. The reinstatement statute retroactively eliminated that avenue of relief for him, solely because of his pre-enactment reentry. This Court has squarely held that the elimination of prospective relief “has a retroactive effect” when it is premised on a prior deportable act. *I.N.S. v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 321 (2001). In *St. Cyr*, the Court applied *Landgraf* to hold that eliminating § 212(c) relief for aliens who pled guilty before the statute’s effective date was impermissibly retroactive. *Id.* at 326. “The enactment of IIRIRA would clearly have had an effect” on the alien’s decisions, the Court explained, because he could not have “considered the possibility of § 212(c) relief” had he known the statute would be repealed. *Id.* at 322-23.

The same logic applies here. If Fernandez-Vargas had known that his 1982 reentry would one day bar him from ever adjusting status, he would have made profoundly different decisions over the ensuing two decades. He lived openly, married a U.S. citizen, and filed an application for status adjustment—all before the Attorney General arrested him. The reinstatement statute “attaches new disabilities” to his 1982 reentry and deprives him of legal benefits he could previously have pursued. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269.

The Tenth Circuit reasoned that Fernandez-Vargas had no “protectable expectation” because he was an illegal entrant. That is not the standard. The presumption against retroactivity protects “reliance interests” even where a party’s prior conduct was unlawful. *See* *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321-22 (holding that a conviction-based removal system did not deprive the alien of a protectable interest in seeking relief). The right at issue is not a right to remain unlawfully; it is the statutory right to adjust status, which was available to Fernandez-Vargas at the time of his reentry. The Attorney General cannot “by later statute alter[] the legal effect of [that] past event” to foreclose a remedy Congress had provided. *Hughes Aircraft*, 520 U.S. at 948.

For these reasons, the judgment below must be reversed.
