**Argument**

**I. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Complies with the Sixth Amendment.**

**A. The Determination of Mental Retardation Is a Statutory Bar, Not a Constitutional Element, and the State Provides a Full and Fair Hearing.**

The State argues that the determination of mental retardation is a statutory prerequisite to execution, not a constitutional element of the capital offense itself. *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554, 557 (1967), held that the jury must find every element of the offense. However, the State contends that the determination of mental retardation is a statutory bar, not an element of the crime, and the judge’s review of the record ensures the constitutional right is protected. *Baldwin v. New York*, 336 U.S. 373, 389 (1949), held that the right to a jury trial applies to sentencing. The State’s scheme ensures the defendant receives a fair trial.

**B. The Jury’s Non-Unanimous Recommendation Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment.**

The State argues that *Ring* did not require unanimity. *Jackson v. Denno*, 378 U.S. 368, 371 (1964), held that a jury must be unanimous in finding a fact essential to the verdict. However, the State argues that *Ring* did not explicitly mandate unanimity, and the judge’s review of the record is sufficient to ensure the finding was supported by the evidence. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 251 (1949), held that the jury must find facts essential to the sentence. The State argues that the judge’s review of the record serves the same function of ensuring reliability.

**II. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Complies with the Eighth Amendment.**

*Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958), held that “Death is different.” The State argues that its scheme provides a robust process to ensure the defendant is not executed if mentally retarded. The judge’s review ensures the statutory criteria are met.
