**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984). The Court has never held that a plaintiff must prove causation. To the contrary, the “relate to” prong is an independent, broader basis for jurisdiction. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017) (quoting *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472 (1985)) (specific jurisdiction requires “a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue”). That connection exists here.

**I. The “arise out of or relate to” standard does not require causation.**

This Court has consistently described specific jurisdiction in the disjunctive: claims must “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum contacts. *Helicopteros*, 466 U.S. at 414. The “relate to” language is not surplusage. It captures claims that have a substantial connection to the defendant’s in-state activities even where those activities did not cause the injury. *Bristol-Myers*, 582 U.S. at 262 (noting that the “relate to” prong “contemplates” that “some relationships” between the defendant and the forum “will support specific jurisdiction” without causation). The touchstone is whether the defendant “purposefully avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State,” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475 (quoting *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958)), and whether the claims “relate to” those activities.

Ford’s argument would collapse “relate to” into “arise out of,” rendering the disjunctive standard meaningless. This Court has never adopted that reading. In *Bristol-Myers*, the Court rejected specific jurisdiction because the claims had “no connection” to the forum—the plaintiffs were not injured there and the defendant’s in-state activities were unrelated to the drugs at issue. 582 U.S. at 265. Here, by contrast, the claims are about a Ford vehicle that was driven in Montana, crashed in Montana, and killed a Montana resident. The connection is direct.

**II. Ford’s extensive contacts with Montana relate to the claims.**

Ford is not a passive out-of-state manufacturer. It maintains a nationwide distribution system, advertises in Montana, sells parts and accessories through authorized dealers, provides warranties, and operates service centers in the State. These contacts are not random or fortuitous; they are deliberate efforts to serve the Montana market. Ford knew that its vehicles would be purchased, registered, and driven in Montana. The claims in this case—design defect, failure to warn, and negligence—concern the safety of a Ford vehicle on Montana roads. The very activities Ford conducts in Montana—selling parts, servicing vehicles, and marketing its brand—are related to the safe operation of its vehicles in the State. The claims therefore “relate to” Ford’s Montana contacts.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford “availed itself of the privilege of doing business in Montana.” That finding is consistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770 (1984), the Court upheld jurisdiction over a magazine publisher whose only in-state contact was circulating magazines, even though the plaintiff’s injury—libel—occurred elsewhere. The Court explained that the defendant’s “regular circulation of magazines in the forum State” was sufficient because the claims “relate to” that activity. *Id.* at 774. Similarly, in *Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783 (1984), jurisdiction was proper because the defendants’ “intentional conduct in California” was “calculated to cause injury” there. *Id.* at 789. Here, Ford’s deliberate marketing and distribution in Montana was calculated to place its vehicles in the hands of Montana drivers.

**III. The stream-of-commerce doctrine supports jurisdiction.**

Ford placed its vehicles into the national stream of commerce with the expectation that they would be sold and used in Montana. This Court has recognized that a defendant who “purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State” by placing products into the stream of commerce may be subject to jurisdiction there. *J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 873, 882 (2011) (plurality opinion). Ford’s contacts are far more extensive than those of the defendant in *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102 (1987), where jurisdiction was rejected because the defendant’s only contact was a single component sale to a manufacturer. Here, Ford is the manufacturer of the entire vehicle, and it directly serves the Montana market through advertising, dealers, and parts distribution. The vehicle at issue was sold through Ford’s authorized distribution chain and ended up in Montana—exactly where Ford intended it to be.

**IV. The lower court’s decision does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.**

Ford has long been subject to suit in Montana for claims arising from its vehicles. It has availed itself of Montana’s laws, its courts, and its markets. Requiring Ford to defend a suit arising from a crash involving one of its vehicles in Montana imposes no unfair burden. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). The claims are not “so unrelated” to Ford’s Montana activities that jurisdiction would be arbitrary. *Bristol-Myers*, 582 U.S. at 264.

The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
