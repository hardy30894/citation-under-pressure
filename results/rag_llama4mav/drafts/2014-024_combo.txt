The Third Circuit's decision affirming Anthony Elonis's conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) for transmitting threats in interstate commerce should be reversed because the trial court's jury instruction improperly omitted the requirement of subjective intent to threaten. The true threats exception to First Amendment protection, as established by this Court, necessitates that a defendant subjectively intend their statements to be understood as threats.

The absence of a subjective intent requirement undermines the principle that the government must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt, as mandated by the Due Process Clause (Lee v. Mississippi, 332 U.S. 742 (1948)). In Cole v. Arkansas, 333 U.S. 196 (1948), this Court emphasized the importance of ensuring that a defendant is tried and convicted for the specific offense charged, highlighting the need for clarity in the elements of a crime.

Moreover, the Third Circuit's reliance on an objective standard, where a statement is considered a true threat if a reasonable speaker would foresee it being interpreted as such (United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991)), is inconsistent with the principles enunciated in cases such as Rogers v. Richmond, 365 U.S. 534 (1961), which stress the importance of voluntariness and intent in the context of confessions and statements.

Furthermore, the threat of prosecution under an unconstitutional statute can constitute irreparable harm, as noted in Hygrade Provision Co., Inc. v. Sherman, 266 U.S. 497 (1925), underscoring the need for clarity and precision in the definition of crimes to avoid chilling protected speech. The requirement of subjective intent to threaten is essential to distinguishing between constitutionally protected speech and true threats, a distinction critical to avoiding the infringement of First Amendment rights.

The history of the true threats doctrine and its application in various contexts, including extortion and coercion cases (Sexton v. California, 189 U.S. 319 (1903); Brown v. Pierce, 74 U.S. 205 (1869)), demonstrates that the intent behind a statement is a crucial factor in determining its legality. Similarly, cases involving duress and coercion (Waley v. Johnston, 316 U.S. 101 (1942); Iron Mountain & Helena Railroad v. Johnson, 119 U.S. 608 (1887)) highlight the significance of intent and the circumstances surrounding a statement or action.

In Ex Parte Century Indemnity Co., 305 U.S. 354 (1938), this Court demonstrated the importance of careful consideration of the legal standards applied in lower courts, emphasizing the need for precision in the application of legal principles. Similarly, in Dombrowski v. Pfister, 380 U.S. 479 (1965), the Court addressed the issue of federal injunctions against state prosecutions, illustrating the complex interplay between federal and state jurisdictions and the need for clarity in legal standards to avoid confusion and potential rights violations.

In conclusion, to align with the constitutional requirements of due process and the protection of First Amendment rights, a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) must be based on a finding that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten. The Third Circuit's affirmance of Elonis's conviction without such a finding was in error, and its decision should be reversed.
