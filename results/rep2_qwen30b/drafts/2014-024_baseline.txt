**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—lies at the heart of the First Amendment’s protection against criminalizing speech absent a clear intent to threaten. The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective “true threat” standard, which permits conviction based solely on whether a reasonable person would interpret the statement as a threat, violates the constitutional guarantee of due process and undermines the foundational principle that “the First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive or deeply disturbing.” *Texas v. Johnson*, 491 U.S. 397, 414 (1989). This Court should reverse the judgment below and remand for a new trial, because the government failed to prove the essential element of subjective intent, and the jury was instructed on a standard that is inconsistent with *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), and the broader jurisprudence of the First Amendment.

### I. The Government Must Prove Subjective Intent to Threaten Under § 875(c)

The statute criminalizes only the transmission of “any threat to injure the person of another.” 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). The term “threat” is not self-defining; it must be interpreted in light of constitutional constraints. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects speech even when it is threatening in tone or content, so long as it lacks the requisite intent to threaten. In *Virginia v. Black*, the Court held that “a true threat is a statement in which the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of intent to commit an act of violence.” 538 U.S. at 359 (emphasis added). The Court explicitly rejected an objective standard: “The First Amendment protects even those who threaten to kill, so long as they do not mean to carry out their threats.” Id. at 360. Thus, *Black* established that the “true threat” exception to the First Amendment requires proof of the speaker’s *subjective intent* to threaten.

The Third Circuit’s contrary holding—that a statement is a true threat if a reasonable person would foresee it as such—directly contradicts *Black*. The court’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced. *Kosma* predated *Black* and did not address the constitutional requirement of subjective intent. Since *Black*, this Court has made clear that “the First Amendment protects speech even when it is threatening in tone or content, so long as it lacks the requisite intent to threaten.” *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 596, 604 (2015). The Third Circuit’s refusal to apply *Black*’s subjective intent standard renders § 875(c) susceptible to the very kind of overbroad enforcement the First Amendment was designed to prevent.

### II. The Objective Standard Violates Due Process

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that the government prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt, including the mental state necessary to constitute the offense. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). When a statute criminalizes speech, the government must prove not only that the statement was threatening in form, but that the speaker *intended* it to be threatening. Without such proof, the law risks punishing speech that is merely offensive, hyperbolic, or artistic—speech that the Constitution protects.

The Third Circuit’s instruction allowed the jury to convict Elonis based solely on whether a reasonable person would interpret his Facebook posts as threats. This standard is constitutionally inadequate. As the Court explained in *Black*, “a threat is not a threat unless the speaker means it to be one.” 538 U.S. at 359. The jury was not required to find that Elonis *intended* to threaten his wife, the police, or the FBI agent. Instead, it was instructed that a statement is a true threat if “a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat.” This instruction effectively eliminated the subjective intent requirement, rendering the statute unconstitutionally vague and overbroad.

The Court has repeatedly struck down laws that criminalize speech based on its effect rather than the speaker’s intent. In *United States v. Stevens*, 559 U.S. 460 (2010), the Court invalidated a statute criminalizing depictions of animal cruelty because it punished speech based on its content and effect, without requiring proof of intent. Similarly, in *United States v. Alvarez*, 567 U.S. 709 (2012), the Court held that the government must prove a speaker’s intent to deceive when criminalizing false statements. These cases stand for the principle that the First Amendment demands a mental state requirement when speech is at issue. The Third Circuit’s failure to require proof of subjective intent in this case violates that principle.

### III. The Conviction Was Based on Speech Protected by the First Amendment

Elonis’s Facebook posts were written in the form of rap lyrics, a genre known for its use of hyperbole, metaphor, and dramatic expression. He testified that he was writing poetry and expressing emotional distress, not making real threats. His wife, who had never seen him write rap lyrics before, testified that she took the posts seriously because of their violent content—but not because she believed he intended to carry them out. The jury was not instructed to consider this context, nor was it required to find that Elonis intended his words to be taken as threats.

The Court has long recognized that “the First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive or deeply disturbing.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 604. The government may not criminalize speech simply because it causes fear or discomfort. *Cohen v. California*, 403 U.S. 15, 21 (1971). The Third Circuit’s standard allows the government to punish speech that is merely “threatening in tone” without proving the speaker meant it to be threatening. That is precisely the kind of overreach the First Amendment was designed to prevent.
