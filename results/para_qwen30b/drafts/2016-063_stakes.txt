**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to resolve a conflict between two lines of precedent: the D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), and the Supreme Court’s holding in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596, 184 L.Ed.2d 433 (2012). The D.C. Circuit, in its opinion below, concluded that *Kloeckner* did not overrule *Powell*, and thus transferred the case to the Federal Circuit. That conclusion is erroneous. *Kloeckner* did not merely clarify the proper forum for reviewing procedural dismissals—it fundamentally restructured the statutory framework governing judicial review of MSPB decisions, rendering *Powell* incompatible with the Court’s reasoning and the text of 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The proper forum for review of the MSPB’s jurisdictional dismissal in this case is the federal district court.

The statutory text governing judicial review of MSPB decisions is clear and unambiguous. Section 7702(a)(1)(A) defines a “mixed case” as one in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” federal law. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The key phrase is “action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” This language does not depend on the Board’s ultimate disposition of the case; it turns on whether the underlying action was one that, *if properly appealed*, would fall within the Board’s jurisdiction. In *Kloeckner*, the Court emphasized that the employee had been “affected by an action (i.e., removal) appealable to the MSPB,” and that the Board’s dismissal was based on untimeliness, not lack of jurisdiction. 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Court held that such a case—where the action is appealable but the appeal is procedurally defective—must be reviewed in district court. Id. at 604–605. The Court did not distinguish between procedural and jurisdictional grounds; it focused on the nature of the underlying action. The Court’s reasoning was grounded in the idea that the employee’s claim of discrimination was “inextricably intertwined” with the adverse action, and that the district court was the appropriate forum to adjudicate both the procedural and substantive issues. Id. at 605.

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* on the ground that it involved a “procedural” dismissal while *Powell* involved a “jurisdictional” one is legally unsound. The distinction between “procedural” and “jurisdictional” dismissals is not a matter of statutory construction but of judicial doctrine, and it has no basis in the text of § 7702(a)(1). The statute does not require the Board to have jurisdiction *before* the employee files a claim; it only requires that the employee be “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” That condition is satisfied when the action—such as a suspension or retirement—would be appealable if the employee had not waived her rights. In this case, Perry was subjected to a thirty-day suspension and mandatory retirement, both of which are appealable actions under 5 U.S.C. § 7512. The fact that the Board later concluded that the retirement was voluntary does not change the fact that the action was appealable. The Board’s jurisdictional determination is not a threshold inquiry into whether the action is appealable; it is a determination of whether the employee’s *claim* is properly before the Board. That is precisely the kind of issue that *Kloeckner* held must be reviewed in district court when discrimination is alleged.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is misplaced. In *Powell*, the court held that when the Board dismisses a case for lack of jurisdiction because the employee voluntarily accepted the action, review lies in the Federal Circuit. 158 F.3d at 599–600. But *Powell* was decided before *Kloeckner*, and it rested on a now-discredited understanding of the statutory framework. The *Kloeckner* Court explicitly rejected the idea that the forum for review depends on whether the Board reached the merits. It held that “the proper forum for review of a mixed case is the district court, regardless of whether the MSPB dismissed the case on procedural grounds or on the merits.” 133 S. Ct. at 605. The Court emphasized that the “statutory language does not distinguish between procedural and jurisdictional grounds” and that “the focus is on the nature of the underlying action, not the reason for the dismissal.” Id. at 604. The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to preserve *Powell* by drawing a distinction between “procedural” and “jurisdictional” grounds is inconsistent with *Kloeckner*’s central holding and with the plain text of the statute.

Moreover, the D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that *Kloeckner* did not “eviscerate” *Powell* is contrary to the principle that a Supreme Court decision that “effectively overrules” a circuit precedent is binding. *Dellums v. U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Comm’n*, 863 F.2d 968, 978 n.11 (D.C. Cir. 1988). *Kloeckner* did not merely clarify the law—it changed it. It held that the forum for review of a mixed case is determined by the nature of the underlying action, not by the Board’s reasoning. That holding renders *Powell* irreconcilable with the current statutory framework. The D.C. Circuit’s refusal to follow *Kloeckner*’s logic is not a matter of judicial restraint; it is a refusal to apply the Supreme Court’s clear directive.
