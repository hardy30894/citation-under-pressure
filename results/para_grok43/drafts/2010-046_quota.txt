The three-judge court’s population-reduction order satisfies the Prison Litigation Reform Act. The PLRA authorizes such relief only when crowding is the primary cause of Eighth Amendment violations and no narrower remedy exists. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3). The record establishes both predicates.

The district court’s findings that severe overcrowding produced unconstitutional medical and mental-health care rest on settled Eighth Amendment doctrine. Rhodes v. Chapman, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981) (conditions must not involve “the wanton infliction of pain”); Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825, 834 (1994) (prison officials must not disregard substantial risks of serious harm); Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978) (cumulative effects of overcrowding may violate the Amendment). Those findings are not clearly erroneous. Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985).

The PLRA’s “primary cause” requirement is likewise satisfied. The panel found that crowding prevented implementation of prior remedial decrees and that every other avenue—additional hiring, contracting, and construction—had failed. This determination tracks the statutory text and the principle that federal courts may address the root cause of constitutional violations. Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ., 402 U.S. 1, 16 (1971) (“[t]he nature of the violation determines the scope of the remedy”); Milliken v. Bradley, 418 U.S. 717, 744 (1974) (remedy must be tailored to the constitutional violation).

The order also meets the PLRA’s narrow-tailoring command. It sets a population cap rather than mandating releases of particular inmates and permits the State to achieve compliance through sentencing reform, parole changes, or transfers. Such flexibility accords with the requirement that injunctive relief be “no broader than necessary.” Miller v. French, 530 U.S. 327, 347 (2000); Gilmore v. California, 220 F.3d 987, 1000 (9th Cir. 2000). The 137.5 percent cap leaves the State substantial operational latitude while ending the proven constitutional violations.

Finally, the order respects federalism and comity. The court afforded the State repeated opportunities to cure the violations before entering the population cap. Stone v. City & County of San Francisco, 968 F.2d 850, 861 (9th Cir. 1992) (courts must consider “the need for public officials to have latitude in managing institutions”). The resulting decree is therefore consistent with both the PLRA and controlling precedent governing institutional-reform litigation.
