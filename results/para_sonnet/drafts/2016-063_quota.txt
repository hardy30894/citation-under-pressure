# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text and Structure of the Civil Service Reform Act Send All Mixed-Case Appeals—Including Jurisdictional Dismissals—to District Court.

Congress designed the Civil Service Reform Act to channel "mixed cases—those appealable to the MSPB and alleging discrimination" into a single forum: federal district court. *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 44 (2012). Section 7702(a)(1) defines a mixed case solely by reference to two facts: whether the employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board," and whether she "alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination." 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). Nothing in that definition turns on the *reason* the Board ultimately declines to adjudicate the appeal. As this Court explained in *Kloeckner*, "[n]o one here contests that Kloeckner brought a mixed case—that she was affected by an action (i.e., removal) appealable to the MSPB and that she alleged discrimination." 568 U.S. at 50. The same is true of Mr. Perry: he was affected by a suspension and forced retirement, actions generally appealable to the Board, and he alleged disability discrimination and retaliation as the basis for those actions.

That the Board ultimately concluded it lacked jurisdiction because it found the underlying settlement voluntary does not transform the case into something other than a mixed case. The statutory inquiry asks what the employee alleged and what kind of action affected her—not whether the Board, after full proceedings, agreed that it could hear the claim. This Court has long recognized that the CSRA's discrimination provisions were meant to preserve, not truncate, an employee's access to de novo judicial resolution of discrimination claims. *Chandler v. Roudebush*, 425 U.S. 840, 863–64 (1976) (federal employees are entitled to a "trial de novo" of discrimination claims, not mere record review). Routing jurisdictional dismissals to the Federal Circuit—a court with no authority to entertain discrimination claims at all—would deny Mr. Perry precisely the de novo forum Congress guaranteed.

## II. Kloeckner's Reasoning Forecloses Any Distinction Between "Procedural" and "Jurisdictional" Dismissals.

*Kloeckner* held that when the Board disposes of a mixed case "on procedural grounds," review lies in district court just as it would if the Board had reached the merits. 568 U.S. at 44. The Court framed the question as "whether an employee seeking judicial review should proceed in the Federal Circuit or in a district court when the MSPB has dismissed her mixed case on procedural grounds." *Id.* at 50. The Court's rationale was not narrowly confined to timeliness; it rested on the structural premise that a single tribunal—the district court—must resolve mixed cases regardless of the procedural posture in which the Board disposed of them, so that discrimination claims are not needlessly split between two judicial systems.

That rationale applies with equal force to a jurisdictional dismissal premised on voluntariness. The Board's voluntariness determination in this case was not a threshold gatekeeping inquiry divorced from discrimination; it was inextricably intertwined with Mr. Perry's claim that his retirement and suspension were coerced *by* discriminatory and retaliatory conduct. Two courts of appeals have recognized the artificiality of separating such jurisdictional and discrimination-based inquiries. *See Downey v. Runyon*, 160 F.3d 139, 145–46 (2d Cir. 1998); *Harms v. IRS*, 321 F.3d 1001, 1007–08 (10th Cir. 2003). Bifurcating review based on the label the Board attaches to its ruling—"jurisdictional" versus "procedural"—elevates form over the statutory definition of a mixed case and reintroduces the very forum-shopping problem *Kloeckner* eliminated.

The Federal Circuit's own effort to preserve this distinction, in *Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board*, 713 F.3d 1111, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013), rested on the observation that the Board may excuse procedural defects but cannot waive jurisdictional ones. That distinction concerns the Board's *administrative* discretion, not the statutory definition of what constitutes a mixed case for purposes of judicial review under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). Congress's definition looks to the nature of the action and the allegation, not to whether the Board's own regulations happen to be waivable.

## III. A Unified Forum for Discrimination-Related Claims Best Serves the CSRA's Remedial Design.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the CSRA's review provisions are meant to operate as an integrated system, avoiding jurisdictional traps for unwary employees navigating a complex statute. *See Elgin v. Dep't of Treasury*, 567 U.S. 1, 10–13 (2012) (describing the CSRA's "integrated scheme of review" for adverse personnel actions). Likewise, in construing the scope of judicial review available under the CSRA, the Court has favored predictable, employee-protective interpretations over ones that fracture review based on technical distinctions the statute itself does not draw. *Lindahl v. Office of Pers. Mgmt.*, 470 U.S. 768, 779–80 (1985).

Requiring employees to divine, before filing, whether their case will be dismissed on a "jurisdictional" or "procedural" basis—only to discover the correct forum after years of litigation—produces exactly the uncertainty *Kloeckner* sought to eliminate. The D.C. Circuit's adherence to *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), cannot survive *Kloeckner*'s holding and rationale. This Court should hold that review of Mr. Perry's mixed case belongs in district court.
