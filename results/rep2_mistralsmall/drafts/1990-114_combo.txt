**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL” WITHIN THE MEANING OF 29 U.S.C. § 630(f).**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s judges fall within the ADEA’s exception for “appointees on the policymaking level.” 898 F.2d at 602–03. This conclusion is compelled by the text and structure of the statute and by the longstanding recognition that judges exercise sovereign authority of the highest order. *See generally Gregory v. City of Chicago*, 394 U.S. 111, 112 (1969) (per curiam) (recognizing the unique constitutional status of judges).

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The legislative history confirms that Congress intended this phrase to reach high-level advisors and department heads in state and local government. *See* Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180. Missouri’s judges are precisely such policymakers. They interpret and apply the state constitution and statutes, establish binding rules of decision, and supervise the administration of justice throughout the state. *See* Mo. Const. art. V, § 4; *State ex rel. McKittrick v. Davis*, 337 Mo. 1107, 88 S.W.2d 363, 366 (1935) (holding that the judiciary is a coordinate branch of government with inherent power to supervise its own affairs).

This Court has long recognized that judges are policymakers whose decisions shape the law and public policy. *See, e.g., Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510, 523 (1927) (“Every procedure which would offer a possible temptation to the average man as a judge to forget the burden of proof required to convict the defendant, or which might lead him not to hold the balance nice, clear and true between the State and the accused denies the latter due process of law.”); *In re Griffiths*, 413 U.S. 717, 722–23 (1973) (Burger, C.J., dissenting) (noting that judges “exercise the sovereign power of the State” and “make policy affecting the entire community”). The policymaking nature of judicial work is not diminished by the fact that judges decide cases rather than formulate statutes. *See generally United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 703 (1974) (recognizing that judges “exercise a . . . judicial power, which involves both discretion and policy judgment”).

The Eighth Circuit’s construction of the ADEA’s exception is consistent with this Court’s teaching that statutes should be read in light of the traditional functions of state government. *See generally Lane County v. Oregon*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 71, 76–77 (1868) (recognizing the autonomy of state governments in structuring their courts). The ADEA’s exception for policymaking appointees reflects Congress’s respect for the traditional separation of powers and the unique role of judges in our constitutional system. *See generally Collector v. Day*, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 113, 124–25 (1870) (holding that the federal government may not interfere with the essential functions of state government).

**II. EVEN IF THE ADEA APPLIED, IT WOULD NOT PREEMPT MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT REQUIREMENT FOR JUDGES.**

The ADEA does not expressly preempt state constitutional provisions governing the tenure of judges. *See generally Pennsylvania v. Nelson*, 350 U.S. 497, 500 (1956) (holding that federal preemption requires a clear expression of congressional intent). The Eighth Circuit correctly applied the presumption against preemption in this context, recognizing that the regulation of state judges is a core state function. 898 F.2d at 601–02. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against construing federal statutes to intrude upon traditional state prerogatives without explicit direction from Congress. *See generally Coyle v. Oklahoma*, 221 U.S. 559, 569–70 (1911) (holding that the federal government may not interfere with the admission of states to the Union or their internal governance).

The ADEA’s legislative history contains no indication that Congress intended to override state mandatory retirement provisions for judges. *See generally Federal Land Bank v. Bismarck Lumber Co.*, 314 U.S. 95, 102 (1941) (holding that courts must construe statutes to avoid constitutional questions). The Eighth Circuit’s conclusion that the ADEA does not preempt Missouri’s constitutional provision is therefore correct.

**III. MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT REQUIREMENT FOR JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

The Equal Protection Clause does not forbid Missouri from requiring judges to retire at age seventy. *See generally McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425–26 (1961) (holding that classifications are valid if they bear a rational relation to a legitimate state interest). This Court has repeatedly upheld mandatory retirement laws for occupations where age affects performance. *See, e.g., Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313–14 (1976) (per curiam) (upholding mandatory retirement for state troopers at age fifty); *Dandridge v. Williams*, 397 U.S. 471, 485 (1970) (upholding welfare benefit caps); *Goesaert v. Cleary*, 335 U.S. 464, 466 (1948) (upholding gender-based bar to bartending).

Missouri has a legitimate interest in ensuring that its judges remain physically and mentally capable of performing their duties. *See Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir.) (noting that mandatory retirement reduces delays caused by death or disability), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979). The state also has an interest in maintaining public confidence in the judiciary by avoiding the perception that judges serve beyond their prime. *See Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 432, 517 N.E.2d 141 (1988) (citing cases). These interests are sufficient to satisfy the rational basis test. *See generally Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949) (holding that a classification need not be the best means of achieving a state’s objectives).

The Eighth Circuit correctly concluded that Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for judges does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. 898 F.2d at 604–05. This Court should affirm that judgment.
