The Bureau of Prisons exceeded its authority by treating a sentencing enhancement as transforming the offense of conviction into a crime of violence. The statute authorizes a reduction only for prisoners convicted of a nonviolent offense. 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). That phrase refers to the offense as defined by Congress, not to facts found at sentencing. United States v. Carll, 105 U.S. 611, 612 (1881) (the indictment must set forth every element that constitutes the offense).

Section 924(c)(3) likewise defines a crime of violence by reference to the elements of the offense or its inherent nature, not to enhancements imposed under the Sentencing Guidelines. The regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, incorporates that definition without alteration. The Program Statement, however, adds a new disqualifier whenever a weapon enhancement was applied. An administrative rule may not enlarge the statute it purports to implement. Morrill v. Jones, 106 U.S. 466, 467 (1882) (“The Secretary cannot by his regulations alter or amend a revenue law.”).

The offense here is conspiracy to distribute a controlled substance, 21 U.S.C. § 846. That crime, by its terms, requires no proof of force or risk of force against person or property. See United States v. Britton, 108 U.S. 199, 205 (1883) (conspiracy is complete upon agreement, without more). The sentencing court’s two-level adjustment addressed conduct separate from the elements of the offense. Because the conviction itself remains nonviolent, the Bureau lacked power to deny eligibility on the basis of the enhancement alone.

The writ should issue directing the Bureau to determine Roussos’s eligibility without regard to the Program Statement’s additional criterion.
