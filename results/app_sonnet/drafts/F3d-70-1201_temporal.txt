ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY TO DISREGARD EVIDENCE OF THE LENGTHENED CHUTE BECAUSE MORBARK’S OWN TRIAL STRATEGY OPENED THE DOOR TO THAT EVIDENCE FOR IMPEACHMENT.

The general rule excluding evidence of measures taken after an accident rests on a narrow rationale: a defendant should not be penalized for making a product safer, because doing so “was not to be construed as an admission of responsibility for the past.” Columbia & Puget Sound R.R. Co. v. Hawthorne, 144 U.S. 202, 207 (1892). That policy protects a defendant from having its own remedial conduct used as an admission of prior fault. It does not license a defendant to exploit the very existence of the rule to plant an affirmative, false impression with the jury — namely, that nothing about the machine had changed and that the seventeen-inch chute remained the standard, unmodified design in use everywhere, including by the Army Corps of Engineers and the City of DeFuniak Springs. Once Morbark’s counsel created that impression, in opening statement and again on cross-examination of Infinger, Wood was entitled to correct it.

This Court need look no further than the settled doctrine, recognized long before Rule 407 was drafted, that a party cannot use an exclusionary principle as “a shield against contradiction of his untruths.” Walder v. United States, 347 U.S. 62, 65 (1954). In Walder, the Supreme Court held that although illegally seized evidence could not be used affirmatively against a defendant, the defendant’s own testimony creating a false impression opened the door to impeachment with that same evidence, because “[i]t is one thing to say that the Government cannot make an affirmative use of evidence unlawfully obtained. It is quite another to say that the defendant can turn the illegal method by which evidence in the Government’s possession was obtained to his own advantage.” Id. The same logic controls here. Morbark cannot invoke the remedial-measures exclusion as a sword to suggest permanence and safety of the unmodified design, and then invoke it again as a shield to prevent Wood from showing that the design had, in fact, been changed.

This principle is not confined to the search-and-seizure context; it reflects a general evidentiary doctrine of curative admissibility that this Court has long applied to civil trials. In Michelson v. United States, 335 U.S. 469 (1948), the Supreme Court explained that a party who introduces favorable evidence on an issue “open[s] the entire subject” to rebuttal, because “[t]he price a defendant must pay for attempting to prove his good name is to throw open the entire subject which the law has kept closed for his benefit and to make himself vulnerable where the law otherwise shields him.” Id. at 479. Morbark, by suggesting to the jury that the chute remained unchanged and was still trusted by government users, threw open the very subject the in limine order had closed for its benefit. The district court itself recognized as much at trial, telling counsel that Morbark had “opened the door” by suggesting “there must not be anything wrong with this chipper if the city still continues to use it,” and that fairness required allowing Wood “to point out that there have been modifications to that chipper.” R4-141-56. That ruling was correct, and the testimony was properly admitted.

Having correctly admitted the impeachment testimony, however, the district court erred when it subsequently instructed the jury to disregard it. An instruction to disregard evidence that was properly admitted for a legitimate purpose is itself error, because it withdraws from the jury’s consideration testimony it was entitled to weigh in assessing the credibility of Morbark’s theory of the case. The court cannot simultaneously rule that Morbark “opened the door” to rebuttal and then close that same door once the rebuttal has served its purpose of correcting the false impression left with the jury.

II. THE ERRONEOUS CURATIVE INSTRUCTION AFFECTED WOOD’S SUBSTANTIAL RIGHTS AND REQUIRES A NEW TRIAL.

An error of this kind cannot be dismissed as harmless. The proper inquiry is whether, “after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole,” one can say “with fair assurance” that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946). Here, the central defect alleged in Wood’s case was the length of the infeed chute. Morbark built its defense on the implication that the seventeen-inch chute was safe, standard, and still trusted by public agencies. The only evidence capable of dispelling that false impression — that the chute had in fact been lengthened after the accident — was placed before the jury and then affirmatively withdrawn by the court’s instruction. Given the centrality of the chute-length issue to Wood’s theory of defect, this Court cannot say with fair assurance that the verdict was unaffected.

A trial court’s discretion over the conduct of trial and the granting of a new trial is broad, but it is not boundless where the court permits a party to secure an unfair advantage through selective and inconsistent application of its own evidentiary rulings. See Montgomery Ward & Co. v. Duncan, 311 U.S. 243, 251 (1940) (trial court bears responsibility to ensure that the proceedings result in a fair trial). The district court’s vacillation — admitting the impeachment testimony as a fair response to Morbark’s misleading suggestion, and then instructing the jury to ignore it — deprived Wood of the benefit of the very ruling the court had made. The motion for a new trial should have been granted.
