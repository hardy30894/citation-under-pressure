ARGUMENT

I. The Plain Text of Exemption 2 Limits Its Reach to Matters "Related Solely" to Internal Personnel Rules, and the Ninth Circuit's "Predominantly Internal" Gloss Cannot Override That Text.

Congress wrote Exemption 2 to cover only matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The word "solely" is not surplusage; it is the operative limitation Congress chose. This Court has long instructed that "where the language of an act is plain, and admits of no more than one meaning, the duty of interpretation does not arise." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The ESQD arc maps and blast-effect documents at issue here do not concern personnel rules at all—they concern the physics of explosions and the placement of ordnance. No amount of judicial reformulation can transform data about blast radii into a "personnel rule or practice."

The Ninth Circuit nonetheless adopted a "predominantly internal" test borrowed from the D.C. Circuit, reasoning that documents presenting a "risk of circumvention of agency regulation" fall within Exemption 2 even though they are not, by the statute's own terms, related "solely" to internal personnel matters. That approach substitutes a judicially crafted balancing standard for the words Congress actually enacted. But "there is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." United States v. American Trucking Assns., 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress could have written Exemption 2 to reach any material whose disclosure risked "circumvention" of agency rules generally; it did not. It chose the narrower path of "solely" internal personnel matters, and courts are not free to enlarge that choice by administrative convenience.

II. Because Exemption 2 Is an Exception to a Broad Disclosure Mandate, It Must Be Narrowly Construed, Not Expanded Beyond Its Terms.

FOIA is fundamentally a disclosure statute, and its nine exemptions are exceptions to that rule. This Court's longstanding practice with analogous remedial legislation confirms that such exceptions must be read narrowly, not stretched to swallow the rule. In A. H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling, 324 U.S. 490 (1945), the Court held that "exemptions from the Fair Labor Standards Act are to be narrowly construed against the employers seeking to assert them," and warned that "[t]o extend an exemption to other than those plainly and unmistakably within its terms and spirit is to abuse the interpretative process and to frustrate the announced will of the people." Id. at 493. The Ninth Circuit's "predominantly internal" test does precisely what Phillips forbids: it extends an exemption to material—blast-effect data shared with contractors, emergency planners, and the public safety apparatus—that is not "plainly and unmistakably within its terms and spirit."

The same principle governed Addison v. Holly Hill Fruit Products, Inc., 322 U.S. 607 (1944), where the Court refused to read an FLSA exemption expansively to cover activities Congress had not clearly placed within it, explaining that such exemptions are "limited to those plainly and unmistakably within their terms." Id. at 617. Exemption 2's text—"related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency"—cannot plainly and unmistakably be read to cover technical data about explosive yields merely because an agency also uses that data for internal planning. Documents can serve multiple purposes; the statute does not ask which purpose "predominates," it asks whether the material relates solely to personnel rules. It does not here.

III. The Remedial Purpose of FOIA Requires Broad Construction of the Disclosure Mandate and Narrow Construction of Its Exceptions.

This Court has repeatedly held that remedial statutes enacted for the public's benefit should be construed broadly to effectuate their purposes, while their exceptions are construed narrowly. In Tcherepnin v. Knight, 389 U.S. 332 (1967), the Court explained that it was "guided by the familiar canon of statutory construction that remedial legislation should be construed broadly to effectuate its purposes." Id. at 336. FOIA's purpose—vindicating the public's right to know what its government is doing—is no less remedial than the securities laws construed in Tcherepnin. The same logic animated Fishgold v. Sullivan Drydock & Repair Corp., 328 U.S. 275 (1946), where the Court insisted that protective legislation enacted "for the benefit" of a defined class be construed generously toward that class, with narrow exceptions. Id. at 285. Here, the beneficiary class is the American public, and Milner's request for information about explosive hazards near populated areas near Puget Sound lies at the core of what FOIA was designed to illuminate.

The Ninth Circuit's decision inverts this hierarchy: rather than construing the disclosure mandate broadly and the exemption narrowly, it construed the exemption expansively by importing an extratextual "circumvention of regulation" rationale untethered to personnel matters. That is the same error this Court has consistently rejected when agencies or lower courts attempt to stretch narrow statutory exceptions to serve broader administrative convenience. See Addison, 322 U.S. at 617; Phillips, 324 U.S. at 493.

CONCLUSION

The text of Exemption 2, read according to its plain terms and construed narrowly as an exception to FOIA's broad disclosure mandate, does not reach documents concerning the physical effects of explosions. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
