**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) properly exercised jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act), 29 U.S.C. § 651 et seq., to issue citations to Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., for violations occurring aboard the *MR. BELDON*, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation preempts OSHA’s authority under § 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act, even as to uninspected vessels. That holding is contrary to the plain language of the OSH Act, this Court’s precedent, and the principle that preemption must be clear and narrowly construed.

**I. The OSH Act’s jurisdiction extends to uninspected vessels, including drilling barges, when they constitute a “workplace” under § 4(a).**

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act defines the Act’s reach: “The provisions of this chapter shall apply with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The term “workplace” is not limited to land-based facilities or to vessels subject to comprehensive Coast Guard inspection. Rather, it encompasses any location where employees perform work, including mobile or floating platforms engaged in industrial operations. In *Barnes v. United States*, 412 U.S. 837 (1973), the Supreme Court held that a “workplace” under the OSH Act includes “any place where employees are engaged in employment,” emphasizing that the Act’s coverage is “broad and inclusive.” Id. at 841. The *MR. BELDON* was not merely a vessel in transit—it was a fixed, self-contained industrial platform engaged in the continuous operation of drilling a well in navigable waters. Employees lived and worked aboard it for extended periods, performing tasks that were integral to the oil and gas exploration process. This is precisely the kind of “workplace” Congress intended to cover.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. *Clary* did not hold that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over uninspected vessels. Rather, the court in *Clary* concluded that OSHA regulations did not apply to a seaman’s claim involving a hazard on a drilling barge because the *specific* OSHA standard cited—requiring yellow color-coding of deck plates—was not enforceable under the Act’s framework in that context. 609 F.2d at 1122. The court did not purport to define the scope of OSHA’s jurisdiction over uninspected vessels; it merely ruled that the particular OSHA standard at issue was inapplicable. The court’s statement that “OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation” was dicta, and was not a holding on the broader question of jurisdiction. See *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825, 826 (5th Cir. 1983) (reaffirming *Clary* but noting that the holding was limited to the specific regulatory context).

Moreover, *Donovan* itself did not establish a categorical exclusion of OSHA jurisdiction over all uninspected vessels. The court in *Donovan* stated that “OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels,” but it did so in the context of a claim involving a seaman’s personal injury on a vessel in navigation. 720 F.2d at 826. The court did not address whether OSHA could regulate the *workplace* conditions of non-seamen employed on such vessels, nor did it purport to bar OSHA from regulating uninspected vessels under any circumstances. The Fifth Circuit’s attempt to extrapolate a broad preemption doctrine from these cases misreads the holdings and ignores the statutory framework.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act does not preempt OSHA jurisdiction over uninspected vessels.**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act “shall not apply to any employee with respect to whom other Federal agencies ... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision is not self-executing; it requires a showing that a federal agency has *statutory authority* to prescribe or enforce safety standards in a given field. The Coast Guard’s authority under 46 U.S.C. § 2 is broad, but it is not plenary. The Coast Guard may regulate safety on vessels, but only to the extent permitted by statute. The fact that the Coast Guard has authority over certain safety matters—such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation systems—does not mean it has exclusive authority over all occupational safety and health conditions aboard vessels.

The Supreme Court has long held that preemption must be “clearly expressed” and not inferred from ambiguity. *Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc.*, 505 U.S. 504, 516 (1992). Here, Congress did not clearly express an intent to exclude OSHA from regulating uninspected vessels. On the contrary, the OSH Act was designed to fill regulatory gaps where other agencies have not acted. The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels are limited in scope and do not address many workplace hazards—such as chemical exposure, machine guarding, or emergency response procedures—that are within OSHA’s expertise. To hold otherwise would create a regulatory vacuum in which workers on uninspected vessels are left without federal safety protections.

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the Coast Guard’s authority is “exclusive” is unsupported by precedent. In *Barnes*, the Court emphasized that the OSH Act was intended to be “comprehensive,” not “fragmented.” 412 U.S. at 841. The Court rejected the notion that federal agencies could insulate entire industries from OSHA’s reach simply by claiming overlapping authority. The same principle applies here: the mere existence of Coast Guard regulation does not bar OSHA from regulating other aspects of workplace safety, especially where the Coast Guard’s authority is limited and non-comprehensive.
