The common law has long confined punitive damages to a narrow class of cases marked by conduct that is itself outrageous or egregious. In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1851), such awards were reserved for “cases of tort, where the injury has been wanton or malicious.” The same limitation appears in *Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore Railroad Co. v. Quigley*, 62 U.S. 202, 214 (1858), which required “such a degree of negligence or misconduct as to evince a reckless disregard of the rights of others.” 

Later decisions reiterated that the character of the act, not merely the mental state accompanying it, supplies the predicate. *Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms*, 91 U.S. 489, 495 (1875), held that exemplary damages lie only when the defendant’s conduct “has been characterized by some aggravating circumstance, such as malice, oppression, or fraud.” *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Humes*, 115 U.S. 512, 521 (1885), likewise demanded “such gross negligence as to amount to a reckless disregard of the rights of others.” 

The requirement of outrageous conduct was stated with particular clarity in *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 562 (1886): punitive damages are proper only where “the act complained of is accompanied by such circumstances of aggravation as to justify the infliction of exemplary damages.” *Denver & Rio Grande Railway Co. v. Harris*, 122 U.S. 597, 609 (1887), repeated the formula, authorizing such relief solely upon proof of “oppression, fraud, or malice, express or implied.” 

The Court returned to the point in *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), explaining that exemplary damages serve “to punish the offender and to deter others from like conduct” and therefore presuppose “a tort committed with fraud, actual malice, or deliberate violence or oppression.” These authorities establish that malice or reckless indifference, standing alone, has never sufficed; the defendant’s conduct must independently cross the threshold of egregiousness. 

Because the record contains no evidence of conduct meeting that historic standard, the judgment denying punitive damages should be affirmed.
