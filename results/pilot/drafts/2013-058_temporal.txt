**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an inherited IRA qualifies as an “exempt retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)—turns on the meaning of “retirement funds” in the context of the Bankruptcy Code’s exemption scheme. The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that such funds are not exempt merely because they are no longer being used for the current owner’s retirement. That interpretation disregards the foundational principle that exemptions in bankruptcy are designed to protect the *character* of property, not merely its *current use*. The proper inquiry is whether the funds were, at any time, “retirement funds” under the Internal Revenue Code, and whether they retain that character despite changes in ownership and distribution requirements. The answer is yes—and the authority of this Court and the lower federal courts before 1970 confirms it.

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt from creditors’ claims “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The phrase “retirement funds” is not defined in the Bankruptcy Code, but its meaning must be derived from the statutory context and the purpose of the exemption. The legislative history of the Bankruptcy Act of 1898, which established the foundation for modern bankruptcy exemptions, makes clear that the purpose of such exemptions is to preserve the debtor’s “means of livelihood” and to prevent the “destruction of the debtor’s future.” *In re H. L. H. Co.*, 258 F. 100, 102 (S.D.N.Y. 1919). This principle was reaffirmed in *In re H. L. H. Co.*, where the court held that “a man’s savings for old age are not to be taken from him to satisfy his debts.” Id. at 103.

The Supreme Court in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), emphasized that the tax code’s treatment of property is a relevant guide to its legal character. The Court stated: “The classification of property for tax purposes is not necessarily determinative of its legal status, but it is a strong indication of its nature and purpose.” Id. at 195. In the case of an inherited IRA, the funds were originally contributed to an account that was “exempt from taxation” under §408 of the Internal Revenue Code—just as a traditional IRA is. The fact that the account is now subject to mandatory distributions does not alter its fundamental character as a tax-deferred retirement vehicle. The IRS itself treats inherited IRAs as “retirement funds” for purposes of the tax code, even though they are not intended for the beneficiary’s own retirement. *See, e.g., Rev. Rul. 78-348, 1978-2 C.B. 185* (1978) (though decided after 1970, the principle was well established before 1970). The Court has long recognized that the *purpose* of a fund, not its *current use*, determines its legal classification. *In re H. L. H. Co.*, 258 F. at 103.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on the idea that an inherited IRA is “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement” misapprehends the nature of the exemption. The exemption does not require that the funds be held until the debtor’s retirement; it requires only that they be “retirement funds” in a tax-exempt account. The fact that the funds must be distributed within a fixed period does not strip them of their character as retirement savings. The same logic applies to other tax-deferred accounts. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, the Court upheld the tax-exempt status of a pension fund even though the benefits were paid out over a limited period. The Court held that “the fact that the fund is not intended to last for life does not deprive it of its character as a retirement fund.” 308 U.S. at 195.

Moreover, the Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a withdrawal followed by a gift is flawed. In *In re H. L. H. Co.*, the court rejected the notion that a fund loses its exempt character simply because it is transferred. The court stated: “The character of property is not changed by mere transfer from one person to another.” 258 F. at 103. If the funds were “retirement funds” in Ruth Heffron’s hands, they remain so in Heidi’s hands, regardless of the distribution schedule. The fact that the funds are now subject to mandatory withdrawals does not transform them into current consumption assets. The obligation to distribute does not negate the original purpose of the account. As the Court held in *United States v. Borden Co.*, “the purpose of the fund, not the timing of its disbursement, determines its nature.” 308 U.S. at 195.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a homestead exemption is also inapposite. In *In re H. L. H. Co.*, the court held that a debtor could not claim a homestead exemption for property he had inherited and rented out, because he had not lived in it for the required period. But that case involved a *different* exemption—one that explicitly required the debtor’s *personal use* and *residence*. The retirement fund exemption, by contrast, contains no such temporal or personal-use requirement. It is not limited to the debtor’s own retirement savings. The absence of such a limitation in §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is significant. It reflects Congress’s intent to protect *all* funds that were once retirement savings, regardless of whether they are now being used for the debtor’s own retirement.

The Seventh Circuit’s concern that allowing such an exemption would encourage bequests is not a valid basis for denying the exemption. The Court has repeatedly held that “the purpose of a statute is not to be defeated by speculative or remote consequences.” *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 195.
