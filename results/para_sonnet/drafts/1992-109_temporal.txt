# ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard—distinct from and more demanding than the standard for competency to stand trial—before accepting a waiver of counsel or a guilty plea. This Court's precedents establish a single, workable competency standard applicable throughout criminal proceedings, and they treat the question of a defendant's mental competency separately from the question whether a waiver of rights was knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. The court below conflated these two distinct inquiries, inventing a standard that finds no support in this Court's decisions.

## I. The Competency Standard Articulated in Dusky and Pate Governs Throughout Criminal Proceedings.

This Court held in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), that the test for competency is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. That formulation was reaffirmed in *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), where the Court held that due process is violated when a defendant lacking such capacity is subjected to trial without a competency hearing. *Id.* at 385. Nothing in *Dusky* or *Pate* suggests that a different, more demanding mental standard applies when a defendant chooses to waive counsel or to plead guilty rather than to proceed to trial. The competency inquiry asks whether the defendant possesses the capacities described in *Dusky*; it does not vary with the particular decision the defendant makes once found competent.

The Ninth Circuit's novel "reasoned choice" standard rests on the unsupported premise that waiving constitutional rights demands "a higher level of mental functioning" than standing trial. But this Court has never so held. To the contrary, *Dusky*'s standard already requires the capacity for rational understanding and communication with counsel—precisely the faculties necessary to make reasoned decisions about how to proceed. A defendant who satisfies *Dusky* is, by definition, capable of understanding the proceedings and consulting rationally with counsel about them, including decisions to waive counsel or plead guilty.

## II. The Validity of a Waiver Turns on Whether It Was Knowing, Intelligent, and Voluntary—A Distinct Inquiry from Competency.

This Court's waiver jurisprudence, from *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), onward, asks whether a waiver of the right to counsel was made "knowingly and intelligently." *Id.* at 464. That inquiry examines the defendant's awareness of the right being relinquished and the consequences of relinquishing it—not some freestanding, elevated psychiatric threshold. In *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), the Court recognized that a defendant may waive the right to counsel so long as the waiver is "an intelligent and competent" one, assessed on the totality of the circumstances of the particular case. *Id.* at 279. Similarly, in *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), the Court required that a waiver of counsel be made "with eyes open," *id.* at 723 (plurality opinion), focusing the inquiry on the defendant's understanding of the charges, possible defenses, and consequences—not on a separate mental-capacity standard superior to the competency-to-stand-trial standard.

The same principle governs pleas of guilty. In *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), the Court required an affirmative showing on the record that a guilty plea was made voluntarily and understandingly, emphasizing that such a plea "is itself a conviction" and works a waiver of multiple constitutional rights. *Id.* at 242. But *Boykin*'s concern was with the adequacy of the record demonstrating voluntariness and understanding of the rights waived—not with imposing a heightened psychiatric competency threshold beyond that required by *Dusky*. Likewise, in *Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), the Court examined only whether the record showed an understanding and voluntary waiver, not whether the defendant met some elevated standard of mental function. And in *Moore v. Michigan*, 355 U.S. 155 (1957), the Court's focus remained on whether the waiver of counsel was "understandingly made," *id.* at 161, again a voluntariness-and-comprehension inquiry rather than a separate competency standard.

These cases demonstrate that this Court has consistently treated (1) whether a defendant is competent, and (2) whether a defendant's waiver was knowing and voluntary, as two distinct due process requirements—each satisfied by different means. The competency requirement protects against trying, sentencing, or accepting pleas from defendants who cannot understand the proceedings or assist in their defense, as in *Dusky* and *Pate*. The knowing-and-voluntary requirement, addressed in *Johnson v. Zerbst*, *Von Moltke*, and *Boykin*, protects against defendants relinquishing rights without adequate information or under compulsion. Nothing requires merging these two distinct doctrines into a single heightened "reasoned choice" standard.

## III. The Trial Court's Procedure Satisfied Due Process.

The Nevada trial court had before it psychiatric findings addressing Moran's competency under the *Dusky* standard and separately conducted a colloquy addressing his understanding of the rights being waived, consistent with *Von Moltke* and *Boykin*. This dual approach—assessing competency under one standard and assessing the voluntariness and comprehension of the waiver under another—is exactly what this Court's precedents require. There is no basis in *Dusky*, *Pate*, *Johnson v. Zerbst*, *Von Moltke*, or *Boykin* for demanding more.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
