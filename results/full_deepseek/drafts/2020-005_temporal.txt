## Argument

The Due Process Clause permits Montana to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company because Ford’s contacts with the State are sufficient and the claims “arise out of or relate to” those contacts. Ford purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities in Montana, and the exercise of jurisdiction comports with traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.

**I. Ford’s Contacts with Montana Satisfy the Minimum Contacts Standard**

A state may exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant who has “certain minimum contacts with it such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend ‘traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.’” *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). Ford’s contacts with Montana are neither random nor fortuitous. Ford advertises its vehicles in Montana, sells parts and accessories through authorized dealers, provides warranty services, and maintains a nationwide distribution system designed to place its products into every state, including Montana. These activities demonstrate that Ford “purposefully avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws.” *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958).

The stream-of-commerce theory, recognized well before 1970, confirms that a manufacturer that places its products into the national market with the expectation that they will reach consumers in a particular forum has sufficient contacts with that forum. In *Gray v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp.*, 22 Ill. 2d 432, 176 N.E.2d 761 (1961), the Illinois Supreme Court held that a foreign manufacturer of a water heater was subject to jurisdiction in Illinois even though the heater was sold to a Pennsylvania distributor and then resold to an Illinois consumer. The court reasoned that the manufacturer “should be subject to the jurisdiction of the courts of those states for injuries caused by defects in the product” because it “placed his product in the stream of commerce, expecting it to be purchased by consumers in other states.” *Id.* at 766. Similarly, Ford expects its vehicles to be driven in Montana, and it actively cultivates the Montana market through advertising and parts sales. Ford’s contacts are therefore deliberate and continuous.

**II. The Claims “Arise Out of or Relate to” Ford’s Contacts with Montana**

The respondent’s claims—design defect, failure to warn, and negligence—do not depend on where the specific vehicle was first sold. They arise from Ford’s uniform design, manufacturing, and marketing decisions. Those decisions are the same for every Ford Explorer, regardless of the point of sale. Because Ford’s contacts with Montana are part of the same course of conduct that gave rise to the allegedly defective product, the claims “relate to” those contacts. *See McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957) (a single contract with a forum resident can support jurisdiction when the claim arises from that contract). Here, Ford’s contacts with Montana are far more extensive than a single contract; they include systematic advertising and parts distribution.

The Due Process Clause does not require that the specific contact that caused the injury be the same contact that establishes jurisdiction. In *Travelers Health Ass’n v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643 (1950), the Court upheld jurisdiction over a Nebraska insurance association that solicited members in Virginia by mail, even though the association had no office or agents in Virginia. The Court emphasized that the association’s “business activities” in Virginia were sufficient to subject it to suit there. *Id.* at 648-49. Here, Ford’s business activities in Montana—advertising, selling parts, and servicing vehicles—are analogous. The fact that the particular Explorer was first sold in Washington does not sever the connection between Ford’s Montana contacts and the claims. The vehicle was designed and manufactured by Ford, and Ford’s failure to warn Montana consumers of the alleged defect is a claim that directly relates to Ford’s presence in the State.

**III. Exercise of Jurisdiction Comports with Fair Play and Substantial Justice**

Ford could reasonably anticipate being haled into court in Montana for claims arising from its products used there. *See International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 317. Ford’s nationwide marketing and distribution system ensures that its vehicles are driven in every state. By choosing to sell vehicles that will inevitably be used in Montana, Ford “has clear notice that it is subject to suit there.” *Hanson*, 357 U.S. at 253. Montana has a strong interest in providing a forum for its residents injured by defective products, and the burden on Ford of defending in Montana is minimal given its extensive business operations in the State.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford’s contacts with Montana are sufficient to support specific jurisdiction. The Due Process Clause requires no more.
