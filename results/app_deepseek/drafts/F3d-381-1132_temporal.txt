**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government because the government’s installation of fiber optic cables with capacity far exceeding its own needs, and its subsequent leasing of that excess capacity to third parties, constitutes a physical occupation of Tuthill’s property beyond the scope of the easement—a per se taking under the Fifth Amendment.

**I. The Government’s Actions Exceed the Scope of the Easement and Constitute a Physical Occupation**

The easement granted in 1953 authorized the government to erect “appurtenant signal lines … necessary in connection with” the power transmission lines. The government admits it installed cables with 36 or 72 fibers, but currently needs only a fraction of those fibers for its own operations. The remainder is leased to a telecommunications company for profit. This use is not “necessary” for the power lines; it is a commercial venture. When a government exceeds the scope of an easement, its physical presence on the land becomes a trespass and a taking. *See United States v. Welch*, 217 U.S. 333, 339 (1910) (government’s use of land beyond the granted easement is a taking); *Western Union Telegraph Co. v. Pennsylvania R.R. Co.*, 195 U.S. 540, 570 (1904) (use of easement for purposes not contemplated by the grant is an additional burden on the servient estate).

The lower court erroneously concluded that “the scope of the easement is not relevant” to a physical taking claim. But the Fifth Amendment protects against any permanent physical occupation, regardless of whether the occupier holds a preexisting easement. *See United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 265 (1946) (government’s use of airspace above land, even if not a trespass, can be a taking if it destroys the owner’s use). Here, the government’s fiber optic cables are a permanent physical fixture on Tuthill’s land. The fact that the government had an easement for “signal lines” does not authorize it to install a vastly oversized system for commercial leasing. *See Pumpelly v. Green Bay Co.*, 80 U.S. 166, 177-78 (1871) (permanent flooding of land, even if authorized by statute, is a taking). The government’s installation of cables with excess capacity is no different from building a larger structure than the easement permits—a physical occupation of the land not consented to.

**II. The Lease of Excess Capacity to Third Parties Is a Separate Physical Occupation**

The government argues that leasing unused fibers does not increase the physical burden on Tuthill’s land. But the physical occupation is the cable itself. Once the government installed cables with fibers it did not need, it occupied Tuthill’s land with property that serves a purpose—commercial telecommunications—wholly unrelated to the easement. That occupation is permanent and exclusive. *See United States v. General Motors Corp.*, 323 U.S. 373, 378 (1945) (a taking occurs when the government “takes possession of the property” and “deprives the owner of all beneficial use”); *Portsmouth Harbor Land & Hotel Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 327, 329 (1922) (repeated firing of cannon over land, though not a physical entry, can be a taking if it substantially impairs the owner’s rights). Here, the government’s cables occupy space that Tuthill could otherwise use or lease. The fact that the government leases the fibers to a third party does not change the character of the occupation; it merely confirms that the government is using the land for a purpose beyond the easement. *See St. Louis v. Western Union Telegraph Co.*, 148 U.S. 92, 98-99 (1893) (erection of telegraph poles on city streets without consent is a taking, even if the city had granted a franchise to others).

**III. The Lower Court’s Focus on “No Additional Physical Intrusion” Is Misplaced**

The lower court reasoned that because the fiber optic cables are within the physical footprint of the easement, there is no “additional physical intrusion.” But the relevant inquiry is whether the government’s use exceeds the scope of the easement, not whether the cables occupy new ground. *See United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 749 (1947) (a taking may occur even if the government does not physically enter the land, as when flooding gradually destroys property). Here, the government’s installation of cables with capacity far beyond its needs is a new and independent physical occupation. The easement did not authorize the government to install a commercial telecommunications network. *See Brown v. United States*, 263 U.S. 78, 82 (1923) (government’s use of land for a purpose not within the scope of the original grant is a taking). The lower court’s error was to treat the easement as a blank check for any physical installation, regardless of purpose. That cannot be squared with the Fifth Amendment’s protection against uncompensated physical occupations.

**Conclusion**

The government’s installation of fiber optic cables with excess capacity and its leasing of that capacity to third parties is a physical occupation of Tuthill’s land beyond the scope of the easement. This is a per se taking under the Fifth Amendment. The judgment of the Court of Federal Claims should be reversed, and the case remanded for determination of just compensation.
