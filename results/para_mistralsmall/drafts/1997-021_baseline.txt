**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Contrary to the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll employees only if it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” is inconsistent with the Act and unsupported by reasoned decisionmaking. *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41 (1987). The Board’s own precedent acknowledges that polling serves a legitimate purpose: enabling employers to obtain “more certain, precise information” about employee support before taking adverse action. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989). Yet the Board insists that an employer must already possess “objective indications” of a loss of majority support—i.e., the very evidence that would justify withdrawal of recognition—before polling. This conflates the evidentiary threshold for polling with that for withdrawal, rendering polling superfluous. *See id.* at 1061–62.

The Board’s approach is arbitrary because it ignores the distinct functions of polling and withdrawal. Withdrawal of recognition is a unilateral act that disrupts bargaining relationships, whereas polling is merely an information-gathering tool. *Compare* 29 U.S.C. § 158(a)(5) (prohibiting refusal to bargain) *with* *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967) (permitting polling to test union support). The Board’s refusal to permit polling absent evidence sufficient for withdrawal is “inconsistent” and “anomalous.” *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). As the Sixth Circuit recognized, the Board’s rule “render[s] employer polls useless” by requiring employers to already possess the information polling is meant to provide. *Id.*

The Board’s standard also conflicts with the Act’s policy favoring elections as the preferred method for resolving representational disputes. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 159(c). If the Board permits elections based on a lesser showing of “substantial, objective evidence” of a loss of support, *see A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981), it cannot rationally require a higher threshold for polling, which serves the same purpose. The Board’s arbitrary distinction between polling and elections undermines the Act’s goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships. *See Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB*, 437 U.S. 483, 500–01 (1978) (deferring to Board’s policy judgments where reasoned).

**II. The Board’s Factfinding That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The Board improperly discounted employee statements and misapplied its own standards.

First, the Board erred in excluding from consideration employees hired after January 25, 1991—the date Allentown Mack announced its poll. The Board’s focus on that date was arbitrary; the relevant inquiry is whether the employer had a reasonable doubt *at the time of polling*. *See Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc. v. NLRB*, 494 U.S. 775, 787 (1990) (employer’s doubt must be “objective” and “reasonable” at the time of action). The Board’s rigid temporal limitation ignored that newly hired employees could provide contemporaneous evidence of waning union support.

Second, the Board improperly discounted statements by Ridgick and Marsh. Ridgick’s 1986 inquiry about decertification, though remote, was probative of his longstanding dissatisfaction with the union. *See Destileria Serralles, Inc. v. NLRB*, 289 N.L.R.B. 51, 53 (1988) (employee dissatisfaction with union services may evidence opposition to representation). Marsh’s statement about union dues was similarly probative; dissatisfaction with union services is a recognized indicator of waning support. *Id.* The Board’s refusal to credit these statements was arbitrary and capricious. *See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 490 (1951) (reviewing courts must defer to Board’s credibility findings but not its unreasonable evidentiary conclusions).

Third, the Board improperly discounted Bloch’s and Mohr’s statements. Bloch’s report that the night shift opposed the union was credible; the Board’s refusal to credit it because Bloch did not testify was unreasonable. *See NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) (Board may not arbitrarily reject probative evidence). Mohr’s statement that the union would lose a vote was likewise probative; the Board’s characterization of it as “off-the-cuff” was unsupported by the record.

Finally, the poll itself—conducted under the supervision of a neutral priest and yielding a 19-to-13 vote against the union—demonstrated that the union lacked majority support. The Board’s refusal to credit the poll’s results was arbitrary, as the poll complied with all procedural safeguards. *See Struksnes*, 165 N.L.R.B. at 1063 (polling is permissible if conducted fairly).

**Conclusion**

The Board’s polling standard is arbitrary and contrary to the Act. Even if the standard were valid, the Board’s factfinding is unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should reverse the Board’s order and remand with instructions to dismiss the complaint.
