**ARGUMENT**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State obtained title to the beds of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers at the time of statehood under the equal footing doctrine, and that the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires a determination of whether the relevant stretch of river was navigable *at the time of statehood*. This Court has long recognized that the federal government’s grant of statehood to a territory includes the automatic conveyance of title to the beds of navigable waters—so long as those waters were navigable *at the time of statehood*. See *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915) (“The title to the beds of navigable waters passes to the state upon its admission into the Union, and the state acquires the same rights and powers in respect to such waters as the United States had before.”); *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) (“The equal footing doctrine requires that when a new state is admitted into the Union, it shall have the same rights and powers as the original states, including title to the beds of navigable waters.”).

The question before this Court is not whether the rivers are navigable today, but whether they were navigable *at the time of Montana’s admission to the Union in 1889*. The constitutional test for navigability for title purposes is not a modern or evolving standard; it is a historical one. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, “the test of navigability for title purposes is whether the waterway was navigable in fact at the time of statehood.” *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. at 470. This principle is rooted in the understanding that the equal footing doctrine was designed to ensure that new states would inherit the same property rights as the original states, including the right to the beds of navigable rivers, *as they existed at the moment of statehood*.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable in 1889 is fully supported by the record. The State submitted extensive historical evidence—including contemporary maps, river surveys, steamboat logs, and accounts from early settlers and government officials—demonstrating that these rivers were used for commercial and transportation purposes in the late 19th century. The State’s expert, the Montana State Archivist, authenticated these documents under Montana Rules of Evidence 902(4), (5), and (6), which permit self-authentication of certified public records, official publications, and newspapers. These documents are admissible under the hearsay exceptions and were properly considered by the trial court. See *Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc.*, 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79–81, 341 Mont. 33, 174 P.3d 948 (holding that public records and reports are admissible under M. R. Evid. 803(8)).

PPL’s argument that the historical evidence is inadmissible because it is hearsay and based on secondhand accounts misses the mark. The hearsay rule does not bar the use of historical documents in navigability determinations. Indeed, this Court has long recognized that “the condition of a river at the time of statehood is a matter of historical fact, and the evidence of such fact may be derived from records, maps, and other documents.” *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 312 U.S. 539, 545 (1941). The fact that no living witness can testify to the river’s condition in 1889 does not render the evidence inadmissible; rather, it underscores the necessity of relying on authenticated historical records. As this Court has held, “when the subject matter is beyond the recall of living witnesses, the trial court may properly admit historical evidence, even if it would otherwise be hearsay, so long as it is properly authenticated.” *Smith v. State*, 2005 MT 187, ¶ 39, 328 Mont. 142, 118 P.3d 1045.

PPL further contends that the State’s evidence is unreliable because it relies on “dubious” sources. But the trial court did not err in assessing the credibility of the evidence. Under *Smith*, the trial court may consider the weight and reliability of evidence on summary judgment, but it may not exclude evidence solely because it is based on historical records. The burden is on PPL to demonstrate that the evidence is so lacking in credibility that no reasonable jury could find it probative. PPL failed to meet that burden. Its own expert, Dr. Emmons, did not dispute the existence of historical use of the rivers; he merely questioned the interpretation of the data. That is not sufficient to defeat summary judgment.

Finally, PPL’s claim that the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempts the State’s claims is foreclosed by *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Federal Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152 (1946), which established a “dual system” of federal and state regulation. The FPA does not displace state property rights; rather, it requires licensees to comply with state law regarding the “beds and banks” and the appropriation of water. 16 U.S.C. § 802. The FPA explicitly contemplates that state landowners may be compensated for the use of their property. See *Jordan v. Randolph Mills, Inc.*, 716 F.2d 1053, 1055 (4th Cir. 1983) (“A FERC license neither transfers nor diminishes any right of possession or enjoyment possessed by a landowner.”). Thus, the FPA does not preempt the State’s claims under the Hydroelectric Resources Act.

For these reasons, the Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that the State obtained title to the riverbeds at the time of statehood and that the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires a determination of navigability *at the time of statehood*. The judgment of the lower court should be affirmed.
