## ARGUMENT

### I. An Inmate Has No Constitutionally Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings

The Due Process Clause does not apply to clemency proceedings because they are not part of the adjudicatory process that determines guilt or sentence. Clemency is an act of grace, not a right. *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981) (pardon and commutation decisions “have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review”). The Ohio Constitution vests the governor with “almost unfettered discretion” to grant clemency. *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St. 3d 513, 520 (1994). The state has not created a liberty interest in clemency because the governing statute imposes no mandatory criteria or substantive restrictions. *See Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 465–66 (statute creating possibility of commutation, without standards, does not create protected interest).

The court of appeals erred by relying on *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), to hold that clemency is “an integral part” of the adjudicatory system. *Evitts* addressed the right to effective assistance of counsel on a first appeal as of right—a proceeding that is a direct extension of the trial. Clemency, by contrast, is an executive function that occurs after all judicial proceedings are exhausted. *Dumschat* specifically rejected the argument that clemency is a “regular and critical component” of the decisionmaking process, *id.* at 470 (Stevens, J., dissenting), and the majority held that no due process attaches. *Id.* at 464. Even if some minimal due process could apply, the court of appeals’ remand for the district court to define such process is unwarranted because Woodard’s underlying life and liberty interests are fully protected by the trial and appellate process that already afforded him due process. *See Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976) (no liberty interest where state retains discretion). The Constitution does not require that every state-created procedure, no matter how far removed from the trial, be constitutionalized.

### II. The Voluntary Interview Option Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment

The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled self-incrimination. Ohio’s clemency regulation does not compel any inmate to speak. The interview is entirely voluntary; the inmate may request it or decline. The regulation provides that the interview is conducted at the inmate’s request and that only the inmate and assigned personnel are present. No provision requires the inmate to answer any question. As the Supreme Court held in *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316 (1976), a prisoner’s “voluntary” choice to speak does not violate the Fifth Amendment, and any claim of compulsion is “premature” when the state has not yet compelled testimony. *Id.* at 324.

Woodard’s argument that the interview presents an unconstitutional condition—requiring him to waive his Fifth Amendment rights to obtain a favorable clemency recommendation—is speculative. The state has not conditioned clemency on participation in the interview. *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 434 (1984) (no violation where the defendant “is not compelled to answer” and “remains free to decline to answer”). The mere possibility that volunteering information could later be used against him does not constitute compulsion. *Id.* at 427–28. The court of appeals’ remand for the district court to assess the “risk” of self-incrimination is premature and unnecessary. No actual threat of use exists because the regulation does not authorize the state to compel answers, and Woodard has not alleged that any prosecutor has threatened to use his statements. *See Baxter*, 425 U.S. at 324 (“no evidence” of compulsion).

Moreover, the Fifth Amendment does not bar the state from offering an inmate the opportunity to speak in the clemency context. The state has a legitimate interest in obtaining information relevant to clemency. *See Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 288 (1998) (clemency interview is “a voluntary proceeding” that does not implicate the privilege against self-incrimination). The court of appeals erred in requiring further proceedings on a claim that is both factually unfounded and legally foreclosed.

### Conclusion

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Woodard has no protected interest in clemency, and Ohio’s voluntary interview procedure does not violate the Fifth Amendment.
