**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court’s certification order violates Rule 23(b)(3) because it rests on an unsupported assumption that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). The Third Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that this predominance inquiry must be rigorous and may not be satisfied by speculative or conjectural evidence. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 391 F.2d 555, 565 (2d Cir. 1968), *aff’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds*, 417 U.S. 156 (1974); *see also* *Kauffman v. Dreyfus Fund, Inc.*, 434 F.2d 727, 734 (3d Cir. 1970) (holding that class certification must be denied where damages cannot be proven on a class-wide basis).

Here, the District Court erred by certifying the class based solely on Dr. McClave’s methodology, which the court itself described as “speculative” and “unverified.” *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 189 (E.D. Pa. 2010). The court’s reliance on this methodology was improper because it failed to ensure that damages could actually be quantified on a class-wide basis. *See* *Kaufman v. Motorola, Inc.*, 167 F. Supp. 174, 178 (E.D. Pa. 1958) (denying class certification where damages were not susceptible to class-wide proof).

II. The District Court’s certification order improperly resolves merits issues at the class certification stage.

The Third Circuit has long cautioned against resolving merits issues during class certification. *See* *Kauffman*, 434 F.2d at 734 (noting that class certification should not be used to adjudicate the merits of a claim). The Supreme Court has likewise warned that courts must not conduct a “merits inquiry” disguised as a class certification analysis. *Eisen*, 391 F.2d at 565.

In this case, the District Court improperly resolved the merits of the damages calculation by accepting Dr. McClave’s methodology without rigorous scrutiny. The court’s order effectively decided that Comcast’s conduct caused damages to the class, a merits determination that cannot be made at the certification stage. *See* *Kaufman*, 167 F. Supp. at 178 (holding that class certification must be denied where the merits of damages are intertwined with liability).

III. The District Court’s certification order fails to satisfy Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance requirement because it does not ensure that common questions will predominate over individual ones.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that common questions “predominate” over individual ones. *See* Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). The Third Circuit has held that this requirement is not met where individual damages calculations would overwhelm the litigation. *Kauffman*, 434 F.2d at 734.

Here, the District Court’s certification order does not ensure that common questions will predominate. The court’s own findings acknowledge that damages will vary among class members, yet it certified the class anyway. This violates Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance requirement. *See* *Kaufman*, 167 F. Supp. at 178 (denying certification where damages would require individualized proof).

IV. The District Court’s certification order conflicts with the Supreme Court’s decision in *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555 (1931), which requires that damages be proven with reasonable certainty.

In *Story Parchment*, the Supreme Court held that damages must be proven with reasonable certainty and cannot be based on speculation. *Id.* at 563. The Third Circuit has followed this principle in class certification contexts. *See* *Kauffman*, 434 F.2d at 734.

Here, the District Court’s certification order allows damages to be calculated based on Dr. McClave’s speculative methodology, which does not satisfy the reasonable certainty standard. *See* *Kaufman*, 167 F. Supp. at 178.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the District Court’s certification order must be reversed. The order violates Rule 23(b)(3) because it rests on speculative damages evidence, improperly resolves merits issues, and fails to ensure that common questions predominate. The Court should remand with instructions to decertify the class.
