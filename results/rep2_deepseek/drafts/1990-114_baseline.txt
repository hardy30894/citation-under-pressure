**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Prohibit Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Provision for State Judges**

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office … or … an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Appointed state judges are indisputably “appointee[s] on the policymaking level” and therefore fall outside the ADEA’s ambit.

Judges engage in policymaking by “lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). Indeed, when Missouri judges decide close or novel questions, their decisions “create law.” *See Gustafson v. Benda*, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc) (adopting comparative negligence). Moreover, Missouri judges exercise supervisory authority—the Chief Justice and presiding judges adopt rules of court, govern the bar, and resolve administrative disputes. *Mo. Const. art. V, § 4*. These responsibilities place judges on the “policymaking level” for purposes of § 630(f).

Reading the exception to exclude appointed judges would produce the absurd result that *all* appointed state judges are covered by the ADEA while *all* elected state judges are exempt. Congress did not intend such a dichotomy. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 680 F. Supp. 455, 460–61 (D. Mass. 1988). The legislative history—stating that the exemption covers “persons appointed … to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments”—confirms that state judiciaries are core “agencies” of state government. *Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers*, 1972 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2179, 2180.

Furthermore, “in traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced … the critical matters involved.” *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). The tenure of state judges is “a matter of considerable importance to a state.” *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 601 (8th Cir. 1990). Congress did not clearly and unequivocally manifest an intent to preempt state constitutional provisions like Missouri’s. Because the text of § 630(f) plainly excludes appointed state judges as “appointee[s] on the policymaking level,” the Supremacy Clause does not require striking down Missouri’s retirement mandate.

**II. Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause**

The Fourteenth Amendment requires only that a state law bear a rational relationship to a legitimate government interest when, as here, it does not burden a suspect class or fundamental right. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 312 (1976) (per curiam). Age is not a suspect classification, *id.* at 314, and no fundamental right to public employment exists, *id.* at 313. Rational-basis review is “especially deferential” and upholds a classification if any “reasonably conceivable state of facts” provides a rational basis. *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993).

Missouri’s mandatory retirement at age 70 serves several legitimate interests. First, mandatory retirement “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged … judges.” *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980). Second, it “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges,” the risk of which increases with age. *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir. 1979). Third, it “assure[s] that important decisions … will be made by judges who command respect because they share experiences and understandings similar to those of the majority of adult citizens.” *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988). These rationales are “not constitutionally invalid under the Equal Protection Clause.” *Vance v. Bradley*, 440 U.S. 93, 112 (1979) (upholding mandatory retirement age of 60 for foreign service officers). That some might disagree with the empirical wisdom of Missouri’s choice does not render it irrational. *Id.* at 111.

Because Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision is rationally related to the legitimate state objectives of maintaining a competent judiciary, avoiding the costs of individualized removal, and ensuring public confidence, it complies with the Equal Protection Clause. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
