**ARGUMENT**

Petitioners Intel Corporation Investment Policy Committee respectfully submit that the three-year statute of limitations in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) bars an ERISA claim when the defendant disclosed all relevant information, even if the plaintiff chose not to read or cannot recall having read that information. The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that “actual knowledge of the breach or violation” requires proof that the plaintiff subjectively knew the defendant’s conduct was imprudent, and by allowing a plaintiff’s self-serving testimony of unawareness to defeat summary judgment despite undisputed evidence of disclosure.

The statutory text is clear. Section 1113(2) provides that no action may be commenced more than “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The statute requires knowledge of the _breach_—the act or omission that violates ERISA—not knowledge of its legal characterization. This Court has long recognized that statutes of limitations run from the date a plaintiff knows the facts giving rise to the claim, not the date he learns their legal significance. _United States v. Kubrick_, 444 U.S. 111, 119-20 (1979) (rejecting argument “that Congress meant to postpone accrual of a claim until the plaintiff has knowledge of the legal significance of the facts”). The same principle applies here. “Actual knowledge of the breach or violation” means knowledge of the underlying transaction or conduct, not knowledge that the conduct was imprudent under § 1104. _See Merck & Co. v. Reynolds_, 559 U.S. 633, 647-48 (2010) (construing analogous “actual discovery” standard to require knowledge of the facts constituting the violation, not the legal violation itself).

The Ninth Circuit’s own precedents before this case uniformly recognized that actual knowledge of the transaction suffices. In _Blanton v. Anzalone_, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that § 1113 is “triggered by the [plaintiff’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law.” In _Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists Pension Plan_, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988), the court applied the same rule: the limitations period begins when the plaintiff “had knowledge of the transaction, though he may not have known at the time that the reduction in benefits was unlawful under ERISA.” And in _Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co._, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990), the court found actual knowledge where the plan administrators were informed of the specific transaction that allegedly violated § 1104. These cases correctly hold that a plaintiff need not know the legal conclusion of imprudence.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision below departed from this uniform authority by requiring actual knowledge both “that those investments occurred, and that they were imprudent.” That extra requirement reads a legal element into the statute. Imprudence is the legal standard for breach under § 1104; it is not a fact that a plaintiff can “know” independently of legal training. By requiring plaintiffs to know that the investments were imprudent, the Ninth Circuit conflates actual knowledge with a legal opinion, contrary to _Kubrick_ and the plain meaning of § 1113(2).

The Ninth Circuit’s error also undermines the summary judgment standard. Intel presented undisputed evidence that it disclosed all material facts about the alternative investments—Fund Fact Sheets, plan documents, and website disclosures—before October 29, 2012. Sulyma admitted he visited the websites and received the documents, but testified that he did not read or could not recall reading the relevant information. That testimony does not create a genuine dispute of material fact. Under Rule 56, “[w]hen the moving party has carried its burden under Rule 56(c), its opponent must do more than simply show that there is some metaphysical doubt as to the material facts.” _Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc._, 477 U.S. 242, 248-49 (1986). A plaintiff’s bare assertion of ignorance, contradicted by evidence of his access to and occasional review of documents, is insufficient to withstand summary judgment. _See Celotex Corp. v. Catrett_, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986) (nonmoving party must come forward with more than a “scintilla” of evidence).

Permitting a plaintiff to defeat the statute of limitations by claiming he did not read or cannot recall reading plainly disclosed information would eviscerate § 1113. Congress deliberately removed the constructive-knowledge provision from § 1113 in 1987, but it did not intend to insulate plaintiffs who willfully ignore available information. _See_ Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. The remaining “actual knowledge” standard requires that a plaintiff know the underlying facts of the alleged breach. When those facts are undisputed and disclosed, a plaintiff cannot avoid the limitations period by claiming he chose not to learn them.

This Court should reverse and hold that a plaintiff has “actual knowledge of the breach or violation” under § 1113(2) when he knows the facts of the transaction or conduct that allegedly violated ERISA, regardless of whether he knew that conduct was legally imprudent. Where, as here, the defendant disclosed those facts and the plaintiff had access to them, summary judgment is proper even if the plaintiff claims he did not read or cannot recall reading the disclosures.
