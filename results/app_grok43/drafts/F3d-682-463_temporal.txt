ARGUMENT

I. The Record Establishes Harassment Because Of Sex.

Title VII forbids employment discrimination because of sex. The evidence shows that Ottobre directed sexually explicit comments and physical contact at Wasek precisely because Wasek is male. In Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303, 310 (1880), the Court explained that the law secures to every person “exemption from legal discriminations” that impose disabilities on account of an immutable personal characteristic. The same principle governs here: Ottobre’s repeated references to Wasek’s “pretty mouth” and “pretty lips,” coupled with the grabbing and prodding, were not random; they were aimed at Wasek’s sex. See also Plessy v. Ferguson, 163 U.S. 537, 559 (1896) (Harlan, J., dissenting) (condemning any “arbitrary separation” founded on a personal trait the law must ignore).

The employer’s refusal to intervene confirms the sex-based nature of the conduct. When Wasek reported the touching and remarks, his supervisors responded with suggestions that he “kick [Ottobre’s] ass” or “duke it out.” Such advice treated the harassment as ordinary male-on-male friction rather than the sex-based abuse the statute prohibits. Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R. Co., 323 U.S. 192, 204 (1944), held that an employer may not “ignore” complaints that arise from prohibited discrimination; the same duty applies under Title VII.

Because the undisputed facts demonstrate that the hostile acts occurred because of Wasek’s sex, summary judgment was improper. The district court’s contrary ruling must be reversed.
