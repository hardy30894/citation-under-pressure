The district court erred in granting summary judgment to BNSF Railway Company on the appellants' retaliation claims under the Federal Railroad Safety Act (FRSA), 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under the FRSA, the appellants must show that (1) they engaged in a protected activity, (2) BNSF knew about the protected activity, (3) they suffered an adverse employment action, and (4) the circumstances raise an inference that the protected activity was a contributing factor in the adverse employment action. See Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co., 768 F.3d 786, 789 (8th Cir. 2014).

The appellants engaged in protected activity by reporting the unsafe conditions at the bridge, including the lack of side rails and lighting, to BNSF Claims Representatives Murphy and in their written statements to Trainmaster Dixon. See 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C) (protecting employees who report, in good faith, a hazardous safety or security condition). BNSF was aware of the appellants' protected activity, as evidenced by the statements they provided. See Araujo v. N.J. Transit Rail Operations, Inc., 708 F.3d 152, 158 (3d Cir. 2013) (holding that an employer's knowledge of an employee's protected activity can be established through circumstantial evidence).

The appellants suffered adverse employment actions when BNSF disciplined them for alleged rule infractions, resulting in Foster's termination, Kline's record suspension, and Snyder's formal reprimand. See 49 U.S.C. § 20109(d)(2)(A) (defining an adverse action as a "discharge, demotion, suspension, reprimand, or reduction in hours or pay"). The discipline imposed on the appellants constitutes an adverse employment action, even though the Public Law Board later modified the discipline. See BNSF Ry. Co. v. U.S. Dep't of Labor Admin. Review Bd., 867 F.3d 942, 946 (8th Cir. 2017) (holding that the FRSA's anti-retaliation provision applies to disciplinary actions, even if later modified or overturned).

The circumstances raise an inference that the appellants' protected activity was a contributing factor in the adverse employment action. The temporal proximity between the appellants' reports of the unsafe conditions and the disciplinary actions taken against them supports an inference of retaliation. See Black v. BNSF Ry. Co., 848 F.3d 1184, 1190 (8th Cir. 2017) (holding that temporal proximity can be sufficient to establish a causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse employment action). Furthermore, the inconsistent testimony of Knutstrom and Dixon, who claimed that the crew-change location was east of the bridge, despite the appellants' assertions that they were told to change crews west of the bridge, raises suspicions about BNSF's true motives. See Gunderson v. BNSF Ry. Co., 850 F.3d 962, 969 (8th Cir. 2017) (holding that an employer's shifting explanations for an adverse employment action can be evidence of pretext).

BNSF's argument that the appellants were disciplined for legitimate, non-retaliatory reasons is not supported by the record. The evidence suggests that BNSF's proffered reasons for disciplining the appellants may be pretextual. See Heim v. BNSF Ry. Co., 849 F.3d 723, 727 (8th Cir. 2017) (holding that an employer's proffered reason for an adverse employment action can be considered pretextual if it is not supported by evidence). The appellants have raised genuine issues of material fact regarding BNSF's motivations, making summary judgment improper. See Tolan v. Cotton, 134 S. Ct. 1861, 1866 (2014) (holding that summary judgment is only appropriate when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact).

In addition, the increased operations testing and other adverse actions taken against the appellants while the investigative hearing was pending raise concerns about BNSF's retaliatory motives. See Norfolk S. Ry. Co. v. Solis, 915 F. Supp. 2d 32, 41 (D.D.C. 2013) (holding that an employer's increased scrutiny of an employee can be evidence of retaliation). The appellants' allegations that they felt forced to avoid inexperienced or sloppy co-workers and signed up for less lucrative work due to fear of being blamed for others' mistakes also support an inference of retaliation. See Coppinger-Martin v. Solis, 627 F.3d 745, 750 (9th Cir. 2010) (holding that an employer's actions can be considered retaliatory if they would dissuade a reasonable employee from engaging in protected activity).

The district court's grant of summary judgment should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings. See City of Arlington v. FCC, 569 U.S. 290, 296 (2013) (holding that summary judgment is only appropriate when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law). The appellants have presented sufficient evidence to raise genuine issues of material fact regarding BNSF's motivations, making summary judgment improper.
