 ARGUMENT

I. The OCSLA Extends Longshore Act Coverage Only to Workers Injured on the Outer Continental Shelf Itself

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") to employees injured "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources" of the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain language of the statute requires a direct connection between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf.

The Supreme Court has noted that "Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA's coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed." Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). Although this statement is dictum, it reflects the Court's understanding of the OCSLA's geographic limitations. The Fifth Circuit has held that the OCSLA requires a "situs-of-injury" test, limiting coverage to injuries that occur on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it. Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc).

The Ninth Circuit erred in rejecting the situs-of-injury test and adopting a "substantial nexus" test, which is not supported by the statutory text or relevant case law. The OCSLA's language, "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf," implies a causal connection between the injury and the operations on the Shelf. However, this causal connection is not sufficient to establish coverage; the injury must also occur in a location that is sufficiently connected to the outer Continental Shelf.

The Fifth Circuit's decision in Mills is consistent with the Supreme Court's dictum in Tallentire and provides a more nuanced understanding of the OCSLA's geographic limitations. The "situs-of-injury" test ensures that the OCSLA's coverage is limited to injuries that are directly related to the operations on the outer Continental Shelf.

II. The BRB Correctly Denied Benefits Under the LHWCA

The LHWCA provides coverage for maritime employees injured on navigable waters or in adjoining areas customarily used by an employer in loading or unloading a vessel. 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Ninth Circuit has adopted a "functional relationship" test to determine whether a facility is an "adjoining area" under the statute. Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978).

The La Conchita facility, where Valladolid was killed, does not meet the "functional relationship" test. Although it is located near the coastline, it is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks and has no direct access to any pier, dock, or other loading facility. The facility's primary purpose is to process crude oil slurry extracted by the offshore platforms, a non-maritime activity. Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414, 422-24 (1985).

The Ninth Circuit correctly affirmed the BRB's denial of benefits under the LHWCA, as La Conchita is not a maritime situs. The facility's use as a dumping ground for scrap metal from the platforms does not convert it into a maritime situs, as the maritime activities began and ended at the Casitas Pass Pier, three miles away.

CONCLUSION

The Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed insofar as it held that the OCSLA does not require a situs-of-injury test. The OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to workers injured on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The BRB's denial of benefits under the LHWCA should be affirmed, as La Conchita is not a maritime situs.
