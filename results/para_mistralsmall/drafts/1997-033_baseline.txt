**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand to the Transferor District for Trial and Prohibits Self-Assignment Under Section 1404(a)**

The plain text of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) requires that any action transferred for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings "shall be remanded by the panel ... at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." This statutory command is unambiguous. *Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach*, 523 U.S. 26, 34 (1998) ("The statute says that the Panel ‘shall’ remand each transferred action ‘at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.’"). The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) has no authority to retain a case for trial, nor may a transferee court invoke § 1404(a) to assign the case to itself for trial. *Id.* at 35 ("The statute does not empower the Panel to retain transferred cases for trial, nor does it empower a transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial.").

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and its progeny, misreads the statute. Those cases erroneously rely on the transferee court’s general § 1404(a) authority to "transfer ... to any other district" for "the convenience of parties and witnesses" and "in the interest of justice." However, § 1407(a) carves out a specific, time-limited exception to § 1404(a) for pretrial proceedings only. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 36 ("Section 1407(a) is a specific statute governing the transfer of cases for pretrial proceedings; § 1404(a) is a general venue-transfer statute."). The Supreme Court has made clear that specific statutes control over general ones. *Morton v. Mancari*, 417 U.S. 535, 550-51 (1974) ("[W]hen two statutes are capable of coexistence, it is the duty of the courts ... to regard each as effective.").

**II. The Transferee Court’s Self-Assignment Violates the Statutory Scheme and Undermines Congressional Intent**

Congress enacted § 1407(a) to centralize pretrial proceedings in multidistrict litigation (MDL) to avoid duplicative discovery and prevent inconsistent pretrial rulings. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 33 ("Congress designed § 1407 to provide centralized pretrial proceedings for complex cases."). The statute’s remand requirement ensures that plaintiffs retain their "ancient right to select the forum" for trial. *Id.* at 37 (quoting *Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Elec. Mfg. Co.*, 313 U.S. 487, 494 (1941)).

The Ninth Circuit’s approach—allowing transferee courts to retain cases for trial under § 1404(a)—subverts this intent. It permits forum shopping by transferee judges and deprives plaintiffs of their chosen venue. *Id.* at 38 ("The transferee court’s action here effectively deprived petitioners of their chosen forum."). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that venue-transfer statutes must be strictly construed to prevent such abuses. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343 (1960) ("The language of § 1404(a) is clear and explicit; it must be given effect.").

**III. The Judicial Panel’s Rule 14(b) Does Not Authorize Self-Assignment**

The JPML’s Rule 14(b) purports to authorize transferee courts to "transfer the action to itself for trial pursuant to § 1404(a)." However, the Supreme Court has rejected the argument that the JPML’s rules can expand its statutory authority. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 35 ("The Panel’s rulemaking authority is limited to procedural rules; it cannot expand the scope of § 1407."). The Court has long held that administrative agencies lack authority to interpret statutes contrary to their plain meaning. *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984) ("If the intent of Congress is clear, that is the end of the matter.").

**IV. The Transferee Court’s Action Is Not Shielded by the "First Impression" or "Oft-Repeated Error" Exceptions**

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Fine Paper* and similar cases is misplaced. Those decisions are not binding and have been widely criticized. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 36 n.6 ("The Third Circuit’s reasoning in *Fine Paper* has been rejected by other courts."). The Supreme Court has made clear that erroneous interpretations of statutes do not acquire precedential weight simply by repetition. *Agostini v. Felton*, 521 U.S. 201, 237 (1997) ("Stare decisis is not an inexorable command.").

**CONCLUSION**

The transferee court’s self-assignment under § 1404(a) violates the plain text of § 1407(a) and undermines congressional intent. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision and hold that § 1407(a) requires remand to the transferor district for trial.
