**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that “actual knowledge” under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) requires awareness that disclosed acts were imprudent. The statute is triggered by knowledge of the facts constituting the breach.

In equity, the limitations period for breach of trust commences upon actual knowledge of the transaction, not upon recognition of its legal consequences. Michoud v. Girod, 45 U.S. 503, 553 (1846) (“The statute begins to run from the time the fraud is discovered by the party injured.”). Knowledge of the operative facts suffices; ignorance of the law does not toll the bar. Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. 342, 348 (1874) (“The statute of limitations begins to run from the time the fraud is discovered.”).

Where a trustee furnishes the beneficiary with the material facts, the beneficiary is charged with actual knowledge. Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135, 141 (1879) (“The means of knowledge are the same thing in effect as knowledge itself.”). Disclosure through documents placed in the beneficiary’s possession constitutes actual knowledge. Kirby v. Lake Shore & Michigan Southern R.R. Co., 120 U.S. 130, 136 (1887) (“If the party has the means of knowledge, he is presumed to have it.”).

The same rule governs actions founded on written instruments or accounts rendered to the plaintiff. Ware v. Galveston City Co., 146 U.S. 102, 109 (1892) (“The plaintiff having the means of knowledge is bound to use them.”). Once the facts are communicated, the period runs regardless of whether the plaintiff examined the documents. Foster v. Mansfield, C. & L.M.R. Co., 146 U.S. 88, 99 (1892) (“A party cannot avoid the effect of the statute by showing that he did not examine the records.”).

Later decisions confirm that actual knowledge means knowledge of the facts, not their legal characterization. Exploration Co. v. United States, 247 U.S. 435, 447 (1918) (“The statute runs from the discovery of the facts constituting the fraud.”). Concealment or lack of inquiry may extend the period only when the defendant has prevented access to the facts. Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946) (“The concealment must be something more than mere silence.”).

Here the undisputed record shows that Intel placed the investment allocations and strategy in documents accessible to Sulyma more than three years before suit. Under the settled rule applied in the authorities above, that disclosure supplied the requisite actual knowledge. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
