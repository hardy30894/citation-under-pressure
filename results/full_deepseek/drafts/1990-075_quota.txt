**ARGUMENT**

**I. Congress’s Delegation of Temporary Scheduling Authority to the Attorney General Satisfies the Intelligible Principle Test**

The nondelegation doctrine does not forbid Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches; it requires only that Congress “lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Supreme Court has repeatedly reaffirmed that this standard is generous, recognizing that “Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives.” *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989).

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision easily satisfies that test. Congress specified the goal—avoiding “an imminent hazard to the public safety,” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1)—and directed the Attorney General to consider three specific factors: the history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health. *Id.* § 811(c)(4)–(6). These are precisely the kind of “well-known and generally acceptable standards” that this Court has upheld. *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). The delegation is also tightly limited: temporary scheduling lasts only one year, with a possible six-month extension, and requires notice to the Secretary of Health and Human Services. *See* 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2), (4). In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944), this Court upheld an emergency price-control delegation with comparable standards, emphasizing the need for flexibility in rapidly changing conditions. The same reasoning applies here.

Petitioners’ emphasis on the criminal penalties attached to scheduling does not alter the analysis. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911), this Court held that “Congress may delegate to an executive officer the power to make regulations, and the violation of such regulations may be punished as a crime.” The two cases in which this Court invalidated delegations—*Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935)—involved statutes that supplied no policy or criteria whatsoever. Here, Congress supplied detailed factors, a binding scientific evaluation, and temporal limits. *See Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374–75 (upholding delegation where Congress specified goals, factors, and limits). The temporary scheduling provision is thus a constitutional exercise of Congress’s power to seek administrative assistance.

**II. The Attorney General’s Subdelegation to the DEA Was Expressly Authorized by Congress**

Petitioners’ second claim—that the Attorney General improperly delegated his temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator—fails because Congress expressly authorized that subdelegation. Section 871(a) of the Controlled Substances Act provides that the Attorney General may delegate any of his functions under the Act to officers of the Department of Justice. The DEA Administrator is such an officer. The Attorney General exercised that authority in 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), assigning to the DEA Administrator all functions vested in the Attorney General by the Act.

This Court’s decision in *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 508–09 (1974), confirms the distinction between statutes requiring personal action and those permitting delegation. There, the Court held that the wiretap statute’s reference to “the Attorney General” required personal authorization because the legislative history showed Congress’s intent to impose a personal responsibility. Here, by contrast, Congress expressly authorized delegation in § 871(a). Where Congress intends a power to be exercised personally, it says so; where it authorizes delegation, courts must give effect to that choice. *See also United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 694 (1974) (recognizing the Attorney General’s authority to delegate functions to subordinates). The Attorney General’s subdelegation was therefore lawful.

Finally, the availability of judicial review of constitutional challenges—as the Third Circuit correctly held—provides an additional safeguard. *See Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542–44 (1952) (considering judicial review as a factor in delegation analysis). Petitioners had a full opportunity to challenge the scheduling of Euphoria, and the district court properly rejected their claims. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
