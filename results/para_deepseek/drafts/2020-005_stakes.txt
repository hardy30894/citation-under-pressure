**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Exercised Specific Personal Jurisdiction Because Ford’s Contacts with the Forum “Relate to” the Claims at Issue.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum contacts. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945). This standard is not a “causation-only” test. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472–73 (1985). Rather, a “relate to” connection suffices when the defendant’s forum contacts “have a connection with the plaintiff’s claim.” *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980). Here, Ford’s extensive contacts with Montana—advertising, selling vehicles and parts, and providing warranties—unquestionably relate to the claims that a Ford Explorer’s defective tire caused a fatal accident in Montana.

**A. Ford Purposefully Availed Itself of the Montana Market, Creating a “Relate To” Nexus.**

Ford does not dispute that it regularly markets, sells, and services vehicles in Montana. It maintains a network of dealerships, sells replacement parts, and advertises nationwide. These contacts are not random or attenuated; they reflect Ford’s deliberate decision to “serve the market for its vehicles” in Montana. *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 774 (1984). The claim here—a design defect in a Ford Explorer—is the same defect that Ford allegedly introduced into the Montana market through its sales of the same model. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that this connection satisfies the “relate to” prong because Ford’s forum activities “are the very activities that gave rise to the *type* of claim at issue.” *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Ct.*, 2020 MT 138, ¶ 20, 400 Mont. 167, 464 P.3d 982 (citing *Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 788–89 (1984)).

**B. Ford’s Contacts Need Not Be the Proximate Cause of the Plaintiff’s Injury.**

Ford contends that specific jurisdiction requires a causal link between its Montana contacts and the plaintiff’s injury. That argument misreads *International Shoe* and its progeny. The “arise out of or relate to” standard is disjunctive; a “relate to” connection is sufficient. *See Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 472–73 (jurisdiction proper when the “controversy is related to or arises out of” the defendant’s contacts). In *World-Wide Volkswagen*, the Court held that a defendant must have “conduct and connection with the forum State such that he should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there.” 444 U.S. at 297. Ford’s nationwide marketing and sales of the Explorer, combined with its Montana operations, make it entirely foreseeable that it would face suit in Montana for alleged defects in that vehicle. The fact that the specific car was originally sold in Washington does not sever the relationship between Ford’s Montana contacts and the claims. *See Calder*, 465 U.S. at 788 (defendant’s “intentional conduct in Florida calculated to cause injury to [the plaintiff] in California” supported jurisdiction); *Keeton*, 465 U.S. at 775 (defendant’s “regular circulation of magazines in the forum State” supported jurisdiction for defamation claim even though the specific issue was not sold there).

**C. The Montana Supreme Court’s Decision Comports with This Court’s Precedent.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the “relate to” standard. It reasoned that Ford’s “advertising, selling, and servicing of the same vehicle model in Montana” created a “connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Ct.*, 2020 MT 138, ¶ 24. This reasoning mirrors this Court’s holding in *Keeton*, where jurisdiction was proper because the defendant “deliberately exploited the Montana market” for its magazine. 465 U.S. at 774. Similarly, in *Calder*, the Court held that jurisdiction existed because the defendant’s “intentional conduct” was “calculated to cause injury” in the forum. 465 U.S. at 789. Here, Ford’s design and marketing of the Explorer were calculated to cause the vehicle to be driven and used in Montana, and the alleged defect caused injury there. The relationship is clear.

**D. Requiring a Causal Nexus Would Undermine the Purpose of Specific Jurisdiction.**

Ford’s proposed rule—that jurisdiction requires the defendant’s forum contact to *cause* the plaintiff’s injury—would allow manufacturers to insulate themselves from liability by selling products through third-party intermediaries. That result would conflict with the fundamental purpose of specific jurisdiction: to ensure that defendants who “purposefully avail” themselves of a forum’s benefits are answerable there for claims arising from that forum-related activity. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319. As this Court has recognized, “the Due Process Clause is not a ‘territorial limitation to the power of the respective States.’” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 474 (citation omitted). Rather, it protects defendants from being forced to defend in a forum with which they have no “minimum contacts.” Here, Ford has abundant contacts with Montana. The Due Process Clause requires nothing more.

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court’s exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Ford was consistent with the Due Process Clause. The judgment should be affirmed.
