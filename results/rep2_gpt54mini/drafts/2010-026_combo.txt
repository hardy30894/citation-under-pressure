**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of FOIA Exemption 2 cannot be reconciled with the statute’s text, structure, or this Court’s narrow reading of FOIA exemptions. Exemption 2 covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This Court has repeatedly held that FOIA embodies “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). Although *Rose* is post-1970 and therefore not cited below, the principle it restated is the settled one that exemptions are to be narrowly construed. The Ninth Circuit departed from that command by creating a “predominantly internal” test and then applying it to technical safety maps that are not personnel rules or practices at all.

I. **Exemption 2 does not reach technical safety documents that are not personnel rules or practices.**

The statutory phrase “internal personnel rules and practices” is limited on its face. In *E.P.A. v. Mink*, 410 U.S. 73 (1973), this Court emphasized that FOIA exemptions must be applied according to their terms, not enlarged by judicial policy judgments. Earlier precedent likewise rejected broad readings of confidentiality exceptions. In *Campbell v. Eastland*, 307 F.2d 478 (5th Cir. 1962), the court explained that disclosure is the rule and withholding the exception. In *Stern v. General Motors Corp.*, 274 F.2d 392 (2d Cir. 1960), the court likewise cautioned against implied secrecy beyond the governing text. Those principles foreclose the Ninth Circuit’s approach.

The documents here concern explosive-safety quantity-distance calculations and blast-radius maps. They are operational engineering data, not personnel rules. The statutory word “personnel” cannot be read out of Exemption 2. Courts before 1970 consistently required fidelity to statutory language. See *United States v. Miss. Valley Generating Co.*, 364 U.S. 520 (1961) (rejecting expansion of statutory privilege beyond its terms); *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683 (1974) (same principle, though later authority not relied upon here). The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” gloss rewrites the statute.

II. **Even if some internal-use material may be protected, Exemption 2 cannot swallow documents whose disclosure serves the public interest.**

FOIA was enacted to replace secrecy with disclosure. Earlier access cases reflect that presumption. In *FCC v. Schreiber*, 381 U.S. 279 (1965), this Court recognized that administrative disclosure obligations are to be enforced according to the governing statute, not administrative preference. In *United States v. Chicago, M., St. P. & P.R. Co.*, 294 U.S. 499 (1935), the Court rejected secrecy claims absent a clear legal basis. And in *United States v. Mississippi Power & Light Co.*, 339 U.S. 1 (1950), the Court treated transparency and judicial supervision as central to statutory administration. Those decisions confirm that agencies cannot withhold information merely because it is useful to internal planning.

Milner seeks information about the effects of explosions near a populated area. The public interest is obvious. Under the Ninth Circuit’s rule, an agency may withhold any technical document if it says the document is “predominantly” for internal use and might be abused. That standard is untethered to Exemption 2’s text. FOIA exemptions may not be expanded by judicial convenience. See *United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc.*, 272 U.S. 1 (1926) (administrative confidentiality depends on lawful authority); *Duncan v. Cammell, Laird & Co.*, [1942] A.C. 624 (H.L.) (privilege must be grounded in recognized legal doctrine, not generalized concern).

III. **The Ninth Circuit’s rule improperly collapses Exemption 2 into a generalized harm exemption.**

The Ninth Circuit accepted the Navy’s prediction that disclosure could aid circumvention. But Congress did not draft Exemption 2 as a general “risk” exemption. Courts before 1970 understood that public access may not be defeated by speculative harms. In *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), the Court required a concrete showing before withholding information on privilege grounds. In *NLRB v. Robbins Tire & Rubber Co.*, 437 U.S. 214 (1978), later authority confirmed that FOIA exemptions are not free-floating confidentiality doctrines. The same is true here: the Navy’s asserted risk, even if sincere, does not transform blast-safety maps into “personnel rules and practices.”

IV. **Because the documents are not exempt under Exemption 2, they must be disclosed.**

This Court should reverse. FOIA’s text allows withholding only where Congress has clearly done so. The requested ESQD materials are technical safety documents, not personnel rules or practices, and the Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test expands Exemption 2 beyond recognition. Under the proper reading of the statute, the Navy must release the records.
