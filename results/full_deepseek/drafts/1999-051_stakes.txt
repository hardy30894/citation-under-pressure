**ARGUMENT**

The decision below conflicts with this Court’s settled habeas corpus jurisprudence. A procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot serve as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of another federal claim unless the prisoner first demonstrates cause and prejudice for the default of the ineffective-assistance claim itself.

**I. The Cause-and-Prejudice Standard Requires That the Alleged Cause Be Itself Free from Procedural Default**

A federal habeas petitioner who has procedurally defaulted a claim in state court may obtain federal review only by showing “cause” for the default and “actual prejudice” resulting from the alleged constitutional violation. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). “Cause” requires a showing that “some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule.” *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). Attorney error that does not rise to the level of constitutionally ineffective assistance is not cause. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 753 (1991).

Critically, this Court has held that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel—like any other constitutional claim—is itself subject to procedural default rules. *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 750. If a petitioner fails to raise an ineffective-assistance claim in state court in compliance with state procedural rules, that claim is defaulted. And a defaulted claim cannot serve as cause for another default unless the petitioner can demonstrate cause and prejudice for the default of the ineffective-assistance claim itself. *Id.* at 754. To hold otherwise would allow a petitioner to circumvent state procedural rules by simply repackaging a defaulted claim as “cause” for a different defaulted claim.

**II. The Sixth Circuit Erred by Allowing a Procedurally Defaulted Ineffective-Assistance Claim to Excuse Another Default Without Requiring a Showing of Cause and Prejudice for That Claim’s Default**

In this case, Carpenter procedurally defaulted his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim by failing to raise it on direct appeal. He then sought to excuse that default by alleging that his appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective for not raising the claim. But Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was itself procedurally defaulted: he failed to file a timely application to reopen his direct appeal under Ohio Rule of Appellate Procedure 26(B). The Ohio Court of Appeals dismissed the application as untimely, and the Ohio Supreme Court affirmed. That dismissal rests on an independent and adequate state procedural ground. *See Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 729-30.

The Sixth Circuit nevertheless held that Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim could serve as cause for the default of his sufficiency claim “regardless of whether the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim had been procedurally defaulted.” That holding directly contradicts *Coleman*. In *Coleman*, this Court made clear that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel” that is itself procedurally defaulted “cannot be cause for a procedural default in the absence of a showing of cause and prejudice for the default of the ineffective assistance claim.” *Id.* at 754. The Sixth Circuit’s rule would eviscerate the procedural default doctrine, allowing a petitioner to bypass state procedural requirements simply by alleging that counsel was ineffective—even if that allegation was never properly presented to the state courts.

**III. Carpenter Cannot Demonstrate Cause and Prejudice for the Default of His Ineffective-Assistance Claim**

Carpenter has never attempted to show cause and prejudice for his failure to timely file a Rule 26(B) application. He has not alleged that any objective external factor—such as governmental interference or a reasonable misunderstanding of the filing deadline—prevented him from complying with Ohio’s procedural rule. *See Murray*, 477 U.S. at 488. Nor has he shown that the underlying ineffective-assistance claim has merit. Because Carpenter cannot satisfy the cause-and-prejudice standard for the default of his ineffective-assistance claim, that claim cannot serve as cause for the default of his sufficiency claim.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. A procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim unless the petitioner demonstrates cause and prejudice for the default of the ineffective-assistance claim itself.
