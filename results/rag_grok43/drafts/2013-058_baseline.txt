**ARGUMENT**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts only “retirement funds” held in accounts qualifying for tax deferral under specified Internal Revenue Code provisions. The text requires that the funds retain their character as retirement savings in the hands of the debtor. An inherited IRA fails this requirement because its mandatory distribution schedule compels withdrawal before the debtor’s retirement and bars any further contributions or rollover. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); § 401(a)(9)(B).

This Court has recognized that retirement exemptions protect assets set aside for the debtor’s own post-employment needs. In *Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320 (2005), the Court construed a parallel exemption, § 522(d)(10)(E), to require that payments be made “on account of illness, disability, death, age, or length of service, to the extent reasonably necessary for the support of the debtor.” *Id.* at 325 (quoting the statute). The same functional limit applies here: once the funds cease to be reserved for the debtor’s retirement, they lose exempt status.

The Seventh Circuit correctly applied this principle. When Ruth Heffron died, the account ceased to be anyone’s retirement fund. It became a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle that the beneficiary must deplete on a schedule unrelated to her own retirement. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 561-62 (7th Cir. 2013). Treating such an account as exempt would shelter assets available for current consumption, contrary to the statute’s purpose.

Petitioners’ reliance on *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (B.A.P. 8th Cir. 2010), is misplaced. Those decisions read the absence of the word “debtor’s” to mean that any funds once held for retirement remain exempt regardless of current use. That construction ignores the statutory term “retirement funds,” which denotes purpose, not mere provenance. As the Seventh Circuit observed, “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525-26 (1987). Congress chose a specific exemption for retirement savings, not a general shield for inherited wealth.

The same logic governs other exemptions. A homestead exemption requires the debtor’s actual use as a residence; a farm-tool exemption requires the debtor’s actual use in farming. *See, e.g.*, § 522(b)(3)(A), (b)(3)(B). Retirement funds are no different. Because the inherited IRA is not held for the Clarks’ retirement, it is not exempt.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
