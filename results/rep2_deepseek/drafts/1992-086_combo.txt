**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches School District violated the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech when it denied Lamb’s Chapel access to school facilities solely because the films it sought to show presented a religious perspective on family values. The District opened its facilities to a broad array of community groups, including those presenting secular views on child-rearing and family life. By excluding Lamb’s Chapel because its films were “church related,” the District engaged in impermissible viewpoint discrimination.

**I. The District Created a Limited Public Forum Open to a Wide Range of Community Groups**

The District’s policy and practice allowed numerous outside organizations to use school premises for expressive activities. Groups such as the Southern Harmonizers, the Center Moriches Free Public Library, and others presented programs on family and cultural topics. When the government opens its property for expressive use by the general public, it creates a forum subject to the same First Amendment protections as traditional public fora. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (streets and parks “have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public” for assembly and discussion). Although the District may limit the forum to certain subjects, it may not discriminate based on the viewpoint of the speaker. *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939) (striking down ordinance that discriminated against certain types of speech). The District allowed secular presentations on family values; it cannot exclude religious presentations on the same subject.

**II. The Exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel Based on the Religious Viewpoint of Its Films Is Unconstitutional Viewpoint Discrimination**

The District denied Lamb’s Chapel access because the films were “church related” and “religious.” This is a content-based exclusion that targets a particular viewpoint. Religious speech is fully protected under the First Amendment. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940) (religious proselytizing is “within the protection of the First Amendment”). When a forum is open to other speakers, the government may not deny access to a speaker because of the religious nature of his message. *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268, 272-73 (1951) (reversing conviction for holding religious meeting in park where permits were granted to other groups); *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290, 293-94 (1951) (striking down permit system that allowed denial for religious meetings). In *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 69-70 (1953), the Court held that a city could not treat a religious meeting differently from other meetings in a public park. Here, the District allowed secular groups to use facilities for family-oriented programs but excluded Lamb’s Chapel because its program was from a Christian perspective. That is precisely the sort of viewpoint discrimination the First Amendment forbids.

**III. The District’s Rule Excluding “Religious Purposes” Is a Content-Based Restriction That Cannot Survive Strict Scrutiny**

The District’s Local Rule No. 7 prohibits use of school premises “by any group for religious purposes.” This rule discriminates on its face against religious speech. Content-based restrictions on speech in a public forum are presumptively invalid and must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest. *Lovell v. Griffin*, 303 U.S. 444, 451 (1938) (striking down permit requirement for distribution of literature); *Martin v. City of Struthers*, 319 U.S. 141, 143 (1943) (protecting door-to-door distribution of religious handbills). The District’s asserted interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation is not compelling because allowing equal access to religious groups on the same terms as other groups does not establish religion. *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 530 (1945) (the First Amendment “commands that the government shall not discriminate against religious speech”). Moreover, the District has not shown that its blanket exclusion is the least restrictive means of achieving any legitimate interest. *Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 164.

**IV. The Lower Court Erred in Concluding That the Forum Was Not Opened to Religious Uses**

The Second Circuit held that the District had not opened its facilities to religious uses by policy or practice. But the relevant inquiry is whether the District opened the forum to a range of speakers on a given subject. Once it did so, it could not exclude Lamb’s Chapel because of the religious viewpoint of its speech. *Niemotko*, 340 U.S. at 272; *Fowler*, 345 U.S. at 69. The District allowed groups that incidentally mentioned religion or had religious overtones, but excluded Lamb’s Chapel because its primary purpose was religious. This distinction is constitutionally impermissible. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 304-05. The First Amendment protects the right to present a religious perspective on a secular topic, just as it protects secular perspectives. *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. at 531.

**Conclusion**

The District’s denial of access to Lamb’s Chapel violated the First Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
