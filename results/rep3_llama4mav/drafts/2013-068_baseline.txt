The Massachusetts statute creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that does not violate the First Amendment. As the First Circuit correctly held, the law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve significant government interests, and leaves open adequate alternative channels of communication.

The buffer zone is a reasonable measure to ensure public safety and prevent harassment of patients and clinic staff. The Supreme Court has previously upheld a similar Colorado statute in Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), which prohibited certain activities within 100 feet of healthcare facility entrances. The Court in Hill recognized that sidewalks and areas outside healthcare clinics are "quintessential public forums" but held that the buffer zone was a valid time, place, and manner regulation. Id. at 715.

The petitioners argue that the law is not content-neutral because it disproportionately affects their "pro-life" message. However, the First Circuit correctly applied the law of the case doctrine, which precludes relitigation of issues already decided in McCullen I. The petitioners' reliance on recent Supreme Court decisions, such as Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), is misplaced, as those cases addressed different factual scenarios and did not undermine the Hill decision.

The buffer zone is narrowly tailored to serve the government's interests in maintaining public safety and order. The zone is limited to 35 feet around clinic entrances, exits, and driveways, and exempts certain individuals, such as patients, employees, and law enforcement officers. The law also provides clear guidelines for enforcement, with signs marking the buffer zone and an Attorney General's letter providing guidance on the exemptions.

The petitioners have adequate alternative channels of communication available to them. They can still engage in oral speech, distribute literature, display signs and symbols, and pray alone or in groups outside the buffer zone. The First Circuit correctly found that these alternatives are sufficient to allow the petitioners to convey their message effectively.

The petitioners' claim that the Attorney General's letter is an unconstitutional prior restraint on speech is also without merit. The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the petitioners leave to amend their complaint to challenge the letter, as they had ample opportunity to raise the issue earlier.

In conclusion, the Massachusetts statute is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that balances the government's interests in public safety and order with the petitioners' First Amendment rights. The First Circuit's decision should be affirmed, and the Hill decision should be reaffirmed as controlling precedent. The Court should reject the petitioners' invitation to overrule or limit Hill, as it remains a valid and controlling decision on the issue of buffer zones around healthcare facilities.
