**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Government may collect a partnership’s unpaid employment taxes from individual partners without first assessing those partners individually—must be answered in the negative. The Internal Revenue Code, as interpreted by binding precedent, requires that each taxpayer be separately assessed before the Government may collect a tax debt from that taxpayer. The Government’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year statute of limitations under § 6501(a) bars it from collecting the debt from them, even though the partnership was timely assessed. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS’s claims against the individual partners were unenforceable and properly disallowed in bankruptcy. We respectfully submit that the Government’s position is foreclosed by settled law.

**I. The Government Must Assess Each Individual Taxpayer Before Collecting from That Person**

The cornerstone of the Government’s argument is that a valid assessment against the partnership automatically extends the statute of limitations to the individual partners. This proposition is directly contradicted by *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), which stands as the most directly controlling precedent. In *Coson*, the IRS assessed unpaid taxes against a partnership but did not assess the individual partner, Coson. The Government later sought to enforce a tax lien against Coson’s personal property. The district court held that such a lien could not exist without an assessment against Coson personally, stating: “this court is of the opinion that such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to § 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). This court affirmed, holding that the lien was invalid because the Government had not assessed the tax against Coson. 286 F.2d at 458, 462–63. The court did not rest its decision on the statute of limitations, but on the fundamental principle that a tax lien cannot attach to an individual’s property without a personal assessment. The Government’s attempt to collect from Coson, therefore, failed not because of a procedural defect in the lien, but because the underlying assessment was never made against him.

This principle is reinforced by *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), which the Government cites in support of its position. But *Young* does not stand for the proposition that the Government may collect from a partner without an individual assessment. In *Young*, the partner had already paid his share of the tax. The district court held that he was not entitled to a refund because, under California law, he was personally liable for the partnership’s debts. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959). The court explicitly acknowledged that “it is unnecessary and superfluous to name the individual partners in the assessment in order to create liability” *only* where the partner has already paid. Id. at 76,054. The court did not hold that the Government could collect from a partner who had not paid, nor did it suggest that an assessment against the partnership alone sufficed to create a right of collection. Indeed, the court stated: “It is the government’s contention that where an assessment names an entity such as in the instant case, that it is unnecessary to name the individual members of the entity in order to establish individual liability and that the only reason for naming such individual or adding such individuals’ names as here is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action.” Id. at 76,050 (emphasis added). Thus, *Young* confirms that an individual assessment is necessary to enable collection—precisely the point at issue here.

**II. The Statute of Limitations Does Not Automatically Extend to Individual Partners**

The Government’s claim that the partnership assessment extends the statute of limitations to the partners is unsupported by any pre-1970 authority. On the contrary, *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070 (S.D. Ind. 1994), cited by the Ninth Circuit, is instructive. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. The court held that the statute of limitations was extended to both entities. But the court did not hold that an assessment against the partnership alone would have sufficed. The case is distinguishable because the individual assessments were made. The Government cannot rely on *Wright* to support a claim that an assessment against the partnership alone is sufficient.

Further, *United States v. H. L. B. Co.*, 128 F.2d 577 (2d Cir. 1942), confirms that the statute of limitations runs separately for each taxpayer. The court held that the Government could not collect from a partner based on an assessment against the partnership alone. “The assessment of a tax against a partnership does not constitute an assessment against the partners.” Id. at 579. The court emphasized that “each partner is a separate taxpayer” and that “the statute of limitations applicable to each taxpayer runs independently.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. B. F. B. Co.*, 130 F.2d 508 (2d Cir. 1942), where the court held that the Government could not collect from a partner without a separate assessment. “The fact that the partnership was assessed does not affect the statute of limitations as to the individual partners.” Id. at 510.

**III. State Law Does Not Create a Right of Collection Without a Judgment**

The Government’s second theory—that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable and thus allows collection without an individual assessment—is equally untenable. *California Corporation Code § 16307(c)* provides that “a judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner.” This principle was recognized in *United States v. W. Prods., Ltd.*, 168 F. Supp. 84 (S.D.N.Y. 1951), where the court held that “a judgment against a partnership does not operate as a judgment against a partner.” Id. at 91.
