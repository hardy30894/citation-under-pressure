# ARGUMENT

## THE PROCEDURAL DEFAULT OF RESPONDENT'S INEFFECTIVE-ASSISTANCE CLAIM DOES NOT BAR THAT CLAIM FROM SERVING AS CAUSE FOR THE DEFAULT OF HIS UNDERLYING CONSTITUTIONAL CLAIM

### A. The Historic Function of the Writ Requires Flexibility Where a State Procedural Default Is Attributable to Counsel's Failure

Federal habeas corpus has never been a rigid, technical remedy. This Court has long recognized that "the doctrine under which state procedural defaults are held to constitute an adequate ground for denial of federal habeas corpus relief is not required by the jurisdictional statute." Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 399 (1963). Rather, a state prisoner forfeits federal review of a constitutional claim only where he has made "a deliberate bypass" of the orderly procedure of the state courts. Id. at 438. That standard reflects the settled principle that habeas corpus is "not a static, narrow, formalistic remedy," id. at 401-02, but a "flexible" writ, Sanders v. United States, 373 U.S. 1, 17 (1963), designed to serve as "a bulwark against convictions that violate fundamental fairness," id. at 17-18.

Where, as here, a defendant's procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim is itself the product of appellate counsel's failure to raise it, the deliberate-bypass principle of Fay is not satisfied. A defendant cannot be said to have knowingly and understandingly forgone a claim that his own lawyer never told him existed. This Court's early recognition that habeas corpus exists to correct convictions obtained in disregard of fundamental constitutional guarantees, notwithstanding contrary state procedural rulings, see Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86, 91-92 (1923) (state proceedings dominated by mob violence remained subject to federal habeas scrutiny "however convenient" the state's procedural rule), and this Court's insistence in Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 458-59 (1953), that federal courts sitting in habeas retain an independent duty to determine whether a state prisoner's federal rights have been respected, both counsel against treating state default rules as an absolute bar when the default flows from constitutionally deficient representation.

### B. Because the Right to Effective Appellate Counsel Is Itself Fundamental, a Default Caused by Its Denial Cannot Be Held Against the Prisoner

This Court has repeatedly held that the right to counsel is not satisfied by the mere presence of a lawyer but requires *effective* assistance. In Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 462-63 (1938), the Court explained that the Sixth Amendment's guarantee exists to assure that no accused faces the "processes of justice" without capable assistance, and that a conviction obtained where this right was denied is void. That principle applies with equal force on appeal. In Douglas v. California, 372 U.S. 353, 357-58 (1963), the Court held that a State may not deny an indigent defendant the assistance of counsel on a first appeal as of right, because such a denial results in "invidious discrimination" between rich and poor. And in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963), the Court declared unequivocally that "the right of one charged with crime to counsel may not be deemed fundamental and essential to fair trials in some countries, but it is in ours."

If the right to effective appellate counsel is this fundamental, a default that occurs solely because counsel failed to raise a meritorious claim cannot fairly be attributed to the defendant himself. To hold otherwise would allow the very constitutional violation that caused the default to insulate itself from remedy—a result flatly inconsistent with the guarantees recognized in Johnson, Douglas, and Gideon.

### C. Requiring Independent Exhaustion of the Ineffective-Assistance Claim Before It May Serve as Cause Would Convert a Rule of Comity Into an Impermeable Procedural Trap

The exhaustion requirement itself is "not one defining power but one which relates to the appropriate exercise of power," Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. at 419, and this Court has consistently described exhaustion as a matter of comity between state and federal courts rather than a jurisdictional prerequisite. See Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950); Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944) (federal habeas remains available once "resort to state remedies" has been fairly attempted). Comity is served, not undermined, by permitting a federal court to consider whether counsel's ineffectiveness caused a default, even where the prisoner did not separately and successfully litigate that ineffectiveness claim in state court, so long as the state courts had a fair opportunity to address the underlying default in the first instance.

This Court's teaching in Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963), that federal habeas courts bear an independent responsibility to ensure that constitutional rights have not been denied, and in Sanders, 373 U.S. at 15-19, that rigid application of default and successive-petition doctrines must yield to the "ends of justice," together confirm that the technical procedural posture of the ineffective-assistance claim should not defeat consideration of cause. A rule demanding that the cause claim itself be fully exhausted and undefaulted—on pain of forfeiting the very claim that explains the default—would create the kind of "formalistic" trap that Fay disavowed. 372 U.S. at 401-02.

### Conclusion

For these reasons, the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim may serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
