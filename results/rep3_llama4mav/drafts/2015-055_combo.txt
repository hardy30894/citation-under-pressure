The rule announced in Johnson v. United States, regarding what constitutes a violent offense under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), should be applied retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. The Supreme Court's decision in Johnson held that the residual clause of the ACCA was unconstitutionally vague, violating the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments.

To determine whether Johnson applies retroactively, we must consider the principles established in Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618 (1965). In Linkletter, the Court held that a new constitutional rule would not be applied retroactively unless it met certain criteria. The Court later clarified in Tehan v. Shott, 382 U.S. 406 (1966), that a new rule would be applied retroactively if it affected the "very integrity of the fact-finding process" (id. at 416, quoting Linkletter, 381 U.S. at 639).

The ACCA's residual clause, held unconstitutional in Johnson, was a critical component of the sentencing enhancement applied to Welch. The clause's vagueness undermined the integrity of the sentencing process, much like the coerced confession rule at issue in Jackson v. Denno, 378 U.S. 368 (1964). The application of the residual clause to Welch's case was therefore fundamentally flawed.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has long recognized the importance of retroactivity in cases involving significant changes to the law. In United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103 (1801), the Court held that a change in the law during the pendency of an appeal should be applied to the case at hand. Similarly, in Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), the Court emphasized the need for equal justice under the law, stating that "there can be no equal justice where the kind of trial a man gets depends on the amount of money he has" (id. at 19).

The Court has also considered the impact of new constitutional rules on collateral attacks, as seen in Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963). In Fay, the Court held that a new rule could be applied retroactively on collateral review if it was a "constitutional rule of criminal procedure" (id. at 426). The holding in Johnson, which struck down the residual clause as unconstitutionally vague, is precisely such a rule.

Additionally, the principles enunciated in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), and its predecessor, Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), demonstrate the Court's willingness to apply new constitutional rules retroactively when they significantly impact the fairness of the trial process. The vagueness of the residual clause, like the denial of counsel in Gideon, undermines the fundamental fairness of the sentencing process.

The decision in Johnson, by invalidating the residual clause, has a direct bearing on Welch's sentence, as it was enhanced under the ACCA based on a prior conviction that may not qualify as a violent felony under the now-clarified law. Applying Johnson retroactively to Welch's case is consistent with the principles established in the aforementioned cases, particularly those emphasizing the importance of fairness and the integrity of the judicial process.

In Boyd v. United States, 116 U.S. 616 (1886), the Court emphasized the importance of strict construction of penal statutes. The ACCA, with its vague residual clause, failed to meet this standard. By applying Johnson retroactively, we ensure that Welch receives the benefit of this clarification, consistent with the principles of fairness and equal justice under the law.

In conclusion, the rule announced in Johnson should be applied retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. The vagueness of the residual clause, held unconstitutional in Johnson, undermined the integrity of the sentencing process, and applying this new rule retroactively is consistent with established principles of retroactivity and fairness.
