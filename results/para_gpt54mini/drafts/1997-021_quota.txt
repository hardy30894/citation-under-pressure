**Argument**

**I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” polling rule is irrational and inconsistent with the Act because it collapses polling into the same showing required to withdraw recognition, while the Act gives the Board latitude only to fill gaps, not to create self-defeating procedures.**

The National Labor Relations Act does not address employer polling of incumbent-union support. See **NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990)**; **Beth Israel Hospital v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978)**. In that gap, the Board may craft rules, but only if they are rational and consistent with the statutory scheme. **Curtin Matheson, 494 U.S. at 786**. The Act’s central policy is stability of bargaining relationships, reinforced by the presumption that an incumbent union retains majority support. **Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987)**. But the Board’s polling rule frustrates that policy by making a poll available only when an employer already has the evidentiary showing sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. A poll under that standard becomes little more than a redundant formality.

This Court should not defer to a rule that is internally incoherent. The Board says elections are the preferred mechanism for determining employee sentiment, yet it allows a poll only on the same showing required to act without any poll at all. That logic is backward. As the courts of appeals recognized in **A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981)**, **Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 866-67 (6th Cir. 1982)**, and **Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984)**, polling can serve a useful purpose if the employer has substantial, objective evidence of loss of support even if that evidence is not yet enough to justify withdrawal. The Board’s contrary rule makes polling “potentially disruptive and unsettling” without any real function. **Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-63 (1989)**.

The better standard is the one the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits adopted: an employer may poll after complying with **Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)** if it has substantial, objective evidence of loss of support, even if that evidence does not itself justify withdrawal. **A.W. Thompson, 651 F.2d at 1145**; **Thomas Industries, 687 F.2d at 866-67**; **Mingtree Restaurant, 736 F.2d at 1299**. That rule harmonizes the employer’s legitimate need to test support with the Act’s policy of stability. The Board’s rule does not.

**II. Even if the Board’s rule were accepted, the finding that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence.**

A Board factual finding must be sustained only if supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole. **Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951)**. Here, the record showed substantial objective evidence of waning support: employee statements indicating antiunion sentiment; the employer’s transition from predecessor operations; and a secret-ballot poll supervised by a priest yielding a 19-to-13 vote against the union. The Board discounted that evidence through hypertechnical line-drawing that is not substantial evidence under **Universal Camera**, 340 U.S. at 488.

The Board’s treatment of individual employee statements was especially flawed. It refused to credit statements by workers expressing dissatisfaction or opposition based on timing or phrasing, despite precedent holding that antiunion statements may be probative when reliable and contemporaneous. See **Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc., 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), enforced, 590 F.2d 4 (5th Cir. 1978)**; **Destileria Serralles, Inc., 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988), enforced, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir. 1989)**; **Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995)**. The Board also discounted reports from employees about the views of other employees, although there is no rule permitting it to disregard such evidence categorically. The cumulative effect was to convert the substantial-evidence standard into deference to a predetermined result.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Board may not substitute speculation for record evidence. In **NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 787-88 (1990)**, the Court upheld a Board rule only because it was grounded in reasonable policy and evidence. In contrast, the Board here treated objective evidence of lost support as irrelevant unless it met the full withdrawal threshold. That approach is precisely what **Thomas Industries, 687 F.2d at 868**, rejected in analogous circumstances, where the court held there was no substantial evidence supporting the Board’s conclusion that the employer lacked a good-faith doubt.

Finally, the Board’s decision cannot be reconciled with the presumption recognized in **Fall River, 482 U.S. at 37-41**. That presumption is rebuttable; it is not irrebuttable. The evidence here—combined with the poll itself—was enough to raise doubt. Because the Board demanded more than the Act requires, and because its contrary finding lacks substantial evidence under **Universal Camera**, the order should not be enforced.
