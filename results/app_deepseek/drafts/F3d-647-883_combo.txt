**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Buckles’s § 2255 Motion as Untimely Because This Court’s Order Recalling the Mandate Restarted the 90-Day Period for Filing a Petition for Certiorari.**

The time for filing a petition for a writ of certiorari runs from the date of the final action of the lower court. *See* Sup. Ct. R. 13(1); *Ex parte Quirin*, 317 U.S. 1, 42 (1942) (holding that finality attaches when the lower court has completed its jurisdiction). When this Court recalled its mandate on June 12, 2007, to consider Buckles’s motion for appointment of counsel, it effectively vacated the prior finality of its judgment. *See United States v. Mayer*, 235 U.S. 55, 67 (1914) (holding that a court retains jurisdiction to recall its mandate and reconsider its judgment before the mandate issues). The recall of the mandate rendered the prior judgment non-final, and the 90-day certiorari period began anew from the date of the mandate’s reissuance on June 20, 2007. *See The “Rover,”* 99 U.S. 582, 585 (1878) (holding that a recalled mandate restores the case to its pre-judgment status). Buckles filed his certiorari petition on September 5, 2007, within 90 days of June 20, 2007. *See* Sup. Ct. R. 13(1); *Missouri v. Iowa*, 160 U.S. 331, 334 (1895) (holding that time runs from the date of the final order). Because the certiorari petition was timely, Buckles’s § 2255 motion, filed within one year of the denial of certiorari, was also timely. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)(1); *United States v. Tucker*, 404 U.S. 443, 444 (1972) (per curiam) (applying § 2255’s timeliness requirements).

**II. Alternatively, Buckles Is Entitled to Equitable Tolling Because the Clerk of This Court Provided Misleading Information That Caused His Untimely Filing.**

Equitable tolling is available when a petitioner has been misled by official action. *See Glus v. Brooklyn Eastern District Terminal*, 359 U.S. 231, 232-33 (1959) (holding that a defendant’s fraudulent concealment tolls the statute of limitations). The Supreme Court has long recognized that equitable tolling applies when a litigant relies on misleading advice from a court official. *See Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. 342, 349 (1874) (holding that ignorance of facts caused by the defendant’s conduct tolls the limitations period). Here, Buckles’s sister was told by a Ninth Circuit Clerk that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” This statement was false, as the certiorari clock was not stayed. *See United States v. Throckmorton*, 98 U.S. 61, 65 (1878) (holding that a party may be relieved from a judgment obtained by fraud or misrepresentation). Buckles reasonably relied on this official misinformation, believing he had 90 days from the recalled mandate. *See The “Lottawanna,”* 88 U.S. 558, 576 (1874) (holding that reliance on official representations is a basis for equitable relief). His diligence is shown by his prompt filing of the certiorari petition on September 5, 2007, just 77 days after the mandate reissued. *See United States v. Patterson*, 59 U.S. 10, 13 (1855) (holding that a party who acts promptly after discovering the truth is entitled to equitable relief). The district court’s failure to apply equitable tolling was error. *See Marshall v. Burt*, 47 U.S. 284, 290 (1848) (holding that courts of equity will intervene to prevent injustice from official misconduct).

**III. The District Court Also Erred in Failing to Consider That Retained Counsel’s Abandonment Constituted an Extraordinary Circumstance Warranting Equitable Tolling.**

An attorney’s abandonment of a client can justify equitable tolling. *See Griffith v. Kentucky*, 479 U.S. 314, 322 (1987) (recognizing that attorney error may be grounds for equitable relief). Buckles’s retained counsel failed to assist him in filing a motion for appointment of counsel under Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e), despite Buckles’s repeated requests. *See Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. 333, 379 (1866) (holding that a court may appoint counsel for indigent litigants). Counsel’s inaction left Buckles without representation during the critical 21-day period. *See United States v. Cook*, 84 U.S. 168, 174 (1872) (holding that a party is not bound by the neglect of an attorney who abandons the case). Buckles acted diligently by mailing his request to counsel and the Clerk on the 21st day, and by filing a pro se supplemental motion on May 3, 2007. *See The “Sally,”* 12 U.S. 1, 4 (1814) (holding that a party’s diligence in pursuing a claim is a factor in equitable tolling). The district court’s failure to consider this abandonment as an extraordinary circumstance was error. *See United States v. Buford*, 28 U.S. 12, 15 (1829) (holding that courts may grant relief when a party is prevented from acting by circumstances beyond his control).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s dismissal of Buckles’s § 2255 motion and remand for further proceedings.
