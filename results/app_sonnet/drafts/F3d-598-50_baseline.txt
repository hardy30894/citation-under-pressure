**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Committed Reversible Error by Admitting Testimony From Two ADAs and a Police Lieutenant That Vouched for Officer Credibility, Offered Legal Conclusions on Probable Cause, and Usurped the Jury's Fact-Finding Role.**

A. *Vouching.* It is settled that "an expert may not testify as to whether a witness is testifying truthfully." *United States v. Rea*, 958 F.2d 1206, 1225 (2d Cir. 1992). Yet the two ADAs here were permitted to tell the jury they found Ramos and Rivera credible when deciding to bring charges. This is precisely the harm this Court condemned in *United States v. Scop*, 846 F.2d 135, 142 (2d Cir. 1988), where testimony amounting to "explicit personal opinions" on witness veracity was reversible because it "invade[d] the province of the jury." Whether Ramos and Rivera lied about the events of July 2 was the central question at trial; permitting government witnesses to answer that question for the jury was error.

B. *Legal Conclusions.* This Court has repeatedly held that a witness may not testify that a particular legal standard—such as probable cause—was or was not satisfied, because "such opinion testimony ... circumvents the jury's decision-making function." *Hygh v. Jacobs*, 961 F.2d 359, 363–64 (2d Cir. 1992) (excluding opinion that officer's conduct was not "justified under the circumstances"). Whether probable cause existed to arrest Cameron and Higgenbottom was a mixed question for the jury to resolve based on the historical facts. Allowing the lieutenant and the ADAs to testify that probable cause existed told the jury the answer to the ultimate legal question in the case, exactly the kind of testimony *Hygh* forbids.

C. *Evidence Interpretation.* Testimony that merely interprets evidence "within the ken of the average juror" is likewise inadmissible because it adds nothing but the witness's imprimatur. *United States v. Duncan*, 42 F.3d 97, 101 (2d Cir. 1994). The ADAs' commentary on whether particular pieces of evidence strengthened or weakened Appellants' case did nothing but substitute the witnesses' judgment for the jury's own evaluation of facts fully within its competence.

D. *Prejudice.* These errors were not harmless. Because credibility and probable cause were "the two most hotly contested issues in this case," the erroneous testimony went to the heart of the jury's task. Under *Perry v. Ethan Allen, Inc.*, 115 F.3d 143, 150–53 (2d Cir. 1997), such error requires reversal unless it is "highly probable" that it did not affect the verdict; that showing cannot be made where three ostensibly neutral government witnesses told the jury exactly how to resolve the case's core disputes.

**II. The District Court Correctly Denied Judgment as a Matter of Law, but Appellants Preserve Their Argument That the Photographic Record Warrants Reconsideration.**

Judgment as a matter of law is appropriate only where "a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for the [non-movant]." Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a); *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 149 (2000). Where video or photographic evidence "so utterly discredit[s]" one party's account "that no reasonable jury could ... believe[ ]" it, that evidence may resolve the factual dispute as a matter of law. *Scott v. Harris*, 550 U.S. 372, 380 (2007). Appellants maintain that the security-camera stills are consistent only with their account and inconsistent with Appellees' claim that Cameron reached into the patrol car. Because the photographs are not, however, a continuous video capturing every second of the encounter, and could be read to permit either side's narrative depending on the instant captured, Appellants recognize this record does not meet the demanding *Scott* standard. Appellants nonetheless preserve the argument for this Court's consideration in the event it disagrees with their characterization of the photographic gaps.

**III. On Remand, the Jury Must Be Instructed That Obstructing Governmental Administration Requires a Lawful Arrest.**

New York law is unambiguous that a conviction for obstructing governmental administration under N.Y. Penal Law § 195.05 requires that the officers be engaged in "authorized," i.e., lawful, official conduct. *People v. Lupinacci*, 191 A.D.2d 589, 590 (2d Dep't 1993) (obstruction charge cannot stand where underlying arrest was unlawful). Because Higgenbottom's arrest for obstruction was predicated entirely on Cameron's arrest, the jury needed to know that if it found Cameron's arrest unlawful, it could not find Higgenbottom guilty of obstruction. The District Court's failure to so instruct permitted the jury to find against Higgenbottom without ever confronting this predicate legal requirement.

**IV. On Remand, Appellants Are Entitled to a Punitive Damages Instruction.**

Punitive damages are available under § 1983 where a defendant's conduct is "motivated by evil motive or intent, or ... involves reckless or callous indifference." *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). Appellants' version of events—that Ramos and Rivera fabricated a story of assault to justify an unlawful arrest—would permit a reasonable jury to infer the kind of intentional or reckless misconduct that *Smith* requires. The District Court therefore erred in declining to instruct the jury on punitive damages, and on remand, an instruction consistent with *Smith* must be given if the evidence again supports it.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial consistent with the instructions above.
