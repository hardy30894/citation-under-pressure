**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Requires Only That a Defendant Be Competent Under the Standard of *Dusky v. United States*, Not Under a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Test.**

This Court has long defined the constitutional floor of competency in a single, uniform way. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that the test is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. *Dusky* did not purport to create a floor applicable only to standing trial; it announced the constitutional measure of mental competency itself. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), reaffirmed that due process forbids the conviction of a person who lacks the mental capacity described in *Dusky*, explaining that a defendant's competency concerns his "capacity ... to understand the nature of the proceedings against him" and to participate rationally. *Id.* at 384–85.

Nothing in the competency inquiry changes when the defendant, rather than proceeding to trial, chooses to plead guilty or to represent himself. The Ninth Circuit's demand for a distinct "reasoned choice" standard has no textual or doctrinal source in this Court's due process jurisprudence. It rests instead on the mistaken premise that the *decision* to waive rights requires greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But the competency inquiry has always asked whether the defendant possesses the mental capacity to understand and participate—not whether the choice he makes is wise, or is the choice a rational and counseled defendant would make. As this Court explained in *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), competency doctrine safeguards a defendant against being subjected to proceedings he cannot comprehend or participate in; it does not superintend the soundness of the particular decisions a competent defendant elects to make. *Id.* at 108.

**II. The Standards Governing Waiver of Counsel and Guilty Pleas Concern the Voluntariness and Knowledge of the Waiver, Not a Separate, Elevated Mental-Capacity Threshold.**

The decisions of this Court addressing waiver of counsel and guilty pleas confirm that the constitutional inquiry is whether the waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent—not whether the defendant met some heightened capacity beyond *Dusky*. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court examined what showing is necessary before a court may conclude that a defendant has given up his right to counsel, and it directed courts to protect that right against loss through inattention to the record before them. *Id.* at 465. That inquiry asks about the defendant's knowledge and intent respecting his rights, a question distinct from the *Dusky* capacity inquiry, and one that in no way implies a separate, more demanding competency standard. *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required that a waiver of counsel be made "with eyes open," *id.* at 724, emphasizing the defendant's awareness of the risks of self-representation—again a voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry, not a mental-capacity inquiry.

*Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), confirms that the focus is on whether the record shows an informed choice, explaining that "want of counsel at ... arraignment ... does not vitiate ... proceedings" where the record shows the plea was "entered understandingly." *Id.* at 174–75. And *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), recognized that "the Constitution does not force a lawyer upon a defendant," *id.* at 279, because he may competently elect to conduct his own defense—an election this Court treated as a matter of intelligent waiver, not of superior mental competence. Similarly, *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), required that the record affirmatively show a guilty plea was voluntary and intelligent, because such a plea "involves the waiver of several constitutional rights," *id.* at 243, but *Boykin* nowhere suggested that the defendant must meet a heightened standard of rationality beyond that required to stand trial.

**III. This Court's Waiver-of-Counsel Decisions Confirm That the Inquiry Is Whether the Record Shows an Intelligent Waiver, Not Whether the Defendant Satisfies a Heightened Competency Standard.**

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard finds no support in this Court's waiver decisions. In *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), the Court held that a waiver of counsel may not be presumed from a silent record, and that the record must affirmatively demonstrate that the accused understandingly declined the offer of counsel. *Id.* at 516. That inquiry, like the inquiries in *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, and *Carter*, asks whether the defendant's decision was made with adequate understanding and voluntariness—not whether he possessed some elevated degree of rationality beyond the *Dusky* standard. Nothing in *Carnley*, or in any other decision of this Court predating the decision below, requires a separate competency threshold applicable only to waivers of counsel or guilty pleas.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard that governs whether a defendant may stand trial, articulated in *Dusky* and *Pate*, likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional inquiries required by *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, *Boykin*, and *Carnley* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
