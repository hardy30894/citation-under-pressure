Arizona’s rule comports with due process because the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees an opportunity to defend, not a constitutional entitlement to have psychiatric evidence admitted for every possible theory of lack of guilt. The State was required to prove the elements of homicide beyond a reasonable doubt, but it was free to define the legal effect of expert mental-health evidence and to confine that evidence to the insanity defense. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970); Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952); Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895).

The controlling pre-1970 cases confirm that mental responsibility has always been a matter largely left to the States. Davis held that, in federal prosecutions, the government must prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt once the issue is raised, 160 U.S. at 484-85, but Davis was a federal common-law rule, not a constitutional command that States must allow psychiatric evidence to negate intent outside insanity. Leland squarely rejected the due process challenge to Oregon’s rule requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, emphasizing the State’s broad power to allocate the burden on criminal responsibility. 343 U.S. at 798-99. If due process permits a State to place the full burden of insanity on the defendant, it surely permits Arizona’s narrower choice to channel psychiatric proof into the insanity issue and exclude it from a separate diminished-capacity theory. See Salinger v. United States, 272 U.S. 542, 548-49 (1926).

Petitioner’s reliance on the Compulsory Process Clause cases goes too far. Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), invalidated a rule that barred the defendant from calling an accomplice witness at all. The Court explained that the accused was denied “the testimony of witnesses to establish a defense.” Id. at 19. But Washington addressed the categorical exclusion of a witness, not a State’s authority to define what a piece of evidence legally proves. Arizona did not prevent Clark from presenting witnesses or from contesting the State’s case; it simply determined that psychiatric expert testimony could be used to support insanity, not to create a separate partial-defense doctrine. That is a conventional evidentiary and substantive-policy judgment, not a denial of due process.

The other due process decisions involving mental illness do not help petitioner either. Blackburn v. Alabama, 361 U.S. 199 (1960), concerned the voluntariness of a confession obtained from a mentally ill defendant in coercive circumstances. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), concerned competency to stand trial. Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), required a hearing when the record raised a bona fide doubt about competence. None of those cases recognizes a constitutional right to use psychiatric testimony to negate intent independently of an insanity defense. They protect the reliability of the process and the defendant’s capacity to participate in it; they do not constitutionalize a diminished-capacity defense.

General due process principles point the same way. Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105-06 (1934), explained that due process is offended only by procedures that violate “some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” No such principle requires a State to permit expert mental-state evidence to operate as a separate route to acquittal. To the contrary, the common law has long permitted jurisdictions to distinguish between insanity and mere evidence of impaired mental condition. Holt v. United States, 218 U.S. 245, 252-53 (1910), recognized that mental evidence does not automatically control criminal liability, and Fisher v. United States, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946), reflects the Court’s consistent respect for the States’ administration of criminal justice.

Petitioner’s theory would constitutionalize a defense this Court has never required and would substantially limit the States’ authority to define insanity. Before 1970, this Court had upheld broad state control over the burden and scope of mental-responsibility evidence, while requiring only that criminal trials remain fundamentally fair. Arizona satisfied that standard. The judgment should be affirmed.
