**Argument**

Title VII does not require proof that an employer acted “egregiously” or “outrageously,” independent of its state of mind, before punitive damages may be awarded. The statute authorizes punitive damages when the employer acts “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That text focuses on the employer’s mental state, not on an additional freestanding requirement of especially reprehensible conduct. This Court has repeatedly instructed that “[w]here the language of a statute is plain, the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms.” United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc., 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). Nothing in the statutory text adds an “egregiousness” threshold.

This Court’s Title VII cases likewise distinguish liability from damages. In St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502 (1993), the Court held that proof that the employer’s explanation is false permits, but does not compel, an inference of discrimination. Id. at 511. That principle confirms that intent is the central question in discrimination cases; it does not create a separate requirement that the discriminatory act be independently outrageous before Congress’s punitive-damages remedy becomes available. Likewise, Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins, 490 U.S. 228 (1989), recognized that Title VII turns on whether sex was a motivating factor in the employment decision. Id. at 250-52. A statutory punitive remedy tied to knowing or reckless violations naturally tracks that same culpable mental state.

The district court’s approach also conflicts with the structure of § 1981a. Congress enacted the Civil Rights Act of 1991 to expand remedies for intentional discrimination after limiting case law had narrowed relief. Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994). Congress chose a mens rea standard—“malice” or “reckless indifference”—and capped awards by employer size. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), (3). Those limits reflect Congress’s calibrated judgment. Courts should not superimpose an extra-statutory “egregiousness” element that Congress did not write. See Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983).

ADA’s contrary rule would also improperly collapse the punitive-damages inquiry into the underlying liability finding. In BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559 (1996), the Court explained that punitive damages serve to punish and deter wrongful conduct, but they must be assessed under standards that give fair notice and channel discretion. Id. at 568-75. Nothing in Gore suggests that punitive damages require conduct beyond the statutory showing of culpable state of mind. To the contrary, punitive damages are appropriate when the defendant knowingly disregards protected rights. See Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (punitive damages may be awarded upon a showing of “reckless or callous disregard” of the plaintiff’s rights). Congress used language closely tracking Smith, not an independent “outrageousness” test.

Nor does Kolstad v. American Dental Association present the kind of conduct that would justify withholding punitive damages as a matter of law. The jury could find intentional sex discrimination under McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), and Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248 (1981), based on preselection, shifting explanations, and evidence of discriminatory remarks. When an employer’s decisionmaker acts with knowledge that Title VII forbids sex discrimination, or in reckless disregard of that risk, punitive damages are available under the statute. See, e.g., Smith, 461 U.S. at 48-49; International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers v. Foust, 442 U.S. 42, 50 (1979) (punitive damages are tied to culpability and deterrence); Newport v. Fact Concerts, Inc., 453 U.S. 247, 266-70 (1981) (punitive damages punish and deter wrongful conduct).

The courts of appeals that have imposed an extra “egregiousness” filter have read too much into legislative history. Even if legislative materials suggested a heightened standard, this Court has refused to use legislative history to defeat clear statutory text. Connecticut National Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992). And where Congress has specified the mental state, courts should not add another layer. See Barnhill v. Johnson, 503 U.S. 393, 401 (1992).

The better reading of § 1981a is straightforward: if a Title VII defendant acts with malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights, punitive damages may be awarded. The question is the employer’s state of mind, not whether the conduct independently satisfies some vague “egregious” or “outrageous” standard. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
