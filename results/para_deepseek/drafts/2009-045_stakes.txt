**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a reasonable attorneys’ fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes, but only in the rare and exceptional case where specific evidence in the record demonstrates that the lodestar does not adequately compensate counsel for superior performance and exceptional success. This Court’s precedents in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), and *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986) (*Delaware Valley I*), establish that such enhancements are permissible, and the district court’s award in this case falls squarely within that narrow band.

**I. THIS COURT’S PRECEDENTS PERMIT ENHANCEMENTS FOR SUPERIOR PERFORMANCE AND EXCEPTIONAL RESULTS IN RARE CASES**

In *Blum*, this Court held that the lodestar is presumptively reasonable but expressly left the door open for enhancements. The Court stated that “an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988” is a position it rejected. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 901. Instead, the Court recognized that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Id.* (quoting *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 435 (1983)). The Court further explained that an enhancement may be warranted in the “rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional.” *Id.* at 899.

*Delaware Valley I* reaffirmed this principle. The Court stated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible . . . in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901). Although the Court cautioned that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” it did not foreclose the possibility of an extraordinary case. *Id.* at 566. Indeed, the Court left “open the possibility of an extraordinary case.” *Id.*

Petitioners’ argument that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* categorically prohibit enhancements for quality of performance and results obtained misreads these decisions. Neither case held that such enhancements are never permissible. To the contrary, both cases explicitly contemplate that in rare circumstances, with specific evidence, an enhancement may be appropriate. The district court here acted within that framework.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT’S ENHANCEMENT WAS SUPPORTED BY SPECIFIC EVIDENCE AND DETAILED FINDINGS**

The district court did not rely on a general sense of counsel’s performance. Instead, it made detailed findings based on its “own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The court noted that counsel’s representation “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee.” *Id.* at 1290. These findings were not, as petitioners contend, improper “testimony” by the judge. Rather, they were permissible observations based on the court’s firsthand experience with the case—observations that this Court has long recognized as within the province of the district court. *See Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 437 (noting that the district court has “discretion in determining the amount of a fee award” because it is “intimately familiar with the case”).

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the district court did not abuse its discretion. The court of appeals noted that the enhancement was supported by “specific evidence” in the record, including the complexity of the litigation, the duration of the case, and the exceptional results obtained—a comprehensive injunction reforming Georgia’s foster care system. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008). This is precisely the type of “rare” and “exceptional” case that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* contemplated.

**III. PETITIONERS’ DUE PROCESS AND REVIEWABILITY ARGUMENTS ARE WITHOUT MERIT**

Petitioners argue that the district court’s reliance on its own observations violated due process because the court effectively “testified” ex parte. This argument mischaracterizes the district court’s role. A district judge is not required to ignore his or her own observations of counsel’s performance. Indeed, this Court has recognized that the district court’s “superior understanding of the litigation” is a critical component of fee determinations. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 437. The district court’s findings were based on the record and its own observations, not on extra-record evidence. There was no due process violation.

Petitioners also contend that the enhancement is unreviewable because the district court’s findings are too vague. But the district court provided detailed findings, and the Eleventh Circuit reviewed them for abuse of discretion. That standard of review is well established and provides sufficient appellate oversight. *See Pierce v. Underwood*, 487 U.S. 552, 558 (1988) (holding that abuse of discretion review applies to fee determinations).

**CONCLUSION**

This Court’s precedents permit enhancements for superior performance and exceptional results in rare cases supported by specific evidence. The district court’s enhancement in this case was supported by detailed findings and specific evidence, and the Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed. The judgment should be affirmed.
