**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board's Rule Equating the Standard for Polling with the Standard for Withdrawing Recognition Is Arbitrary and Cannot Be Sustained.**

This Court has long recognized that a union's certified or incumbent majority status carries a presumption of continuing employee support, but that presumption is not irrebuttable. *Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 702 (1944); *Brooks v. NLRB*, 348 U.S. 96 (1954). In *Brooks*, the Court explained that the presumption exists to secure industrial stability, not to freeze employee sentiment in place regardless of subsequent events, and that after the certification year the presumption becomes rebuttable precisely because "employees' rights may outrun the life of a particular certification." 348 U.S. at 98-100. The whole premise of a rebuttable presumption is that an employer may test it through a reliable, lawful method — a secret-ballot poll — without first proving the very fact the poll is designed to discover.

The Board's rule defeats that premise. By requiring the same "good-faith reasonable doubt" showing for polling as for outright withdrawal of recognition, the Board has made polling — the most protective, most accurate method of ascertaining employee sentiment — no more available to an employer than the most drastic and unilateral one. An employer with enough evidence to justify a poll already has enough evidence to withdraw recognition outright; the poll becomes surplusage. That result cannot be squared with the Act's central concern, repeatedly emphasized by this Court, that employee free choice be honored and ascertained through the most reliable means available. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 602-03 (1969) (secret elections are "the preferred method of ascertaining whether a union has majority support," and card or other proxy showings are properly used only where elections or comparable safeguards cannot fairly be had). If secret-ballot procedures are the Act's favored device for testing employee wishes, an agency rule that discourages employers from ever using them — by making them pointless — cannot be a reasonable implementation of the statute.

An agency is not free to adopt a rule that undermines the very purpose it invokes to justify the rule. *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 332 U.S. 194 (1947), teaches that an agency's exercise of discretion must rest on a reasoned basis connected to the statute it administers. So too *NLRB v. Metropolitan Life Insurance Co.*, 380 U.S. 438 (1965), where this Court refused to enforce a Board order because the Board offered no reasoned explanation for treating similarly situated classifications differently under the Act. Here the Board itself acknowledged, in *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, that Board-conducted elections are preferable to unilateral polling because elections carry greater procedural protection — yet it applied an identical evidentiary threshold to both. That internal inconsistency is the hallmark of arbitrary agency action. *Burlington Truck Lines v. United States*, 371 U.S. 156, 167 (1962) (an agency's order must reflect reasoned decisionmaking, not a mechanical repetition of a standard untethered to its own stated rationale).

This Court has also cautioned that the Board's considerable expertise in accommodating the competing interests protected by the Act does not exempt it from rational line-drawing. *Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793 (1945); *NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221 (1963). An employer's interest in confirming, through the most neutral and protective means, whether it may lawfully continue to recognize a union is no less a legitimate interest than the employees' interest in freedom from coercion. A polling standard identical to the withdrawal standard serves neither interest; it only forecloses the polling option altogether.

**II. The Board's Finding That Petitioner Lacked a Reasonable, Good-Faith Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence on the Record as a Whole.**

Even under the Board's own standard, its finding cannot stand. Review of Board factfinding is governed by the substantial-evidence standard, but that standard "must take into account whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Substantial evidence requires an examination of the entire record, not merely the evidence that supports the agency's conclusion. *Id.* at 487-88.

Here the ALJ and the Board disregarded probative, unrefuted statements from numerous employees — volunteered without solicitation — expressing dissatisfaction with continued union representation, and instead discounted them one by one on speculative grounds untethered to the record: that statements made during hiring interviews were per se unreliable, that expressions of dissatisfaction with dues could never reflect opposition to representation, and that one employee's report of his coworkers' sentiments was inherently suspect absent independent corroboration from each individual. Nothing in the record establishes that these statements were coerced, insincere, or unrepresentative; the Board's rejection of them rests on categorical assumptions rather than record evidence. Where an agency's rejection of contrary evidence rests on unsupported inference rather than record fact, the reviewing court cannot say the agency's ultimate finding is supported by substantial evidence on the whole record. *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 488; *Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. at 236 (Board findings must rest on the record, not administrative surmise).

For these reasons, the Board's order should not be enforced.
