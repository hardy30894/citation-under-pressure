# ARGUMENT

## I. FLORIDA'S CAPITAL SENTENCING SCHEME COMPORTS WITH THE SIXTH AMENDMENT BECAUSE THE JURY, NOT THE JUDGE, FINDS THE AGGRAVATING CIRCUMSTANCES THAT EXPOSE HURST TO A DEATH SENTENCE

Petitioner's Sixth Amendment claim rests on a fundamental misreading of both Florida law and this Court's decision in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). *Ring* held that "[c]apital defendants, no less than noncapital defendants, . . . are entitled to a jury determination of any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in their maximum punishment." *Id.* at 589. The vice in Arizona's scheme was that "the trial judge, sitting alone, determine[d] the presence or absence of the aggravating factors required by Arizona law for imposition of the death penalty." *Id.* at 588. Florida's scheme suffers from no such defect.

Under Florida's procedure, the jury—not the judge—hears all evidence and returns an advisory verdict on sentence. This Court has already recognized that critical distinction. In *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), Justice Stevens's dissent noted that Florida's jury "does not make specific factual findings with regard to the existence of mitigating or aggravating circumstances," *id.* at 648, but the *majority* in *Walton* rejected the notion that Florida and Arizona schemes are constitutionally indistinguishable in the manner petitioner now urges. Indeed, the very passage petitioner would have this Court rely upon in *Walton* was addressing the Arizona-Florida comparison in a different sentencing context, where the Court concluded that a judge could constitutionally make the ultimate findings—not that the presence of a jury recommendation was constitutionally irrelevant.

The Florida jury in this case heard extensive evidence in the penalty phase, weighed aggravating and mitigating circumstances, and returned a recommendation of death by a vote of seven to five. The trial judge did not act "sitting alone" as in Arizona; rather, the judge's role was to give appropriate weight to the jury's recommendation, informed by the same aggravating and mitigating evidence the jury considered. This structure is precisely what distinguishes Florida from the invalidated Arizona scheme in *Ring*.

Moreover, this Court has consistently required that capital defendants receive individualized sentencing determinations that account for mitigating evidence—a requirement Florida's scheme satisfies. In *Hitchcock v. Dugger*, 481 U.S. 393 (1987), the Court described Florida's procedure as one where the sentencing jury heard evidence "as to any matter that the court deem[ed] relevant to sentence," and rendered "an advisory verdict" addressing both aggravating and mitigating circumstances. *Id.* at 394 (quoting Fla. Stat. § 921.141(1) (1975)). That structure remains intact today. The jury in Hurst's case was properly instructed on aggravating and mitigating factors and rendered its considered judgment. Nothing in *Ring* requires more.

## II. THE ABSENCE OF JURY UNANIMITY DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT

Petitioner's argument that Florida's non-unanimous jury recommendation violates the Sixth Amendment finds no support in this Court's precedent. *Ring* addressed only *who*—judge or jury—must find the aggravating facts necessary to impose death; it said nothing about the *unanimity* requirements for that finding. 536 U.S. at 588–89. Extending *Ring* to mandate unanimity would require this Court to break new constitutional ground that the *Ring* Court itself did not tread.

Notably, even members of this Court who have expressed concern about outlier state sentencing schemes have done so through dissents from denials of certiorari, not through binding precedent. *See Woodward v. Alabama*, 134 S. Ct. 405, 405 (2013) (Sotomayor, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari, expressing "deep concerns" about Alabama's scheme). Such dissents, by their nature, reflect the views of individual Justices rather than the holdings of this Court and cannot supply the rule of decision petitioner seeks.

## III. FLORIDA'S TREATMENT OF MENTAL RETARDATION EVIDENCE SATISFIES THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT

This Court's decision in *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), held that the Eighth Amendment bars execution of the mentally retarded, but the Court expressly "le[ft] to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction." *Id.* at 317. Florida has done precisely that, establishing a three-pronged test requiring proof of subaverage intellectual functioning, concurrent adaptive deficits, and manifestation before age eighteen.

Petitioner received full process on this issue. He was afforded an evidentiary hearing on his mental retardation claim in his initial postconviction proceeding, where competing expert testimony was presented and evaluated. This Court's decision in *Bobby v. Bies*, 556 U.S. 825 (2009), confirms that a defendant's mental retardation may properly be considered as a mitigating factor at sentencing without violating the Eighth Amendment, particularly where—as here—the defendant also receives dedicated process to litigate retardation as a categorical bar. *Id.* at 829–30 (describing pre-*Atkins* practice of treating retardation as mitigation, consistent with *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989)).

That the jury also considered Hurst's cognitive limitations as mitigating evidence—rather than as a threshold jury question—does not offend the Eighth Amendment. This Court has never held that a *jury*, as opposed to a court, must make the *Atkins* determination. The trial court's independent finding, following full evidentiary development, that Hurst is not mentally retarded satisfies *Atkins*'s mandate.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
