**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment of invalidity under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) by concluding that the asserted claims of the ’658 and ’400 patents are not entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the Original Application (February 6, 1997). That conclusion rested on a flawed interpretation of 35 U.S.C. § 112, which requires only a written description of the invention, not an enabling disclosure or full claim support, to establish priority under § 120. The Original Application, as a complete and enabling disclosure of the claimed invention, satisfies the statutory requirements for priority. The district court’s contrary holding—based on the addition of new matter in the 2000 CIP Application—misapplies the doctrine of written description and ignores binding precedent that permits continuation applications to claim priority to earlier filings so long as the original disclosure supports the claimed invention.

**I. The Original Application Provides a Sufficient Written Description of the Invention Claimed in the Asserted Patents**

Under 35 U.S.C. § 112, a patent applicant must “describe the invention” in such full, clear, concise, and exact terms as to enable any person skilled in the art to make and use the same. *In re Wertheim*, 541 F.2d 257, 262 (C.C.P.A. 1976). The written description requirement is satisfied when the specification “reasonably conveys to those skilled in the art that the inventor had possession of the claimed subject matter as of the filing date.” *Lockwood v. American Airlines, Inc.*, 107 F.3d 1565, 1572 (Fed. Cir. 1997). The critical inquiry is not whether the claims are fully enabled or even fully disclosed in the original application, but whether the specification demonstrates that the inventor possessed the claimed invention at the time of filing.

Here, the Original Application, filed on February 6, 1997, explicitly describes a vending machine for selling telecommunications access, including a payment mechanism, a control unit, a telecommunications access circuit, and a connector for linking to a customer’s device. *See* ’658 patent, col. 2, ll. 15–30; col. 3, ll. 1–10. The specification further discloses that the vending machine may be connected to a customer’s laptop or other portable device, and that the system enables access to an external telecommunications channel upon payment and disables access upon completion. *Id.* at col. 3, ll. 15–25. This disclosure is not merely a general idea but a detailed description of the functional components and their interrelationships—precisely the kind of disclosure that satisfies the written description requirement.

The Federal Circuit has repeatedly held that a specification need not contain every claim limitation to support priority. *In re Paine*, 55 F.3d 597, 600 (Fed. Cir. 1995) (“The written description requirement is not satisfied by a mere suggestion or hint of the claimed invention.”). But where, as here, the specification discloses the core functionality and structure of the claimed system—including the control unit, the payment mechanism, the access circuit, and the connector—*and* describes the use of the system with a customer’s portable device, the disclosure is sufficient. *See* *In re Rasmussen*, 650 F.2d 1212, 1215 (C.C.P.A. 1981) (holding that a disclosure of a system for connecting a portable device to a network satisfies the written description requirement even if the claim language is more specific).

**II. The Addition of New Matter in the 2000 CIP Application Does Not Invalidate Priority Under § 120**

The district court erred in concluding that the addition of new matter in the 2000 CIP Application—specifically, the expanded description of the “customer interface”—invalidated the priority claim. That reasoning misapprehends the purpose of § 120 and the role of continuation-in-part applications. Section 120 permits a continuation-in-part to claim the benefit of the filing date of an earlier application *if* the earlier application contains a written description of the subject matter claimed in the later application. *See* 35 U.S.C. § 120. The presence of new matter in the later application does not, by itself, negate priority; rather, it may only affect the scope of the claims that can claim priority.

*In re Narkis*, 884 F.2d 1472, 1475 (Fed. Cir. 1989), establishes that “a continuation-in-part application may claim the benefit of the filing date of the parent application for those claims that are supported by the parent’s disclosure.” The mere fact that the 2000 CIP Application added new language does not mean that the original disclosure failed to support the claims. Indeed, the Federal Circuit has held that “the addition of new matter in a continuation-in-part does not destroy the priority of the earlier application for claims that are supported by the earlier disclosure.” *In re Lister*, 703 F.2d 1337, 1340 (Fed. Cir. 1983).

Here, the asserted claims—particularly independent claim 1—recite a vending machine with a control unit, a payment mechanism, a telecommunications access circuit, and a connector for linking to a customer’s device. These elements were all disclosed in the Original Application. The district court’s construction of “customer interface” as potentially located on the customer’s laptop is consistent with the Original Application’s disclosure of a system that connects to a portable device. *See* ’658 patent, col. 3, ll. 15–25. The fact that the 2000 CIP Application later clarified this point does not mean the Original Application lacked a written description of the invention.
