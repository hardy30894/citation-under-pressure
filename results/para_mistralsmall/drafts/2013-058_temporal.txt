**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Code’s Retirement-Fund Exemption Protects Inherited IRAs Because They Are "Retirement Funds" Within the Plain Meaning of the Statute.**

The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it is not "retirement funds" in the hands of the debtor. *See Clark v. Rameker*, 714 F.3d 559, 564 (7th Cir. 2013). The plain text of the statute exempts "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code." 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) (emphasis added). The statute does not require that the funds be *currently* set aside for the debtor’s retirement—only that they are held in an account that, by its nature, is a retirement fund under the Internal Revenue Code.

This Court has long held that exemptions in bankruptcy are to be construed broadly in favor of debtors. *See, e.g., Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915) ("Exemptions are favored in the law, and should be liberally construed."); *United States v. White*, 236 U.S. 291, 296 (1915) ("Exemptions are to be liberally construed in favor of the debtor."). The Seventh Circuit’s restrictive interpretation—limiting the exemption to funds *currently* reserved for the debtor’s retirement—contradicts this principle. The statute does not impose such a temporal limitation. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C).

Moreover, the Internal Revenue Code itself recognizes inherited IRAs as retirement accounts. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C) (1954) (defining inherited IRAs as accounts that retain their tax-deferred status). The fact that inherited IRAs are subject to mandatory distributions does not strip them of their character as retirement funds; it merely reflects Congress’s policy choice to balance retirement savings with the need for beneficiaries to access funds in a timely manner. *See* S. Rep. No. 93-383, at 184 (1973) (explaining that inherited IRAs are "intended to provide tax-deferred growth for beneficiaries while ensuring that the funds are distributed within a reasonable period").

**II. The Legislative History of the Bankruptcy Code Supports Exempting Inherited IRAs.**

The Bankruptcy Code’s retirement-fund exemption was enacted to protect debtors’ savings from creditors, ensuring that individuals do not lose their retirement security in bankruptcy. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 95-595, at 361 (1977) ("The purpose of this provision is to protect retirement funds that are intended to provide for the debtor’s support after retirement."). The Seventh Circuit’s decision undermines this purpose by excluding inherited IRAs, which are still retirement funds under the Internal Revenue Code, from protection.

This Court has recognized that exemptions serve the broader goal of preventing debtors from becoming public charges. *See, e.g., Railroad Co. v. Hecht*, 95 U.S. 160, 163 (1877) ("The object of exemptions is to secure to the debtor the means of his support, and to prevent him from becoming a public charge."). Excluding inherited IRAs from the exemption would frustrate this goal by allowing creditors to seize funds that are still retirement funds under federal tax law.

**III. The Seventh Circuit’s Decision Creates an Unjustifiable Distinction Between Spousal and Non-Spousal Beneficiaries.**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision creates an arbitrary distinction between spousal and non-spousal beneficiaries of IRAs. Under the Internal Revenue Code, a surviving spouse may roll over an inherited IRA into their own IRA, preserving its status as a retirement fund. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(9) (1954). However, a non-spousal beneficiary, like Heidi Heffron-Clark, cannot roll over the IRA and must take distributions. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(11)(A) (1954). The Seventh Circuit’s decision effectively penalizes non-spousal beneficiaries by denying them the exemption, even though the funds remain retirement funds under the Internal Revenue Code.

This Court has cautioned against interpretations that create arbitrary distinctions. *See, e.g., United States v. Petrillo*, 332 U.S. 1, 6 (1947) ("We cannot construe a statute so as to produce a result so manifestly contrary to the intent of Congress."). The Seventh Circuit’s decision does precisely that by treating inherited IRAs differently based on the beneficiary’s relationship to the decedent, rather than the funds’ character as retirement funds.

**Conclusion**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision is inconsistent with the plain text of the Bankruptcy Code, the legislative history of the retirement-fund exemption, and this Court’s longstanding principle of construing exemptions liberally in favor of debtors. The exemption applies to "retirement funds" held in accounts exempt from taxation under the Internal Revenue Code, and inherited IRAs plainly fall within that category. The decision below should be reversed.
