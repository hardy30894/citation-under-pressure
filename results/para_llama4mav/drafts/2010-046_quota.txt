ARGUMENT

I. The District Court's Order Requiring California to Reduce its Prison Population Does Not Violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) does not prohibit a court from ordering a state to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions. The PLRA requires that any relief ordered be "narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The district court's order meets this standard.

In Brown v. Plata, the Supreme Court held that a court-ordered reduction in prison population was permissible under the PLRA if it was necessary to remedy unconstitutional conditions. 563 U.S. 493 (2011). Similarly, in this case, the district court's order is necessary to remedy the unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons.

The Ninth Circuit has also upheld a district court's order requiring a state to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions. See Toussaint v. McCarthy, 801 F.2d 1080, 1086-87 (9th Cir. 1986), abrogated on other grounds by Sandin v. Conner, 515 U.S. 472 (1995). The court held that the district court's order was "narrowly drawn" and "necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right." Id. at 1087.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has held that a court's power to order remedial relief includes the authority to order a state to take affirmative steps to correct unconstitutional conditions. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978). In this case, the district court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population is an appropriate exercise of this authority.

The State argues that the district court's order violates the PLRA because it requires the release of prisoners. However, the PLRA does not prohibit the release of prisoners as a remedy for unconstitutional conditions. See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i) (permitting a court to order the release of prisoners if certain conditions are met). In fact, the Supreme Court has held that release is a permissible remedy under the PLRA. See Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 511.

In addition, the district court's order is supported by the consent decrees entered into by the State. See United States v. Swift & Co., 286 U.S. 106, 114-15 (1932) (holding that a consent decree is a binding contract between the parties). The State's consent to the decrees demonstrates its acknowledgment of the unconstitutional conditions in its prisons and its agreement to take steps to remedy them.

The State's reliance on cases such as Miller v. French, 530 U.S. 327 (2000), is misplaced. In Miller, the Supreme Court held that the PLRA's automatic stay provision applies to prospective relief, but it did not hold that the PLRA prohibits a court from ordering a state to reduce its prison population. Id. at 344.

II. The District Court's Order Does Not Violate California's Eleventh Amendment Immunity

The State argues that the district court's order violates its Eleventh Amendment immunity. However, the Eleventh Amendment does not bar a federal court from ordering a state to comply with federal law. See Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 159-60 (1908) (holding that a federal court may enjoin a state official from violating federal law).

In this case, the district court's order is an exercise of its authority to enforce federal law and remedy unconstitutional conditions. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. at 687. The order is not a suit against the State for damages, but rather a prospective remedy to correct ongoing constitutional violations.

The Ninth Circuit has held that a district court's order requiring a state to comply with federal law does not violate the Eleventh Amendment. See Armstrong v. Wilson, 124 F.3d 1019, 1025-26 (9th Cir. 1997). In Armstrong, the court held that a district court's order requiring a state to comply with the Americans with Disabilities Act was permissible under the Eleventh Amendment.

In conclusion, the district court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population does not violate the PLRA or the Eleventh Amendment. The order is a necessary remedy to correct unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons, and it is supported by the consent decrees entered into by the State.
