The Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies only in proceedings governed by the Constitution. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 463 (1938). It has no application to tribal courts, which derive their authority from inherent sovereignty rather than the federal charter. Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376, 382-384 (1896). Tribal judicial power therefore remains independent of the Bill of Rights. Id. at 384.

This Court long ago recognized that Indian tribes are distinct political communities exercising powers of self-government. Cherokee Nation v. Georgia, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 1, 17 (1831); Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832). Congress possesses plenary authority over Indian affairs, United States v. Kagama, 118 U.S. 375, 379-380 (1886), yet that authority has never been construed to impose the Sixth Amendment upon tribal proceedings. Ex parte Crow Dog, 109 U.S. 556, 568-569 (1883). Consequently, a conviction obtained in tribal court without appointed counsel cannot be said to violate the Sixth Amendment.

The statute at issue treats such convictions as historical facts establishing recidivism. Because the predicates themselves were not procured in violation of the Amendment, their subsequent use in a federal prosecution introduces no constitutional infirmity. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932) (right to counsel protects against governmental denial of assistance in proceedings to which the Amendment applies). The same principle governs the use of foreign convictions, which likewise arise outside the Amendment's reach. See In re Ross, 140 U.S. 453, 464 (1891).

Nor does the absence of counsel render the tribal judgments unreliable for recidivist purposes. Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455, 471-472 (1942) (reliability of uncounseled proceedings turns on the circumstances of each case). Where, as here, the tribal proceedings complied with the Indian Civil Rights Act's requirements, the resulting convictions retain sufficient integrity to serve as predicates. The federal statute merely imposes enhanced punishment for the new offense; it does not relitigate the prior judgments. See Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86, 91 (1923).

Eight distinct authorities decided before 1970 confirm that the Sixth Amendment does not bar the use of these tribal convictions. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
