# ARGUMENT

**I. Milford's Denial of Access Was a Permissible Subject-Matter Limitation, Not Viewpoint Discrimination.**

Milford's Community Use Policy, like the policy this Court sustained in *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), simply "forecloses use for religious purposes" altogether. In *Lamb's Chapel*, the district court found that because the school "had not opened its facilities to organizations similar to Lamb's Chapel for religious purposes, the . . . denial in this case was viewpoint neutral." *Lamb's Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 384 (excerpt). Milford's Policy operates identically: it bars *all* groups, regardless of creed, from using school facilities "for religious purposes," and Milford has never granted such access to any group. This is subject-matter regulation of the kind the Second Circuit correctly upheld, not the viewpoint discrimination condemned in *Lamb's Chapel* itself, where the film series merely discussed child-rearing "from a Christian perspective." Id.

*Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), and *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), are likewise distinguishable, not controlling. Both involved speakers who wished to address an otherwise-permitted secular topic—student journalism, campus discussion groups—"from a religious perspective," a perspective this Court described as "a specific premise, a perspective, a standpoint from which a variety of subjects may be discussed and considered." *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831 (excerpt). Milford does not exclude discussion of morals or character "from a religious perspective." It has excluded the Good News Club because the Club's own submissions describe a meeting whose "teaching objective" is that a "saved" child give God "first place," complete with an "Invitation" segment scripting a conversion prayer for the "unsaved." That is not commentary on a secular subject; it is the religious exercise itself—prayer, evangelism, and instruction in belief—the very activity the Policy withholds from every group, secular or religious.

**II. Even If the Exclusion Touched Viewpoint, Milford's Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation Justified It.**

This Court has never held that a school's Establishment Clause concerns can *never* justify excluding religious exercise from its forum. In *Widmar*, the Court held only that "an open-forum policy, including nondiscrimination against religious speech, would" not offend the Establishment Clause *in the university setting*—among "mature" students voluntarily present on a campus with "no...compulsion." *Board of Ed. of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 226 (1990) (quoting Widmar analysis). *Bender v. Williamsport Area School District*, 475 U.S. 534 (1986), confirms that this Court's precedents distinguish "state establishment of religion" from "individual participation and advocacy of religion," id. at 534, but that distinction depends on context—precisely why *Widmar*'s university holding does not automatically transfer to a building full of six- and seven-year-old elementary schoolchildren, gathered immediately after the school day, on school property, under the school's own roof.

This Court's decision in *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), illustrates the line Milford drew. The released-time program in *Zorach* was permissible because it "involve[d] neither religious instruction in public school classrooms nor the expenditure of public funds," id. at 306, and because children left the school building entirely for religious instruction elsewhere. By contrast, the program condemned in *McCollum*, as *Zorach* itself explained, failed because "the classrooms were turned over to religious instructors." Id. Milford's own building, immediately after the school day, filled with young children summoned to a "Challenge and Invitation" leading to a scripted salvation prayer, is far closer to the *McCollum* model than to *Zorach*'s. Milford reasonably concluded that lending its facilities to such proselytizing activity, targeted at impressionable elementary-school children who had just spent the day under compulsory attendance in that very building, created a serious risk that the community would perceive the school as sponsoring the Club's religious message.

**III. Milford's Content-Based, Viewpoint-Neutral Forum Limitation Is Consistent With This Court's Forum Doctrine.**

The government need not throw open every forum to every message. Just as the city in *New York State Club Assn., Inc. v. City of New York*, 487 U.S. 1 (1988), could limit associational rights where "amply justified by the city's compelling interest," id. at 1 (excerpt), and just as this Court in *Board of Directors of Rotary International v. Rotary Club of Duarte*, 481 U.S. 537 (1987), balanced associational freedom against countervailing state interests, Milford's Establishment Clause concern is at least as compelling. A school's obligation to avoid even the appearance of establishing religion among the young children within its walls is a distinct and powerful interest that justifies a subject-matter line excluding religious worship and instruction from an otherwise-open forum, without regard to viewpoint.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
