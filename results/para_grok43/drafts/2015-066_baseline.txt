**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not prohibit the use of Michael Bryant’s prior tribal-court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Those convictions were obtained in proceedings that complied with the Indian Civil Rights Act and therefore “do not violate the Sixth Amendment.” Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738, 746-47 (1994) (citing Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367 (1979)). Because the Bill of Rights “does not apply to Indian tribes,” Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008), the convictions are constitutionally valid and may serve as the basis for enhanced federal punishment of a subsequent offense.

Section 117(a) is a recidivist statute that “penalize[s] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747; see also United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008) (“100% of the punishment is for the offense of conviction. None is for the prior convictions.”). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding rests on United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), which treated tribal convictions as “constitutionally infirm” for want of appointed counsel. That premise is irreconcilable with the settled rule that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel “does not obtain” in proceedings where no sentence of imprisonment is imposed, Scott, 440 U.S. at 372, and does not extend to tribal courts at all, United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978).

Nichols itself confirms that an uncounseled but valid conviction may be used to enhance punishment for a later offense. 511 U.S. at 746-48. The same principle governs when the prior conviction is an element of a recidivist offense. Alabama v. Shelton, 535 U.S. 654, 661-62 (2002), makes clear that invalidation of a sentence does not invalidate the underlying conviction. Every other circuit to address the question has so held. United States v. Shavanaux, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011); United States v. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011).

Requiring tribal courts to furnish counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment before their judgments may be given effect would “intrude[] needlessly on tribal self-government,” Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978), and disregard the comity owed tribal adjudications, Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987). Congress has already calibrated the balance between tribal sovereignty and due process through the Indian Civil Rights Act and subsequent enactments. The Ninth Circuit’s rule supplants that legislative judgment with a judicial one foreign to the Sixth Amendment.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
