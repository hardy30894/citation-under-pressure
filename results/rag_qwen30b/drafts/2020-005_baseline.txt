**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the defendant has established “minimum contacts” with the forum state such that the exercise of jurisdiction does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This principle requires that the defendant’s contacts with the forum be purposeful, not random or fortuitous, and that the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” those contacts. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472 (1985). The Montana Supreme Court’s decision in this case—affirming personal jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company based solely on the company’s general advertising and sale of parts within Montana—violates this foundational requirement.

The facts here are stark: Ford Motor Company has no physical presence in Montana. It is incorporated in Delaware and headquartered in Michigan. The vehicle at issue was assembled in Kentucky and first sold to a dealership in Washington State. The vehicle was then transferred through Oregon before being brought into Montana by a private purchaser. The accident occurred on a Montana highway, but the tire failure was not caused by any activity Ford conducted in Montana. Indeed, Ford did not sell the vehicle to a Montana resident, nor did it market or distribute the vehicle in Montana. The only connection between Ford and Montana is the sale of replacement parts—parts that were not involved in the accident and were not used in the vehicle that crashed.

The Montana Supreme Court held that Ford’s sale of parts within the state constituted “purposeful availment” of the forum, thereby establishing specific personal jurisdiction. But this reasoning is fundamentally flawed. The Court’s conclusion rests on a misreading of *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915 (2011), which made clear that “specific jurisdiction” requires a connection between the forum and the underlying controversy. *Id.* at 924. The Court emphasized that “the episode-in-suit” must have a “substantial connection” with the forum state. *Id.* at 925. Here, the episode-in-suit—the tire failure and fatal crash—occurred in Montana, but the tire was manufactured and sold abroad, and Ford’s only contact with Montana was the sale of unrelated parts. These contacts are “attenuated” and “unrelated” to the claims at issue. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980).

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on Ford’s advertising and sale of parts in Montana is legally insufficient. As this Court has repeatedly held, “the unilateral activity of those who claim some relationship with a nonresident defendant cannot satisfy the requirement of contact with the forum State.” *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. at 474. A defendant does not “purposefully avail” itself of a forum merely by engaging in general commercial activity that is not tied to the specific claims brought. *Id.* at 475. The sale of parts, even if it occurs in Montana, does not establish the kind of “affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy” necessary for specific jurisdiction. *Goodyear*, 564 U.S. at 924. The parts sold in Montana were not used in the vehicle that crashed, nor were they related to the design or manufacturing of the tire that failed. There is no causal link between Ford’s part sales and the injury.

Moreover, the Montana Supreme Court’s decision undermines the predictability and fairness that due process demands. If a corporation can be haled into court in any state where it sells parts—even if those parts are unrelated to the injury—then the boundaries of personal jurisdiction become meaningless. As this Court has warned, “the foreseeability that is critical to due process analysis ... is that the defendant’s conduct and connection with the forum State are such that he should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there.” *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297. Ford did not sell the vehicle in Montana, did not market it there, and had no reason to anticipate that a tire failure in Montana would lead to litigation in that state. The mere fact that a part was sold in Montana does not transform that state into a forum for claims arising from a crash thousands of miles away.

The dissent in *Goodyear* correctly observed that “the mere fact that a product is sold in a state does not mean that the state has jurisdiction over all claims arising from that product.” 564 U.S. at 935 (Scalia, J., dissenting). The same principle applies here. The Montana Supreme Court’s decision allows plaintiffs to “shop” for favorable forums based on the availability of parts sales, regardless of the actual connection between the defendant’s conduct and the injury. This is precisely the kind of “random, fortuitous, or attenuated” contact that due process forbids. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475.

The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court must be reversed. The Due Process Clause does not permit a state to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when none of the defendant’s contacts with the state caused the plaintiff’s claims. Ford’s sale of parts in Montana is not such a contact. The judgment below is inconsistent with *International Shoe*, *Burger King*, *Goodyear*, and *World-Wide Volkswagen*. It violates the constitutional guarantee of fair notice and due process.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be reversed.
