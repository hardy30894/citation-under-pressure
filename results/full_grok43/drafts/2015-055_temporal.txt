ARGUMENT

The rule announced in Johnson v. United States applies retroactively on collateral review. Constitutional rules that narrow the class of persons subject to criminal punishment must receive full retroactive effect. Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 17 (1956). This principle rests on the recognition that a new substantive limitation on punishment cannot be withheld from those already convicted under the invalidated provision without denying equal protection of the laws. Eskridge v. Washington State Bd. of Prison Terms & Paroles, 357 U.S. 214, 216 (1958).

A penal statute violates due process when it fails to give fair notice of the conduct it proscribes. Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) ("No one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes."). The same prohibition applies when a statute requires courts to assess hypothetical risks posed by an abstract version of a crime, rendering application arbitrary. United States v. Harriss, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954). Once such a provision is declared void, prior sentences resting upon it cannot stand, for the Constitution forbids punishment under an invalid law. See Bouie v. City of Columbia, 378 U.S. 347, 353 (1964) ("If a state legislature is barred by the Ex Post Facto Clause from passing such a law, it must follow that a State Supreme Court is barred by the Due Process Clause from achieving precisely the same result by judicial construction.").

Retroactive application of such a ruling follows directly from the nature of the constitutional defect. Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618, 628 (1965) (new rules that affect the integrity of the criminal process apply retroactively). Because the Johnson rule eliminates an entire category of predicate offenses previously used to impose enhanced sentences, it is substantive and therefore retroactive. Griffin, 351 U.S. at 17. Petitioner's sentence, predicated on the now-invalid residual clause, must be vacated.
