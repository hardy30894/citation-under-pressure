**ARGUMENT**

**I. Arizona’s Rule Channeling Mental-Disease Evidence to the Insanity Defense Is a Permissible Exercise of the State’s Authority to Define the Elements of Crimes and Defenses.**

This Court has long recognized that “[p]reventing and dealing with crime is much more the business of the States than it is of the Federal Government.” *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201 (1977). States possess broad authority “to define the elements of and defenses to crimes.” *Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 210. That authority necessarily includes the power to decide how competing evidentiary theories relating to mental condition will be presented to the jury.

This Court’s decision in *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996), controls the outcome here. In *Egelhoff*, the Court upheld a Montana statute barring the use of voluntary intoxication evidence to negate the mental-state element of a crime, even though such evidence was logically relevant to whether the defendant possessed the requisite intent. The plurality explained that due process does not guarantee a defendant “the right to introduce all evidence bearing on the question of intent,” and that a State may, consistent with due process, “channel” such evidence to a different stage of trial. *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 56 (Scalia, J., plurality op.). The plurality further observed that “the doctrine of mens rea” has historically been subject to numerous limitations reflecting the State’s judgment about how best to balance the interests of accurate fact-finding against the danger that certain classes of evidence will mislead a jury. *Id.* at 49–50.

Arizona’s rule, articulated in *State v. Mott*, 187 Ariz. 536, 931 P.2d 1046 (1997), does precisely what *Egelhoff* permits: it restricts evidence of mental disease or defect to the insanity inquiry, where the defendant bears the burden of proof, rather than allowing it to be used more diffusely to negate the *mens rea* element that the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. That channeling rule reflects a considered judgment—shared by numerous other jurisdictions—that psychiatric testimony about a defendant’s mental capacity is more reliably and fairly evaluated within the structured insanity framework than as freestanding evidence offered to rebut intent.

**II. Due Process Does Not Require a State to Permit Mental-Disease Evidence to Be Used to Negate Mens Rea Independent of an Insanity Defense.**

This Court has repeatedly rejected the proposition that due process compels the admission of all evidence a defendant considers relevant to his mental state. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon’s requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, holding that the choice of legal test for insanity, and the allocation of the burden of proving it, are matters of state policy that do not offend due process. The Court emphasized that “the fact that a substantial minority of the States have rejected the [prevailing] rule is not persuasive evidence that the concept of due process” requires a different approach. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 800–01.

Likewise, in *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), this Court held that a trial court did not err in refusing to instruct the jury that it could consider evidence of the defendant’s mental deficiency in determining whether he possessed the premeditation necessary for first-degree murder. The Court reasoned that questions concerning “the consequences that flow from partial insanity” are appropriately left “to the legislative process” of the several jurisdictions rather than compelled by the Constitution. *Fisher*, 328 U.S. at 476. The same reasoning applies with full force here: Arizona has made a legislative and judicial judgment, through *Mott*, about how psychiatric evidence bearing on mental capacity should be deployed at trial, and that judgment falls squarely within the zone of permissible state choice recognized in *Fisher* and *Leland*.

**III. Clark’s Due Process Rights Were Fully Respected Because He Retained an Adequate Opportunity to Present His Mental-State Evidence Through the Insanity Defense and Through Observation Evidence.**

Arizona’s rule did not silence Clark’s evidence; it directed it to the proper channel. Clark was permitted to—and did—introduce extensive expert testimony regarding his schizophrenia in support of his insanity defense. What Arizona’s rule precluded was the use of that same diagnostic evidence for the distinct purpose of negating the *mens rea* element, a limitation this Court sanctioned in *Egelhoff*. Just as Montana could, without violating due process, prevent a defendant from using intoxication evidence to challenge intent while still permitting that evidence to inform other aspects of the case, Arizona may direct psychiatric evidence to the insanity defense without offending the Due Process Clause.

Moreover, Arizona’s framework preserved space for “observation evidence”—testimony describing Clark’s behavior and mental state at the time of the offense—separate from expert “mental-disease” or “capacity” evidence. This distinction, grounded in the trial court’s evidentiary rulings, ensured that the jury received relevant information about Clark’s actual conduct while avoiding the risk that diagnostic labels would be misused to redefine the *mens rea* standard itself.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, and consistent with this Court’s decisions in *Egelhoff*, *Leland*, and *Fisher*, Arizona’s rule confining mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense comports with due process. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
