**ARGUMENT**

The question here is whether Congress, by granting Temporary Protected Status under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a, also deemed Petitioners “admitted” for purposes of adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The answer is no. Under the Immigration and Nationality Act, “admission” means “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This Court has repeatedly recognized that “admission” and “status” are distinct concepts. Hanif v. Attorney General, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012); Taveras v. Attorney General, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013). TPS confers lawful status; it does not retroactively create the lawful entry that § 1255(a) requires.

Section 1255(a) provides that only an alien who “was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States” may adjust status. 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). That threshold requirement is unmistakable. Congress knew how to relax it when it wanted to. In the same statute, it created specific exceptions for certain categories of aliens. See 8 U.S.C. § 1255(i), (h); see also I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987). Congress did not create a comparable exception for TPS recipients. Instead, it said only that TPS beneficiaries “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). That language addresses lawful status, not admission. Had Congress intended TPS to satisfy § 1255(a)’s separate admission requirement, it could have said so. Its silence is dispositive. See Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. at 432.

Petitioners’ contrary reading collapses the distinction Congress drew between “admission” and “lawful status.” This Court has already rejected that conflation. In Hanif, the Court explained that “the date of gaining a new status is not the same as the date of the physical event of entering the country.” 694 F.3d at 485. Likewise, Taveras held that “admission” involves “physical entrance into the country,” while adjustment proceedings occur “entirely within the United States.” 731 F.3d at 290. TPS is, by design, an in-country humanitarian protection. It is not “a program of entry for an alien.” De Leon-Ochoa v. Attorney General, 622 F.3d 341, 353–54 (3d Cir. 2010). Petitioners entered without inspection or admission in 1997 and 1998; TPS did not change that historical fact.

The structure of the INA confirms the point. Section 1255(c)(2) bars adjustment for aliens who have failed to maintain lawful status since entry, while § 1255(a) separately requires prior inspection and admission or parole. If TPS status itself satisfied § 1255(a), the distinct admission requirement would do no work. Courts must avoid interpretations that render statutory text superfluous. Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004). Petitioners’ reading would do just that. It would also nullify Congress’s careful calibration in § 1254a(h), which provides a special mechanism for certain status changes by supermajority legislation. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(h)(2). Congress did not enact that elaborate mechanism only to allow TPS status itself to operate as an implied admission under § 1255(a).

Petitioners rely on cases describing adjustment of status as an “admission,” but those cases address a different question: whether a lawful adjustment can itself count as an admission for other purposes. See In re Espinosa-Guillot, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (B.I.A. 2011). They do not suggest that the grant of TPS is equivalent to inspection and admission. Nor does the humanitarian purpose of TPS justify rewriting the statute. TPS “was designed to shield aliens already in the country from removal,” not to confer a permanent pathway to lawful permanent residency absent the statutory prerequisites. De Leon-Ochoa, 622 F.3d at 353–54.

Because Petitioners were never “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States,” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a), they are ineligible for adjustment of status. The judgment below should be reversed.
