# ARGUMENT

**AN UNCOUNSELED TRIBAL COURT CONVICTION, VALID WHEN ENTERED, MAY BE USED AS A PREDICATE OFFENSE UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)**

The Ninth Circuit's decision cannot be reconciled with this Court's precedent, creates an unwarranted circuit split, and undermines both tribal sovereignty and Congress's considered response to the epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country. The court of appeals erred in holding that Bryant's tribal court convictions—concededly valid when entered, since the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376 (1896)—became retroactively infirm once used to prove an element of a subsequent federal offense.

**I. Nichols v. United States Controls This Case**

This Court's decision in *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), establishes the controlling principle: a valid uncounseled misdemeanor conviction may be used in subsequent proceedings so long as no sentence of imprisonment was imposed *in that conviction* without counsel. The Court held that "an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid under *Scott* because no prison term was imposed, is also valid when used to enhance punishment at a subsequent conviction." *Id.* at 748-49. Bryant's tribal convictions, entered pursuant to the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303, were valid because the Sixth Amendment simply does not extend to tribal proceedings. *See Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (recognizing it is "beyond dispute" that "the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes").

The Ninth Circuit attempted to distinguish *Nichols* as a sentencing case, reasoning that Winship's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard for elements of an offense demands greater reliability than the preponderance standard used at sentencing. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). But this reasoning conflates the reliability of the *fact* of conviction with the reliability of the underlying prosecution. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized in recidivist-statute cases, the prior conviction is not being relitigated for its own sake—it is being used as a jurisdictional or status element proving the defendant's recidivism. "[R]ecidivism statutes ... penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant." *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747. As this Court later confirmed, "[w]hen a defendant is given a higher sentence under a recidivism statute ... 100% of the punishment is for the offense of conviction. None is for the prior convictions." *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008). Section 117(a) operates identically: Bryant's federal punishment is entirely for his 2011 assaults; the tribal convictions merely establish his status as a repeat offender, precisely as in *Nichols*.

**II. Alabama v. Shelton Confirms That Only the Sentence, Not the Conviction, Is Constitutionally Vulnerable**

This Court's decision in *Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654 (2002), forecloses the Ninth Circuit's reasoning. *Shelton* held that a suspended sentence could not be activated based on an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, but the Court's remedy left the underlying conviction intact. *Id.* at 674 (leaving the "judgment of guilt" undisturbed while vacating only the sentence). If an uncounseled conviction resulting in an *actual* term of imprisonment were void ab initio, *Shelton* would have vacated the conviction itself. It did not. Every circuit to consider this question has recognized that *Shelton*'s remedy demonstrates that convictions and sentences are severable. *See United States v. Ortega*, 94 F.3d 764, 769 (2d Cir. 1996); *United States v. Moskovits*, 86 F.3d 1303, 1309 (3d Cir. 1996). The Ninth Circuit's rule—that imposition of a prison sentence retroactively invalidates the underlying misdemeanor conviction for all future purposes—has no home in this Court's jurisprudence and conflicts with the reasoning of every other circuit to address analogous statutes, including the Eighth and Tenth Circuits construing § 117(a) itself. *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 604 (8th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011).

**III. Principles of Tribal Sovereignty and Comity Confirm That Tribal Convictions Complying With ICRA Are Not Constitutionally Deficient**

The Ninth Circuit's approach also disregards fundamental principles of comity owed to tribal courts. As this Court has explained, tribal courts retain "the continued exercise of retained tribal sovereignty" over their members, *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978), and federal courts must extend comity to tribal judgments, *Iowa Mutual Insurance Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987). Congress calibrated ICRA's procedural protections—including its counsel provisions—to the sentencing authority it granted tribal courts, and later amended those provisions through the Tribal Law and Order Act, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(c), and the Violence Against Women Reauthorization Act, 25 U.S.C. § 1304, when it expanded tribal jurisdiction further. Congress did not extend a Sixth Amendment right to counsel to the misdemeanor tribal proceedings at issue here. Imposing such a right through backdoor invalidation of convictions used in subsequent federal prosecutions "intrude[s] needlessly on tribal self-government," *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978), and treats ICRA-compliant proceedings as inherently suspect—a result contrary to congressional intent and this Court's repeated instruction that tribal sovereignty be respected absent clear congressional abrogation. *See Lara*, 541 U.S. at 200.

**CONCLUSION**

Because Bryant's tribal court convictions were valid when entered, and because *Nichols* and *Shelton* confirm that such convictions retain their validity for use in subsequent federal recidivist prosecutions, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
