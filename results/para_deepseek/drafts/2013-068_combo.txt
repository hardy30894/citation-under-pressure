ARGUMENT

The Massachusetts Act is a constitutional time-place-manner regulation under principles this Court settled long before the modern era. The decision below correctly applied those principles, and no later precedent compels a different result.

I. Public Streets and Sidewalks Are Subject to Reasonable Regulation.

*Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939), recognized that streets and parks “have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public” and are “used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions.” But this Court has never treated speech on a sidewalk as absolutely immune from regulation. In *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 160 (1939), the Court explained that “a state may regulate the conduct of its citizens in the use of the streets.” *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941), emphasized that a municipality has “the duty to prevent a disturbance of the peace and to regulate the use of its streets and other public places in order to promote the convenience and safety of the public.” The Act does exactly that: it establishes a modest fixed zone to protect ingress and egress to healthcare facilities.

II. The Act Is a Content-Neutral Regulation.

The Act prohibits any person from knowingly entering or remaining on a sidewalk within 35 feet of a reproductive health facility’s entrance, exit, or driveway. It references no viewpoint, no subject, and no message. It applies identically to all speakers—pro-life counselors, pro-choice advocates, and others. That is the hallmark of a content-neutral law. In *Cox*, the ordinance applied to all parades “regardless of the cause the parade may espouse.” *Id.* at 575. In *Cameron v. Johnson*, 390 U.S. 611, 617 (1968), the Court upheld a statute forbidding picketing that obstructs court ingress because it was “limited to interference with ingress and egress.” The Massachusetts Act is similarly limited to a narrow buffer around clinic entrances, not to any particular message.

III. The Act Serves Significant Governmental Interests.

The State has compelling interests in protecting the safety of patients, employees, and pedestrians, and in ensuring “unimpeded ingress and egress” to a medical facility. *Cameron*, 390 U.S. at 617. The 35-foot zone is a modest, narrow measure—far smaller than other restrictions this Court has approved. In *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 89 (1949), the Court upheld a ban on sound trucks because it was “a reasonable regulation of the use of the streets.” The Act is no broader than necessary: it does not prohibit speech anywhere else in the city, and it exempts persons entering or leaving the facility, employees, emergency responders, and pedestrians passing through. *Giboney v. Empire Storage & Ice Co.*, 336 U.S. 490, 502 (1949), confirms that the State may act to prevent expressive conduct that interferes with lawful activities. The buffer zone is a reasonable and proportionate response to documented obstruction and intimidation outside clinics.

IV. The Act Leaves Ample Alternative Channels.

The Act does not silence petitioners; it merely moves them 35 feet back. They may still pray, display signs, distribute literature, and engage in sidewalk counseling. *Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88, 105 (1940), acknowledged that “the State may, in the exercise of its police power, regulate the methods and means of picketing.” Here, the methods are not destroyed; they are adjusted in location. *Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 163, recognized that streets are “natural and proper places for the dissemination of information and opinion,” but that principle does not mean every speaker is entitled to stand at every point on every sidewalk. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 576, upheld a parade regulation despite the fact that it relocated speakers. The Act leaves open a vast range of alternative communication immediately outside the buffer zone.

V. The Act Is Not a Prior Restraint.

The Act contains no permit requirement and grants no official discretion. It is a fixed geographical rule, akin to the ordinance upheld in *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 405 (1953), where the Court explained that a general and reasonable regulation of public assembly is not an invalid prior restraint. The Act imposes no censorship and no official review of speech. It is a simple rule of conduct designed to keep sidewalks passable near medical facilities.

The First Circuit’s judgment rests on these settled precedents. The Act is a narrow, content-neutral measure that protects public safety and clinic access while leaving abundant room for speech. The judgment should be affirmed.
