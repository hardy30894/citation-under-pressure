# ARGUMENT

## I. Having Opened Its Facilities to Community Groups for Expressive Use, the District May Not Exclude Speech Because of Its Religious Content.

The First Amendment's protection against content-based exclusion does not turn on whether the property at issue is a street, a park, or a school auditorium; it turns on whether the government has opened that property to expressive use by outside speakers. Streets and parks receive the highest constitutional protection because they "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public and, time out of mind, have been used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). The same principle of open access governs wherever government voluntarily throws its property open to community expression—as the Center Moriches School District did here by regularly admitting civic clubs, musical performers, and lecturers on subjects ranging from parapsychology to metaphysics. Having created such a forum, the District could not then draw a line excluding only religious speech.

That Lamb's Chapel's message took the form of a film does not weaken its claim to protection. This Court has long held that motion pictures are "a significant medium for the communication of ideas" entitled to full First Amendment protection. *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495, 501 (1952). The District cannot escape its obligations by treating a film on family values differently from a live lecture on the identical subject merely because the film's perspective happened to be religious. "A function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute," *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), and it was precisely the District's discomfort with the religious perspective of Lamb's Chapel's message that produced its exclusion.

## II. Excluding Religious Speech While Tolerating Comparable Secular Speech Is Impermissible Viewpoint Discrimination.

This Court has repeatedly struck down governmental denial of access to public facilities where the exclusion rested on the religious character of the speaker's message while comparable secular activity was permitted. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court invalidated the denial of a park permit to Jehovah's Witnesses where other groups had used the same park for their own meetings and the denial rested on official disapproval of the applicants' religious views rather than any neutral standard. The Court reached the same result in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), condemning the discriminatory application of a park ordinance against a religious meeting while similar secular gatherings proceeded undisturbed. And in *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290 (1951), the Court held that officials could not exercise licensing discretion to exclude a speaker because they disapproved of the religious content of his message.

The record here reveals the identical defect. The District permitted the Southern Harmonizers to perform and allowed a library-sponsored lecture series touching on "spiritual" and "metaphysical" topics, yet categorically barred anything "church related"—the very words its own clerk used, not once but three times, to justify refusing Lamb's Chapel's applications. That is viewpoint discrimination of precisely the sort *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, and *Kunz* forbid.

## III. The Establishment Clause Neither Requires Nor Permits the District's Discrimination Against Religious Speech.

Any suggestion that the Establishment Clause compelled the District's exclusion finds no support in this Court's precedent. The Establishment Clause demands governmental neutrality toward religion; it does not license hostility toward it. As this Court explained, the First Amendment "does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952). Nor does neutrality require government to treat religious speakers worse than secular ones; it requires only that government neither advance nor inhibit religion through its own action. *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947); *School District of Abington Township v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963). Permitting Lamb's Chapel to use the auditorium on the same terms afforded to other community groups would not establish religion; it would simply extend to religious speech the neutral access already extended to secular speech in the same forum.

## IV. Public School Property Is Not an Enclave Exempt from First Amendment Scrutiny.

That the property at issue belongs to a public school does not diminish the constitutional analysis. This Court has made clear that "it can hardly be argued that either students or teachers shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate," and that "state-operated schools may not be enclaves of totalitarianism." *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503, 506, 511 (1969). If students and teachers retain full First Amendment protection within the schoolhouse, a fortiori the District may not use its control over school facilities to silence a community organization's protected expression once it has opened those facilities to similar secular use. The collective, associational character of Lamb's Chapel's proposed activity—a congregation seeking to share its views on family life with the broader community—is itself protected activity of the kind this Court has long safeguarded. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415 (1963).

## CONCLUSION

The District's exclusion of religious speech from a forum otherwise open to comparable secular expression cannot be reconciled with *Hague*, *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, *Kunz*, *Burstyn*, *Terminiello*, *Zorach*, *Everson*, *Schempp*, *Tinker*, or *Button*. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
