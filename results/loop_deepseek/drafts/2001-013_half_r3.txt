**ARGUMENT**

The Fifth Circuit erroneously held that the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) lacks jurisdiction over the working conditions aboard Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52, an uninspected oil and gas exploration barge. The plain text of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, coupled with the limited regulatory reach of the Coast Guard over uninspected vessels, compels the conclusion that OSHA’s citation was lawful.

**I. Rig 52 Is a “Workplace” Under the Act**

The Act applies broadly to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). A drilling barge engaged in extracting oil from the seabed is not a “vessel in navigation” within the traditional maritime meaning. *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565, 572 (1944) (a floating workshop “permanently affixed to the shore or riverbed” is not a vessel). *The Tonawanda*, 274 U.S. 145, 150 (1927) (a floating drydock is not a ship). The *Daniel Ball* Court held that “[t]he term ‘vessel’ includes every description of watercraft, used in navigation.” 77 U.S. 557, 559 (1870). Like those structures, Rig 52 was stationary while drilling—its sole purpose—and thus was not a vessel used in navigation. *Desper v. Starved Rock Ferry Co.*, 342 U.S. 187, 191 (1952) (a ferry that actively navigated was a vessel). The fact that Rig 52 could be moved does not transform it into a navigational vessel. *See The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. at 559 (defining a vessel by its use in navigation). Consequently, Rig 52 is a land-based workplace subject to OSHA’s authority.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction**

Section 4(b)(1) exempts working conditions where another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Mere jurisdiction is insufficient; the agency must *actually* regulate the same condition. This Court has long held that preemption requires an actual conflict, not speculative overlap. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (preemption necessary only when “the federal government has so far occupied the field as to preclude state regulation”). *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (preemption applies when “the federal government has so completely occupied the field as to leave no room for state regulation”). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is minimal. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 grants it power over only fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and similar equipment. It has no authority to prescribe comprehensive safety standards for drilling operations. *The City of Panama*, 101 U.S. 453, 455 (1880) (the inspection laws apply only to vessels navigating the high seas and carrying passengers or freight). The Coast Guard’s failure to regulate the working conditions that gave rise to this explosion—well control, blowout prevention, and general workplace safety—means it has not “exercise[d] statutory authority” over those conditions.

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s Reliance on Post-1970 Precedents Is Misplaced**

The court below rested on its own decisions in *Clary* and *Donovan*, both decided after the OSH Act’s 1970 enactment and thus inapplicable to this pre-1970 analysis. But even under pre-1970 law, workers on uninspected drilling barges are not “seamen” entitled to an industry-wide exemption. *Norton*, 321 U.S. at 571 (admiralty jurisdiction does not extend to structures like this barge). Rig 52’s employees performed industrial oil well work, not traditional maritime duties. Their working conditions are analogous to those on a fixed platform—areas indisputably covered by OSHA. *The Blackheath*, 195 U.S. 361, 367 (1904) (admiralty jurisdiction does not extend to structures permanently attached to the land). Overlapping regulation is not the anomaly the lower court feared; it is the norm when maritime safety touches landside operations. *The Arizona v. Anelich*, 298 U.S. 110, 123 (1936) (seamen’s remedies are governed by maritime law, but that law does not preempt all other safety regulation).

In sum, Rig 52 is a workplace, the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over its working conditions, and OSHA’s citation is valid. The judgment below should be reversed.
