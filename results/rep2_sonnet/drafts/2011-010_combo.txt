# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEXT OF SECTION 1333(b) IMPOSES A CAUSATION REQUIREMENT, NOT A SITUS-OF-INJURY REQUIREMENT

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage to "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." The statute locates the *operations*—not the *injury*—on the Shelf. When Congress wished to impose a geographic situs requirement, it knew how to write one, and did so elsewhere in the same statute governing choice of law. Where Congress instead selects causal language such as "resulting from" and "as a result of," this Court has refused to graft an additional locational limitation onto words that Congress chose to define by cause rather than place.

This Court has long recognized that "there is . . . no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress's chosen words in Section 1333(b) express a wish to compensate injuries causally connected to Shelf operations, wherever those injuries occur. Petitioner's situs-of-injury gloss disregards this textual choice and imports a requirement Congress did not enact.

## II. LIBERAL CONSTRUCTION OF THIS REMEDIAL WORKERS' COMPENSATION SCHEME FORECLOSES PETITIONER'S CRAMPED READING

The Longshore Act and its OCSLA extension are remedial legislation designed to compensate injured workers, and this Court has repeatedly instructed that such statutes must be construed to serve that purpose rather than to defeat it. In *Voris v. Eikel*, 346 U.S. 328 (1953), this Court held that the Longshore Act "must be liberally construed in conformance with its purpose," and that ambiguous coverage provisions should be read in a manner that "avoids harsh and incongruous results." *Id.* at 333. A situs-of-injury rule produces precisely the "harsh and incongruous results" *Voris* condemned: an offshore roustabout whose death arises entirely out of Shelf drilling operations is denied compensation solely because the forklift that killed him happened to be parked onshore rather than on the platform.

This Court's decision in *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251 (1940), likewise construed Longshore Act coverage broadly according to the character of the employment and its connection to maritime commerce, not according to rigid locational formulas, deferring to the administrative fact-finder's assessment of that connection. And in *Calbeck v. Travelers Insurance Co.*, 370 U.S. 114 (1962), this Court extended Longshore Act coverage to injuries within state territorial waters despite textual ambiguity, reasoning that Congress intended a uniform, complete compensation scheme rather than gaps created by fortuitous geography. The same reasoning compels rejection of a situs-of-injury rule here: Congress designed Section 1333(b) to eliminate compensation gaps for workers whose injuries arise from Shelf extraction, not to recreate them based on which side of an arbitrary line the injury occurred.

## III. THIS COURT'S TREATMENT OF ANALOGOUS SITUS LANGUAGE CONFIRMS THAT CAUSAL PROVISIONS SHOULD NOT BE READ AS GEOGRAPHIC LIMITATIONS

When this Court considered the original Longshore Act's express situs requirement in *Baltimore & Philadelphia Steamboat Co. v. Norton*, 284 U.S. 408 (1932), it enforced that requirement because Congress had explicitly conditioned coverage on injury "upon the navigable waters of the United States." *Id.* at 413. Congress used comparably explicit locational language in the very same Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act to fix the situs of the Shelf itself and of the choice-of-law provision governing "the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). Congress's contrasting choice of causal language in Section 1333(b) must be given effect rather than collapsed into the situs formula it enacted elsewhere. *Cf. Pennsylvania R. Co. v. O'Rourke*, 344 U.S. 334 (1953) (treating the statutory situs question as distinct from the question of causal connection to covered operations).

## IV. THE DIRECTOR'S REASONABLE INTERPRETATION OF SECTION 1333(b) IS ENTITLED TO DEFERENCE

The Director of the Office of Workers' Compensation Programs administers this statute and has consistently interpreted Section 1333(b) as a causation test rather than a situs-of-injury test. This Court has long held that "great deference" is owed to the interpretation given a statute by the agency charged with its administration. *Udall v. Tallman*, 380 U.S. 1, 16 (1965). Even where such interpretations do not carry the force of formal regulation, this Court has accepted an agency's determination of statutory coverage where it has "warrant in the record" and a reasonable basis in law. *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 131 (1944). The Director's substantial-nexus approach satisfies both criteria, reflects the statute's remedial purpose, and merits this Court's respect.

## CONCLUSION

The text, purpose, and administrative history of Section 1333(b) all confirm that Congress conditioned coverage on the *cause* of an injury—its connection to Outer Continental Shelf operations—not on the *place* where the injury occurred. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
