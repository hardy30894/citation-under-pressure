## ARGUMENT

### I. The Release Does Not Bar Bancorp’s Claims Because Its Plain Language Covers Only Claims Arising from the OCC’s Decision to Close the Bank, Not from the FDIC’s Subsequent Conduct as Receiver

The release executed by Bancorp on June 6, 1988, releases the FDIC only from claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” This language is unambiguous. Under New York law, a release “is a contract, and its construction is governed by contract law.” *Lee v. BSB Greenwich Mortg. Ltd. P’ship*, 267 A.D.2d 85, 86 (1st Dep’t 1999). Where the language is clear and unambiguous, a court must give effect to its plain meaning. *See W.W.W. Assocs., Inc. v. Giancontieri*, 77 N.Y.2d 157, 162 (1990).

Bancorp’s claims do not arise from the OCC’s decision to close the Bank. Rather, they challenge the FDIC’s *post-closure* conduct: paying itself post-insolvency interest, selling Bank assets below “true franchise value,” charging legal and office expenses to the receivership estate, and providing an inadequate accounting. These actions occurred years after the closure and are independent of the OCC’s decision. The release does not mention post-receivership conduct, and it cannot be read to immunize the FDIC from all future misconduct simply because the closure was a but-for cause of the receivership. *See, e.g., Consarc Corp. v. Marine Midland Bank, N.A.*, 996 F.2d 568, 573 (2d Cir. 1993) (release limited to “claims arising out of” a specific agreement did not bar claims based on separate conduct).

The FDIC’s alternative argument—that the release covers all claims “with respect to” the closure—is overbroad. The phrase “with respect to” does not transform the release into a blanket waiver of all claims tangentially related to the bank’s failure. *See, e.g., In re Prudential Lines Inc.*, 158 F.3d 65, 71 (2d Cir. 1998) (release of claims “arising out of” a transaction does not bar claims based on separate, subsequent conduct). Bancorp’s claims target the FDIC’s independent fiduciary duties as receiver, not the OCC’s closure decision.

### II. The Statute of Limitations Did Not Begin to Run Until the Receivership Terminated on November 1, 1995

Bancorp’s claims for breach of fiduciary duty, unjust enrichment, corporate waste, and accounting are governed by New York’s six-year statute of limitations. N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7). The critical question is when these claims accrued. Under New York law, a claim for breach of fiduciary duty accrues when the plaintiff “suffers damages” or “has knowledge of facts from which the breach could be reasonably inferred.” *Matter of Estate of Barabash*, 31 N.Y.2d 76, 80 (1972). However, where the fiduciary relationship is ongoing—as with a receivership—the statute of limitations may be tolled until the relationship ends. *See, e.g., Golden Pac. Bancorp v. FDIC*, 273 F.3d 509, 518 (2d Cir. 2001) (recognizing that “the fiduciary relationship between a receiver and the shareholders of a failed bank continues until the receivership is terminated”).

Here, the FDIC acted as receiver from June 21, 1985, until November 1, 1995. During that entire period, the FDIC owed Bancorp—as the Bank’s majority shareholder—a fiduciary duty to manage the receivership estate prudently. *See, e.g., FDIC v. Bank of Am. Nat’l Trust & Sav. Ass’n*, 701 F.2d 831, 834 (9th Cir. 1983) (receiver owes fiduciary duties to creditors and shareholders). The FDIC’s alleged misconduct—charging expenses, paying itself interest, selling assets—occurred within the receivership. Because the fiduciary relationship was continuous, Bancorp could not bring a complete claim until the receivership ended and the FDIC rendered its final accounting. *See, e.g., In re Estate of Rothko*, 43 N.Y.2d 305, 320 (1977) (statute of limitations for breach of fiduciary duty runs from termination of the fiduciary relationship).

The district court erred in holding that the statute began to run from the dates of the individual alleged wrongful acts. *See Golden Pac. Bancorp v. FDIC*, 2000 WL 776928, at *5 (S.D.N.Y. June 15, 2000). That approach ignores the continuing nature of the fiduciary duty and the fact that Bancorp could not ascertain the full extent of its damages until the receivership was wound up. *See, e.g., City of New York v. Lead Indus. Ass’n*, 222 A.D.2d 119, 126 (1st Dep’t 1996) (continuing wrong doctrine tolls statute where wrongful conduct is ongoing). Bancorp filed its complaint on October 31, 1995—one day before the receivership terminated—well within six years of the termination date. Accordingly, the statute of limitations does not bar its claims.

### III. Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
