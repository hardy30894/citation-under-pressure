**Argument**

California’s challenge to the population-reduction order fails because the Prison Litigation Reform Act authorizes precisely this kind of relief when it is necessary to cure unconstitutional prison conditions and no less intrusive remedy will suffice. The PLRA provides that prospective relief in prison-conditions litigation must be “narrowly drawn,” “extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right,” and is “the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). Where prison overcrowding is the primary cause of ongoing Eighth Amendment violations, a population cap may be ordered if the statutory findings are made. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 511-12, 531-32 (2011); 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii).

This Court has long recognized that federal courts possess broad equitable authority to remedy constitutional violations in state prisons. In Hutto v. Finney, the Court upheld a limit on punitive isolation as part of a remedial decree, explaining that “federal courts have the authority to order appropriate relief” when necessary to end unconstitutional confinement conditions. 437 U.S. 678, 687-88 (1978). The Court further held that remedies must be evaluated in light of the “wide discretion” afforded district courts in fashioning equitable relief. Id. at 687. Likewise, in Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail, the Court explained that institutional reform decrees are designed to cure systemic constitutional violations and may be adjusted when changed circumstances require. 502 U.S. 367, 379-80 (1992). Nothing in the PLRA strips courts of the power to order population relief when overcrowding itself drives the violation.

The district court’s order here fits comfortably within the PLRA’s express framework. Congress specifically authorized “prisoner release order[s]” as a last-resort remedy for systemic constitutional violations, but required that such an order be entered only by a three-judge court after findings that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the violation and that no other relief will remedy it. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii), (b)(3). Those findings were made here, and California does not seriously dispute the underlying record of severe, persistent Eighth Amendment violations. Under Brown v. Plata, when the statutory predicates are satisfied, a population-reduction order is not only permissible but sometimes necessary because “[c]ourts must not shrink from their obligation to enforce the constitutional rights of all persons.” 563 U.S. at 511.

California’s contrary position would convert the PLRA from a remedial statute into a shield for ongoing constitutional violations. That is not what Congress enacted. The PLRA’s “need-narrowness-intrusiveness” requirements do not bar effective relief; they require courts to tailor it. Gilmore v. California, 220 F.3d 987, 998-1000 (9th Cir. 2000). Here, the challenged order does exactly that by tying relief to a population benchmark designed to alleviate overcrowding-related constitutional harms, rather than dictating prison administration beyond what is necessary. See Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 531-32.

Nor does the Eleventh Amendment bar enforcement of this remedy. Under Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 159-60 (1908), federal courts may order prospective relief against state officials to end continuing violations of federal law. The order at issue is prospective and remedial; it seeks to cure ongoing Eighth Amendment violations, not to impose retroactive monetary liability. See Edelman v. Jordan, 415 U.S. 651, 664-68 (1974). California cannot invoke sovereign immunity to avoid compliance with a federal decree enforcing the Constitution. Frew ex rel. Frew v. Hawkins, 540 U.S. 431, 437 (2004).

This Court should therefore affirm. The order is authorized by the PLRA, supported by the district court’s findings, and necessary to cure unconstitutional prison overcrowding. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493 (2011); Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678 (1978); Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail, 502 U.S. 367 (1992).
