The respondent respectfully submits that the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity plea.

The Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent. The Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure in their courts. As the Court held in Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278 (1936), "the due process clause does not impose a rigid set of rules on the states." Id. at 285. This principle is echoed in Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), where the Court noted that "the state is not bound to provide the same procedure for its citizens in the prosecution of crimes that is required by the federal Constitution for the prosecution of crimes in the federal courts." Id. at 101.

The respondent argues that the trial court's limitation on the use of Clark's expert evidence did not deprive him of a fundamental right. The due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present all evidence he desires. See Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893); Sinclair v. United States, 279 U.S. 263, 269 (1929). States have the right to regulate the presentation of evidence in their courts, Jordan v. Massachusetts, 225 U.S. 167, 176 (1912), and the Arizona rule limiting the use of expert evidence to the insanity claim is a reasonable exercise of this authority.

The Court has also recognized that the insanity defense is a "substantive" issue that is separate from the question of whether the defendant formed the requisite criminal intent. See Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895). In Davis, the Court held that the insanity defense is a distinct issue that requires separate consideration from the elements of the crime. Id. at 488. The Arizona rule is consistent with this principle, as it requires the defendant to prove insanity separately from the prosecution's burden to prove the elements of the crime.

Furthermore, the Court has upheld state laws that restrict the use of certain types of evidence. In Shepard v. United States, 290 U.S. 96 (1933), the Court held that a state law excluding certain evidence did not violate the due process clause. Similarly, in Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the Court upheld a state law that limited the defendant's right to be present at certain stages of the trial.
