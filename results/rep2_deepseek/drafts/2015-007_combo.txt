## Argument

The Florida Supreme Court correctly affirmed Timothy Hurst’s death sentence. Neither the Sixth Amendment’s jury-trial guarantee nor the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishments requires a jury to find aggravating circumstances, to render a unanimous advisory sentencing recommendation, or to adjudicate a capital defendant’s mental retardation.

### I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Extend to Capital Sentencing

The Sixth Amendment right to trial by jury, incorporated against the States in *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145, 149 (1968), is a right to a trial of guilt. It has never been an essential part of the sentencing process. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246 (1949). The common-law jury determined only whether the accused committed the crime; the judge imposed the sentence. *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930). Florida’s capital scheme mirrors that division: the jury renders a binding verdict of guilt, while the judge, after weighing aggravating and mitigating factors, chooses between life and death. The jury’s seven-to-five death recommendation is merely advisory.

Aggravating factors are not elements of a separate capital offense requiring jury findings. When a legislature has not made an aggravator an element of the crime, a judge may make the necessary sentencing findings without invading the province of the jury. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. 76, 95 (1820) (penal statutes are strictly construed). *Williams* is instructive: due process does not prevent a judge from considering information outside the guilt-phase record in exercising sentencing discretion. *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 248-49. Because Florida’s judge, not the jury, makes the ultimate determination of death-eligibility, the Sixth Amendment is not implicated.

Nor does the Constitution require unanimity for a jury’s advisory sentencing recommendation. In *Jordan v. Massachusetts*, 225 U.S. 167, 176 (1912), this Court upheld a nine-to-three state-court verdict and observed that the Constitution does not compel unanimous verdicts in state criminal trials. If a non-unanimous verdict of guilt is constitutional, a fortiori a non-unanimous advisory recommendation of death cannot offend the Sixth Amendment.

### II. The Eighth Amendment Imposes Only Substantive Limits on Punishment

The Eighth Amendment forbids only punishments of a barbarous or torturous nature; it does not mandate jury participation in sentencing. *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1878); *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890) (prohibiting only “the punishment of torture” or “unnecessary cruelty”). The Amendment’s reach is directed at the character of the punishment, not the procedure by which it is imposed. *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947) (asking whether a method of execution “involves torture or a lingering death”). Florida’s death sentence is neither.

The States possess broad authority over the administration of their criminal laws. *United States v. Hudson & Goodwin*, 11 U.S. 32, 34 (1812). That authority includes defining the categories of offenders eligible for execution and the procedures by which eligibility is adjudicated. Nothing in the Eighth Amendment’s text or history requires that mental retardation be determined by a jury. Florida, like many States, entrusts that threshold question to the sentencing judge after a full evidentiary hearing. Here, the judge found, under a clear-and-convincing-evidence standard, that Hurst is not mentally retarded. That finding was independent, reasoned, and supported by conflicting expert testimony. The Eighth Amendment requires no more.

Hurst’s argument that the jury must unanimously find aggravators or unanimously recommend death confuses a modern policy preference with a constitutional mandate. The Eighth Amendment “must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society,” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958), but that flexible standard does not, of its own force, adopt the procedural apparatus Hurst demands. Absent a categorical rule—which no pre-1970 decision of this Court recognized—each State may calibrate its own capital sentencing procedures within the substantive confines of the Amendment. Florida’s judge-centric scheme, which requires a unanimous guilt verdict and a judge’s independent review of aggravators and mitigators, fully satisfies those confines.

The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
