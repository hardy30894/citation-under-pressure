# ARGUMENT

## I. THE BOARD'S "GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT" STANDARD FOR POLLING IS IRRATIONAL AND INCONSISTENT WITH THE ACT

An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of continuing majority support, which an employer may overcome through "objective considerations" that raise a reasonable doubt as to that support. *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990); *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 38 (1987). Under existing Board precedent, an employer with such doubt may pursue any of three options: withdraw recognition outright, petition for a Board-conducted RM election, or conduct an internal poll. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057 (1989). The Board, however, requires the identical evidentiary showing—"sufficient objective evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition from the union"—for all three options. *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984).

This equivalence renders polling illusory. Three courts of appeals have so held, reasoning that if an employer already possesses evidence sufficient to withdraw recognition outright, it has no need to poll at all. *See Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 866-67 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1298; *A.W. Thompson, Inc.*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145. As the Ninth Circuit explained, polling can be a "useful and legitimate tool for the employer who is in sincere doubt of the union's majority status" only if the standard for polling is lower than the standard for withdrawal. *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1299. Three circuits have accordingly adopted a "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support" standard—distinct from and lower than the showing required for outright withdrawal. *Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 867.

The Board's justification for maintaining a single standard collapses under its own internal logic. In *Texas Petrochemicals*, the Board acknowledged that RM elections are its "preferred" mechanism for testing union support because they carry procedural safeguards that unsupervised polls lack. 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061-62. Yet the Board applies the same evidentiary threshold to the less-protected mechanism (polling) as to the more-protected one (RM elections), and again to the wholly unprotected act of outright withdrawal. This is backwards. If procedural safeguards justify a *more* permissive threshold—as they plainly do—then withdrawal of recognition, which affords employees no process whatsoever, should require the *most* rigorous showing, not the same showing as a supervised poll conducted by neutral observers, as occurred here.

Nothing in the National Labor Relations Act compels the Board's flattening of these three distinct mechanisms into a single undifferentiated standard. The statute is silent on internal employer polling altogether. Where an agency's chosen standard produces the anomalous result of rendering a lawful employer option practically meaningless, and where three circuits have independently concluded that the standard cannot be reconciled with the purposes it purports to serve, this Court should decline to defer. *Cf. Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB*, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978) (Board's rules must have a "reasonable basis in law").

## II. THE BOARD'S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN LACKED REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

Even under the Board's own standard, its finding cannot stand. Substantial evidence means "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion," *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 477 (1951), and a reviewing court must consider the record as a whole, including evidence that "fairly detracts" from the Board's conclusion. *Id.* at 488.

The unrebutted record shows that Allentown Mack received statements from numerous employees—among a unit of only thirty-two—expressing opposition to or lack of support for continued union representation. The Board discounted this evidence through categorical exclusionary rules that themselves lack evidentiary grounding. It refused to credit employee reports of coworkers' sentiments (such as those from Bloch and Mohr) as inherently unreliable hearsay, notwithstanding that such secondhand reports are precisely the sort of real-world evidence employers rely upon in ordinary workplace decision-making. It further discounted statements made during job interviews (Ridgick) and expressions of dissatisfaction with union dues (Marsh) as categorically insufficient, without regard to the totality of the circumstances.

This case is, if anything, stronger than *Thomas Industries*, where the Sixth Circuit found no substantial evidence supporting the Board's conclusion that the employer lacked good-faith doubt, notwithstanding a presumption of continuing majority support that had become rebuttable simply through passage of time. 687 F.2d at 868. Here, the presumption rests on weaker footing still: the thirty-two-employee unit at Allentown Mack was never itself certified or independently sampled for majority support, but was carved from a larger predecessor unit of forty-five employees. The statistical foundation for presuming that this smaller successor unit retained the same proportional union support is inherently attenuated.

Given the totality of the objective evidence—including statements from more than one-fifth of the unit's employees expressing anti-union sentiment, obtained through ordinary workplace interactions rather than coercion—the Board's conclusion that Allentown lacked good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
