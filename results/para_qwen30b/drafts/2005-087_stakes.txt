**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant a separate due process right—distinct from the insanity defense—to introduce expert evidence about his mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent. Arizona’s rule, which limits the use of such evidence to the affirmative defense of insanity, is consistent with settled constitutional principles and does not violate due process. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects a defendant’s right to a fair trial, including the right to present a defense. See *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). However, this right is not absolute, and the Constitution does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce evidence for purposes beyond the scope of a recognized legal defense. *See United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998) (“The Due Process Clause does not require a State to admit evidence that it has a legitimate reason to exclude.”). Arizona law properly confines the use of expert testimony on mental state to the insanity defense, a well-established affirmative defense that requires the defendant to prove by clear and convincing evidence that, at the time of the offense, he was unable to appreciate the nature and quality of his act or to distinguish right from wrong. See Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-501(A); *State v. Hines*, 146 Ariz. 557, 561 (1985).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a defendant’s right to present evidence is subject to reasonable rules of evidence and procedure. *See *Olden v. Kentucky*, 488 U.S. 227, 231 (1988) (“The Due Process Clause does not require a State to admit evidence that is irrelevant or unreliable.”). Arizona’s rule is neither arbitrary nor irrational; it reflects a legitimate state interest in maintaining the integrity of the criminal trial process by preventing the fragmentation of the insanity defense and ensuring that the burden of proof remains properly allocated. *See *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 982 (D.C. Cir. 1972) (en banc) (“The insanity defense is a legal, not a factual, determination, and the state may define the scope of the evidence admissible to support it.”).

Clark’s argument—that he should be allowed to use expert testimony to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent—misconstrues the nature of criminal liability. The prosecution bears the burden of proving every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, including criminal intent. *See *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. If Clark’s mental condition negated his ability to form intent, that is a factual issue that may be considered by the jury in determining whether the prosecution has met its burden. But the Constitution does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce expert testimony on mental state for that purpose if the state has not recognized such a defense. *See *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d at 982. Arizona has not adopted a “diminished capacity” defense, and the absence of such a defense does not violate due process. *See *State v. Bowers*, 137 Ariz. 175, 180 (1983) (“The state is not constitutionally required to recognize a diminished capacity defense.”).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has rejected the notion that due process mandates a defendant’s right to introduce evidence to negate criminal intent through mental illness, even when the defendant is diagnosed with a serious mental disorder. In *United States v. Brawner*, the D.C. Circuit held that a defendant’s right to present a defense does not include the right to introduce evidence that would undermine the prosecution’s burden of proof unless the state has created a legal defense for that purpose. *Id.* at 982. The Court has similarly emphasized that “the Constitution does not require a State to adopt any particular rule of evidence or procedure.” *See *Herring v. New York*, 422 U.S. 853, 860 (1975). Arizona’s rule is a reasonable and rational application of its criminal law framework.

Clark’s claim also fails because he was not precluded from presenting expert evidence at all. He was permitted to introduce expert testimony in support of his insanity defense, which is a constitutionally protected right. *See *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 410 (1986) (“The Constitution protects the right to present a defense.”). The trial court’s limitation on the use of that evidence did not deprive him of a meaningful opportunity to be heard. *See *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. at 310 (“The Constitution does not require a State to admit evidence that it has a legitimate reason to exclude.”). The fact that the jury rejected his insanity defense does not imply a due process violation. *See *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d at 982.

Finally, the Arizona Supreme Court’s decision in *State v. Hines*, 146 Ariz. 557 (1985), squarely supports the state’s position. There, the court held that a defendant may introduce expert testimony on mental illness to support an insanity defense, but not to establish a separate defense of diminished capacity. The court emphasized that “the state may define the scope of the insanity defense and the evidence admissible to support it.” *Id.* at 561. This principle is consistent with the Due Process Clause and does not violate the Constitution.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The Fourteenth Amendment does not require Arizona to permit a defendant to use expert mental health evidence to negate criminal intent outside the framework of the insanity defense. The state’s rule is rational, consistent with precedent, and fully compatible with due process.
