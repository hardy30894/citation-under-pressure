**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption Favoring Scienter Requires This Court to Construe §875(c) to Demand Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Communication as a Threat.**

Section 875(c) is silent as to the mental state required for conviction, but that silence does not authorize a strict-liability or purely objective reading. This Court has long recognized that Anglo-American criminal law has historically conditioned criminal liability on proof of a culpable mental state, a requirement so deeply rooted in the common law that congressional silence as to intent is ordinarily read as an invitation to import the traditional mens rea requirement rather than to dispense with it. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250–251, 263 (1952).

That presumption applies with full force here, because §875(c) does not fall within the narrow category of "public welfare" offenses in which Congress may dispense with scienter, such as regulatory violations involving inherently dangerous or heavily regulated conduct. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–252 (1922). A prosecution for "threat[s]" transmitted through ordinary speech is far removed from the regulatory context of *Balint*; it is a true crime carrying substantial felony penalties and stigma, the paradigm case for reading in a subjective intent element. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260.

This Court has repeatedly construed statutes to require specific intent where a purely objective reading would create constitutional difficulty. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court read a "willfully" requirement into a federal civil-rights statute to require that the defendant act "in reckless disregard of constitutional prohibitions or guarantees fairly implied from the fact that they are so often expressed and understood," 325 U.S. at 105, precisely to avoid punishing defendants for conduct they did not appreciate as unlawful. Likewise, this Court has long insisted that penal statutes give fair warning of what conduct they proscribe, explaining that although "it is not likely that a criminal will carefully consider the text of the law before he murders or steals," it remains true that "a fair warning should be given to the world in language that the common world will understand" of what conduct is forbidden. *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931). The same interpretive caution compels reading §875(c) to require that the defendant subjectively intend to communicate a threat.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Turns on the Speaker's Intent, Not Merely a Listener's Reaction.**

Even apart from statutory construction, the Constitution independently requires proof of subjective intent before speech may be punished as a "true threat." Only narrow, historically unprotected categories of speech fall outside the First Amendment's shelter. This Court has recognized such narrow categories sparingly and has cautioned that restrictions on speech must be defined with precision; for example, in upholding a narrowly drawn obscenity law, the Court emphasized that "obscenity is not within the area of constitutionally protected speech or press," a holding premised on the historically narrow and carefully bounded nature of the category being excluded from protection. *Roth v. United States*, 354 U.S. 476, 485 (1957). The same care must attend any doctrine — such as the "true threat" exception — that purports to remove speech from the Amendment's coverage; an objective, listener-focused standard that punishes speech based on how it might be received, rather than on what the speaker meant to convey, sweeps far beyond the narrow and carefully defined categories this Court has recognized.

Consistent with that caution, this Court has held that speech restrictions must be confined to circumstances in which the danger from the speech is grave and unambiguous, explaining that "the substantive evil must be extremely serious and the degree of imminence extremely high before utterances can be punished." *Bridges v. California*, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941). Free expression that provokes discomfort, anger, or alarm in listeners does not thereby lose its protected character; indeed, this Court has recognized that a central function of free speech is to invite dispute and unsettle prevailing sentiment. *Terminiello v. City of Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949). If speech may not be suppressed merely because it provokes anger or alarm in its audience, a fortiori a speaker's own words may not be transformed into an unprotected "true threat" solely because a listener, applying a reasonable-person standard, perceives them as threatening — without regard to whether the speaker intended any such meaning.

An objective standard threatens to chill vast amounts of protected expression — dark humor, hyperbole, rap lyrics, venting — because speakers cannot know in advance how an unknown audience will receive ambiguous words. Where scienter requirements are absent, "the ordinance tends to impose a severe limitation on the public's access to constitutionally protected matter," because a speaker of ordinary prudence will steer far wider of the forbidden zone than the Constitution requires. *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 154 (1959). Where advocacy or expression is at issue, this Court has likewise insisted on proof of intent before punishment may attach. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951).

**III. An Objective, Negligence-Based Standard Punishes the Merely Careless and Cannot Be Reconciled With Due Process.**

Finally, dispensing with subjective intent effectively converts §875(c) into a negligence offense — punishing defendants for failing to anticipate how their words might be received, rather than for what they meant to convey. This Court has cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where fair notice is at a premium, holding that "no one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes." *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939); *see also Screws*, 325 U.S. at 101–102 (plurality opinion). The judgment below should be reversed, and this Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statement to be understood as a threat.
