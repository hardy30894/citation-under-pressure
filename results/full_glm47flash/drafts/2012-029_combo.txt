**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Patent Issue is a Necessary Element of the Malpractice Claim, Triggering Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction Under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a).**

The Supreme Court of Texas erred in holding that exclusive federal jurisdiction does not lie in this case. The Court focused on the "substantiality" of the patent issue in isolation, rather than its necessity to the resolution of the underlying state law claim. Under the "well-pleaded complaint" rule, federal jurisdiction is determined by the plaintiff's well-pleaded complaint, not by the defendant's defenses. *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 112 (1936). Here, the patent issue is not a defense raised by Gunn, but a necessary element of Minton’s malpractice claim. As the Supreme Court has held, a case "arises under" federal law if the right to relief depends upon the construction of that law. *United States v. Wunderlich*, 342 U.S. 98, 101 (1951). The experimental use exception is a specific construction of 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). Without resolving this issue, the state court cannot determine whether Gunn’s failure to assert the defense constituted legal malpractice. Therefore, the patent issue is a "necessary element" of the cause of action, satisfying the jurisdictional requirements of § 1338(a).

**II. The Malpractice Claim is a "Case Within a Case" Requiring Resolution of the Federal Patent Issue.**

The malpractice claim is a "case within a case" that cannot be decided without resolving the underlying federal patent validity question. The Supreme Court has held that the federal law must be a "necessary element" of the cause of action for a case to arise under federal law. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). In this instance, the validity of the '643 Patent and the applicability of the on-sale bar are essential to determining whether Gunn’s conduct fell below the standard of care. The state court’s decision to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction effectively prevents Minton from proving the elements of his malpractice claim. Because the federal patent issue is a necessary element of the malpractice claim, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over the matter.

**III. The Patent Issue is Substantially Involved in the Dispute.**

A case "arises under" federal law only if it "really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). The experimental use exception is a complex area of patent law that requires the application of specific statutory interpretation. It is not a mere incidental fact or a peripheral issue. The Supreme Court has recognized that the federal interest in uniformity in patent law is paramount. *Cohen v. Beneficial Indus. Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). Allowing state courts to decide the validity of the experimental use exception would create a patchwork of conflicting interpretations, undermining the uniformity of patent law. Thus, the patent issue is substantially involved in the dispute.

**IV. The State Court’s Decision Disturbs the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities.**

The Supreme Court has cautioned against allowing state plaintiffs to open the "arising under" door by simply pleading a federal issue. *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117-18. The "well-pleaded complaint" rule is a "selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Id.* at 118. Here, the patent issue is not a "substantial cause" in the abstract, but it is a necessary element of the malpractice claim. The Supreme Court has also noted that federal courts may entertain the application of federal law within a state-based suit without disturbing the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 313-14 (2005). However, the Court of Appeals’ decision to apply the Federal Circuit’s post-*Grable* test, which focuses on the "substantiality" of the patent issue rather than its "necessity" to the malpractice claim, is inconsistent with the well-pleaded complaint rule established by the Supreme Court. The state court’s decision to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction effectively prevents Minton from proving the elements of his malpractice claim. Because the federal patent issue is a necessary element of the malpractice claim, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over the matter.
