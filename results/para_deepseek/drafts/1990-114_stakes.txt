**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT COVER APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL.”**

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) defines “employee” to exclude “any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Appointed state judges fall squarely within this exception.

**A. Judges Exercise Policymaking Authority.**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). When judges decide cases, they “necessarily will resolve issues previously unsettled and thus will create law.” *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 603 (8th Cir. 1990). For example, the Missouri Supreme Court has the authority to adopt rules governing professional conduct for the judiciary and bar, and to resolve disputes within the judicial hierarchy. *See In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc). These responsibilities are quintessentially policymaking.

Petitioners’ argument that judges merely “decide cases between litigants involving questions of law in which there are no interstices or lacunae to fill” ignores the common law tradition. *EEOC v. Vermont*, 717 F. Supp. 261, 265 (D. Vt. 1989). Even if that view were accurate for some cases, it does not describe the broad spectrum of judicial work. As the Eighth Circuit observed, “the reality of judging is that it is not always—and perhaps not even usually—merely a matter of mechanically applying settled, unambiguous principles of law.” *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 603.

**B. The Policymaking Exception Applies to the Judicial Branch.**

Petitioners argue that the exception for “appointee on the policymaking level” applies only to executive and legislative branch officials. But the text of § 630(f) contains no such limitation. If the exception were limited in that way, “none of the four categories of exemptions from the coverage of the ADEA would apply to employees in the judicial branch in those states where judges are appointed.” *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 604. Congress could not have intended to cover appointed judges while exempting elected judges, whose duties are identical. *Id.*

The legislative history, which states that the exemption covers “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments,” does not compel a contrary result. Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers on H.R. 1746, 1972 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2179, 2180. The state judiciary is an “agency” of state government in the broad sense intended by Congress. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 604. Excluding judges would create an irrational distinction between elected judges (exempt) and appointed judges (covered), despite their identical functions. This Court should avoid such an absurd result. *See United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971) (requiring clear statement before federal law intrudes on traditional state functions).

**II. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT FORBID MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION.**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause does not bar Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision because it is rationally related to legitimate state interests.

**A. Age Classifications Are Subject to Rational Basis Review.**

The mandatory retirement provision does not target a suspect class or burden a fundamental right. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that age is not a suspect classification). Nor does mandatory retirement infringe a fundamental right—there is no constitutional right to continued public employment. *Id.* at 313; *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 608 n.6. Therefore, rational basis review applies. *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 314.

**B. Missouri Has a Rational Basis for Mandatory Retirement at Age 70.**

A classification survives rational basis review if there is “any reasonably conceivable state of facts that could provide a rational basis for the classification.” *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993). The burden is on the challenger to negate every conceivable basis for the law. *Id.* at 315.

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision is supported by multiple rational justifications. First, it “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged” judges whose faculties may have declined. *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980). Second, it “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges.” *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir. 1979). Third, it “assure[s] that important decisions will be made by judges who command respect because they share experiences and understandings similar to those of the majority of adult citizens.” *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988). These justifications are plausible, and this Court does not “require that the State convincingly demonstrate the empirical accuracy of the legislative judgment.” *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 606-07.

**III. THE CLEAR STATEMENT RULE REQUIRES THIS COURT TO RESOLVE AMBIGUITY IN FAVOR OF THE STATES.**

Because the regulation of state judicial qualifications is “a matter of considerable importance to a state, and one that traditionally has been left to each state to regulate,” this Court must apply the clear statement rule. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 601. Under that rule, courts “will not interpret the ADEA as conflicting with the Missouri Constitution unless we find that Congress clearly has expressed its intention to bring state judges into the ambit of the federal statute.” *Id.* (citing *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 54). Congress did not do so. The ADEA’s definition of “employee” is ambiguous as applied to appointed state judges, and the exception for “appointee[s] on the policymaking level” resolves that ambiguity in favor of the States. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 602-04.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Eighth Circuit should be affirmed.
