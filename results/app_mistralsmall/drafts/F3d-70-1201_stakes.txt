**ARGUMENT**

I. **RULE 407 DOES NOT PERMIT THE EXCLUSION OF SUBSEQUENT REMEDIAL MEASURES WHEN ADMITTED FOR IMPEACHMENT PURPOSES.**

The district court erred in excluding evidence of Morbark’s post-accident design changes under Rule 407 of the Federal Rules of Evidence. Rule 407 bars the admission of subsequent remedial measures to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect in a product, or a need for a warning or instruction. Fed. R. Evid. 407. However, this exclusionary rule does not apply when such evidence is offered for purposes other than those prohibited by the rule. *See* Fed. R. Evid. 407 advisory committee’s note (“The rule does not, however, require exclusion when the evidence is offered for another purpose, such as proving ownership, control, or feasibility of precautionary measures, if controverted, or impeachment.”); *Tuer v. McDonald,* 44 Md. App. 511, 523 (1980) (“The rule does not bar the admission of evidence of subsequent remedial measures when offered for impeachment purposes.”).

Here, Morbark’s counsel repeatedly implied to the jury that the seventeen-inch infeed chute was the safest design and that no changes had been made to the wood chipper since the accident. See R4-141-22. By doing so, Morbark opened the door to rebuttal evidence demonstrating the falsity of that implication. The district court acknowledged this when it stated that Morbark had “opened the door” to the rebuttal testimony regarding the subsequent remedial measures. R4-56. The impeachment purpose of the evidence was clear: to correct Morbark’s misleading implication that the seventeen-inch chute remained the industry standard. *See United States v. Wise,* 979 F.2d 1544, 1550 (11th Cir. 1992) (evidence is admissible for impeachment when it contradicts a witness’s prior statement or casts doubt on the witness’s credibility).

The district court’s reliance on Rule 407 to exclude this impeachment evidence was misplaced. As the Advisory Committee’s Note to Rule 407 makes clear, the rule does not bar evidence of subsequent remedial measures when offered for impeachment. *See also* 2 Jack B. Weinstein & Margaret A. Berger, *Weinstein’s Federal Evidence* § 407.05[2] (2d ed. 2023) (“Rule 407 does not bar evidence of subsequent remedial measures when offered to impeach a witness who has testified that no such measures were taken.”). The district court’s erroneous exclusion of this evidence prejudiced Wood by preventing her from rebutting Morbark’s misleading implication, thereby denying her a fair trial. *See United States v. Meros,* 864 F.2d 1300, 1305 (11th Cir. 1989) (prejudicial error occurs when evidence is excluded in a manner that deprives a party of a fair trial).

II. **THE DISTRICT COURT’S ERROR IN EXCLUDING THE EVIDENCE REQUIRES A NEW TRIAL.**

The district court’s exclusion of the impeachment evidence was not harmless. Under the harmless error standard, the error must be more than harmless; it must be harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. *Chapman v. California,* 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967). Here, the excluded evidence was central to rebutting Morbark’s implication that the seventeen-inch chute was the safest design. Without this evidence, the jury was left with the false impression that the original design was still the industry standard, which likely influenced their assessment of the product’s defectiveness and unreasonableness. *See Tuer,* 44 Md. App. at 524 (excluding impeachment evidence of subsequent remedial measures was reversible error where it prevented the jury from assessing the credibility of testimony).

Moreover, the district court’s admonition to the jury to disregard the impeachment evidence further compounded the error. Such an instruction is unlikely to cure the prejudice caused by the exclusion, particularly where the excluded evidence was the only means of rebutting Morbark’s misleading implication. *See United States v. Olano,* 507 U.S. 725, 739 (1993) (prejudice arises when an error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings”).

Because the exclusion of the impeachment evidence was erroneous and prejudicial, the district court abused its discretion in denying Wood’s motion for a new trial. *See United States v. Lipscomb,* 702 F.2d 1049, 1053 (11th Cir. 1983) (abuse of discretion standard applies to denial of a motion for a new trial).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Wood respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand the case for a new trial.
