**Argument**

**I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Applies to Noncapital Sentencing Proceedings That Possess the "Hallmarks of a Trial."**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings that possess the "hallmarks of a trial." In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Supreme Court held that a defendant has a right to a jury determination of facts that increase the maximum penalty, and that a judge’s decision to impose a greater sentence violates the Double Jeopardy Clause if the jury would have imposed a lesser one. The Court later extended this principle to noncapital cases in *Rumsey v. Arizona*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984). There, the Court held that a sentencing proceeding is a "trial" for Double Jeopardy purposes if it involves a "trial of guilt or innocence" or has the "hallmarks of a trial." *Id.* at 209-210. The California Supreme Court’s majority opinion failed to apply this objective test, instead relying on an impermissible distinction between capital and noncapital sentencing.

**II. The Majority’s Distinction Between Capital and Noncapital Cases Is Unpersuasive.**

The majority opinion attempts to distinguish *Bullington* and *Rumsey* by claiming they are limited to capital cases. This argument is without merit. Nothing in *Bullington* suggests its analysis is limited to capital cases. The Court in *Bullington* focused on the fact that the Missouri statute provided for a jury trial, not on the nature of the penalty. *Id.* at 439. The Supreme Court has never held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies only in capital sentencing. The majority’s reliance on *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), and *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28 (1985), is equally unpersuasive. In *Caspari*, the Court noted that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases, but it did not hold that the rule is limited to them. *Caspari*, 510 U.S. at 392. Similarly, *Goldhammer* did not limit the rule. Because the majority has not identified a principled reason to exclude noncapital sentencing from the Double Jeopardy Clause’s protection, it must apply the *Bullington* analysis.

**III. The Majority’s Reliance on the Distinction Between Statutory and Constitutional Rights Is Flawed.**

The majority also argues that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply because California’s procedural protections, such as the right to a jury trial, are statutory rather than constitutional. This distinction is wholly unsupported. *Bullington* itself involved a situation where the defendant was entitled to a jury trial under state law, but the judge sentenced him to death. The Supreme Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applied because the state had provided a jury trial. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439. The majority’s attempt to distinguish *Bullington* on these grounds is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s holding.

**IV. The California Proceeding Here Possesses the Hallmarks of a Trial.**

Under *Rumsey*, a sentencing proceeding is a "trial" for Double Jeopardy purposes if it involves a "trial of guilt or innocence" or has the "hallmarks of a trial." *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209-210. The California Supreme Court’s majority opinion failed to apply this test. The proceeding in this case involved a jury trial on the prior conviction allegation, a determination of guilt or innocence regarding that allegation, and the application of the state’s "Three Strikes" law. These are precisely the "hallmarks of a trial" that trigger Double Jeopardy protection. Therefore, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars the State from retrying the prior conviction allegation.
