**Argument**

The court of appeals correctly held that Peguero is not entitled to collateral relief. A district court’s failure to give the Rule 32 appeal advisement is not, by itself, a ground for habeas relief when the defendant already knew of the right to appeal and chose not to pursue it.

Rule 32(a)(2) required the sentencing court to advise a defendant of any right to appeal. But a violation of that rule does not automatically warrant vacatur on collateral review. Habeas relief lies only for an error that resulted in prejudice or a miscarriage of justice. See *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428-429 (1962) (collateral relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 is reserved for “a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice”); *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 783-785 (1979) (technical violations of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure do not justify collateral relief absent “a fundamental defect”); *Reed v. Farley*, 512 U.S. 339, 348-349 (1994) (plurality opinion) (not every violation of a federal rule is cognizable on habeas).

That principle is dispositive here. The district court found as a factual matter that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. The court of appeals therefore correctly concluded that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) had been served. Where a defendant is aware of the right to appeal, the omission of the formal advisement causes no prejudice and furnishes no basis for collateral relief. Cf. *Rodriquez v. United States*, 395 U.S. 327, 330 (1969) (recognizing that denial of appellate rights can require relief when a defendant is deprived of the opportunity to appeal); *Peguero v. United States*, 526 U.S. 23, 27-30 (1999) (holding that no habeas relief is warranted when the defendant knew of his appeal right and therefore suffered no prejudice from the court’s omission).

Peguero’s contrary rule—that any Rule 32 violation automatically requires vacatur—cannot be squared with this Court’s collateral-review cases. In *Timmreck*, the Court rejected the proposition that a procedural error in a plea colloquy required relief without a showing of prejudice, explaining that § 2255 is not a substitute for direct appeal. 441 U.S. at 783-785. The same reasoning applies with equal force here. Peguero knew he could appeal; he simply elected not to do so. That choice breaks any causal link between the Rule 32 omission and the loss of appellate review. See *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428; *Timmreck*, 441 U.S. at 784.

Nor does harmless-error review help petitioner. Even assuming a Rule 32 violation is analyzed for harmlessness, the error was harmless because Peguero had actual knowledge of his appeal rights. An error that does not affect the defendant’s decision whether to appeal is harmless. See *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946) (error warrants relief only if it had a “substantial and injurious effect or influence”); *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 637-638 (1993) (habeas relief requires actual prejudice). The court of appeals properly applied that principle in holding that the rule’s purpose had been satisfied.

Peguero’s position would also create an unwarranted windfall. Collateral relief exists to remedy actual constitutional or jurisdictional injury, not to enforce formal compliance for its own sake. *Timmreck*, 441 U.S. at 784-785; *Reed*, 512 U.S. at 349. Because Peguero was aware of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it, he suffered no injury cognizable on habeas review.

The judgment should be affirmed.
