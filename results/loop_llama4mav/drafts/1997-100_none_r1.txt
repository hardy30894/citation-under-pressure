The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, as established in Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 794 (1969), precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal, as stated in North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969), citing Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. 163 (1873), and United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662 (1896).

The Court's precedents suggest that the Clause's protections extend beyond traditional trials to proceedings that are "in substance and effect, a trial." In re Nielsen, 131 U.S. 176, 188 (1889). California's statutory scheme for determining prior conviction allegations under Penal Code section 1025 provides a trial-like process, with procedural guarantees such as the right to a jury trial and the requirement that the prosecution prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt, akin to the trial on guilt or innocence. Although Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), established that due process does not require a trial-like procedure for sentencing, it does not preclude the application of double jeopardy protections when a state chooses to provide such a process.

The application of double jeopardy protections in this context is consistent with the Court's recognition that sentencing proceedings can have trial-like characteristics. In Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616 (1912), the Court discussed the importance of protecting a defendant's rights during sentencing proceedings involving prior convictions. Similarly, in Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184 (1957), the Court emphasized the importance of protecting a defendant's rights during sentencing proceedings.

The respondent's argument that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court's precedents, including Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), do not preclude the application of double jeopardy protections in sentencing proceedings where the initial sentence is based on a factual determination, such as the existence of a prior conviction.

In conclusion, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court's precedents, including Benton v. Maryland, North Carolina v. Pearce, Ex parte Lange, United States v. Ball, Green v. United States, Graham v. West Virginia, In re Nielsen, Williams v. New York, and Stroud v. United States, support the application of double jeopardy protections in this context. Therefore, the petitioner is entitled to relief under the Double Jeopardy Clause.
