**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand After Pretrial Proceedings; a Transferee Court Cannot Use §1404(a) to Retain a Case for Trial.**

The plain text of 28 U.S.C. §1407(a) commands that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” The word “shall” imposes a duty, not a discretionary option. Congress vested the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation—not individual district judges—with the exclusive authority to remand. A transferee court that invokes §1404(a) to assign a case to itself for trial usurps that authority and violates the statutory command.

This Court has long recognized that §1404(a) is a general venue statute designed to permit transfer “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice.” *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 616 (1964). But §1407(a) is a specific, later-enacted provision that creates a comprehensive scheme for multidistrict litigation. Under settled canons, the specific governs the general. *See, e.g.,* *Parsons v. Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co.*, 375 U.S. 71, 72 (1963) (per curiam) (applying specific statute over general venue principles). Section 1407(a) limits the Panel’s transfer authority to “pretrial proceedings” and requires remand before trial. A transferee court cannot circumvent that limitation by invoking §1404(a) to retain a case for trial.

**II. The Practice of Self-Transfer Conflicts with the Statutory Scheme and This Court’s Precedents.**

The Ninth Circuit’s dissent correctly observed that the federal courts have “simply disregarded the statutory mandate” by allowing self-transfer. *Lexecon Inc. v. United States District Court*, 61 F.3d 911, 915 (9th Cir. 1995) (Kozinski, J., dissenting). That practice began with *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 124-25 (2d Cir. 1971), which candidly admitted that its result was not supported by the statutory language. The Judicial Panel’s Rule 14(b) and the *Manual for Complex Litigation* cannot override the statute. This Court has never endorsed such a practice, and it should not do so now.

Section 1404(a) authorizes transfer only to a district “where [the action] might have been brought.” *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343-44 (1960). But a case transferred under §1407(a) is not “brought” in the transferee district for purposes of trial; it is there only for pretrial proceedings. The transferee court cannot treat itself as a district where the action “might have been brought” because the action was not originally filed there and the Panel’s remand obligation prevents permanent retention. *See* *Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 21-22 (1960) (transfer under §1404(a) requires proper venue in transferee district). Allowing self-transfer would effectively permit a transferee court to rewrite the venue rules that Congress carefully preserved in §1407.

**III. The Legislative Purpose of §1407 Requires Remand to Protect Plaintiffs’ Forum Choice.**

Congress enacted §1407 to coordinate pretrial proceedings while preserving plaintiffs’ right to choose the trial forum. *See* *Ferens v. John Deere Co.*, 494 U.S. 516, 522-23 (1990) (recognizing that §1404(a) does not override state choice-of-law rules that protect plaintiffs’ forum choice). The mandatory remand provision reflects a deliberate policy: plaintiffs should not be forced to trial in a distant district simply because they were required to participate in consolidated pretrial proceedings. The practice of self-transfer undermines that policy by making the transferee court a permanent venue, often hundreds of miles from the plaintiff’s chosen forum. Here, Lexecon was “dragged hundreds of miles from [its] home district” and forced to trial in Arizona. *Lexecon*, 61 F.3d at 915 (Kozinski, J., dissenting). That result is precisely what §1407 was designed to prevent.

**IV. The Court Should Reject the Contrary Interpretation and Enforce the Plain Text.**

The lower courts’ contrary interpretation has no basis in the statute. This Court has consistently held that federal courts must follow the plain meaning of jurisdictional statutes. *See* *Kilpatrick v. Texas & Pacific Ry. Co.*, 337 U.S. 75, 77 (1949) (rejecting transfer that would circumvent statutory venue requirements). The fact that the practice has persisted for decades does not make it lawful. “Seldom have so many been so consistently wrong.” *Lexecon*, 61 F.3d at 915 (Kozinski, J., dissenting). This Court should now correct that error.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to order the district court to remand the action to the Northern District of Illinois.
