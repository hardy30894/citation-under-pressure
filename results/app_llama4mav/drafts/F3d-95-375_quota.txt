Argument

I. The District Court Abused its Discretion in Striking the Testimony of Four Expert Witnesses

The district court's decision to strike the testimony of four expert witnesses was an abuse of discretion. The court's order was based on the plaintiffs' failure to comply with the court's discovery orders, specifically the deadline for completing depositions of expert witnesses. However, the court's order was overly harsh and not proportionate to the plaintiffs' noncompliance. See, e.g., United States v. $49,576.00 U.S. Currency, 116 F.3d 425, 427 (9th Cir. 1997) (holding that a district court's dismissal of a claim for failure to comply with discovery orders was an abuse of discretion where the noncompliance was not willful or prejudicial).

The court's warning at the June 3, 1993 hearing did not provide sufficient notice to the plaintiffs that their experts would be struck if they failed to comply with the July 30, 1993 deadline. The court's statement that "those experts will be struck and no additional experts will be permitted on this case" was not a clear warning that the plaintiffs' entire case would be jeopardized by their noncompliance. See, e.g., Outley v. City of New York, 837 F.2d 587, 591 (2d Cir. 1988) (holding that a district court's warning must be clear and specific to justify a harsh sanction).

Furthermore, the district court's decision to strike the testimony of the four expert witnesses was not justified by the circumstances. The plaintiffs had identified their experts and provided some information about their expected testimony. See, e.g., Klonoski v. Mahlab, 156 F.3d 255, 269 (1st Cir. 1998) (holding that a district court's exclusion of expert testimony was an abuse of discretion where the plaintiff had provided some information about the expert's expected testimony).

II. The District Court Erred in Finding the Testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson Inadmissible under Daubert

The district court's decision to exclude the testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), was erroneous. The court's application of Daubert was overly restrictive and failed to consider the relevance and reliability of the experts' testimony. See, e.g., Ambrosini v. Labarraque, 101 F.3d 129, 134 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (holding that a district court's exclusion of expert testimony under Daubert was an abuse of discretion where the court failed to consider the relevance and reliability of the testimony).

The Supreme Court in Daubert emphasized that the trial judge's role is to ensure that the expert's testimony is both relevant and reliable. 509 U.S. at 589. The district court failed to properly apply this standard, instead focusing solely on the experts' methodology. See, e.g., Ruiz-Troche v. Pepsi Cola of Puerto Rico Bottling Co., 161 F.3d 77, 85 (1st Cir. 1998) (holding that a district court's exclusion of expert testimony under Daubert was an abuse of discretion where the court failed to consider the expert's qualifications and experience).

III. The District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment was Erroneous

The district court's grant of summary judgment was based on its erroneous decisions to strike the testimony of four expert witnesses and to exclude the testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson. Without the testimony of these experts, the plaintiffs were unable to establish a genuine issue of material fact. See, e.g., Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986) (holding that summary judgment is appropriate where there is no genuine issue of material fact).

However, if the district court had not erred in its evidentiary rulings, the plaintiffs would have been able to establish a genuine issue of material fact, and summary judgment would not have been appropriate. See, e.g., Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986) (holding that summary judgment is not appropriate where there is a genuine issue of material fact).

IV. The Appellants' Notice of Appeal was Timely

The appellants' notice of appeal was filed on October 19, 1994, after the district court granted summary judgment against the Jackson and Lemond Plaintiffs. Although the district court did not enter a final order until March 1, 1995, the appellants' notice of appeal was timely under F.R.A.P. 4(a)(2), which provides that a notice of appeal filed after the announcement of a decision or order but before the entry of the judgment or order is treated as filed on the date of and after the entry. See, e.g., FirsTier Mortgage Co. v. Investors Mortgage Ins. Co., 498 U.S. 269, 273 (1991) (holding that a notice of appeal filed after the announcement of a decision but before the entry of the judgment is timely).

In conclusion, the district court's decisions to strike the testimony of four expert witnesses, to exclude the testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson, and to grant summary judgment were erroneous. The appellants' notice of appeal was timely, and this court has jurisdiction to hear the appeal. See, e.g., Budinich v. Becton Dickinson & Co., 486 U.S. 196, 202 (1988) (holding that a notice of appeal is timely if filed within the prescribed time period).
