### Argument

The judgment below should be affirmed because the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, by its text and structure, targets those who collect debts for another, not entities that collect debts they own. Statutory interpretation begins with the words Congress chose. *United States v. Sischo*, 262 U.S. 165, 168 (1923). And “where the words of a statute are unambiguous, the judicial inquiry is complete.” *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

The Act’s operative language regulates a person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due another.” That formulation reflects a familiar distinction between an agent who collects for a creditor and the creditor who seeks payment for itself. This Court has long recognized that a creditor collecting its own account is not acting as a collection agent. *Harris v. Balk*, 198 U.S. 215, 222-23 (1905). Congress used the same basic distinction here, and courts are not at liberty to erase it by rewriting “owed or due another” to mean “formerly owed or due another.” *Gooch v. United States*, 297 U.S. 124, 128 (1936).

Petitioners’ theory collapses that distinction by making default, rather than ownership, the controlling factor. But this Court has repeatedly rejected constructions that treat a term as varying with historical antecedents rather than present legal relations. In *Tyler v. Court of Registration*, 175 U.S. 380, 385 (1899), the Court explained that statutory words must be read according to “the sense in which they are used.” Here, the present-tense phrase “owed or due another” naturally refers to debt owed to someone other than the collector at the time of collection. A debt purchased outright is no longer “owed or due another.” A contrary reading would require ignoring the ordinary meaning of ownership and debt.

That reading is also confirmed by the Act’s purpose. Congress enacted the statute to curb abusive collection practices by those who pursue claims on behalf of others. The Supreme Court has long distinguished between a creditor’s own enforcement of its rights and the conduct of an outside collector. See *Murray’s Lessee v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co.*, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 272, 279-80 (1856) (recognizing the legal consequences that flow from ownership of the claim sought to be enforced). When Santander purchased the receivables, it stepped into the shoes of the owner of the debt. From that point forward, it was collecting for itself, not “another.”

Petitioners’ argument depends heavily on the fact that the loans were already in default when purchased. But default does not transform an owner into a third-party collector. This Court has never adopted a rule that the timing of acquisition alone controls whether an owner of a debt becomes a collection agency by virtue of ownership. To the contrary, where the legal right asserted is the purchaser’s own right, the purchaser is not acting for another. *Brown v. United States*, 12 U.S. (8 Cranch) 110, 122-23 (1814).

Nor does the Act’s use of the word “collector” alter the analysis. A collector is one who gathers for another’s benefit. *Peirce v. Van Dusen*, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 486, 493 (1871). Santander’s collection efforts after purchase were for its own account; any recovery belonged to Santander, not CitiFinancial. That is the hallmark of a creditor, not a debt collector. See *Smythe v. United States*, 188 U.S. 156, 172-73 (1903) (distinguishing between the party owning the claim and the party acting on another’s behalf).

Petitioners’ reading would also produce arbitrary results. Two entities engaging in identical post-purchase collection conduct would be treated differently depending only on whether the receivable was bought before or after default. Courts avoid interpretations that lead to irrational distinctions absent a clear command from Congress. *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459-60 (1892). No such command appears here.

In short, the Act regulates debt collection by outsiders, not a company collecting debts it owns after lawful purchase. Because Santander sought to collect its own account, it was not a “debt collector” within the meaning of the Act, and the dismissal was correct. *In re Rahrer*, 140 U.S. 545, 562 (1891).
