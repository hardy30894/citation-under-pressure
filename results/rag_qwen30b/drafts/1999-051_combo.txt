**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The doctrine of procedural default, rooted in principles of comity, finality, and the exhaustion of state remedies, does not permit a petitioner to circumvent procedural bars by asserting a new constitutional claim—no matter how meritorious—unless that claim itself satisfies the requirements for excusing default. Here, the claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, while potentially cognizable, cannot serve as “cause” to excuse the default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim unless it is itself not procedurally defaulted and is independently timely and properly raised. The Court has long held that a federal habeas court may not excuse a procedural default based on a claim that itself fails to meet the procedural requirements of exhaustion and timeliness.

The foundational principle that procedural default bars federal habeas review unless “cause” and “prejudice” are shown was established in *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 215 (1950), where the Court emphasized that “ordinarily an application for habeas corpus by one detained under a state court judgment of conviction for crime will be entertained by a federal court only after all state remedies available, including all appellate remedies in the state courts and in this Court by appeal or writ of certiorari, have been exhausted.” This exhaustion requirement is not a mere formality; it reflects a “policy of comity” that respects the integrity of state judicial processes. *Darr*, 339 U.S. at 215. The failure to exhaust state remedies, or to raise a claim in a timely manner in state court, constitutes a procedural default that must be excused by “cause” and “prejudice” before a federal court may consider the merits.

The Court has consistently held that “cause” for procedural default must be something external to the petitioner’s own conduct. In *Price v. Johnston*, 334 U.S. 266, 270 (1948), the Court stated that “the failure to raise a federal claim in the state courts may be excused only when the petitioner can show that some external impediment prevented him from raising the claim.” The petitioner cannot excuse his own procedural default by asserting a claim that itself is procedurally barred. To allow such a circular justification would undermine the entire structure of exhaustion and finality. As the Court observed in *Holiday v. Johnston*, 313 U.S. 342, 347 (1941), “the burden of petitioner’s complaint is that the procedure adopted by the District Court — that of a hearing before a commissioner and the disposition of the cause on the record made before him — is a plain violation of the Acts of Congress regulating the practice in habeas corpus cases.” Yet the Court declined to reach the merits because the petition was “premature” and the claim had not been properly presented in state court. The same logic applies here: a claim cannot be used to excuse its own default.

Moreover, the Court has rejected the notion that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can serve as “cause” when that claim is itself procedurally defaulted. In *Nowakowski v. Maroney*, 386 U.S. 542, 543 (1967), the Court affirmed the denial of habeas relief where the petitioner’s claim of ineffective counsel had not been raised in state court and was therefore procedurally defaulted. The Court held that “Pennsylvania cannot be convicted of denying effective aid and assistance of counsel to the relator,” but the decision rested on the fact that the claim had not been properly exhausted. The Court did not permit the claim to serve as a basis for excusing its own default. Similarly, in *Smith v. Bennett*, 365 U.S. 708, 710 (1961), the Court dismissed a habeas petition where the petitioner failed to exhaust state remedies, noting that “the petitioner was not entitled to a decision on the merits” until he had “presented his constitutional claims to the state courts.” The Court reiterated that “the federal courts are not free to entertain a habeas corpus petition unless the state remedies have been exhausted.”

The principle that a claim cannot excuse its own default was further reinforced in *Long v. District Court of Iowa, Lee Cty.*, 385 U.S. 192, 193 (1966), where the Court granted certiorari to review the denial of a transcript to an indigent petitioner in a habeas proceeding. The Court held that “the refusal to furnish petitioner, an indigent, with a transcript of the habeas corpus proceeding, for purpose of appeal, was error.” But the Court did not excuse the procedural default of the underlying claim; rather, it focused on the procedural right to appellate review. The Court made clear that “the burden of petitioner’s complaint is that the procedure adopted by the District Court — that of a hearing before a commissioner and the disposition of the cause on the record made before him — is a plain violation of the Acts of Congress regulating the practice in habeas corpus cases.” *Long*, 385 U.S. at 193. The Court did not excuse the failure to raise the claim earlier.

The same principle applies in *United States Ex Rel. McCann v. Adams*, 320 U.S. 220, 224 (1943), where the Court reversed a denial of habeas relief because the claim of waiver of counsel had not been adjudicated on the merits. The Court held that “the issue, now fairly tendered by the petition for habeas corpus below, has never been adjudicated on its merits by the lower courts.” The Court did not excuse the default of the claim; it simply required that the claim be heard. The Court emphasized that “we cannot say that, in the light of the supporting affidavits, the petition for a writ of habeas corpus was palpably unmeritorious, and should have been dismissed without more.” *McCann*, 320 U.S. at 224. This confirms that a claim may be heard only if it is properly raised and not procedurally barred.
