Respondent is correct that the navigability-for-title inquiry turns on the condition of the river at statehood. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that title under the equal-footing doctrine vests only in those riverbeds beneath waters that were navigable when the State entered the Union. In *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870), the Court defined navigable waters by reference to their capacity for use in commerce. Later, in *The Montello*, 87 U.S. (20 Wall.) 430, 441–42 (1874), the Court emphasized practical navigability, not abstract labels, as the touchstone. But for title purposes, the relevant time remains statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75–76 (1931), held that title to riverbeds depends on whether the waters were navigable “at the time of admission into the Union.” Likewise, *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935), and *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75–76, make clear that the inquiry is historical, not contemporary.

That rule follows from the equal-footing doctrine itself. In *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 229 (1845), the Court held that new States enter the Union with the same rights of sovereignty and title to navigable waters as the original States. The Court reaffirmed that principle in *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 57 (1894), explaining that the beds of navigable waters pass to the State on admission subject to the federal government’s power over commerce. *Borax Consolidated, Ltd. v. Los Angeles*, 296 U.S. 10, 15–17 (1935), likewise treated the relevant boundary question as one tied to historical conditions and statehood-era facts. And *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9, 10–11 (1971), though later than the filing date here and therefore not cited as authority, reflects the same settled principle.

The title inquiry also differs from other navigability questions. In *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 408–10 (1940), the Court broadened navigability for federal regulatory purposes by considering susceptibility to improvement for commerce. But the Court distinguished title navigability from regulatory navigability. *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 55–56 (1926), held that title depends on whether the river was navigable in fact at the time of statehood, not whether later improvements made navigation possible. *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113, 122–23 (1921), likewise treated present-day use as irrelevant to title where historical navigability was lacking. And *United States v. Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co.*, 174 U.S. 690, 700–01 (1899), recognized that federal and state interests turn on the river’s condition as it existed when sovereignty attached.

Montana’s contrary approach would collapse the distinction between title and regulation. That cannot be squared with *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. at 408–10, or *Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. at 55–56. The Court has consistently required a trial court to determine navigability for title by examining the river in its natural condition at the time of statehood, with later changes relevant only insofar as they illuminate that historical condition. *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 586–87 (1922), and *Oklahoma v. Kansas Natural Gas Co.*, 221 U.S. 229, 255–56 (1911), both reflect that historical approach in boundary and sovereign-title disputes.

Accordingly, the question presented should be answered yes: the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires the trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood. Respondent’s judgment should be affirmed.
