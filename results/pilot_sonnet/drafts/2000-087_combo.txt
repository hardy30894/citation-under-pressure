ARGUMENT

I. The Canon of Constitutional Avoidance Requires Reading § 1231(a)(6) to Contain an Implicit Limit on the Duration of Post-Removal Detention

This Court has long held that when a statute is susceptible of two constructions, one of which raises grave constitutional doubts, courts must adopt the construction that avoids those doubts. Ex parte Endo, 323 U.S. 283 (1944). In Endo, the Court refused to read a wartime detention statute to authorize continued confinement of a concededly loyal citizen, explaining that Congress should not be presumed to have intended so severe a step without express command, particularly where constitutional liberty interests were at stake. The same interpretive discipline governs here. Section 1231(a)(6) nowhere states that detention may continue indefinitely, forever, after the ninety-day removal period expires. Where Congress used similarly open-ended language authorizing "supervision" of aliens pending deportation, this Court refused to read it as conferring unlimited investigatory or custodial authority, holding instead that the provision must be construed narrowly "to avoid a serious constitutional doubt." United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194, 202 (1957). Witkovich confirms that Congress does not lightly delegate untrammeled power over an alien's physical liberty, and that courts must supply limiting constructions rather than assume the worst of an ambiguous grant.

II. Indefinite Civil Detention of a Resident Alien Present in the United States Raises Grave Constitutional Concerns Because Due Process Protections Extend to All Persons Within Our Borders

The liberty protected by the Due Process Clause is not the exclusive province of citizens. As this Court declared in Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886), the Constitution's protections are "universal in their application, to all persons within the territorial jurisdiction, without regard to any differences of race, of color, or of nationality." That principle applies with full force to physical detention. In Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), this Court struck down a statute authorizing imprisonment at hard labor of Chinese aliens without judicial trial, holding that even aliens may not "be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law." Wong Wing, 163 U.S. at 238. The deprivation at issue here—years of continued confinement—is no less a deprivation of liberty than the imprisonment condemned in Wong Wing.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that the stakes for a resident alien facing removal-related detention are enormous. In Ng Fung Ho v. White, 259 U.S. 276 (1922), the Court observed that erroneous deportation "may result also in loss of both property and life, or of all that makes life worth living," id. at 284, and required that determinations bearing on an alien's liberty be attended by due process safeguards. And in Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding, 344 U.S. 590 (1953), this Court made clear that a lawfully admitted resident alien's constitutional status is not the same as that of an alien seeking initial entry; a resident alien who has established ties within the country is entitled to the protections the Constitution affords "to all people within our borders." Id. at 596 n.5. Zadvydas, admitted as a resident alien in 1956 and present in this country for decades, falls squarely within the class of persons whose liberty interest this Court has consistently protected. See also Wong Yang Sung v. McGrath, 339 U.S. 33 (1950) (requiring that deportation proceedings comport with due process because of the liberty interests at stake).

III. Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei Does Not Control Because It Addressed an Excluded Alien Who Had Never Effected Entry

The Fifth Circuit's reliance on Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), was misplaced. Mezei involved an alien stopped at the border and denied entry altogether; he was, in the Court's words, treated "as if stopped at the border." Id. at 215. He had never been admitted and therefore stood outside the community of persons whose liberty this Court has protected under Yick Wo and Wong Wing. Zadvydas, by contrast, was lawfully admitted in 1956 and lived in this country as a resident alien for nearly forty years before removal proceedings began. The distinction drawn in Kwong Hai Chew—between an alien who has effected entry and one who has not—forecloses any reliance on Mezei to sanction open-ended detention of an admitted alien like Zadvydas.

IV. Detention Incident to Removal Must Bear a Reasonable Relation to the Purpose of Removal and Cannot Be Permanent

Even where detention pending deportation has been sustained, this Court has insisted that such detention be tethered to a legitimate, achievable governmental purpose. In Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), the Court upheld detention of aliens pending deportation proceedings only because detention served the purpose of ensuring availability for a deportation that remained practically attainable, and the Court took care to note that such custody "is not for punishment." Id. at 537. Where, as here, removal is not reasonably foreseeable because no receiving country will accept the alien, detention no longer serves the purpose that justified it in Carlson, and the constitutional concerns identified in Wong Wing, Ng Fung Ho, and Kwong Hai Chew are fully realized. The statute should therefore be construed, consistent with Endo and Witkovich, to permit detention only for the period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal—not indefinitely.
