# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Section 1333(b) Imposes a Causation Requirement Keyed to Where the *Operations* Occur, Not Where the *Injury* Occurs

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage to "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The geographic modifier "conducted on the outer Continental Shelf" attaches grammatically to "operations," not to "injury." Congress thus asked where the extractive *activity* took place, not where the worker happened to be standing when he was hurt. This Court has long recognized that "there is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Had Congress wished to impose a situs-of-injury requirement, it could easily have done so, as it did in the Longshore Act itself, which requires that a covered injury occur "upon the navigable waters of the United States." 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). Congress used no comparable phrase in § 1333(b). The absence of any textual situs-of-injury limitation is telling, particularly because Congress legislated against the backdrop of the Longshore Act, whose own situs limitation it chose not to replicate.

## II. This Court's Construction of Analogous Causal Language Confirms that "Resulting From" Requires Only a Causal Nexus, Not a Geographic Coincidence

This Court has repeatedly construed similarly worded causation provisions in worker-protective statutes broadly, refusing to graft additional locational or proximate-cause restrictions onto them. In the context of the Federal Employers' Liability Act, which makes a carrier liable for injury "resulting in whole or in part" from its negligence, this Court held that the statute imposes liability whenever employer conduct "played any part, even the slightest, in producing the injury." *Rogers v. Missouri Pac. R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 500, 506 (1957). The Court reached that result notwithstanding the absence of any requirement that the negligent act and the injury occur at the same place or moment. The same logic applies here: § 1333(b)'s "resulting from" language asks whether outer Continental Shelf operations caused the injury, a question of causation, not location.

This Court's treatment of the Longshore Act's own coverage provisions points the same way. In *Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc.*, 314 U.S. 244 (1941), the Court construed the Act's coverage provisions to reach the results Congress intended rather than the narrowest reading of isolated words, and in *Voris v. Eikel*, 346 U.S. 328 (1953), the Court reaffirmed that doubts concerning coverage under the Act are to be resolved in favor of the injured worker consistent with the Act's remedial purpose. Section 1333(b) borrows the Longshore Act's compensation machinery precisely because Congress wanted the same protective philosophy applied to shelf workers; it would be incongruous to read into the borrowing statute a restriction more parsimonious than the one Congress imposed in the donor statute itself.

## III. A Substantial-Nexus Test Best Serves the Remedial Purpose Congress Enacted

This Court has consistently instructed that worker-compensation statutes be read to avoid "harsh and incongruous results" that would leave injured workers without remedy for reasons unrelated to the risks Congress meant to cover. *Parker*, 314 U.S. at 249. A situs-of-injury rule would produce exactly that incongruity: an employee hurt one minute before boarding a crew boat for the platform would be covered, but one hurt one minute after stepping ashore, performing the identical shelf-related task, would not be. Nothing in the causal language Congress chose compels so arbitrary a line. A substantial-nexus test, asking whether the injury arose out of and in the course of extractive operations conducted on the shelf, gives effect to the statutory text and to Congress's evident purpose of ensuring that workers whose employment is bound up with shelf extraction are not deprived of compensation by the fortuity of an onshore worksite.

## IV. Any Suggestion of a Contrary Situs Requirement in Passing Statements Addressing a Different Statutory Provision Is Unconsidered Dictum Entitled to No Weight

To the extent isolated language elsewhere might be read to impose a situs-of-injury gloss on § 1333(b), that language was neither necessary to, nor considered in connection with, any question actually presented for decision. This Court has long cautioned that "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used," because "their possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." *Humphrey's Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935) (quoting *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399–400 (1821)). Where, as here, the meaning of § 1333(b) was not briefed, argued, or essential to a prior decision resolving an entirely different statutory question, such passing dictum cannot substitute for the textual and purposive analysis that properly governs this case.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that § 1333(b) does not impose a situs-of-injury requirement, and should affirm the substantial-nexus standard applied below.
