# ARGUMENT

## I. THE EQUAL FOOTING DOCTRINE REQUIRES A TITLE-BY-TITLE NAVIGABILITY DETERMINATION AS OF THE DATE OF STATEHOOD, WHICH THE MONTANA SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED

The equal footing doctrine holds that upon admission to the Union, each new State acquires title to the beds of all waters navigable in fact, "in trust for the people." *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230 (1845). This Court has consistently reaffirmed that the touchstone of navigability for title purposes is a river's condition at the moment of statehood. In *United States v. Utah*, this Court explained that "the question of navigability" is "determinable as of the date of the state's admission to the Union." 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). The inquiry is not whether a river is navigable today, nor whether it was navigable at some other historical moment—it is whether the river, *at the time the State entered the Union*, was "used, or susceptible of being used, in [its] ordinary condition, as highways for commerce." *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871).

The Montana Supreme Court faithfully applied this settled framework. It recognized that the State acquired title to the beds and banks of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers "when it became a state in 1889 under the 'equal footing doctrine.'" Pet. App. ¶ 8. This is precisely the inquiry *Utah* commands: a segment-specific, statehood-date determination, not a generalized assessment of a river's navigability at some other time or under modern conditions.

Petitioner does not seriously dispute that statehood is the relevant temporal benchmark; rather, it seeks to escape that benchmark's rigor by urging shortcuts—reliance on post-statehood or segmented evidence divorced from the historical record. But this Court's precedents make clear that navigability-for-title determinations must rest on the river's *actual* condition at statehood, assessed through competent historical evidence, segment by segment where geography warrants. *See United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (navigability determined by "the capacity of the river in its natural state" at the relevant time). The Montana courts did precisely this, canvassing historical records—reports, survey data, and contemporaneous accounts—bearing on the condition of each disputed stretch of river as it existed in 1889.

## II. THE MONTANA SUPREME COURT PROPERLY RELIED ON HISTORICAL RECORD EVIDENCE TO ESTABLISH NAVIGABILITY AT STATEHOOD

Petitioner's evidentiary objections do not undermine the correctness of the navigability determination. Because no living witness can testify to river conditions in 1889, courts necessarily rely upon historical documentation—precisely the type of evidence the Montana Supreme Court admitted below. This approach is consistent with *Utah*, where this Court itself examined historical surveys, explorer accounts, and expert reconstruction of river conditions decades removed from the litigation to determine navigability at statehood. 283 U.S. at 75-82. Historical evidence is not merely permissible in this context—it is indispensable.

The lower court correctly recognized that documents such as the historical archives compiled by the State Archivist were properly authenticated and fell within recognized exceptions to the hearsay rule, including the public records and ancient documents exceptions. Pet. App. ¶¶ 92-94. That evidentiary methodology, grounded in reliable historical sources rather than speculation, is the only workable means of implementing the statehood-date rule this Court has long required. Petitioner's suggestion that such evidence should be excluded would render the equal-footing inquiry unworkable in virtually every case, since the passage of over a century since statehood makes contemporaneous testimony impossible by definition.

## III. THE FEDERAL POWER ACT DOES NOT PREEMPT THE STATE'S RIGHT TO COMPENSATION FOR USE OF STATE-OWNED RIVERBEDS

Even accepting, as it must, that the State holds title to the navigable riverbeds at issue, Petitioner errs in suggesting that the Federal Power Act (FPA) forecloses the State from seeking compensation for PPL's use of that land. This Court held in *Federal Power Commission v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.* that the exercise of federal authority over navigable waters requires "a clear and specific congressional authorization" before it may be understood to displace state-created property rights. 347 U.S. 239, 249 (1954). No such clear authorization exists here.

To the contrary, the FPA's structure—establishing what this Court in *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Cooperative v. Federal Power Commission* called a "dual system" of state and federal control, 328 U.S. 152, 167-68 (1946)—expressly preserves state authority over property rights in the "beds and banks" of waters within its borders. *Id.* at 161 n.6 (quoting 16 U.S.C. § 802). A FERC license does not extinguish or diminish state ownership of the riverbed; it merely authorizes construction and operation of a facility, subject to compliance with applicable state property law. The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded, consistent with these principles, that the HRA's compensation requirement operates alongside, not in conflict with, the federal licensing scheme.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
