**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONSTITUTION'S QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES ARE EXCLUSIVE**

The Constitution establishes the exclusive qualifications for Members of Congress. Article I, § 2, cl. 2 provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the State at the time of election. Article I, § 3, cl. 3 sets identical age, citizenship, and inhabitancy requirements for Senators. These provisions represent the complete and exclusive list of qualifications that may be imposed on candidates for federal office.

In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), this Court held that the Constitution’s enumerated qualifications are “the sole qualifications” for membership in Congress. *Id.* at 522. The Court reasoned that the Framers intended the qualifications to be fixed and uniform, “prevent[ing] the national Legislature from capriciously adding qualifications.” *Id.* at 539. That same logic applies equally to the States. If Congress cannot add qualifications, neither can the States. The Framers designed a uniform national legislature, not a patchwork of fifty different eligibility standards.

**II. AMENDMENT 73 IMPOSES ADDITIONAL QUALIFICATIONS FOR FEDERAL OFFICE**

Arkansas Amendment 73 provides that any person who has served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate “shall not be certified as a candidate and shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot” for federal election. This is not a mere regulation of the time, place, or manner of elections. It is a direct prohibition on candidacy based on prior service—a qualification that appears nowhere in the Constitution.

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that the Amendment’s ballot-access restriction is “a restriction on the qualifications of persons seeking federal congressional offices.” The distinction between an outright bar and a ballot-access restriction is a distinction without a difference. Both achieve the same unconstitutional result: preventing a candidate who meets the constitutional qualifications from being elected. As this Court has explained, “the right to be a candidate for federal office” is a fundamental right that cannot be burdened by a state-imposed qualification. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 788 (1983) (recognizing that state laws that “determine whether a candidate may appear on the ballot” directly affect the “interests of voters and candidates”).

The Amendment’s effect is to disqualify otherwise eligible candidates. The Constitution’s qualifications are “both necessary and sufficient” for membership. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 550 n.53. A state may not add to or subtract from them.

**III. THE STATES LACK POWER TO ALTER FEDERAL QUALIFICATIONS**

The Framers deliberately rejected proposals to allow States to impose additional qualifications for Congress. *See* 2 Records of the Federal Convention of 1787, at 249 (Max Farrand ed., 1911) (rejecting a proposal to empower States to “establish qualifications for election” of federal representatives). The Constitution’s structure confirms that the national government is “the supreme Law of the Land,” U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2, and that the States retain only those powers not taken away or restricted. Allowing States to set eligibility standards for federal office would undermine the “uniformity of qualifications” that the Framers deemed “paramount.” *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 540.

This Court has consistently held that the Elections Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1, does not grant States the power to alter qualifications. The Clause authorizes States to regulate the “Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections,” but that power is “limited to the regulation of the election process, not to the prescription of qualifications for candidates.” *Cook v. Gralike*, 531 U.S. 510, 523 (2001) (citing *Powell*). An amendment that bars a candidate from the ballot based on prior service regulates *who* may be a candidate, not *how* the election is conducted. It is a qualification, not a manner regulation.

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly concluded that “the specter of the hodge-podge of qualifications which a contrary holding might engender is daunting enough to swing the balance.” That court recognized that uniformity is essential. If Arkansas may impose term limits, then other States may impose age, residency, or property qualifications. The Constitution’s carefully crafted uniformity would be destroyed.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed. The States may not alter the qualifications for federal office that the Constitution exclusively enumerates.
