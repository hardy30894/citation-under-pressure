**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Appellants’ FRSA Retaliation Claims Because Appellants Presented Sufficient Evidence to Raise a Genuine Issue of Material Fact as to Whether BNSF’s Disciplinary Actions Were Motivated by Retaliatory Animus.**

The Federal Railroad Safety Act (FRSA) prohibits railroads from retaliating against employees for reporting safety concerns. *See* 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under the FRSA, an employee must show: (1) he engaged in protected activity; (2) the railroad knew of the protected activity; (3) the employee suffered an adverse employment action; and (4) there was a causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse action. *See, e.g., Tinsley v. Soo Line R.R. Co.,* 834 F.3d 837, 842 (8th Cir. 2016) (citing *Kasten v. Saint-Gobain Performance Plastics Corp.,* 563 U.S. 1, 10 (2011)). Once the employee establishes a prima facie case, the burden shifts to the employer to articulate a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason for the adverse action. *Id.* If the employer does so, the burden shifts back to the employee to show that the employer’s proffered reason is pretextual. *Id.*

Here, Appellants engaged in protected activity by reporting unsafe working conditions at the Creston bridge—specifically, the lack of handrails and lighting—and by participating in BNSF’s subsequent investigation. *See* 49 U.S.C. § 20109(c)(2) (protecting employees who "provide[] information relating to any Federal Railroad Safety Act violation"). BNSF was aware of this protected activity, as Appellants’ statements to Trainmaster Dixon and Claims Representative Murphy documented the hazards. Appellants suffered adverse employment actions when BNSF disciplined them—Foster was terminated, Kline received a "record suspension," and Snyder received a formal reprimand and a one-year review period. *See Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White,* 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006) (holding that an adverse action is one that "well might have dissuaded a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination").

Appellants also presented sufficient evidence to raise a genuine issue of material fact as to causation. Appellants testified that BNSF subjected them to increased operations testing and other scrutiny following their reports of unsafe conditions. *See, e.g., Tinsley,* 834 F.3d at 843 (finding evidence of "heightened scrutiny" sufficient to support a retaliation claim). Moreover, the temporal proximity between Appellants’ protected activity and BNSF’s disciplinary actions—particularly Foster’s termination just days after her statement to the Claims Representative—supports an inference of retaliation. *See id.* at 844 (noting that "temporal proximity alone may be sufficient to establish causation" where the timing is "unusually suggestive").

BNSF argues that its disciplinary actions were justified by rule infractions observed during the investigative hearing. However, Appellants presented evidence that the hearing was procedurally flawed and that the alleged infractions were disputed. *See, e.g., Macktal v. Bd. of Trustees of Univ. of Ark.,* 243 F.3d 828, 832 (8th Cir. 2001) (holding that an employee may show pretext by "undermining the credibility of the employer’s proffered reasons"). The Public Law Board’s decision to reinstate Foster and expunge the discipline against Kline and Snyder further undermines BNSF’s asserted justification. *See Tinsley,* 834 F.3d at 844 (finding that an employer’s failure to follow its own progressive discipline policy supported a finding of pretext).

**II. The District Court Erred in Concluding That BNSF’s Post-Investigation Scrutiny Did Not Constitute Adverse Actions.**

The FRSA’s anti-retaliation provision protects employees from actions that "dissuade a reasonable employee from engaging in protected activity." *White,* 548 U.S. at 68. The district court erred in holding that BNSF’s increased operations testing and other scrutiny did not constitute adverse actions. *See Foster v. BNSF Ry. Co.,* No. 14-cv-04063, 2016 WL 1056555, at *6 (D. Minn. Mar. 16, 2016). Appellants testified that they felt compelled to avoid inexperienced coworkers and even changed shifts to less desirable work out of fear of further scrutiny. Such actions are precisely the type of "chilling effect" that the FRSA was designed to prevent. *See id.* at 68 (noting that the FRSA’s "dissuade" standard is broader than the Title VII standard).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, Appellants respectfully request that this Court reverse the district court’s grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
