**Argument**

A state prisoner may not use a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim as “cause” to excuse the default of another habeas claim unless he first establishes that the ineffectiveness claim itself is properly preserved in state court. The governing doctrine is settled: federal habeas review does not reach a claim defaulted in state court unless the petitioner shows both cause for the default and actual prejudice. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72 (1977); *Francis v. Henderson*, 425 U.S. 536 (1976); *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963). And this Court has repeatedly recognized that federal courts must respect state procedural rules because “[n]o principle of procedural regularity is more firmly established than that a state court will not consider constitutional claims that have been waived.” *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443, 447 (1965); see also *Davis v. Wechsler*, 263 U.S. 22 (1923).

The Sixth Circuit’s rule—that any ineffective-assistance claim may serve as cause even if itself defaulted—cannot be squared with the principles of exhaustion, comity, and orderly review that underlie habeas doctrine. A federal constitutional claim not fairly presented to the state courts is ordinarily unavailable in federal habeas. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953); *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950). A petitioner may not bypass state corrective processes by recasting the defaulted issue as a separate constitutional theory. *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944); *Urquhart v. Brown*, 205 U.S. 179 (1907). That rule is especially important where the asserted “cause” depends on facts and law the state courts never had a fair opportunity to pass upon. *Ex parte Royall*, 117 U.S. 241 (1886).

Equally important, ineffective assistance is not a freestanding magic key that automatically opens the federal courthouse. It is itself a constitutional claim that must be established in the ordinary manner. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), recognized the right to counsel in capital cases; *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60 (1942), held that counsel must provide effective assistance free from conflicting loyalties; and *Avery v. Alabama*, 308 U.S. 444 (1940), explained that the constitutional inquiry turns on whether counsel had a fair opportunity to render assistance. But none of those cases suggests that a defaulted ineffectiveness claim may be used as cause without first satisfying state procedural requirements. To the contrary, because counsel-ineffectiveness allegations are often fact-bound and state-law dependent, state courts must be given the first chance to resolve them. *Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114; *Darr*, 339 U.S. 200.

This Court’s cause-and-prejudice cases reinforce that conclusion. “Cause” means some external impediment, not merely the petitioner’s own failure to preserve a claim. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), confirms that attorney error may sometimes constitute cause, but only when the attorney error itself is a constitutional violation properly presented and not itself procedurally barred. The Court has also stressed that a petitioner cannot obtain federal review by layering default on default. *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107 (1982); *Reed v. Ross*, 468 U.S. 1 (1984). Those decisions reflect the same basic rule long recognized in the Court’s earlier habeas precedents: waiver in state court matters, and federal courts do not sit to rescue litigants from their own failures to follow state procedure. *Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72; *Francis*, 425 U.S. 536.

Here, Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was itself dismissed by the Ohio courts as untimely. That procedural ruling bars federal reliance on the claim as cause unless Carpenter can show an adequate excuse for *that* default. Otherwise, the federal habeas court would excuse one forfeited claim with another forfeited claim, nullifying state procedural rules and converting the exception into the rule. That result would conflict with the settled understanding that habeas relief is an extraordinary remedy, not a mechanism for relitigating every issue a defendant neglected to present at the proper time. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443; *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114; *Francis v. Henderson*, 425 U.S. 536.

Finally, the state’s interest in finality is particularly strong where, as here, the underlying conviction followed a guilty plea and the petitioner later pursued collateral attack through successive procedural steps. Longstanding precedent requires federal courts to honor state judgments unless the petitioner has properly invoked and preserved federal review. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391; *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443; *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72. Carpenter did not. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
