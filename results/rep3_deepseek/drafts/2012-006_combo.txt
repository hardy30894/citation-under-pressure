**ARGUMENT**

The government bears the burden of proving every element of a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt. Davis v. *United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 487 (1895). This includes the requirement that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy within the applicable statute of limitations period. *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943). A presumption that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, without proof, violates the principle that the government must prove each element. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 525–26 (1958). The lower court erred by placing the burden on petitioners to disprove their membership during the limitations period.

When a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal from a conspiracy before the limitations period, the burden shifts to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member during the relevant period. *Morrison v. California*, 291 U.S. 82, 88–89 (1934) (the government must prove all elements even when a defense is raised). Withdrawal is an affirmative defense, but once some evidence is presented, the government must disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952) (the state may not shift the burden of proof on an element of the offense). The government cannot rely on the mere existence of the conspiracy to impute ongoing membership. *United States v. Trenton Potteries Co.*, 273 U.S. 392, 402 (1927) (conspiracy requires a meeting of minds and continuing participation).

The record here shows that the leaders of the alleged conspiracy, Moore and Gray, split up before the limitations period began. This evidence triggered the government’s burden to prove that petitioners were still members of a single, ongoing conspiracy during the limitations period. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) (withdrawal requires an affirmative act of abandonment). The government failed to carry that burden. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 608 (1910) (a conspiracy ends when its objectives are abandoned). The lower court’s refusal to require the government to prove membership during the limitations period deprived petitioners of their right to have each element proved beyond a reasonable doubt. *United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d 834, 845 (2d Cir. 1939) (the government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period). *United States v. Cohen*, 145 F.2d 82, 87 (2d Cir. 1944) (once withdrawal is shown, the burden is on the government to prove continued participation).

The government’s burden is not satisfied by showing that the conspiracy existed at some earlier time. *United States v. Bayer*, 331 U.S. 532, 542 (1947) (the government must prove each element as of the time of the offense). By failing to require proof of membership during the limitations period, the lower court effectively presumed petitioners’ guilt. *Morrison*, 291 U.S. at 89. That presumption cannot stand. *Tot*, 319 U.S. at 467.

The judgment should be reversed.
