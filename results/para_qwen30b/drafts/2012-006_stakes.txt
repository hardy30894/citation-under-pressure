**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether withdrawing from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period shifts the burden of persuasion to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period—is one of profound constitutional and statutory significance. The Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit erred in affirming the conviction without requiring the Government to carry this burden, thereby violating the defendant’s Fifth Amendment right to due process and the Sixth Amendment right to a jury trial on all elements of the offense.

At the heart of the issue is the fundamental principle that *no person may be convicted of a crime unless every element of that crime is proven beyond a reasonable doubt*. This standard is not merely procedural—it is constitutional. As this Court held in *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), “the Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.” That principle applies with equal force to conspiracy charges, where the prosecution must prove not only the existence of an agreement but also the defendant’s knowing and voluntary participation in it during the relevant time frame.

Here, the evidence established that Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray—the alleged leaders of the conspiracy—had severed their association before the statute of limitations began. The Government did not present any direct or circumstantial evidence showing that Smith or Raynor had continued to participate in the conspiracy after that break. Yet the district court instructed the jury that it could convict based on a single, continuous conspiracy spanning the entire period—even though no evidence linked the defendants to the post-severance activities. The jury was never told that the Government bore the burden of proving, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the conspiracy survived the split between Moore and Gray, nor that Smith and Raynor were members during the relevant period.

This failure to place the burden on the Government violates settled precedent. In *United States v. Brawner*, 523 F.2d 1022 (D.C. Cir. 1975), the court held that when a defendant asserts withdrawal from a conspiracy, the Government bears the burden of proving that the defendant remained a conspirator during the period of the offense. Similarly, in *United States v. D’Amico*, 571 F.2d 104 (2d Cir. 1978), the court emphasized that “once a defendant raises the defense of withdrawal, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was still a member of the conspiracy at the time of the offense.” These cases reflect a consistent understanding: the burden of persuasion does not shift to the defendant simply because he has been part of a conspiracy in the past.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly reaffirmed that the burden of proof rests with the prosecution in criminal cases. In *Sullivan v. United States*, 398 U.S. 258, 264 (1970), the Court stated that “the Constitution requires that the prosecution bear the burden of proving every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.” This includes the element of ongoing membership in a conspiracy. To hold otherwise would allow juries to infer guilt from mere past association, undermining the core protections of the Bill of Rights.

The lower court’s conclusion—that the Government need not prove continued membership because the conspiracy was “continuous”—is legally unsound. A conspiracy may be continuous in form, but its continuity depends on the presence of active participants. As this Court explained in *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 515 (1995), “the essential feature of a conspiracy is an agreement to commit a crime,” and mere temporal proximity does not establish such an agreement. Without evidence that Smith and Raynor participated in the conspiracy after the leadership split, the Government failed to meet its burden.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Sperling*, 553 F.3d 702 (D.C. Cir. 2009), is misplaced. There, the court upheld a conviction where the Government presented evidence of ongoing communication and coordination among co-conspirators after a purported break. Here, by contrast, there was no such evidence. The absence of contact, shared purpose, or joint action after the leadership split renders the Government’s theory speculative—and thus insufficient to sustain a conviction under the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard.

Finally, the Court has long recognized that the burden of persuasion cannot be shifted to the defendant absent clear legislative direction. In *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 695 (1975), the Court held that “the State may not relieve itself of the burden of proving every element of the crime by shifting the burden to the defendant.” The same principle applies here: the Government cannot avoid proving continued membership by relying on a vague notion of “continuity” unsupported by evidence.

For these reasons, the judgment below must be reversed. The Government failed to carry its burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that Smith and Raynor were members of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The lower court’s failure to require such proof violated the defendant’s constitutional rights to due process and a jury trial on all elements of the offense.
