**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth Amendment as interpreted in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). In *Ring*, the Supreme Court held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury—not a judge—to find any fact that increases a defendant’s maximum punishment. *Id.* at 609. The Court expressly distinguished Florida’s scheme, noting that in Florida, “the jury’s recommendation [of death] is not binding on the trial judge,” and the judge makes the ultimate sentencing determination. *Id.* at 607 (citing *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 640-41 (1990)). Thus, the Court implicitly recognized that Florida’s scheme does not run afoul of *Ring* because the jury’s role is advisory, not dispositive.

Florida’s scheme further ensures that the jury’s advisory verdict is informed by the statutorily required findings. The jury is instructed to consider whether sufficient aggravating circumstances exist and whether they outweigh mitigating circumstances. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2). The trial judge then independently weighs the aggravating and mitigating factors before imposing sentence. *Hitchcock v. Dugger*, 481 U.S. 393, 398 (1987) (noting that the trial judge “weigh[s] the aggravating and mitigating circumstances itself”). This structure ensures that the jury’s advisory role does not implicate the Sixth Amendment concerns raised in *Ring*.

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

Florida’s scheme also withstands Eighth Amendment scrutiny. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “death is different” and requires heightened reliability in sentencing. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 605-06 (quoting *Gardner v. Florida*, 430 U.S. 349, 357 (1977)). Florida’s scheme ensures reliability by requiring the trial judge to independently weigh aggravating and mitigating factors and to provide detailed written findings. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.141(3); *Hitchcock*, 481 U.S. at 398. This process guarantees that the death penalty is imposed only after a thorough and individualized consideration of the defendant’s culpability.

Moreover, Florida’s scheme does not permit the imposition of the death penalty based on arbitrary or capricious factors. The jury’s advisory verdict is guided by statutorily enumerated aggravating and mitigating circumstances, and the trial judge must articulate specific findings to support a death sentence. *See* Fla. Stat. §§ 921.141(5), (6). This structured process ensures that the death penalty is reserved for the most culpable offenders, consistent with the Eighth Amendment’s demands. *See Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 189 (1976) (plurality opinion) (holding that a capital sentencing scheme must “genuinely narrow the class of persons eligible for the death penalty”).

**III. FLORIDA’S SCHEME DOES NOT IMPERMISSIBLY RESTRICT THE JURY’S ROLE IN DETERMINING MENTAL RETARDATION.**

Hurst’s claim that Florida’s scheme violates the Sixth or Eighth Amendment by failing to require the jury to determine mental retardation is meritless. The Supreme Court has held that the Eighth Amendment forbids the execution of mentally retarded defendants, but it has left to the states the manner in which this restriction is enforced. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002). Florida has done so by requiring the trial judge to make the ultimate determination of mental retardation, but the jury is permitted to consider such evidence in its advisory verdict. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4). This process ensures that the defendant’s mental retardation is fully considered while preserving the trial judge’s role in sentencing.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that states have flexibility in structuring their capital sentencing schemes. *See Walton*, 497 U.S. at 640-41 (upholding Arizona’s scheme where the judge, not the jury, found aggravating factors). Florida’s scheme is similarly flexible, and it does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendment by assigning the ultimate determination of mental retardation to the trial judge.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
