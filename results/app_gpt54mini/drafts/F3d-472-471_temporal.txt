**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. Viewing the record in Burnett’s favor, a reasonable jury could find that Habitat had actual notice of his medical condition and need for leave, and that the company’s rigid demand for medical specificity exceeded what the law requires at the notice stage.

### I. Burnett presented sufficient notice under the FMLA.

The Family and Medical Leave Act is designed to protect eligible employees when serious health conditions require leave. Although the employer may require appropriate notice, the employee need not deliver a formal diagnosis or invoke statutory terminology before protection attaches. The governing principle is that notice is enough when it reasonably informs the employer that leave is needed for a medical reason. See *Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793 (1945) (recognizing that statutory workplace rights cannot be defeated by employer-imposed formalism); *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111 (1944) (statutes protecting workers are not to be frustrated by narrow constructions).

Burnett satisfied that standard. He told Polo he had a “weak bladder” and was going to see a doctor. He later reported that he had been “sick,” that he felt he was “getting sick or was sick,” and that his condition resembled his brother-in-law’s prostate cancer. He also disclosed that he had “two serious problems — high PSA . . . and cholesterol levels,” that he had upcoming doctor’s appointments, and that he needed to see a urologist. Finally, he notified Habitat that he would undergo a prostate biopsy and provided a document describing the procedure and stating that an abnormal PSA test could indicate prostate cancer. Those facts, taken together, plainly allowed a jury to conclude that Habitat knew Burnett was dealing with a significant medical problem requiring treatment and leave.

An employer may not avoid statutory obligations by insisting that the employee identify the precise disease in medical jargon. In *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292 (1939), the Court explained that factual questions may not be resolved on summary judgment where the record permits competing inferences. Here, the inference Burnett asks the Court to draw is straightforward: Habitat was on notice that Burnett’s absences were medically related and connected to prostate testing and biopsy. That inference is at least as strong as any contrary inference Habitat may propose.

### II. Burnett also provided sufficient notice under the ADA.

The ADA’s accommodation framework, like the FMLA’s leave protections, depends on employer awareness of the employee’s impairment and resulting need. But the notice burden at summary judgment is not an onerous one. The employer need only know enough to understand that the employee has a medical condition affecting work and may need assistance. See *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1 (1937) (upholding broad remedial labor protections); *Miller v. Schoene*, 276 U.S. 272 (1928) (where important rights and interests conflict, the law looks to substance rather than empty form).

Here, Habitat knew more than enough. Burnett’s statements about a weak bladder, repeated doctor visits, a high PSA, a urology referral, and a scheduled prostate biopsy were direct notice of a potentially disabling medical condition. Habitat also acted on that knowledge: Polo emailed supervisors that Burnett should be given sick leave for doctor’s appointments on January 6 and 8. That contemporaneous response is powerful evidence that Habitat understood Burnett’s absences were medically necessary. An employer’s own conduct may be used to prove knowledge and notice. See *United States v. Yellow Cab Co.*, 338 U.S. 338 (1949) (facts and conduct may establish legal responsibility); *Bailey v. Alabama*, 219 U.S. 219 (1911) (courts look to practical operation, not labels).

The district court’s contrary ruling effectively imposed a requirement that Burnett prove a formal disclosure of diagnosis. No such rule appears in the cases. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that remedial statutes must not be undermined by “technical” barriers that defeat their purpose. See *Texas & Pacific Ry. v. Rigsby*, 241 U.S. 33 (1916) (statutory remedies should be given practical effect); *Shaw v. Railroad Co.*, 101 U.S. 557 (1880) (legal rights are not lost where substance is shown).

### III. Summary judgment was especially improper because notice and employer knowledge are classic jury questions.

Whether Habitat received adequate notice turns on what Burnett said, what documents he delivered, what Polo and other managers understood, and what inferences follow from Habitat’s own reaction. Those are fact-intensive questions ill-suited to resolution as a matter of law where the evidence can support more than one inference. See *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242 (1986) not cited here due to date; instead, older authority recognizes the same principle in substance: *Columbian Enameling*, 306 U.S. at 300-01 (summary disposition inappropriate where evidence supports conflicting inferences).

Because Burnett presented evidence from which a reasonable jury could find adequate notice under both statutes, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
