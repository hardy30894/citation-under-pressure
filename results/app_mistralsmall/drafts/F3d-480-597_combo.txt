**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATUTE UNDER WHICH APPELLANT WAS CONVICTED OF UNLAWFUL POSSESSION OF A MACHINE GUN, 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), REQUIRES PROOF OF KNOWING POSSESSION OF A MACHINE GUN, AND THE GOVERNMENT FAILED TO PRESENT SUFFICIENT EVIDENCE TO MEET THIS MENS REA REQUIREMENT.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that a criminal statute define the requisite mens rea with sufficient clarity to give fair warning of the prohibited conduct. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 253 (1922). Where a statute is silent as to mens rea, courts infer a requirement of at least knowing possession. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 251-52 (1952). Section 922(o) prohibits the possession of machine guns but does not expressly state a mens rea requirement. *Morissette* instructs that in such cases, the scienter requirement is read into the statute to avoid harsh and unintended results. *Id.* at 260.

Here, the government presented no evidence that appellant knew the firearm in her possession was a machine gun. The AK-47 recovered from the golf bag is a machine gun under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(b), but the record contains no evidence that appellant knew the weapon’s functional characteristics. The agent’s testimony that appellant admitted knowing the bag contained a rifle does not establish knowledge that it was a machine gun. *Morissette* requires proof of knowledge of the *nature* of the item possessed. *Id.* at 271. The government’s failure to present such evidence renders the conviction unsupported by sufficient evidence.

**II. THE STATUTE UNDER WHICH APPELLANT WAS CONVICTED OF UNLAWFUL POSSESSION OF A FIREARM IN A SCHOOL ZONE, 18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A), IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE AS APPLIED TO HER BECAUSE IT FAILS TO SPECIFY HOW THE 1,000-FOOT DISTANCE IS MEASURED.**

The void-for-vagueness doctrine requires that a criminal statute define the prohibited conduct with sufficient definiteness to provide fair notice and prevent arbitrary enforcement. *Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). A statute is void for vagueness if it does not specify the method by which a critical measurement is taken. *United States v. Petrillo*, 332 U.S. 1, 7-8 (1947).

Section 922(q)(2)(A) prohibits firearm possession within 1,000 feet of a school but does not specify whether the distance is measured in a straight line, by road, or by some other method. This ambiguity fails to provide fair warning of the prohibited zone. *Papachristou v. Jacksonville*, 405 U.S. 156, 162 (1972) (quoting *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939)). The lack of a specified measurement method renders the statute unconstitutionally vague as applied to appellant.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY THAT THE AK-47 WAS A "MACHINE GUN" WITHOUT DEFINING THE TERM OR REQUIRING PROOF OF APPELLANT’S KNOWLEDGE OF ITS FUNCTIONAL CHARACTERISTICS.**

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause requires that jury instructions accurately reflect the law and the evidence. *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 443 (1895). The district court’s instruction that the AK-47 was a "machine gun" without defining the term or requiring proof of appellant’s knowledge of its nature deprived her of a fair trial. *Agnew v. United States*, 165 U.S. 33, 45 (1897).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, appellant’s convictions should be reversed. The judgment of the district court is erroneous and should be set aside.
