**ARGUMENT**

This Court should answer the question presented **no**. On plain-error review, an appellate court may not roam outside the record to determine prejudice or to decide whether any error undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. Plain-error review is necessarily bounded by the appellate record and by the historic rule that errors must be shown from the proceedings below, not supplied by new matter on appeal. See *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896) (stating that plain error may be noticed only where necessary to prevent “a clear miscarriage of justice”); *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (recognizing corrective power for “errors which seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings”); *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 467–68 (1946) (appellate review is confined to “the record” and the Court “cannot travel outside the record” to resolve factual matters).

First, appellate review is inherently record-bound. This Court has long held that a reviewing court “can only know what is in the record.” *Nalle v. Oyster*, 230 U.S. 165, 171 (1913). Likewise, “[t]he rule is settled” that appellate courts do not consider matters not made part of the record below. *Baldwin v. Kansas*, 129 U.S. 52, 55 (1889). That principle is especially forceful where a defendant asks an appellate court to decide whether an error affected substantial rights, because prejudice depends on what happened at trial, not on later-assembled materials. See *United States v. Fletcher*, 148 U.S. 84, 88 (1893) (appellate court reviews “the case made by the record”); *Cleveland, Cincinnati, Chicago & St. Louis Ry. Co. v. Halliday*, 223 U.S. 352, 356 (1912) (review is limited to “the record” and the facts therein shown).

Second, plain-error doctrine does not authorize factfinding on appeal. The doctrine developed as a narrow exception for obvious legal mistakes apparent from the proceedings, not as an invitation to supplement the evidentiary record. In *Wiborg*, this Court exercised its authority only because the error was apparent from the record itself. 163 U.S. at 658. In *Atkinson*, the Court again spoke in terms of an error that “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings,” 297 U.S. at 160, but nothing in that formulation permits an appellate court to look beyond the record to manufacture prejudice. To the contrary, the point of the doctrine is to correct egregious errors that are already demonstrated in the trial proceedings. See *Oppenheimer v. United States*, 242 U.S. 85, 87–88 (1916) (error review depends on what appears in the proceedings and record); *United States v. Frady*, 49 F.2d 112, 114 (D.C. Cir. 1931) (plain error must be “obvious on the face of the record”).

Third, allowing extra-record materials to establish prejudice would undermine the adversarial process and the division between trial and appellate courts. The jury verdict or trial judge’s ruling is to be judged on the evidence actually presented below. See *Thompson v. City of Louisville*, 362 U.S. 199, 206 (1960) (constitutional sufficiency depends on “the record”); *Parker v. Gladden*, 385 U.S. 363, 365 (1966) (reviewing effect of error based on record of the proceedings). If a defendant could defeat plain-error doctrine by adducing post hoc documents or appellate-argument materials outside the record, the government would lose the benefit of trial-level factfinding and the trial court would be deprived of the first opportunity to correct or explain the asserted error. That is precisely why appellate courts have repeatedly refused to consider materials not introduced below. See *Moyer v. Peabody*, 212 U.S. 78, 84 (1909); *Sims v. United States*, 217 U.S. 325, 331 (1910).

Greer’s position also conflicts with the settled rule that prejudice must be shown by the appellant. Plain error is not presumed; it must be affirmatively established from the record before the appellate court. See *Forster v. United States*, 268 U.S. 271, 274 (1925) (reversal requires a showing that the error affected the outcome); *Hammond v. United States*, 269 U.S. 364, 366 (1926) (same). If the appellate court may look outside the record, that burden becomes illusory, because the defendant can supplement the failed trial showing with materials never tested below. The result would be not appellate review but appellate reconstruction.

Finally, the fairness-inquiry component of plain-error review does not enlarge the record. “Fairness, integrity or public reputation” is a standard for deciding whether an identified error warrants correction, not a license to gather new facts to identify prejudice in the first place. *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160. The appellate court’s task is to determine, from the existing record, whether the error likely affected the judgment and whether correction is necessary to prevent a miscarriage of justice. *Wiborg*, 163 U.S. at 658. It may not do so by considering matters outside the trial record.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
