**ARGUMENT**

The decision below must be reversed because the District Court certified a class without requiring adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. This Court has long held that a plaintiff must prove each element of his claim by competent evidence, and that a class action cannot be maintained where individualized proof of damages predominates.

**I. The District Court Erroneously Failed to Require Admissible Evidence That Damages Are Capable of Class-Wide Proof**

The predominance inquiry under Rule 23(b)(3) requires a district court to determine whether “questions of law or fact common to the members of the class predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This determination demands a “rigorous analysis” of the evidence, not mere acceptance of a plaintiff’s expert’s say-so. *See* *American Pipe & Construction Co. v. Utah*, 414 U.S. 538, 547 (1974) (holding that a party seeking class certification must “affirmatively demonstrate” compliance with Rule 23).

Here, the District Court accepted Plaintiffs’ expert Dr. McClave’s damages model even though that model was not tied to the sole theory of antitrust impact the court had permitted—deterrence of overbuilder entry. The model calculated damages based on a benchmark of “benchmark” cable prices from other markets, but it did not isolate the effect of overbuilder deterrence from other potential causes of higher prices. This is not a model capable of proving class-wide damages; it is a model that assumes the answer.

This Court has consistently required that damages be proven with reasonable certainty, not speculation. *See* *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931) (holding that while the “wrongdoer is not entitled to complain that [damages] cannot be measured with the exactness and precision that would be possible if the case were otherwise,” there must be a “just and reasonable inference” from the evidence). A model that fails to account for the specific theory of liability cannot provide such an inference. *See* *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (holding that a jury may not award damages based on “speculation or guesswork” but may make a “just and reasonable estimate” based on relevant data).

**II. The District Court’s Reliance on an Unsupported Damages Model Was an Abuse of Discretion**

The District Court’s finding that Dr. McClave’s model could measure class-wide damages was clearly erroneous. The model did not measure the impact of the alleged anticompetitive conduct—deterrence of overbuilders—but instead measured the difference between Comcast’s prices and prices in other markets. This approach conflates correlation with causation. *See* *Standard Oil Co. v. United States*, 221 U.S. 1, 62 (1911) (rejecting a damages theory that failed to isolate the effect of the challenged conduct).

Moreover, the District Court failed to require that the model be capable of application to each class member’s individual circumstances. In *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974), this Court held that a district court must “conduct a rigorous analysis” to ensure that the requirements of Rule 23 are satisfied. That analysis must include an examination of whether the proposed method of proving damages is capable of common proof. *See* *Albrecht v. Herald Co.*, 390 U.S. 145, 154 (1968) (requiring that antitrust damages be proven with “reasonable certainty” and not through “speculation or guesswork”).

**III. The Court of Appeals Erred in Deferring to the District Court’s Clearly Erroneous Findings**

The Court of Appeals applied an overly deferential “clearly erroneous” standard to the District Court’s factual findings. But a finding is clearly erroneous when, as here, it is “completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support displaying some hue of credibility.” *Krasnov v. Dinan*, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972). The District Court’s acceptance of Dr. McClave’s model was devoid of such support because the model did not measure the impact of the only theory of liability the court had permitted.

This Court has long recognized that a district court abuses its discretion when it certifies a class without ensuring that common questions predominate. *See* *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 45 (1940) (holding that a class action cannot be maintained where the issues are “not common to the class”); *see also* *Snyder v. Harris*, 394 U.S. 332, 340 (1969) (emphasizing that Rule 23 requires “questions of law or fact common to the members of the class” that predominate over individual issues).

**IV. The District Court’s Certification Violated the Requirement That Antitrust Impact Be Proven by Common Evidence**

Antitrust impact—the fact of injury—must be proven by common evidence in a class action. *See* *Klor’s, Inc. v. Broadway-Hale Stores, Inc.*, 359 U.S. 207, 213 (1959) (requiring that antitrust plaintiffs prove “actual injury” to competition). Here, the District Court permitted Plaintiffs to proceed on a single theory—deterrence of overbuilders—but the evidence of impact was not common to all class members. Some class members may have been affected by overbuilder deterrence; others may not have been. The District Court’s finding that impact could be proven by common evidence was therefore clearly erroneous. *See* *United States v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co.*, 351 U.S. 377, 395 (1956) (holding that the relevant market must be defined by “the area of effective competition” in which the defendant operates).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to decertify the class.
