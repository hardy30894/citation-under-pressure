# ARGUMENT

## I. Equity Has Long Measured Reasonable Attorney Compensation by the Quality of Representation and the Results Achieved, Not by Hours Alone

The premise underlying petitioners' position—that a "reasonable" fee is fixed solely by multiplying hours by a market rate—cannot be reconciled with nearly a century of this Court's equitable fee jurisprudence, the very tradition from which modern fee-shifting statutes descend.

In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1881), this Court approved compensating an attorney out of a common fund because his exertions produced a benefit for the class of beneficiaries he represented, establishing that the value of legal services is measured by what counsel actually accomplished for those he served, not merely by the clock. The Court reaffirmed that principle in *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), holding that an attorney's right to compensation from a fund turns on the benefit his efforts conferred. The Court applied the same logic in *Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co. of New York*, 259 U.S. 101 (1922), scrutinizing a fee allowance against the value the litigation produced for the parties who bore its cost.

This Court extended the analysis in *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), rooting fee awards in the chancellor's traditional authority to compensate counsel according to the equities of the particular case—an inquiry that necessarily looks beyond hours to the substance and success of the representation. And in *Woods v. City National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago*, 312 U.S. 262 (1941), the Court examined both the benefit conferred on the estate and the character of the legal work performed in assessing whether a fee award was proper.

These decisions establish a settled principle: from the earliest exercises of federal equity jurisdiction over attorney compensation, courts have treated the *quality* of representation and the *result* obtained as legitimate—and at times decisive—components of a reasonable fee. Petitioners ask this Court to abandon that tradition in favor of a rigid formula that equity itself never employed even in the common-fund cases from which fee-shifting statutes take their lineage.

## II. Congress Enacted Fee-Shifting Statutes to Secure Counsel Capable of Producing Exceptional Results, Not Merely to Reimburse Hours Billed

Congress did not legislate against a blank slate. It wrote § 1988 against the equitable backdrop described above, with the specific purpose of ensuring that civil rights plaintiffs could obtain counsel capable of litigating complex, resource-intensive cases to a meaningful conclusion. This Court's decision in *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400 (1968), construing the analogous fee-shifting provision in Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, confirms that purpose. The Court explained that "if successful plaintiffs were routinely forced to bear their own attorneys' fees, few aggrieved parties would be in a position to advance the public interest by invoking the injunctive powers of the federal courts." *Id.* at 402. That reasoning presupposes fee awards adequate not merely to reimburse time spent, but to make possible the sustained, skilled advocacy that actually vindicates the underlying right—precisely the advocacy the district court found here.

*Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714 (1967), confirms the point from the opposite direction. This Court there declined to fashion a fee-shifting remedy absent statutory authorization, emphasizing that Congress deliberately chooses when and how to displace the American Rule to serve particular statutory ends. *Id.* at 719–20. Where Congress has made that choice, as it did in § 1988, courts must give it practical effect. A categorical bar on enhancement—even in the rare case where the lodestar demonstrably fails to capture extraordinary performance and results—would frustrate rather than serve the statutory design that *Piggie Park* and *Fleischmann* together illuminate.

## III. The District Court's Firsthand Assessment of Counsel's Performance Warrants the Deference Traditionally Accorded the Tribunal Closest to the Evidence

No court is better positioned than the one that presided over the litigation to judge whether counsel's performance and the result obtained were exceptional. This Court has long recognized that the tribunal that observes proceedings firsthand deserves deference precisely because an appellate court cannot recreate that vantage point from a paper record. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), the Court held that a factual determination should be disturbed only when the reviewing court, examining the entire record, is "left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *Id.* at 395. That standard, rooted in the recognition that trial-level observation cannot be duplicated on appeal, applies with equal force to a district judge's assessment—formed over years presiding over this litigation—of whether counsel's advocacy and the relief secured were extraordinary.

## IV. This Is the Rare Case in Which Enhancement Was Warranted

The district court did not rely on generalized praise. It made detailed, record-based findings concerning the scope of Georgia's foster-care crisis, the extraordinary relief obtained, and counsel's singular commitment, before concluding that the lodestar alone could not capture the value of what was accomplished. Consistent with the equitable principles reflected in *Greenough*, *Pettus*, *Newton*, *Sprague*, and *Woods*, the statutory purpose confirmed in *Piggie Park* and *Fleischmann*, and the deference commanded by *United States Gypsum Co.*, the enhancement below should be affirmed.
