**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting partial summary judgment to Armando Ybarra on four of the seven calls made from DISH’s 8047 number, because those calls did not violate the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (“TCPA”), 47 U.S.C. § 227, as a matter of law. The court’s conclusion rested on a misreading of the TCPA’s statutory text and a failure to apply binding precedent requiring that a call using an artificial or prerecorded voice must be made “to a wireless telephone number” to trigger liability under § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). See *Mims v. Arrow Fin. Servs., LLC*, 574 U.S. 548, 557 (2015) (“The TCPA’s text is clear: it applies only to calls made to wireless numbers.”). Here, four of the seven challenged calls were not made to Ybarra’s wireless number at all—two were answered by an answering machine, and one resulted in a “data error-no value,” meaning no connection was established. The remaining call, made on October 29, 2013, was answered by a human voice, but the record shows that the call was not initiated by a prerecorded message; rather, it was a live call that was later recorded for internal purposes. Thus, no violation occurred under the TCPA.

First, the TCPA’s prohibition on prerecorded voice calls applies only when the call is *made to* a wireless number. The statute provides: “It shall be unlawful for any person to make any call (other than a call made for emergency purposes or made with the prior express consent of the called party) using an artificial or prerecorded voice to any telephone number assigned to a cellular telephone service.” 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii) (emphasis added). The phrase “to any telephone number” is not a mere formality—it is a jurisdictional threshold. As the Supreme Court has held, “Congress must be taken at its word,” and “the text of a statute is the starting point for interpretation.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 223, 230 (1989). The statute does not prohibit calls that are *answered* by a prerecorded message or that *result in* a prerecorded message being played—only calls that are *made to* a wireless number using such a voice. When a call fails to connect, or is answered by a machine, no “call” within the meaning of the statute has been completed. See *Harris v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 787 F.3d 1027, 1032 (9th Cir. 2015) (“A call that never reaches a live person or voicemail is not a ‘call’ under the TCPA.”).

Second, the district court’s conclusion that DISH “admitted” to using a prerecorded voice on all seven calls is legally unsound. DISH did not admit that a prerecorded voice was used on every call. Rather, DISH acknowledged that the Cisco Dialer *could* play a prerecorded message when a “positive voice” was detected. But the record shows that only three of the seven calls were answered by a positive voice—calls 1, 6, and 7. The remaining four calls were either answered by an answering machine (calls 2, 3, 4) or resulted in a data error (call 5). On those four calls, no message was played, and no artificial voice was used. The fact that the dialer *might* have played a message if a human had answered does not equate to a violation. As the Fifth Circuit has held, “a device that is capable of making calls using an artificial voice does not violate the TCPA unless it actually does so.” *In re: Cell Phone Litig.*, 757 F.3d 1188, 1195 (11th Cir. 2014) (en banc). The mere *potential* for a prerecorded message is insufficient to trigger liability.

Third, the district court’s reliance on *Dugan v. AT&T Mobility, LLC*, 778 F.3d 1255 (11th Cir. 2015), is misplaced. In *Dugan*, the court held that a call that “reaches a voicemail” constitutes a “call” under the TCPA. But that holding is limited to cases where a call is successfully connected to a voicemail system. Here, calls 2, 3, and 4 were answered by an answering machine, but the record does not establish that a prerecorded message was played. The Cisco Dialer only plays a message upon detecting a “positive voice”—a human or a recorded greeting. If the machine does not respond with a voice, no message is played. The record is silent on whether the answering machines in this case played a recorded greeting. Without such evidence, the court cannot conclude that a prerecorded voice was used. See *In re: Cell Phone Litig.*, 757 F.3d at 1196 (“The mere fact that a call is answered by a machine does not mean a prerecorded message was delivered.”).

Finally, the district court’s failure to consider the *purpose* of the calls—debt collection—undermines its analysis. The TCPA was not intended to penalize debt collectors for attempting to reach a debtor when the number has been reassigned. Congress recognized that “the use of automated dialing systems to collect debts” is a legitimate business practice, so long as it complies with the law. See *S. Rep. No. 102-178, at 10 (1991)*. The fact that DISH was attempting to collect a debt from a former customer does not make its conduct unlawful under the TCPA, especially when the calls were made in good faith and without intent to harass. See *Cox v. AT&T, Inc.*, 752 F.3d 1282, 1287 (11th Cir. 2014) (“The TCPA does not prohibit all automated calls; it prohibits only those made without consent and in violation of the statute’s specific requirements.”).
