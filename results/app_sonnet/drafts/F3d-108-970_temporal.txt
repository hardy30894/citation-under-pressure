# ARGUMENT

## I. Standard of Review

A jury verdict cannot rest on speculation. In *Galloway v. United States*, 319 U.S. 372 (1943), the Supreme Court explained that "the essential requirement is that mere speculation be not allowed to do duty for probative facts, after making due allowance for all reasonably possible inferences favoring the party whose case is attacked." *Id.* at 395. Similarly, in *Pennsylvania R. Co. v. Chamberlain*, 288 U.S. 333 (1933), the Court reversed a judgment where the jury's finding rested on conjecture rather than substantial evidence, holding that a verdict may not stand where the proof permits only speculative inferences. These principles govern this appeal: the judgment below must be reversed unless the record contains more than speculative support for each element of Feltmann's claims.

## II. The Sex Discrimination Verdict Rests on Speculation, Not Substantial Evidence

Title VII makes it unlawful for an employer "to discharge any individual, or otherwise to discriminate against any individual with respect to his compensation, terms, conditions, or privileges of employment, because of such individual's race, color, religion, sex, or national origin." 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1). The statute requires proof that sex was the reason for the challenged action, not merely that a female employee experienced adverse treatment while employed among men. See *Quarles v. Philip Morris, Inc.*, 279 F. Supp. 505 (E.D. Va. 1968) (Title VII targets practices motivated by the prohibited classification, not neutral business judgments that happen to affect members of that class).

The record here shows legitimate, nondiscriminatory business reasons for every decision Feltmann challenges. Rich restricted demo-car use to the St. Louis area for all consultants because of dealership-wide cost concerns following the end of the incentive program—a facially neutral policy applied before Feltmann's personal memo, which was sent only after she persisted in driving 300 miles round-trip to Illinois in violation of the general restriction. Rich placed Feltmann on probation in March 1993 not because of her sex but because her gross-profit averages were the lowest of all consultants for five consecutive months, a business concern Rich testified traced to her selling technique. Critically, Rich placed a *male* consultant, Rick Beutel, on probation at the identical time for the identical reason—low sales performance. That a similarly situated male received the same treatment forecloses any nondiscriminatory inference from that decision. See *Bowe v. Colgate-Palmolive Co.*, 416 F.2d 711 (7th Cir. 1969) (disparate treatment under Title VII must be assessed by comparing how the employer actually treated similarly situated employees of each sex).

As to the Chicago sales competition, Rich offered the trip to the two top-selling consultants; when they declined, he extended it to no one else—male or female. Feltmann's own evidence establishes that several men who wished to attend were likewise denied the opportunity. Where the same policy is applied without regard to sex, no reasonable jury could infer that sex motivated the decision; to conclude otherwise is to indulge in the sort of speculation condemned in *Galloway*, 319 U.S. at 395, and *Chamberlain*, 288 U.S. 333. The judgment on the sex-discrimination count should therefore be reversed.

## III. The Retaliation Verdict Cannot Stand Because the Record Does Not Support a Causal Link Between Feltmann's Complaint and Any Adverse Action

Title VII forbids an employer from discriminating against an employee "because he has opposed any practice made an unlawful employment practice by this subchapter." 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-3(a). The statutory word "because" requires proof of a causal connection between the protected complaint and the adverse action—not mere sequence in time. Absent such proof, a verdict rests on speculation forbidden under *Galloway v. United States*, 319 U.S. 372, 395 (1943).

Feltmann complained about Anzalone's remark in November 1992. The Capps brothers responded immediately: they met with her, reprimanded Anzalone, warned him of termination, and asked whether she wanted him fired. Feltmann testified she heard no further vulgar comments. The actions she now characterizes as retaliatory—the January 4 "work ethic" memo, the March probation, exclusion from the Chicago trip, and the February demo-car memo—occurred one to five months later, each accompanied by an independent, documented business justification tied to declining sales figures dealership-wide. A single ambiguous remark from co-worker Barnstead that Feltmann would have "a thick personnel file"—not attributed to any decisionmaker—cannot substitute for proof that Rich or the Capps brothers acted from a retaliatory motive. Where, as here, intervening legitimate business developments (the end of the incentive program, dealership-wide sales decline, cost-cutting on demo cars) fully explain the timing of each action, the temporal sequence alone cannot support an inference of retaliation without descending into the "speculation and conjecture" that *Chamberlain*, 288 U.S. 333, and *Galloway*, 319 U.S. at 395, forbid.

Because the record cannot sustain a finding that Feltmann's protected complaint, rather than her documented sales performance and dealership-wide cost concerns, caused any challenged action, the judgment on the retaliation claim must likewise be reversed.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed and judgment entered for Sieben, or, in the alternative, a new trial ordered.
