The petitioners challenge the constitutionality of the Massachusetts law creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics, arguing that it restricts their First Amendment rights. However, the First Circuit correctly upheld the law as a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time-place-manner regulation.

The law is not a restriction on the content of speech, but rather a regulation of the place where speech can occur. As the Supreme Court held in Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574-75 (1941), the government has the authority to regulate the use of public spaces to ensure the safety and convenience of citizens. The buffer zone in this case serves to protect the safety and privacy of individuals entering and exiting the abortion clinics.

The petitioners argue that the law is not narrowly tailored because it restricts their ability to engage in "sidewalk counseling." However, the government has a legitimate interest in maintaining public order and safety, and the buffer zone is a reasonable means of achieving that interest. As the Court held in Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77, 87 (1949), the government may regulate the use of sound trucks and other noise-making devices to prevent disturbance of the peace.

The petitioners also argue that the law leaves them without adequate alternative channels of communication. However, the record shows that they can still engage in oral speech, distribute literature, and display signs outside the buffer zone. The Court has held that the First Amendment does not guarantee a right to communicate at a particular location or in a particular manner. See Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939).

The petitioners' reliance on cases such as Lovell v. City of Griffin, 303 U.S. 444 (1938), is misplaced. In Lovell, the Court struck down a city ordinance that prohibited the distribution of literature without a permit. However, the Massachusetts law at issue here is not a prior restraint on speech, but rather a content-neutral regulation of the place where speech can occur.

The First Circuit correctly applied the principles of Cox, Kovacs, and Schneider in upholding the Massachusetts law. The law is a reasonable regulation of the use of public spaces, and it does not unduly restrict the petitioners' First Amendment rights. The judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
