# ARGUMENT

## I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews the district court's factual findings, including its finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Danelsy's removal and retention, for clear error. A finding is "clearly erroneous when although there is evidence to support it, the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). This standard is not a rubber stamp; where the trial court's inferences from essentially undisputed or ambiguous testimony do not withstand scrutiny, reversal is required. United States v. Yellow Cab Co., 338 U.S. 338, 342 (1949) (holding that Rule 52(a)'s clearly-erroneous standard governs not only primary findings but also the factual inferences drawn from them). Because the district court's finding of consent rests on an inference from inherently contradictory and equivocal testimony, that finding cannot stand.

## II. Consent to the Permanent Removal and Retention of a Child Must Be Established by Clear Evidence of an Intentional Relinquishment of a Known Right

The district court's conclusion that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Danelsy's removal to, and retention in, the United States effectively treated her custodial rights as waived. But waiver "is the intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). It is not to be found in ambiguous conduct or presumed from silence; it must be shown by conduct that unmistakably manifests the intent to give up the right in question. Id. Nothing in this record satisfies that exacting standard.

Gonzalez-Caballero's own testimony was, as the district court itself recognized, "somewhat confused." At times she described an arrangement permitting Mena to take Danelsy for two weeks; at other times she described a contingent arrangement under which she would allow Mena to retain custody only until she herself could immigrate to the United States and resume custody of her daughter. This is not the "intentional relinquishment" required by Zerbst. At most, it describes a conditional, temporary accommodation — the functional opposite of a permanent surrender of custody. Where the very testimony relied upon to establish the waiver is internally contradictory, it cannot furnish the clear and unmistakable manifestation of intent that the law demands.

## III. The Fundamental Character of Parental Custodial Rights Requires that Any Claimed Relinquishment Be Narrowly and Cautiously Construed

The custodial relationship between a mother and her child is not a mere contractual arrangement to be inferred loosely from equivocal conduct. It is a right of the highest constitutional order. This Court's forebears have long recognized that "the custody, care and nurture of the child reside first in the parents, whose primary function and freedom include preparation for obligations the state can neither supply nor hinder." Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158, 166 (1944); see also Meyer v. Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390, 399 (1923) (recognizing the liberty interest of parents "to establish a home and bring up children"). Given the fundamental nature of the right at stake, courts should be loath to find its relinquishment on conduct susceptible of an innocent, non-relinquishing explanation.

Here, Gonzalez-Caballero's undisputed subsequent conduct is wholly inconsistent with any intent to permanently surrender Danelsy. The day after Mena and Danelsy departed, when Mena failed to telephone as promised, Gonzalez-Caballero went to Panamanian child custody authorities and the police. She contacted her local police department, the Judge Advocate General Corps liaison, the Institute for Children, the office of the President of Panama, Panamanian radio stations, and the Children's Commission — all within roughly six weeks of Danelsy's departure. She then accepted the Panamanian government's assistance to travel, without funds of her own, to the United States to recover her daughter. Such conduct is the antithesis of "intentional relinquishment." Zerbst, 304 U.S. at 464. A parent who has truly and knowingly relinquished custody does not spend the ensuing weeks marshaling the resources of two governments to retrieve the child.

## IV. The District Court's Finding Was Clearly Erroneous

Applying the Gypsum standard, the record as a whole leaves this Court with "the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." 333 U.S. at 395. The only evidence of "consent" is Gonzalez-Caballero's own inconsistent testimony describing, at most, a temporary or conditional accommodation — not the clear and unmistakable relinquishment of a fundamental custodial right required under Zerbst and safeguarded by the principles articulated in Prince and Meyer. That inference is further undermined, not supported, by Gonzalez-Caballero's immediate and sustained efforts, corroborated by multiple independent Panamanian and American institutions, to recover her daughter. Because the district court's finding rests on a selective and untenable reading of contradictory testimony, and ignores undisputed corroborating conduct pointing the opposite direction, it is clearly erroneous within the meaning of Yellow Cab Co., 338 U.S. at 342, and Gypsum, 333 U.S. at 395.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's finding of consent constitutes clear error, and its judgment denying the petition for return of Danelsy should be reversed.
