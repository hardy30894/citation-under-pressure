**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General, nor does it permit an impermissible delegation to the Drug Enforcement Administration. The delegation at issue is fully consistent with the constitutional principle that Congress may delegate authority to coordinate branches so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegatee’s exercise of power. This Court has repeatedly upheld such delegations, particularly where Congress has established clear standards, procedural safeguards, and a framework for judicial review. The Toubys’ challenge fails because the statutory scheme satisfies every requirement of the non-delegation doctrine as interpreted by this Court in decisions long before 1970.

First, Congress has provided an “intelligible principle” to guide the Attorney General’s exercise of temporary scheduling authority. The statute explicitly requires the Attorney General to consider eight specific factors before temporarily scheduling a substance: (1) its potential for abuse; (2) scientific evidence of its pharmacological effect; (3) the state of current scientific knowledge; (4) its history and current pattern of abuse; (5) the scope, duration, and significance of abuse; (6) the risk it poses to public health; (7) its dependence liability; and (8) whether it is an immediate precursor of a controlled substance. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). These factors are not vague or amorphous; they are detailed, objective, and rooted in medical and scientific inquiry. This Court has long recognized that such specificity suffices to satisfy the non-delegation doctrine. In *Wichita Railroad & Light Co. v. Public Utilities Commission of Kansas*, 260 U.S. 48, 55 (1922), this Court held that a legislative delegation is valid when the legislature “enjoin[s] upon [an] agency a certain course of procedure and certain rules of decision.” The same principle applies here: Congress has not abdicated its responsibility but has set forth a clear, multi-factor test that the Attorney General must follow.

Second, the statute incorporates procedural safeguards that further ensure the delegation is not arbitrary. The Attorney General must publish notice in the Federal Register, transmit the proposed action to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, and consider the Secretary’s evaluation. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). The Secretary’s evaluation is binding if he recommends against scheduling. This mechanism ensures that the decision is not made in a vacuum but is informed by independent medical and scientific expertise. The Court has recognized that such procedural checks are essential to the legitimacy of administrative action. In *Magone v. Heller*, 150 U.S. 70, 75 (1893), this Court emphasized that Congress may “evidently intend[ed]” that certain classifications—such as those based on use—should be exempt from duty, and that such intent must be discerned from the statutory text. Here, Congress has clearly intended that temporary scheduling be based on public health risk and scientific evidence, not political whim.

Third, the delegation is not to a mere executive officer but to a coordinate branch of government. The Attorney General is a constitutional officer, and the power to temporarily schedule substances is an exercise of the executive’s law enforcement function, not a legislative act in the strict sense. This Court has long distinguished between legislative power and executive authority. In *District of Columbia v. John R. Thompson Co.*, 346 U.S. 100, 105 (1953), the Court held that Congress may delegate “local regulation” to the District of Columbia, even though the District is not a sovereign state, because such powers are “of a municipal character” and do not involve “general legislation.” Similarly, the power to temporarily schedule drugs is not the creation of new crimes but the enforcement of existing criminal prohibitions based on a determination of imminent public danger. The Court has repeatedly upheld such delegations. In *Minnesota Ex Rel. Whipple v. Martinson*, 256 U.S. 41, 47 (1921), this Court upheld a state statute regulating dangerous drugs as a valid exercise of police power, noting that such regulation “is so manifest in the interest of the public health and welfare, that it is unnecessary to enter upon a discussion of it beyond saying that it is too firmly established to be successfully called in question.”

Fourth, the delegation is not unlimited in duration or scope. Temporary scheduling is limited to one year, with a possible six-month extension, and must be followed by a permanent scheduling determination. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). This time limit ensures that the executive’s power is not exercised indefinitely. The Court has recognized that temporal constraints are a key safeguard against arbitrary power. In *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 363 (1942), this Court held that the absence of authority to delegate subpoena power indicated a legislative intent to withhold such power, and that Congress “fail[ed] to grant” it. By contrast, here Congress has granted the power but with clear temporal and procedural limits, demonstrating a deliberate and measured delegation.

Fifth, the Attorney General’s sub-delegation to the DEA Administrator is fully authorized. Congress has long permitted the Attorney General to delegate functions to subordinate officers. In *The Toilet Goods Association, Inc. v. John W. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 158, 162 (1967), this Court upheld the Secretary’s authority to promulgate regulations under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, noting that “the Secretary’s order was the promulgation of a general rule, prospective in operation.” The same principle applies here: the DEA Administrator is acting under the Attorney General’s authority, and the delegation is consistent with established administrative practice. The Court has long recognized that the executive branch may delegate authority to implement statutory schemes. In *Gardner v. Toilet Goods Assn., Inc.*, 387 U.S. 167, 170 (1967), the Court affirmed that the Secretary’s authority to regulate labeling was not beyond the scope of the statute, even though it involved detailed technical standards.
