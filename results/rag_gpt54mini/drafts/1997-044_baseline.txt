**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 523(a)(2)(A) makes fraud debts nondischargeable, including damages that flow from the fraud.**

The Bankruptcy Code “does not discharge” an individual debtor from any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by” “false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). In **Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279 (1991)**, this Court explained that § 523(a) governs “certain kinds of obligations, including those for money obtained by ‘actual fraud.’” 498 U.S. at 282. The Court held that a creditor need prove fraud only by a preponderance of the evidence, confirming Congress’s judgment that fraudulent debts are not entitled to the Bankruptcy Code’s fresh start. Id. at 287-88.

That judgment is reinforced by **Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. 59 (1995)**, which described § 523(a)(2)(A) as one of the Bankruptcy Code’s provisions excepting from discharge debts obtained by “false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud.” 516 U.S. at 63-64. The statute’s focus is on the debt “to the extent obtained by” fraud, not merely on the bare return of the money originally taken. When a debtor obtains property by fraud, the resulting liability is nondischargeable.

Cohen’s liability falls squarely within that rule. The bankruptcy court found that he knowingly charged rents beyond lawful limits and thereby committed actual fraud under § 523(a)(2)(A). The tenants’ debt arose from that fraud. A debtor cannot evade § 523(a)(2)(A) by saying the creditor’s damages were later measured by statutory remedies rather than by the exact amount first taken. Once the debt is “obtained by” fraud, the Code excepts the resulting obligation from discharge.

**II. The nondischargeability extends to treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs awarded on account of the fraud.**

Petitioner’s central point is simple: if a debt is nondischargeable because it was obtained by fraud, the debtor cannot discharge the full judgment flowing from that fraud merely because part of the recovery is labeled punitive, statutory, or ancillary. This Court’s cases support that principle.

In **Grogan**, the Court noted that the fraud judgment at issue included both actual and punitive damages, and it treated the fraud judgment as the debt implicated by § 523(a). 498 U.S. at 281-82. More broadly, **Kelly v. Robinson, 479 U.S. 36 (1986)**, teaches that discharge exceptions are construed in light of the Code’s text and purposes, but not in a way that allows wrongdoers to retain the benefit of misconduct. 479 U.S. at 43-50. And in **United States v. Sotelo, 436 U.S. 268 (1978)**, this Court held that the label attached to an obligation does not control its dischargeability; the question is the substance of the debt. 436 U.S. at 275-79.

The same principle governs here. The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act required treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs once Cohen’s fraud was established. Those amounts were not separate, voluntary obligations unrelated to the fraud; they were part of the legal consequence of the fraud itself. Under § 523(a)(2)(A), the relevant inquiry is whether the debt was obtained by fraud. It was. Therefore the entire judgment is nondischargeable.

The Third Circuit’s analysis below accords with the better view. It recognized that the Code’s reference to “debt” is broad enough to include the full judgment entered on account of fraud. That understanding is consistent with the statutory text and with this Court’s refusal to let formal labels defeat federal bankruptcy policy. **United States v. Energy Resources Co., 495 U.S. 545 (1990)**, and **Davenport, 495 U.S. 552 (1990)** likewise reflect that courts enforce the Code according to its terms. When Congress excepts fraud debts from discharge, courts should not narrow that exception by carving out the remedial consequences of the fraud.

**III. The fresh-start policy does not protect a debtor who committed actual fraud.**

Cohen relies on the Bankruptcy Code’s “fresh start” policy, but that policy is reserved for the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” **Grogan, 498 U.S. at 286-87**. Fraudulent debtors are not within that category. As this Court explained in **Kelly**, the Bankruptcy Code’s structure and exceptions show that Congress did not intend to extinguish obligations that federal law treats as especially blameworthy. 479 U.S. at 45-50.

Cohen’s conduct was exactly the kind § 523(a)(2)(A) targets: he charged unlawful rents, failed to investigate the law despite ample opportunity, and then sought to wash the debt away in bankruptcy. That is not the fresh start Congress preserved. It is an attempt to convert bankruptcy into a shield for fraud.

**CONCLUSION**

Because § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge debts obtained by actual fraud, and because the fraud-based judgment here includes the damages, fees, and costs imposed on account of Cohen’s misconduct, the decision below should be affirmed.
