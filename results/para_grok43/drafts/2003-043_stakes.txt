ARGUMENT

The Internal Revenue Code requires the government to assess a tax against each separate taxpayer within three years of the return’s filing. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). An assessment “shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer,” 26 U.S.C. § 6203, and must “provide identification of the taxpayer,” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The Code defines “taxpayer” to include both a “partnership” and an “individual.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Because each is a distinct taxpayer, an assessment against one does not extend the ten-year collection period of 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) to the other.

This Court has long recognized that an assessment creates a lien only against the person against whom it is made. In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the court held that no lien arises “against a particular individual’s property pursuant to §§ 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” *Id.* at 462 (quoting district court). The same principle governs the statute of limitations: the assessment against the partnership extended § 6502(a)’s ten-year period only for the partnership.

The government’s reliance on state-law joint-and-several liability does not alter the procedural requirement. California law provides that “[a] judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner” and that a partner’s personal assets may not be reached “unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The government obtained neither an individual assessment nor a judgment within the three-year period of § 6501(a). Its claims are therefore time-barred.

The government’s contrary authorities are inapposite. In *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), the partner had already paid the tax; the court merely denied a refund. *Id.* at 910. It did not hold that collection could proceed without an assessment or judgment. Likewise, *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), involved assessments against both the partnership and the partners, so the court never addressed whether an assessment against the partnership alone suffices.

Because the government failed to assess the individual partners within three years, the bankruptcy court properly disallowed the claims under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
