**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may consider matters outside the trial record when reviewing for plain error under Rule 52(b)—is answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that, in evaluating whether a constitutional error affected the defendant’s substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings, a reviewing court may look beyond the jury’s record to the full factual context, including the defendant’s prior convictions and other relevant background information. This approach is not only consistent with established precedent but is essential to ensuring that the plain error standard serves its intended purpose: to prevent fundamental miscarriages of justice.

The plain error standard, codified in Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), permits appellate courts to correct errors that are “plain” and that “affect substantial rights.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The Supreme Court has long recognized that the “substantial rights” inquiry is not confined to the evidence presented to the jury. Rather, it requires a holistic assessment of whether the error “affected the outcome of the trial.” *Id.* at 734. This assessment necessarily includes consideration of the entire factual and legal context, including the defendant’s criminal history, the nature of the charges, and the overall fairness of the proceeding. To limit review to the trial record alone would render the plain error standard toothless in cases where the error’s impact can only be discerned through a broader examination of the case.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Rehaif v. United States*, 583 U.S. ___ (2019), where the Court held that the government must prove both possession and knowledge of prohibited status in a § 922(g) prosecution. The Court emphasized that the error in failing to instruct the jury on the knowledge element “undermines the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings” when the defendant’s status as a felon was not only known to him but was a central feature of his criminal history. *Id.* at ___ (slip op., at 10). The Court did not limit its inquiry to the jury’s record; instead, it considered the broader context, including the defendant’s prior convictions, to assess whether the error was prejudicial. The Court’s analysis implicitly recognized that a defendant’s prior felony convictions—though not part of the jury’s evidence—may be relevant to determining whether the failure to instruct on knowledge affected the outcome.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision to consider Greer’s prior convictions and the full factual record was therefore not only permissible but required. Greer’s five prior aggravated burglary convictions under Ohio law were the very basis for his ACCA enhancement. These convictions were not merely collateral; they were central to the sentencing determination and, by extension, to the fairness of the entire proceeding. The court’s review of these convictions was not an impermissible “fact-finding” but a necessary component of assessing whether the failure to instruct on knowledge of status—particularly given Greer’s long history of felony convictions—affected his substantial rights.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that appellate courts may consider matters outside the record when necessary to determine whether an error was prejudicial. In *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 630 (2002), the Court stated that “a reviewing court may consider the entire record, including matters outside the trial record, when assessing whether an error affected substantial rights.” The Court emphasized that “the plain error standard is not a technical rule of procedure but a vehicle for correcting errors that would otherwise result in a miscarriage of justice.” *Id.* at 631. To restrict appellate review to the jury’s record would contradict this purpose and allow errors to go uncorrected simply because they were not explicitly presented to the jury.

The respondent’s position is further supported by the practical realities of criminal sentencing. In cases involving the Armed Career Criminal Act, the government’s reliance on prior convictions is not merely evidentiary—it is foundational. When a defendant’s status as a felon is central to the sentencing enhancement, and when that status was known to the defendant through his own criminal history, the failure to instruct on knowledge of status is not a mere technicality. It is a constitutional error that undermines the integrity of the entire proceeding. To ignore the defendant’s prior convictions in assessing prejudice would be to blind the court to the very facts that make the error significant.

The Eleventh Circuit did not overstep its bounds. It did not reweigh evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the jury. It simply applied the plain error standard as intended: to ensure that errors that affect substantial rights are corrected, even when those rights are not apparent from the jury’s record alone. The court’s conclusion—that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice—was based on a careful, factually grounded assessment of the entire record, including his prior convictions and the nature of the charges.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed. The plain error standard permits, and indeed requires, appellate courts to consider the full factual context when determining whether an error affected substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The court’s decision to look beyond the jury’s record was not only proper but essential to the administration of justice.
