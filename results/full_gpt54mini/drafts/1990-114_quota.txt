**Argument**

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for state judges does not violate either the ADEA or the Equal Protection Clause.

I. **Congress did not clearly bring state judges within the ADEA’s coverage.**

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office” and “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). In a field of obvious state concern—qualification and tenure of state judges—courts require a clear congressional statement before construing a federal statute to displace state law. United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971); Gregory v. Ashcroft, 898 F.2d 598, 603-04 (8th Cir. 1990).

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that appointed Missouri judges fall within the policymaking exception. Judges do far more than apply settled rules mechanically; they resolve open questions, fill statutory and constitutional interstices, and create law that binds future cases. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 606-07; EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). That conclusion accords with the ordinary understanding that judicial decisions often shape public policy. See also Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 145-46 (1988); In re Stout, 521 Pa. 571, 559 A.2d 489, 492-93 (1989).

The state judges’ contrary position rests on a cramped view of policymaking. But the ADEA’s text does not confine the exception to executive branch officials. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 608. Nor does the legislative history compel that narrow reading; the statute itself controls. See Consumer Product Safety Comm’n v. GTE Sylvania, Inc., 447 U.S. 102, 108 (1980). The First Circuit reached the same result in EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d at 54-56, and the district courts in Illinois and Vermont, which held otherwise, stand against the better reading of the statute. EEOC v. Illinois, 721 F. Supp. 156 (N.D. Ill. 1989); EEOC v. Vermont, 717 F. Supp. 261 (D. Vt. 1989).

Even if one looked beyond the adjudicatory function, Missouri judges exercise substantial administrative and supervisory authority over the state judiciary. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 607-08. That further confirms that they are appointed officials on the policymaking level. See also Schlitz v. Virginia, 681 F. Supp. 330 (E.D. Va. 1988), rev’d on other grounds, 854 F.2d 43 (4th Cir. 1988); Orzel v. City of Wauwatosa Fire Dep’t, 697 F.2d 743, 752 (7th Cir. 1983).

II. **The ADEA does not clearly preempt Missouri’s constitutional retirement rule.**

Federal statutes that intrude upon the States’ structure of government are not lightly read to upset state constitutional arrangements. Bass, 404 U.S. at 349; Gregory, 898 F.2d at 603-04. Missouri’s retirement provision is part of the State’s constitutional design for its judiciary, and Congress did not speak with the clarity necessary to override that design. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 604-05. The Supremacy Clause applies only if the federal statute validly reaches these judges in the first place. Orzel, 697 F.2d at 752.

III. **The retirement age satisfies rational-basis review.**

Age is not a suspect classification, and mandatory retirement rules for judges need only bear a rational relation to a legitimate state interest. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313-14 (1976) (per curiam); Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97-112 (1979). Missouri’s rule easily meets that standard. States may rationally conclude that a fixed retirement age promotes judicial competence, avoids the difficulty of individualized decline hearings, reduces delays caused by incapacity or death, and supports a judiciary that remains responsive to current social conditions. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 612-13; Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979); Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980); Vance, 440 U.S. at 108-12; Murgia, 427 U.S. at 314-17.

The Equal Protection Clause does not require a State to prove its chosen means perfect or empirically uncontested. See Minnesota v. Clover Leaf Creamery Co., 449 U.S. 456, 464 (1981); FCC v. Beach Commc’ns, Inc., 508 U.S. 307, 313-15 (1993). It is enough that reasonable lawmakers could believe the rule advances legitimate ends. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 613. Missouri’s judgment is plainly within that range.

**Conclusion**

The judgment should be affirmed. Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision neither violates the ADEA nor offends the Equal Protection Clause.
