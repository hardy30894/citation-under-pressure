**ARGUMENT**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), requires that any fact increasing the maximum penalty be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Florida satisfies that command because the jury’s advisory verdict, rendered after hearing evidence of aggravating circumstances, authorizes the trial judge to impose death only upon an independent finding that at least one statutory aggravator exists. See § 921.141(3), Fla. Stat. (2012). The Court has never held that the Sixth Amendment demands jury unanimity on the existence of aggravators or on the ultimate sentence. See McKoy v. North Carolina, 494 U.S. 433, 449 n.5 (1990) (White, J., concurring in judgment) (noting that “unanimity is not constitutionally required”).

The same framework governs mental retardation. Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002), left “to the States the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” Florida’s three-prong statutory test, requiring proof by clear and convincing evidence of significantly subaverage intellectual functioning, adaptive deficits, and onset before age eighteen, § 921.137(1), Fla. Stat., supplies such a procedure. The trial court’s determination after a full evidentiary hearing therefore satisfies due process. See Schriro v. Smith, 546 U.S. 6, 7-8 (2005) (per curiam) (reaffirming state authority to choose procedures implementing Atkins).

Petitioner’s Eighth Amendment claim fares no better. The Court has never required jury unanimity as a condition of a death sentence, and Florida’s scheme contains sufficient safeguards—statutory aggravators, mandatory appellate review, and individualized sentencing—to avoid the arbitrary imposition condemned in Furman v. Georgia, 408 U.S. 238 (1972). Because the Florida Supreme Court’s affirmance rests on settled precedent applying Ring and Atkins, the judgment should be affirmed.
