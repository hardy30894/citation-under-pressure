**ARGUMENT**

**I. Bryant Has Standing to Enforce the Arbitration Award**

Bryant has standing to seek enforcement of the arbitration award because he is the real party in interest and the award was issued in his favor. *See generally* *McDonald v. City of West Branch*, 466 U.S. 284, 287 (1984) (holding that a party may enforce an arbitration award if they are the beneficiary of the award). The district court erred in dismissing this claim on the ground that Bryant lacked standing, as the plain language of the award and the Federal Arbitration Act ("FAA") vests enforcement rights in the prevailing party. *See* 9 U.S.C. § 9 (authorizing a party to petition the court for confirmation of an arbitration award).

**II. The District Court Erred in Applying an Outdated Burden-Shifting Framework**

The district court failed to apply the Supreme Court’s clarified burden-shifting standard in Title VII and § 1981 cases. In *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 142 (2000), the Court held that a plaintiff’s prima facie case, coupled with sufficient evidence to rebut the employer’s asserted nondiscriminatory reason, permits a rational factfinder to conclude that discrimination occurred. The district court, however, required Bryant to present "direct evidence" of discrimination, a standard rejected in *Reeves*. *See id.* at 147-48 (rejecting the "direct evidence" requirement as inconsistent with the statutory scheme).

Similarly, in *St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 515 (1993), the Court clarified that once the employer proffers a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason, the burden does not shift back to the employer to prove its reason was not discriminatory. The district court’s analysis improperly shifted the burden to Bryant to disprove Bell Atlantic’s asserted reasons for termination. *See id.* at 510-11.

**III. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Remain in Dispute**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because material facts are in dispute regarding whether Bell Atlantic’s proffered reasons for termination were pretextual. *See* Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c) (summary judgment is inappropriate if there is a genuine dispute as to any material fact). Bryant presented evidence that Bell Atlantic’s stated reasons for termination—repeated insubordination—were pretextual, including:

1. **Inconsistent Application of the Policy**: Bryant’s pre-policy refusals of overtime went unpunished, yet he was disciplined under the Red Letter Day policy. *See* *Texas Dept. of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 254 (1981) (an employer’s inconsistent application of policies may support an inference of discrimination).

2. **Failure to Accommodate**: Bell Atlantic refused the CWA’s offer to allow co-workers to cover Bryant’s Red Letter Days, despite Bryant’s childcare obligations. *See* *U.S. Postal Serv. Bd. of Governors v. Aikens*, 460 U.S. 711, 716 (1983) (an employer’s failure to accommodate a disabled employee may support a discrimination claim).

3. **Shifting Justifications**: Bell Atlantic’s stated reasons for termination evolved over time, from insubordination to performance issues. *See* *Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins*, 507 U.S. 604, 611 (1993) (an employer’s shifting explanations for an adverse action may indicate pretext).

**IV. The District Court Erred in Dismissing the Retaliation Claim**

Bryant’s retaliation claim under Title VII also survives summary judgment. To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, Bryant must show: (1) he engaged in protected activity; (2) he suffered an adverse employment action; and (3) a causal connection exists between the two. *See* *Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 67-68 (2006). Bryant engaged in protected activity by filing a grievance protesting the Red Letter Day policy, and he suffered adverse actions (suspensions and termination) shortly thereafter. *See id.* at 68 (adverse actions include materially adverse changes to employment conditions).

Bell Atlantic’s asserted reasons for termination—insubordination—are insufficient to rebut Bryant’s prima facie case, as they are pretextual for the reasons stated above. *See* *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 143 (a plaintiff’s prima facie case, combined with sufficient evidence of pretext, may permit a jury to infer retaliation).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Bryant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
