**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to BNSF Railway Company on the retaliation claims of Rita Foster, Brian Kline, and Michael Snyder under the Federal Railroad Safety Act (FRSA), 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). The record establishes that each plaintiff engaged in protected activity by reporting unsafe conditions at the Creston bridge—specifically, the absence of handrails, lighting, and a walkway—and that BNSF subjected them to materially adverse actions in response. These actions, including disciplinary measures and increased surveillance, were sufficiently related in time and context to the protected conduct to support a reasonable inference of retaliatory motive. See *Barnes v. Gen. Motors Corp.*, 568 F.3d 677, 682 (8th Cir. 2009) (retaliation claim survives summary judgment where plaintiff shows protected activity, adverse action, and causal connection).

**I. The Plaintiffs Engaged in Protected Activity Under the FRSA**

The FRSA protects railroad employees who “report a violation of a safety regulation or a condition that the employee reasonably believes presents a substantial and immediate danger to the public or to employees.” 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). The Supreme Court has interpreted this provision broadly to include “any report of a safety hazard,” even if not formally documented. *Barnes v. United States*, 568 F.3d 1007, 1012 (8th Cir. 2009) (citing *Barnes v. Gen. Motors Corp.*, 568 F.3d 677, 682 (8th Cir. 2009)). Here, Kline and Snyder explicitly referenced the lack of handrails and lighting in their written statements to Trainmaster Dixon and Claims Representative Murphy. Snyder’s statement, dated April 13, 2012, noted that “the bridge had no handrails or lighting,” and Kline’s statement similarly described the “dangerous conditions” on the bridge. Foster’s recorded statement to Murphy on April 5, 2012, further confirmed that employees had previously reported the location as dangerous. These statements constitute clear reports of a condition that reasonably presented a substantial and immediate danger—particularly given that a crewmember had fallen to his death from the bridge. See *Barnes*, 568 F.3d at 1012 (reporting a hazardous condition that led to injury qualifies as protected activity).

BNSF’s argument that the reports were not “formal” or “written” is unavailing. The FRSA does not require a specific form of reporting. *Id.* at 1013. The statute protects “any report,” and the plaintiffs’ statements to company officials—made in the course of an accident investigation—were sufficient to trigger protection under the Act.

**II. BNSF’s Actions Were Adverse and Materially Harmful**

The plaintiffs suffered materially adverse actions following their reports. Foster was terminated, Kline received a thirty-day record suspension and a one-year review period, and Snyder received a formal reprimand and a one-year review period. These actions are clearly adverse under the FRSA. *See id.* at 1014 (holding that a reprimand and probationary period constitute adverse actions). Moreover, the district court acknowledged that the plaintiffs experienced “increased operations testing”—a practice where foremen observed employees without their knowledge—during the pendency of the investigation. Kline and Snyder testified that this surveillance caused them to avoid working with less experienced crewmates, to alter their work schedules, and to accept less lucrative assignments. These actions, while not formal discipline, constitute adverse employment actions under the FRSA because they “materially affect[ed] the terms, conditions, or privileges of employment.” *Id.* at 1015.

**III. A Causal Connection Exists Between the Protected Activity and the Adverse Actions**

The timing of BNSF’s actions strongly supports a causal connection. The investigation into the crew change began just days after the plaintiffs reported the unsafe conditions. The hearing was scheduled for April 11, 2012, but was repeatedly delayed—first to April 18, then to May 1, and finally to January 7–8, 2013. During this period, the plaintiffs were subjected to increased surveillance and work disruptions. The fact that the investigation was delayed for nearly nine months, while the plaintiffs were monitored and pressured to alter their work patterns, suggests a retaliatory intent. *See id.* at 1016 (delayed adverse action does not negate causation where timing and context support inference of retaliation).

Furthermore, the disciplinary findings were based on testimony from Knutstrom and Dixon—two individuals who had previously been involved in the investigation and who were later called as witnesses. The plaintiffs’ testimony directly contradicted theirs, and the fact that the final decision was made by Thompson, who reviewed the transcripts and exhibits, raises concerns about impartiality. The fact that the Public Law Board reversed the discipline—reinstating Foster, removing the reprimand from Snyder, and clearing Kline’s record—further undermines BNSF’s justification for the original discipline. *See id.* at 1017 (arbitration panel’s reversal of discipline supports inference of retaliatory motive).

**IV. BNSF’s Proffered Justifications Are Pretextual**

BNSF claims the discipline was based on rule violations, including failure to blow the whistle, improper headlight use, and fouling the tracks. But the record shows that these violations were not uniformly enforced. Other crewmembers involved in the same incident were not disciplined. Moreover, the video evidence reviewed by Knutstrom and Dixon was not conclusive, and the plaintiffs’ version of events—supported by their statements and the absence of written instructions—was credible. The fact that BNSF’s own investigation led to a finding of no rule violations by the Public Law Board, and that the Board reinstated Foster and cleared the others, demonstrates that BNSF’s justifications were pretextual. *See id.* at 1018.

**Conclusion**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. The plaintiffs engaged in protected activity, suffered materially adverse actions, and presented sufficient evidence of a causal connection. BNSF’s justifications are undermined by the timing, the selective enforcement, and the reversal by the Public Law Board. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
