Petitioners are eligible to adjust status because Congress deemed TPS recipients to be “in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4), and that legal fiction supplies the “inspected and admitted or paroled” predicate in 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). Statutory text, structure, and settled interpretive rules compel that conclusion. See United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486–87 (1868); United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486–87 (1868) (courts should not read statutes to produce absurd results); Holy Trinity Church v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459–60 (1892); United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940).

First, § 1254a(f)(4) does not merely confer a label. Congress said a TPS recipient “shall be considered” to be in lawful nonimmigrant status for purposes of adjustment of status. That formulation creates a statutory legal fiction, and courts must give such language its full intended effect. See Louisiana v. Ferris, 37 U.S. (12 Pet.) 212, 229 (1838); U.S. ex rel. Atty. Gen. v. Delaware & Hudson Co., 213 U.S. 366, 407–08 (1909); Mastro Plastics Corp. v. NLRB, 350 U.S. 270, 278 (1956). Once Congress directs that an alien “shall be considered” in lawful nonimmigrant status, the ordinary consequence is that the alien is treated as if lawfully admitted for the status-adjustment mechanism that turns on lawful presence, not physical entry. See Leocal v. Ashcroft, 543 U.S. 1, 12 (2004) (recognizing Congress’s use of defined immigration terms matters because courts must respect statutory terminology); but cf. Schwegmann Bros. v. Calvert Distillers Corp., 341 U.S. 384, 389–90 (1951) (courts may not add words Congress omitted).

Second, § 1255(a) and § 1254a(f)(4) must be read together, not in isolation. The INA is “to be construed as a whole.” United States v. Hartwell, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 385, 393 (1868); see also United States v. Bowen, 100 U.S. 508, 513 (1879). Congress expressly linked TPS to adjustment of status by providing that a TPS recipient is treated as a lawful nonimmigrant “for purposes of adjustment of status under section 1255 and change of status under section 1258.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). If Congress intended TPS to affect status for § 1258 but not to satisfy the threshold for § 1255, it would have said so. See Redwood v. Northern Pac. R. Co., 105 U.S. 214, 217–18 (1882); Nix v. Hedden, 149 U.S. 304, 306–07 (1893).

Third, the Government’s contrary reading collapses a carefully drawn distinction between unlawful entry and lawful status that § 1254a was designed to ameliorate. Petitioners entered without inspection years before TPS existed, but Congress later authorized TPS precisely to permit already-present nationals to remain and work lawfully. Reading § 1254a(f)(4) to confer lawful status yet deny its ordinary adjustment consequences defeats the statute’s remedial purpose. See Church of the Holy Trinity, 143 U.S. at 459–60; United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. at 486–87; United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. at 486–87. Courts have long refused constructions that “work[] injustice, oppression, or absurdity.” United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. at 486.

Fourth, the Government’s reading would create internal inconsistency in § 1255. Congress knew how to bar adjustment for specified classes and how to create exceptions. It did both elsewhere in § 1255. See United States v. Palmer, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 610, 631–32 (1818); McDonald v. Hovey, 110 U.S. 619, 628–29 (1884). The better reading is that TPS recipients, once deemed lawful nonimmigrants, satisfy the statutory gateway Congress supplied in § 1255(a). See Holy Trinity Church, 143 U.S. at 459–60; The Isabella, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 123, 135–36 (1866).

Finally, longstanding precedent requires that doubts in remedial statutes be resolved to effectuate congressional solicitude for the protected class. See United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. at 486–87; Holy Trinity Church, 143 U.S. at 459–60. TPS is a humanitarian protection statute, and § 1255 should not be construed to turn that protection into a trap for the very people Congress meant to help. The judgment below should be reversed.
