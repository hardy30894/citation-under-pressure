The Ninth Circuit erred in exempting documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA because they are "predominantly" for internal use and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard is inconsistent with the statutory language and Supreme Court precedent.

The FOIA exemptions are to be narrowly construed, as the Supreme Court held in EPA v. Mink, 410 U.S. 73, 79 (1973) is not available, however,  the Court in Rose stated that the "limited exemptions [under FOIA] do not obscure the basic policy that disclosure, not secrecy, is the dominant objective of the Act." Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976) (citing S.Rep. No. 813, 89th Cong., 1st Sess., 3 (1965)). The exemptions are "explicitly exclusive" (Adm'r, FAA v. Robertson, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)), and the agency bears the burden of proving that a particular exemption applies (Renegotiation Board v. Bannercraft Clothing Co., 415 U.S. 1, 19-20 (1974)).

Exemption 2, which the Ninth Circuit relied on, exempts from disclosure matters that are "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has interpreted this exemption narrowly, holding that it applies only to matters that are "trivial" or "not genuinely in the public interest" (Rose, 425 U.S. at 369). The Court in Rose also noted that the exemption was intended to cover "routine matters" such as "parking facilities, sick leave, lunch hours" (Rose, 425 U.S. at 363, quoting S.Rep. No. 813, 89th Cong., 1st Sess., 8 (1965)).

The Ninth Circuit's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard is not supported by the statutory language or Supreme Court precedent. In fact, the Court has cautioned against expansive interpretations of the exemptions (Renegotiation Board, 415 U.S. at 19-20; Robertson, 422 U.S. at 262). The "predominantly internal" standard is also inconsistent with the holding in Rose, which emphasized the need for a narrow construction of the exemptions (Rose, 425 U.S. at 361).

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's reliance on Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, which was decided after the relevant Supreme Court cases, is misplaced. Instead, the court should have looked to the language of the statute and the guidance provided by the Supreme Court in Rose and other cases. As the Court noted in Kent v. Dulles, 357 U.S. 116, 129 (1958), "the right to know is a 'fundamental right' that is 'essential to the proper functioning of our democracy'" (quoting United States v. Reynolds, 345 U.S. 1, 9-10 (1953)).

Additionally, the Court has held that exemptions from disclosure should be narrowly construed to effectuate the broad disclosure policy of the FOIA (Rose, 425 U.S. at 361-362; Robertson, 422 U.S. at 262). In United States v. Reynolds, the Court emphasized the importance of disclosure, stating that "fullest possible knowledge of the rules and practices" of government agencies is essential to the public interest (345 U.S. at 9-10).

The Ninth Circuit's decision also conflicts with the principle that exemptions are to be interpreted in light of the statutory purpose (Kent, 357 U.S. at 129). By exempting documents relating to the effects of explosions, the court has effectively allowed the Navy to withhold information that is not "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency" (5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2)).

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision to exempt documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA is inconsistent with the statutory language and Supreme Court precedent. The court should have narrowly construed Exemption 2 and required the Navy to disclose the requested documents. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
