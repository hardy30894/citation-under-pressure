**Argument**

A district court’s failure to give the notice of appeal required by Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32 does not itself entitle a defendant to habeas relief when the defendant already knew of the right to appeal and deliberately chose not to take one. Habeas corpus is a remedy for custody in violation of the Constitution or laws of the United States, not for every departure from a procedural rule. *Waley v. Johnston*, 316 U.S. 101, 104-05 (1942); *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). Where the asserted error is purely procedural and caused no prejudice, collateral relief is unavailable. *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947); *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428.

Rule 32’s appeal-notice requirement serves the practical purpose of ensuring that a defendant who may be unaware of appellate rights receives timely notice. But that purpose is satisfied when the defendant already knows he may appeal. A court’s omission cannot be converted into a basis for vacating a sentence absent some showing that the omission mattered to the defendant’s ability to seek review. This Court has repeatedly distinguished between jurisdictional or constitutional defects, which may warrant collateral relief, and technical nonprejudicial errors, which do not. *United States v. Johnson*, 327 U.S. 106, 112-13 (1946); *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 70-71 (1942). Habeas is not a device for correcting every failure to follow the rules of criminal procedure. *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428.

That principle controls here. The District Court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that Peguero knew of his right to appeal when sentence was imposed. Once that factual finding is accepted, the omission of formal advisement did not deprive him of any opportunity to appeal. The law does not require reversal for an error that has no injurious effect. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 760-65 (1946); *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86-87 (1963). On collateral review, the question is not whether the district court complied perfectly with a rule, but whether the petitioner suffered a miscarriage of justice or a fundamental defect resulting in a complete failure of the adversary process. *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428; *Sunal*, 332 U.S. at 178-79.

Peguero’s contrary position would transform Rule 32 into a per se habeas entitlement divorced from prejudice. That approach is inconsistent with the established understanding that procedural errors are subject to harmless-error principles. *Fahy*, 375 U.S. at 86-87; *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 764-65. It is also inconsistent with the principle that collateral review is not a substitute for appeal. *Sunal*, 332 U.S. at 178-79. A defendant who already knows his appellate rights and elects not to exercise them cannot later invoke habeas to obtain a second chance simply because the court failed to repeat what he already understood.

Nor does the absence of formal notice itself create a constitutional violation. The Constitution does not require any particular form of advisement where the defendant in fact knows of the right. *Bute v. Illinois*, 333 U.S. 640, 655-56 (1948). The relevant inquiry is whether the alleged omission impaired a protected right. *Johnson*, 327 U.S. at 112-13. Here, the District Court’s finding forecloses that claim.

In sum, the Court of Appeals correctly held that Peguero is not entitled to habeas relief. Rule 32 was designed to ensure defendants learn of appellate rights; it was not designed to furnish collateral relief to defendants who already knew those rights and chose not to appeal. Because the district court’s omission caused no prejudice and no constitutional or jurisdictional defect, the judgment should be affirmed. *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428; *Sunal*, 332 U.S. at 178-79; *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 760-65.
