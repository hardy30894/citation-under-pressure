# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 1981a's "Malice or Reckless Indifference" Standard Must Be Anchored to Conduct That Is Itself Egregious

Congress amended Title VII in 1991 to permit punitive damages "if the [plaintiff] demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Petitioner reads this clause as requiring proof of nothing more than a culpable mental state, wholly divorced from the character of the employer's conduct. That reading cannot be squared with the statute's history, the common-law backdrop against which Congress legislated, or this Court's punitive-damages jurisprudence.

This Court has long recognized that punitive damages are an "extraordinary remedy," reserved for conduct that is independently blameworthy, not simply a windfall attached to any finding of intentional discrimination. In *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), the Court approved a reckless-indifference standard for punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, but did so only after confirming that the standard tracked the common law's demand for "conduct involving some element of outrage similar to that usually found in crime." *Id.* at 48. Congress's use of parallel "malice or reckless indifference" language in § 1981a signals its intent to import that same common-law limitation, not to jettison it. See *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 251-52 (1994) (Congress modeled the 1991 Act's damages provisions on existing federal remedial schemes, including § 1981 and § 1983, whose punitive-damages standards had already been construed to require aggravated misconduct).

The legislative history respondent's opponents dismiss as immaterial in fact confirms this understanding. The House Report accompanying the 1991 Act stated that plaintiffs seeking punitive damages must satisfy "an even higher standard" than that required for liability itself. Senator Dole and his co-sponsors likewise explained that punitive damages would be available only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Interp. Mem. of Sen. Dole et al.). These statements are not idiosyncratic; they reflect a consistent congressional understanding that Title VII's compensatory scheme, capped and newly created, was not meant to convert every finding of intentional discrimination into a punitive-damages case.

## II. Requiring Independently Egregious Conduct Is Consistent With This Court's Due Process and Punitive-Damages Precedents

This Court's decisions in *Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Haslip*, 499 U.S. 1 (1991), and *BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559 (1996), confirm that punitive damages are constitutionally tolerable only when tethered to conduct of a particularly reprehensible character. In *Gore*, the Court identified the "degree of reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct" as the "most important" guidepost in assessing the propriety of a punitive award. 517 U.S. at 575. A standard that permits punitive damages based solely on an employer's state of mind—without regard to whether the underlying conduct was itself egregious—invites exactly the unmoored, unpredictable awards that *Haslip* and *Gore* caution against.

Nor is it enough to say, as petitioner does, that a jury's finding of intentional discrimination coupled with "malice" satisfies this concern. This Court has repeatedly distinguished the showing necessary to establish liability from the heightened showing necessary to justify punishment. In *Patterson v. McLean Credit Union*, 491 U.S. 164 (1989), the Court emphasized that Title VII's remedial structure, prior to the 1991 amendments, was calibrated to make victims whole, not to punish employers—a purpose Congress preserved even as it added capped damages remedies. The addition of punitive damages in § 1981a was a significant departure from that make-whole model, and Congress cabined it accordingly, both through statutory caps, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3), and through the egregiousness limitation reflected in the statute's history.

## III. The Circuit Courts' Experience Under Analogous Statutes Confirms the Need for an Egregiousness Requirement

Courts construing the closely related punitive-damages standard under 42 U.S.C. § 1981 have recognized that something more than the mens rea necessary for liability is required. The Seventh Circuit's decision in *Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co.*, 95 F.3d 627 (7th Cir. 1996), held that a punitive damages award under § 1981a itself must surmount a "higher hurdle" than mere proof of intentional discrimination. *Id.* at 636. And in *Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co.*, 817 F.2d 1290 (7th Cir. 1987), the court limited punitive damages to cases in which "the application of the law to the facts at hand was so clear at the time of the act that reasonably competent people would have agreed on its application." *Id.* at 1296. These decisions reflect the sound judgment that a defendant's mere awareness of a general prohibition against discrimination should not, standing alone, expose it to punishment; the conduct itself must reflect the kind of aggravated wrongdoing the common law has always demanded before allowing punitive damages.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should hold that an employer's conduct must be shown to be egregious, independent of a generalized finding of malice or reckless indifference, before a jury may award punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act.
