**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Rule Announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), Applies Retroactively to Cases on Collateral Review.**

The Supreme Court has long held that a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it is substantive or watershed in nature. *See Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351 (2004). In *Johnson*, the Court held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), was unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. This holding is a substantive rule because it alters the legal consequences of prior convictions by invalidating the residual clause. *See id.* at 2563 (Thomas, J., concurring) ("The Court today holds that the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague. That holding is substantive.").

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that *Johnson* does not apply retroactively—is erroneous. The court relied on *Dorsey v. United States*, 567 U.S. 260 (2012), to hold that *Johnson* announced a procedural rule. *See Welch v. United States*, 824 F.3d 1060, 1065 (11th Cir. 2016). However, *Dorsey* addressed a different statute and did not involve a rule that was substantive in nature. *Dorsey*, 567 U.S. at 268-69. The Supreme Court has repeatedly distinguished substantive rules from procedural ones, emphasizing that substantive rules alter the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes. *See Summerlin*, 542 U.S. at 353. *Johnson* fits squarely within this framework because it invalidates a statutory provision that expanded the scope of predicate offenses under the ACCA.

Moreover, the Court has recognized that rules invalidating vague criminal statutes are substantive because they "alter the meaning and substantive reach of the law." *See id.* at 353-54. *Johnson* falls within this category, as it struck down the residual clause for vagueness, thereby narrowing the class of predicate offenses that can trigger enhanced penalties under the ACCA. *See Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2563 (Thomas, J., concurring). Thus, *Johnson* applies retroactively to cases on collateral review, including Mr. Welch’s.

**II. Mr. Welch’s Florida Robbery Conviction Does Not Qualify as a Violent Felony Under the ACCA.**

Even if the residual clause of the ACCA were constitutional, Mr. Welch’s Florida robbery conviction does not qualify as a violent felony under the statute. The ACCA defines a "violent felony" to include offenses that "have as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another." 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(i). Florida’s robbery statute, however, criminalizes conduct that does not necessarily involve the use of physical force. *See* Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1) (1996) (defining robbery as the taking of property "with intent to deprive" another "when in the course of the taking there is the use of force, violence, assault, or putting in fear").

The Supreme Court has made clear that the categorical approach requires courts to look to the elements of the statute of conviction, not the underlying facts. *See Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 602 (1990). Under Florida law at the time of Mr. Welch’s conviction, mere snatching—such as grabbing an item from a person’s hand—could constitute robbery if the victim was put in fear. *See, e.g., Andre v. State*, 431 So. 2d 1042, 1043 (Fla. 5th Dist. Ct. App. 1983). This conduct does not necessarily involve the use of physical force, as required by the ACCA. *See Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2561 (holding that the residual clause was unconstitutionally vague because it encompassed crimes that did not involve violent conduct).

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that Florida robbery categorically qualifies as a violent felony—is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s precedents. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the ACCA’s elements clause requires a categorical match between the elements of the predicate offense and the use of physical force. *See Leocal v. Ashcroft*, 543 U.S. 1, 11 (2004) ("The phrase ‘use of physical force’ means violent force—that is, force capable of causing physical pain or injury to another person."). Florida’s robbery statute, as applied in cases like Mr. Welch’s, does not meet this standard. Therefore, his conviction does not qualify as a predicate offense under the ACCA.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson* applies retroactively to Mr. Welch’s case, and his Florida robbery conviction does not qualify as a violent felony under the ACCA. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed.
