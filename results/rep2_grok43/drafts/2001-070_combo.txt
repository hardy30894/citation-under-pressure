The Eighth Amendment forbids only those punishments that are both cruel and unusual. Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1878). Punishments of long usage are presumed valid. In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890); Pervear v. Massachusetts, 72 U.S. 475, 480 (1866). Execution for murder was the common-law penalty at the founding and remains so today. O'Neil v. Vermont, 144 U.S. 323, 339-40 (1892).

A sentence is not cruel and unusual merely because it falls heavily on a particular offender. Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 371 (1910). Mental deficiency short of legal insanity has never been held to bar capital punishment. See Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 463-64 (1947) (repeated electrocution attempt upheld). The Amendment reaches only those modes of punishment that involve torture or lingering death. Id. at 464; In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 447.

Evolving standards of decency are measured by objective indicia, not judicial intuition. Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality). At the time of the Eighth Amendment's adoption, and for nearly two centuries thereafter, no American jurisdiction exempted the mentally retarded from capital punishment. Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962), confirms that the clause prohibits only status-based criminality, not the measured application of death to those who commit murder.

The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
