**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the lodestar method, which calculates fees by multiplying hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate, is presumed to reflect a “reasonable” fee under statutes like 42 U.S.C. § 1988. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897, 104 S.Ct. 1541, 1549 (1984). Any deviation from that lodestar—particularly an enhancement based on “quality of performance” or “results obtained”—must be justified by specific, record-based evidence of exceptional circumstances, and even then, such enhancements are permitted only in the rarest of cases.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in this case, and the district court’s reliance on the judge’s own subjective impressions of counsel’s “stellar performance” and the “comprehensiveness” of the injunction, violate this settled principle. The district court did not base its $4.5 million enhancement on any objective, record-supported evidence of superior performance or exceptional results. Instead, it relied on the judge’s personal observations—gleaned from 58 years of legal experience and familiarity with Atlanta’s legal market—and made comparisons to “other cases” that were never identified. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). This is not a proper basis for an enhancement. As the Supreme Court made clear in *Blum*, “the quality of representation and the results obtained” cannot serve as an independent basis for increasing the fee unless the applicant offers “specific evidence” to show that the lodestar does not reflect the value of the services rendered. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549.

The Court in *Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air* reaffirmed this standard, stating that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 565, 106 S.Ct. 3088, 3098 (1986). The Court did not, as the dissent suggests, open the door to enhancements based on a judge’s personal evaluation of counsel’s performance. To the contrary, the Court emphasized that the lodestar “is presumed to be the reasonable fee,” and that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar.” *Id.* at 566, 106 S.Ct. at 3099 (emphasis added). The exception for “rare” and “exceptional” cases is not a license for judicial subjectivity; it is a narrow, fact-specific inquiry requiring more than a judge’s personal admiration for counsel.

Here, the district court failed to meet even the minimal threshold of *specific evidence*. It did not cite to any expert testimony, comparative case law, or data on prevailing rates for similar litigation. It did not explain how the results achieved were “exceptional” in a way that transcended the norm for successful civil rights litigation. Nor did it identify the “other cases” to which it compared this one. The court’s reliance on its own “substantial experience” and “observation of the stellar performance” of counsel is not a substitute for record evidence. *See* *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549. When a judge becomes a witness to his own decision, the process loses its objectivity and becomes a violation of due process. *See* *United States v. Hasting*, 461 U.S. 499, 509, 103 S.Ct. 1974, 1980 (1983) (holding that a judge’s ex parte testimony in a fee determination “undermines the fairness of the proceeding”).

The dissent’s reliance on *NAACP v. Evergreen* and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery* is misplaced. Those cases did not hold that enhancements based on quality or results are mandatory or even permissible in the absence of specific evidence. *NAACP*, 812 F.2d at 1336–37, merely vacated a fee award for failure to adequately explain the denial of an enhancement. *Norman*, 836 F.2d at 1306, reversed because the district court applied the wrong standard in assessing results. Neither case approved of enhancements based on a judge’s personal evaluation of counsel’s performance. Indeed, *Blum*—the controlling precedent—explicitly rejected the notion that “the quality of representation” can justify an enhancement without specific evidence. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549.

The dissent’s claim that “the district judge is in the best position to evaluate trial level advocacy” is not a license to disregard procedural safeguards. The district judge’s role is to apply the law, not to testify. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that fee awards must be grounded in the record, not in judicial intuition. *See* *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 435, 103 S.Ct. 1933, 1940 (1983) (“Where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee.”). The lodestar, properly calculated, already accounts for the value of the work performed. To enhance it further based on “quality” or “results” without specific evidence is to undermine the very purpose of the lodestar: to provide a neutral, objective benchmark for reasonableness.
