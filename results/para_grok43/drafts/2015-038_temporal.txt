The Property Clause empowers Congress to “make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States.” U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2. That authority permits the United States to protect its own lands by regulating conduct on adjacent nonfederal property when the regulation is necessary to prevent injury to the federal holding. Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 525 (1897) (“The government has, with respect to its own lands, the rights of an ordinary proprietor, to maintain its possession and to prosecute trespassers.”). The same power extends to navigable waters within federal reservations. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 197 (1824).

States entering the Union acquire title to submerged lands only “subject to the paramount right of the United States to control the same for the purposes of navigation and commerce.” Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230 (1845); see also Martin v. Waddell, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 367, 410 (1842). Consequently, state ownership of the bed of a river lying inside a national preserve does not divest the United States of its authority to adopt rules that safeguard the preserve from harm originating on or in the river. United States v. Gratiot, 39 U.S. (14 Pet.) 526, 537 (1840).

Congress may therefore authorize an agency to promulgate regulations applicable throughout the exterior boundaries of a federal reservation, even though some parcels within those boundaries remain in nonfederal hands. Light v. United States, 220 U.S. 523, 536-37 (1911); Utah Power & Light Co. v. United States, 243 U.S. 389, 404 (1917). Such regulations rest on the United States’ proprietary interest in the reserved lands and on its constitutional power to make rules for their protection; they do not depend upon fee title to every acre or every submerged acre within the reservation.

Nothing in the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 alters this settled constitutional framework. The statute creates conservation system units and defines their boundaries, but it does not purport to surrender the federal government’s pre-existing authority to regulate activities that threaten the units’ values. Because the hovercraft prohibition is a valid exercise of that authority, it remains enforceable on the Nation River within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve.
