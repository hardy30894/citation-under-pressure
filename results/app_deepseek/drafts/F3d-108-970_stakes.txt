## ARGUMENT

### I. The evidence was insufficient to support a finding of sex discrimination.

Feltmann failed to establish a prima facie case of discrimination because she was not meeting her employer’s legitimate expectations. *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). Rich placed Feltmann on probation in March 1993 after her sales profits fell to the lowest of all consultants for four consecutive months. Her male co-worker, Rick Beutel, was also placed on probation for the same reason. A plaintiff who receives the same adverse treatment as a male comparator cannot show that she was treated differently because of her sex. *Texas Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 258 (1981).

Even if Feltmann could establish a prima facie case, Sieben articulated legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for each challenged action. Rich’s memo regarding her work ethic identified specific performance deficiencies: excessive guest drives, failure to explain vehicle features, and lack of teamwork. Her low gross profit per vehicle—a metric that directly affected dealership profitability—justified both the probation and the restriction on demo-car use. No male consultant received a memo about demo use because no male consultant was found to have violated the plainly stated geographic restriction. Feltmann admitted she continued to drive her demo 300 miles round-trip to Illinois *after* receiving the memo.

Feltmann failed to show that these reasons were pretextual. *St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993). She offered no evidence that Rich’s criticisms were false or that similarly situated male consultants with identical performance deficiencies were treated more favorably. The fact that she was not invited to the Chicago sales competition—a prize awarded to the two top-selling consultants, both of whom declined—does not establish discrimination. Sieben did not invite any other consultant either; the opportunity was offered to the top performers, regardless of sex.

### II. The retaliation claim fails for lack of a causal connection.

Feltmann’s retaliation claim is based on her November 1992 complaint about a vulgar remark made by Gordon Anzalone, an employee at a different Sieben franchise. To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, Feltmann must show that her protected activity was a “but-for” cause of the adverse employment action. *Burlington Indus., Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742, 765 (1998). Here, the only adverse action Feltmann identifies—her termination—occurred months after her complaint. The temporal proximity is too attenuated to support an inference of causation. *Clark Cnty. Sch. Dist. v. Breeden*, 532 U.S. 268, 273 (2001) (holding that a 20-month gap between complaint and adverse action was insufficient).

Moreover, Sieben responded promptly and appropriately to Feltmann’s complaint: the owners met with her, asked if she wanted Anzalone fired, reprimanded him, and warned that any future incident would result in termination. This remedial action—not retaliation—was the company’s response. Feltmann never reported any further incidents. The subsequent performance issues (low profits, probation, demo misuse) were unrelated to her complaint and were documented by Rich before any alleged retaliation could have occurred. Because the employer’s legitimate, non-retaliatory reasons for the adverse action are undisputed, the judgment cannot stand. *Hicks*, 509 U.S. at 511.

### III. The district court erred in its evidentiary rulings and jury instructions.

The court admitted evidence of Anzalone’s vulgar comment—a statement made by a non-supervisory employee at a different franchise—without a showing that Sieben ratified or knew of the remark before Feltmann’s complaint. Such evidence is irrelevant to whether Sieben’s subsequent actions were motivated by sex. *Meritor Sav. Bank v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 69 (1986). The court also failed to instruct the jury that a plaintiff must show that the employer’s stated reason is not only false but that discrimination was the real reason. *Hicks*, 509 U.S. at 515. These errors, individually and cumulatively, denied Sieben a fair trial.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment and direct entry of judgment in favor of Sieben.
