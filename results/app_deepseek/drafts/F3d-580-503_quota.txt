## Argument

The district court abused its discretion by denying Winbush’s motion for a fingerprint expert and his related motion to continue, thereby violating his Fifth and Sixth Amendment rights to present a meaningful defense. These errors were not harmless and require reversal.

### I. The Denial of a Fingerprint Expert Deprived Winbush of Due Process and a Fair Trial

Under *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 83 (1985), a defendant is entitled to the assistance of an expert when the State makes a “threshold showing” that the expert’s testimony is “likely to be a significant factor” at trial. Here, the government planned to call two experts—a fingerprint examiner and a deputy commander—to testify that no latent fingerprints were found on the evidence and to explain *why* prints are often absent. This testimony, if unchallenged, allowed the jury to infer that the absence of fingerprints was legally insignificant. Winbush needed his own expert to rebut this inference and to explain that the lack of prints could actually *support* his defense that he never possessed the gun or the Kentucky crack.

The magistrate judge denied the motion solely because it was filed “woefully late”—five days before trial in a case pending two years. While timing is a factor, the court must weigh it against the defendant’s constitutional right to a fair trial. *United States v. Goodlow*, 500 F.3d 636, 641 (7th Cir. 2007) (reversing conviction where late-filed motion for expert was denied without adequate consideration of prejudice). The government had served its expert notice over a month earlier, and Winbush had only recently learned that the government’s experts would go beyond the “no prints” conclusion to explain *why* prints are often absent. The late filing was not dilatory but stemmed from the evolving scope of the government’s expert testimony. Moreover, Winbush offered to stipulate to the government’s fingerprint examiner, showing his good faith.

When an indigent defendant makes a timely request for expert assistance that is “necessary for an adequate defense,” the court must grant it. *United States v. Hall*, 93 F.3d 1337, 1342 (7th Cir. 1996) (citing *Ake*). Here, the government’s expert testimony on the *difficulty* of obtaining fingerprints directly undercut the only evidence that could exculpate Winbush—namely, the absence of his prints on the gun and drug bag. Without an expert to cross-examine or present alternative explanations, Winbush was left defenseless on a critical issue. This error was not harmless. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967) (error is harmless only if the government proves beyond a reasonable doubt that it did not contribute to the verdict). The jury could have credibly doubted the government’s narrative if an expert testified that the absence of prints was *more* consistent with Winbush’s version of events. The denial therefore violated *Ake* and requires reversal.

### II. The Denial of a Continuance Was an Abuse of Discretion

A continuance should be granted when the defendant shows “good cause” and that the denial results in “actual prejudice.” *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964). Here, Winbush needed additional time to locate, retain, and prepare a fingerprint expert. The government had only two experts; Winbush had none. The district court’s refusal to grant even a brief continuance—balanced against the case’s age and Winbush’s diligence—was an abuse of discretion. *United States v. Sargent*, 448 F.3d 954, 958 (7th Cir. 2006) (trial court must consider “the reasons for the continuance, the prejudice to the defendant, and the inconvenience to the court and the government”).

Winbush filed his motion five days before trial, but the government filed its expert notice only *one month* earlier. The “last-minute” nature was not his fault; he acted promptly after learning the full scope of the government’s expert testimony. The case had been pending for two years, but much of that delay was due to pretrial motions and continuances sought by both sides. The government would not have been prejudiced by a short postponement, and the court’s docket could have accommodated it. *United States v. King*, 627 F.3d 641, 647 (7th Cir. 2010) (abuse of discretion to deny continuance where defendant had no opportunity to prepare rebuttal expert testimony). The denial forced Winbush to trial without any expert to counter the government’s fingerprint narrative, resulting in actual prejudice.

### III. The Errors Were Not Harmless

When a trial court erroneously denies a defendant’s request for an expert or a continuance, the conviction must be reversed unless the government shows harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. *Chapman*, 386 U.S. at 24. Here, the fingerprint evidence was central to the case. The government’s experts testified that the absence of prints was expected, effectively neutralizing Winbush’s argument that he never handled the gun or drugs. With his own expert, Winbush could have shown that the absence of prints could *only* be explained by someone else handling the evidence, supporting his claim of non-possession. The jury’s verdict thus rests on a constitutional violation. *United States v. Jones*, 224 F.3d 621, 626 (7th Cir. 2000) (reversing where denial of expert “undermined confidence in the outcome”).


For these reasons, this Court should reverse Winbush’s convictions and remand for a new trial.
