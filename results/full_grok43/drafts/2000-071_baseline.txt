**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford’s exclusion of the Club’s proposed meetings was permissible subject-matter discrimination in a limited public forum.**

A school district may “reserve its forum for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics,” *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995), provided the distinctions drawn are “reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum” and viewpoint neutral, *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Milford’s Community Use Policy opens school facilities only for “social, civic and recreational meetings” and expressly excludes “religious purposes.” The record establishes that the Club’s meetings consist of prayer, memorization of scripture, Bible stories, and an explicit invitation to accept Jesus Christ as personal Savior. These activities constitute religious instruction and worship, not the discussion of morals or character from a religious perspective.

The Second Circuit correctly held that “the subject matter of the Club’s was ‘quintessentially religious’” and therefore fell outside the limited forum Milford had created. *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000). Because the exclusion turned on the nature of the proposed speech rather than hostility to its religious perspective, it was constitutional subject-matter discrimination. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (distinguishing between permissible subject-matter limits and impermissible viewpoint discrimination).

**II. Any free-speech violation would be justified by the Establishment Clause.**

Even if the exclusion constituted viewpoint discrimination, Milford’s interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation supplies a compelling justification. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 271 (1981) (recognizing that an interest in complying with the Establishment Clause “may be characterized as compelling”). Permitting the Club’s meetings would place Milford in the position of “lend[ing] direct support to” religious worship conducted on school property immediately after the school day, *see Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 844, and would convey to “impressionable youngsters” the message that the school endorses the Club’s religious message, *see Board of Educ. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 250 (1990) (plurality opinion). Milford therefore could lawfully exclude the Club without violating the First Amendment.
