ARGUMENT

Clemency is an act of executive grace that creates no enforceable right. United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833) (“A pardon is an act of grace, proceeding from the power intrusted with the execution of the laws.”). The Constitution vests this power exclusively in the executive; courts have never subjected its exercise to judicial superintendence. Ex parte Wells, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 307, 310 (1855); Ex parte Garland, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866). Because the decision to grant or withhold clemency lies outside the judicial process, it cannot generate a life or liberty interest protected by the Due Process Clause. Murray’s Lessee v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 272, 276 (1856) (due process applies only to “judicial” deprivations).

The same principle precludes any claim that clemency procedures must satisfy constitutional minima. Hurtado v. California, 110 U.S. 516, 535 (1884) (due process does not require “any particular form” of proceeding when the matter is one of executive discretion). Historical practice confirms that pardons and commutations have always been administered without the incidents of trial. Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 110 (1908).

The Fifth Amendment claim fares no better. The interview is offered only if the inmate requests it; participation is therefore voluntary. Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 597 (1896) (“The constitutional privilege is not violated by a statute which merely affords a person an opportunity to testify.”). No compulsion arises from the mere existence of an option to speak. Counselman v. Hitchcock, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892) (privilege protects only against “compelled” testimony). An inmate who elects to appear remains free to decline any question; the regulation imposes no penalty for silence. Burdick v. United States, 236 U.S. 79, 94 (1915) (waiver of the privilege must be knowing and voluntary; mere presence does not constitute waiver).

Because the clemency interview rests on the inmate’s consent and carries no legal compulsion, it cannot be said to exact an unconstitutional condition. The lower court’s remand for further proceedings on these issues therefore rests on a premise the Constitution does not support. The judgment should be reversed.
