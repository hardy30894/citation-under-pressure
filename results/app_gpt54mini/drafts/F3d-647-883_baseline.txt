**ARGUMENT**

### I. Buckles’s § 2255 motion was timely because the Ninth Circuit’s June 12, 2007 order recalling the mandate and denying appointed certiorari counsel reset the time to seek Supreme Court review.

A conviction becomes “final” for § 2255 purposes when the time for filing a petition for certiorari expires. *Clay v. United States*, 537 U.S. 522, 532 (2003). Buckles’s petition was timely only if the Ninth Circuit’s June 12, 2007 order recalling the mandate restarted the certiorari clock. It did.

Supreme Court Rule 13 provides that the 90-day period runs from the “date of the denial of rehearing” when a lower court “appropriately entertains an untimely petition for rehearing.” Sup. Ct. R. 13(3). Buckles’s request for appointed certiorari counsel was filed within the Ninth Circuit’s own 21-day window under Rule 4-1(e), and the court expressly considered it on the merits by construing and denying the motion. Under Rule 13(3), when the lower court entertains a postjudgment filing of that kind, the certiorari period runs from the later denial, not the original judgment date. See *Flynn v. U.S. ex rel. Kujawa*, 619 F.2d 441, 443 (5th Cir. 1980) (recognizing that a later lower-court ruling can affect finality for certiorari purposes where the court “entertains” the postjudgment filing).

The Supreme Court’s certiorari rules are applied by the date of the operative lower-court action, not by labels attached to a filing. See *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 98-99 (2004). Here, the Ninth Circuit did more than ministerially docket Buckles’s request: it recalled the mandate and issued an order denying the motion. That action reopened the period for Supreme Court review. Buckles filed his certiorari petition on September 5, 2007, within 90 days of the June 20, 2007 reissuance of the mandate and well within 90 days of the June 12 denial. His certiorari petition was therefore timely, and so was his § 2255 motion. See 28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)(1); *Clay*, 537 U.S. at 532.

At minimum, the Ninth Circuit’s handling of the motion created a reasonable, court-induced basis for believing the certiorari clock had been restarted. Where a court’s own postjudgment action reasonably affects a litigant’s time to seek further review, the limitations period should not be measured in a way that punishes the litigant for relying on the court’s process. See *Houston v. Lack*, 487 U.S. 266, 270-72 (1988). Buckles’s petition and § 2255 motion should have been deemed timely.
