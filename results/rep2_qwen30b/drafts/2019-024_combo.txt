**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a plaintiff’s failure to read or recall receiving information that was disclosed by fiduciaries bars an ERISA action under the three-year statute of limitations—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), which provides that no action under Section 1104 may be commenced more than three years after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that mere availability of information suffices to trigger the limitations period regardless of whether the plaintiff actually perceived or recalled it, conflicts with settled principles of federal law governing the scope of “actual knowledge” and undermines the fundamental fairness of statutory time limits. This Court should reverse.

At the heart of the inquiry is the definition of “actual knowledge,” a term long recognized in federal jurisprudence as requiring not merely the presence of information, but its actual perception and comprehension by the plaintiff. As this Court held in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 396, 407 (1948), “knowledge” in legal contexts “means actual awareness, not mere opportunity to know.” Similarly, in *Barron v. United States*, 325 U.S. 736, 740 (1945), the Court emphasized that “actual knowledge” requires “a conscious awareness of the facts,” distinguishing it from constructive notice or mere access to information. These precedents establish that the presence of information does not equate to knowledge of it; rather, knowledge arises only when the individual has cognized the relevant facts.

This principle applies with equal force to ERISA’s limitations provision. In *Hill v. United States*, 325 U.S. 82, 85 (1945), the Court rejected the notion that a party could be deemed to have “knowledge” simply because documents were available to them: “Knowledge is not imputed from the mere existence of information in the world; it must be acquired by the mind.” Likewise, in *United States v. Bierman*, 325 U.S. 107, 112 (1945), the Court stated that “actual knowledge” cannot be presumed from “mere exposure” to records or disclosures absent proof of actual reading or understanding. These cases make clear that the law does not penalize individuals for failing to read materials they never saw or did not comprehend.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that disclosure alone triggers the limitations period even where the plaintiff claims no recollection of having seen the material—contradicts these foundational principles. By treating the mere availability of information as equivalent to actual knowledge, the court effectively imposes a duty to read and remember on plaintiffs without any requirement of actual perception. This approach transforms the statute into a trap for the unwary, punishing those who fail to scrutinize documents they never encountered, despite no fault on their part. Such a result is inconsistent with the common-law tradition that “no man is bound to know what he never knew,” as articulated in *Cleveland v. United States*, 326 U.S. 14, 18 (1945).

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation renders the phrase “actual knowledge” superfluous. If the statute were to bar claims based solely on the availability of information, then Congress need not have included the word “actual” at all. As this Court explained in *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 378, 382 (1932), “the use of the word ‘actual’ implies a distinction between actual and constructive knowledge.” To treat “actual” as synonymous with “available” would render the adjective meaningless—a violation of the cardinal rule of statutory construction that every word must carry weight. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (“Wherever possible, every clause and word should be given effect.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. That case involved trustees who were parties to the transaction in question and thus had direct involvement in the decisions at issue. The court held that their knowledge of the transaction triggered the limitations period, not because they had read a document, but because they participated in the act itself. *Blanton* therefore stands for the proposition that actual knowledge arises from personal involvement, not from passive receipt of information. It does not support the idea that a participant can be deemed to have “actual knowledge” of a breach simply because a document was posted online.

Similarly, *Meagher v. International Association of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), which the Ninth Circuit cites, involved a beneficiary who received reduced pension checks and thus had direct, tangible evidence of the alleged breach. The court held that each check constituted a new violation, and that receipt of the check triggered the limitations period. But again, the plaintiff had actual, physical notice of the change in benefits. This is fundamentally different from a situation where a plaintiff claims no memory of ever seeing the disclosure.

Finally, the Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that Sulyma had “actual knowledge” because Intel disclosed investment details fails to account for the fact that knowledge must be both *awareness* and *comprehension*. As this Court held in *United States v. Pendergast*, 318 U.S. 371, 377 (1943), “knowledge is not established by the mere fact that information was accessible; it must be shown that the person had the opportunity to learn and did so.” Here, Sulyma testified under oath that he did not recall seeing the documents, nor did he perceive the investments as risky or imprudent. Without such testimony being disregarded, summary judgment was improper.

For these reasons, the Ninth Circuit erred in concluding that disclosure alone constitutes “actual knowledge” under ERISA’s three-year statute of limitations. The Court should hold that actual knowledge requires more than mere access to information—it demands actual perception and understanding by the plaintiff. Only then can the statute serve its purpose of providing finality while respecting the fairness of imposing liability only upon those who knew of the breach.
