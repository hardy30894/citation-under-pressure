**ARGUMENT**

I. **Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision does not violate the ADEA because state judges are “appointee[s] on the policymaking level.”**

The ADEA’s definition of “employee” excludes “any person elected to public office in any State,” as well as “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). In *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, the Eighth Circuit correctly held that appointed Missouri judges fall within that exception. 898 F.2d 598, 601–06 (8th Cir. 1990). The court reasoned that judging is not merely mechanical application of settled law; judges necessarily resolve ambiguities, fill gaps in authority, and thereby make law in a way that is properly characterized as policymaking. *Id.* at 603–05. That conclusion is consistent with the First Circuit’s analysis in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), and with the statutory structure Congress adopted in 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). See also *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 680 F. Supp. 455, 462 (D. Mass. 1988).

The statutory text should be read in light of federalism principles. Where Congress seeks to intrude into a “traditionally sensitive area,” it must make its intent clear. *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). State regulation of judicial tenure is such an area. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 601–02. Congress did not clearly express an intent to subject state judges to the ADEA, and the better reading of § 630(f) is that appointed judges are excluded because they occupy policymaking positions. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55; *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 432–35, 517 N.E.2d 141, 144–46 (1988); *In re Stout*, 521 Pa. 571, 559 A.2d 489 (1989).

The legislative history confirms that Congress intended the exemption to cover “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments.” 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180. State judges fit comfortably within that description. As the Eighth Circuit observed, judges exercise discretion of the same kind Congress associated with policymaking appointees, and their decisions may establish rules that guide future litigants, lawyers, and courts. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 603–05. Missouri judges also exercise substantial supervisory and administrative authority over the judicial system. *Id.* at 604–05; see *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc); *In re Rules of Circuit Court of Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 757 S.W.2d 590 (Mo. 1988) (en banc). Accordingly, the ADEA does not invalidate Missouri’s retirement provision.

II. **Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause because age is not a suspect classification and the rule is rationally related to legitimate state interests.**

Age-based classifications are reviewed under rational-basis scrutiny, not heightened scrutiny. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313–14 (1976) (per curiam). Under that deferential standard, a law “must be upheld against equal protection challenge if there is any reasonably conceivable state of facts that could provide a rational basis.” *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993). Missouri’s mandatory retirement rule easily satisfies that test.

In *Gregory*, the Eighth Circuit held that the State had a rational basis for requiring judges to retire at age seventy. 898 F.2d at 606–08. The court relied on several legitimate objectives: avoiding the difficult and potentially embarrassing process of individualized competence evaluations for aging judges; reducing the risk that age-related decline will impair performance; promoting the efficient administration of justice by minimizing delays caused by death or disability; and maintaining public confidence in the judiciary. *Id.* at 606–08. These are precisely the kinds of judgments that belong to state legislatures and state constitutions, not federal courts. See *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 314–15.

Other courts have sustained mandatory retirement provisions for judges on the same basis. *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979); *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980); *Apkin*, 401 Mass. at 434–35, 517 N.E.2d at 145–46. Missouri’s rule also advances the State’s interest in preserving a judiciary that remains responsive to contemporary understandings and broadly representative of the citizenry. *Apkin*, 401 Mass. at 435, 517 N.E.2d at 146. Even if reasonable minds disagree with those policy judgments, disagreement does not establish irrationality. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 607–08.

Because Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision is rationally related to legitimate state purposes, it does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 314–17; *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 606–08.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment should be affirmed.
