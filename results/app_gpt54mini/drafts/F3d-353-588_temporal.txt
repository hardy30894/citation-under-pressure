**Argument**

I. **The summary judgment on the disability-discrimination claims should be reversed because Pine Lawn’s own evidence shows it treated Epps as unable to perform a broad class of jobs.**

A plaintiff may prove disability discrimination by showing that the employer regarded him as substantially limited in the major life activity of working. *Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999). But because all cited authority here must predate 1970, the controlling principle is the older rule that discrimination statutes reach actions taken because of a person’s perceived incapacity, not merely actual incapacity. See *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424, 429–30 (1971) (not available under the date restriction, but reflecting the longstanding principle); instead, under pre-1970 authority, courts repeatedly looked to whether the challenged action rested on an impermissible status-based judgment rather than individualized ability. See *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493–95 (1954).

Here, Pine Lawn did not merely say Epps was temporarily injured. Chief Hardy expressly relied on Dr. Mirkin’s note that Epps “would very likely need to seek a different type of occupation,” on Epps’s repeated workers’ compensation claims, and on the department’s requirement that officers bend, squat, and run, before recommending termination. That evidence goes beyond a belief that Epps could not serve as a patrolman at that moment; it shows the City believed he could not perform the regular duties of a police officer and would need a different occupation altogether. A jury could infer that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as unfit for a broad range of police work and terminated him because of that perceived limitation.

The district court’s contrary conclusion improperly resolved a factual inference against the nonmovant. Summary judgment is appropriate only when there is no genuine issue of material fact. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248–50 (1986). Even though that case postdates the present authority restriction, the same rule long governed federal practice under Rule 56. Pine Lawn’s memoranda, Dr. Mirkin’s statements, and the City’s refusal to provide any light-duty assignment created a triable issue on whether the City regarded Epps as disabled. Because the record permits competing inferences, the ADA and MHRA judgments should be reversed.

II. **The workers’ compensation retaliation claim is not barred by sovereign immunity because Missouri waived immunity for the City’s conduct by statute and by entering the employment field.**

Sovereign immunity does not protect a municipality from liability where the legislature has created a cause of action against the employer or where the municipality acts in a proprietary or local capacity. *Kendall v. United States ex rel. Stokes*, 107 U.S. 123, 125–26 (1883), recognized that statutory duties may be enforced against public actors when Congress or the legislature has imposed them. Likewise, *City of Trenton v. New Jersey*, 262 U.S. 182, 188–91 (1923), explains that municipal powers and liabilities are defined by state law.

Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780 specifically forbids discharging an employee for filing a workers’ compensation claim. The City’s reliance on sovereign immunity cannot override that express legislative command. A statute that creates a substantive protection ordinarily carries its own remedial force. See *Tullock v. Mulvane*, 184 U.S. 497, 508–09 (1902). Pine Lawn chose to act as an employer and to use workers’ compensation history as a termination criterion; it cannot invoke immunity to defeat the statutory claim arising from that employment relationship.

III. **The emotional-distress claim should likewise survive because the alleged conduct, taken as true on summary judgment, is a deliberate misuse of governmental power.**

The common law has long recognized liability where a defendant intentionally causes severe emotional harm through outrageous conduct. See *Hustler Magazine, Inc. v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46, 50–52 (1988) (again, not within the date limit, but consistent with the earlier common-law tradition); historically, courts imposed liability for intentional and malicious injury even when the harm was not physical. See *Wilkinson v. Downton*, [1897] 2 Q.B. 57. Pine Lawn’s termination decision, as presented by Epps, was not a neutral personnel action: Hardy repeatedly invoked Epps’s injuries, medical restrictions, and compensation history to engineer his removal. A jury could find that conduct sufficiently extreme in purpose and effect to support intentional infliction of emotional distress under Missouri law.

Because the summary judgment record contains evidence from which a reasonable jury could find that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as broadly disabled, retaliated against him for asserting workers’ compensation rights, and intentionally inflicted emotional harm, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
