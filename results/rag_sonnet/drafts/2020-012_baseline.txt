# ARGUMENT

## THE BOARD'S DENIAL OF SALINAS'S REQUEST TO REOPEN HIS 2006 CLAIM IS A "FINAL DECISION" SUBJECT TO JUDICIAL REVIEW UNDER 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)

### A. The Text of Section 355(f) Compels Review of Reopening Denials

Section 355(f) grants circuit courts jurisdiction to review "any final decision of the Board." The Board's order denying Salinas's request to reopen his 2006 application is unquestionably a "decision"—it resolved a discrete request, following a hearing, and disposed of Salinas's claim with finality. The Board itself treats such orders as final for purposes of triggering the four-year reopening window under 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, and it afforded Salinas a hearing before a hearing officer, the very hallmark this Court has identified as separating agency action into distinct final decisions.

Nothing in the statutory text carves out an exception for decisions declining to reopen. The Fifth Circuit's contrary holding in *Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003)—which the panel below followed only because it was "not at liberty" to depart from circuit precedent—rested entirely on analogy to the Social Security Act rather than on the distinct text and structure of the Railroad Retirement Act.

### B. *Califano v. Sanders* Does Not Control Because the RRA's Structure Differs From the Social Security Act's

The courts that have denied review, including the panel below, rely on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), which held that the Social Security Act does not permit judicial review of refusals to reopen. But *Sanders* was expressly grounded in features of the Social Security scheme that are absent here. This Court explained that Social Security's reopening procedure is a creature purely of regulation, not statute: "the opportunity to reopen final decisions and any hearing convened to determine the propriety of such action are afforded by the Secretary's regulations and not by the Social Security Act." *Sanders*, 430 U.S. at 108. The Court further reasoned that permitting review "would frustrate the congressional purpose, plainly evidenced in § 205(g), to impose a 60-day limitation upon judicial review of the Secretary's final decision on the initial claim for benefits." *Id.*

Section 355(f) contains no comparable 60-day limitation tied to a single "final decision on the initial claim." Congress structured the Railroad Retirement Act differently, and this Court has cautioned against mechanically importing Social Security precedents into other benefit schemes with distinct textual and structural features. *See Lindahl v. Office of Personnel Management*, 470 U.S. 768, 779–80 (1985) (declining to import Tucker Act limitations from one benefits scheme into the distinct statutory review provisions of another). Just as *Henderson v. Shinseki*, 562 U.S. 428 (2011), rejected wholesale importation of jurisdictional rules from one review context into another because "Veterans Court review of a VA decision denying benefits differs in many respects" from other agency review schemes, *id.* at 431, this Court should decline to graft *Sanders*'s Social-Security-specific reasoning onto the Railroad Retirement Act's differently structured judicial-review provision.

### C. The Better-Reasoned Circuit Authority Confirms That Reopening Denials Are Reviewable

The D.C. Circuit correctly held in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), that "the Railroad Retirement Act grants the [circuit] Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations." *Id.* at 502. That holding tracks the earlier, equally sound reasoning of the Second Circuit in *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), and the Eighth Circuit's decision in *Sones v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 933 F.2d 636 (8th Cir. 1991)—both of which the Fifth Circuit acknowledged but declined to follow. *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 141.

This Court's decision in *Your Home Visiting Nurse Services, Inc. v. Shalala*, 525 U.S. 449 (1999), is not to the contrary. There, the Medicare statute expressly withheld Board jurisdiction over refusals to reopen by regulation, and this Court simply confirmed that the agency's own regulation validly divested review. *Id.* at 452. No comparable regulatory divestiture provision exists in the Railroad Retirement Act; to the contrary, the Board's regulations affirmatively provide a four-year reopening mechanism with hearing rights, integrating reopening into the ordinary claims process rather than segregating it as an unreviewable discretionary act.

### D. Treating Reopening Denials as Unreviewable Produces Anomalous and Unfair Results

Salinas's case illustrates the injustice of the rule below. The Board itself convened a hearing, applied its own four-year regulatory standard, and rendered a reasoned decision that Salinas's request fell outside that window. Yet under the Fifth Circuit's rule, that decision escapes all judicial scrutiny no matter how arbitrary, mistaken, or contrary to the Board's own regulations it might be. This Court has recognized that statutes governing disability and retirement benefits are to be construed to protect access to review, not to foreclose it. *See Heimeshoff v. Hartford Life & Accident Ins. Co.*, 571 U.S. 99, 108 (2013) (recognizing the "unusually protective" character of benefits review schemes). Denying review of a reasoned agency decision, made after a hearing, that determines the availability of benefits, cannot be squared with that protective purpose or with the plain text of § 355(f).

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed.
