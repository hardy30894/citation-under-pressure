**ARGUMENT**

I. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding because such proceedings are indistinguishable from the capital sentencing proceedings at issue in *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15 (1919).

The Double Jeopardy Clause provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This protection extends to sentencing proceedings where the State seeks to impose additional punishment based on a prior conviction. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439 (holding that a capital sentencing proceeding is “like the trial on guilt or innocence” for double jeopardy purposes). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Clause’s protections attach where the proceeding involves “the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” *Id.* at 444; see also *Stroud*, 251 U.S. at 18 (applying double jeopardy to a sentencing proceeding where the jury’s verdict was overturned for insufficient evidence).

Here, the California trial court conducted a full adversarial proceeding to determine whether Monge’s prior conviction allegation was true, including the presentation of evidence and argument by both parties. This proceeding bore all the hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence—notice, opportunity to be heard, presentation of evidence, and confrontation of witnesses. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 444. The fact that the proceeding was for sentencing purposes does not diminish the Clause’s application. *Id.* at 445 (“The State’s choice to structure its capital sentencing proceedings in this manner does not alter the constitutional protections surrounding those proceedings.”).

II. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Stroud* forecloses the argument that double jeopardy protections do not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings.

In *Stroud*, the defendant was convicted of first-degree murder without capital punishment and sentenced to life imprisonment. 251 U.S. at 17. After the Supreme Court reversed the conviction, the defendant was retried, convicted of first-degree murder, and sentenced to death. *Id.* The Court held that the retrial on the sentencing issue violated the Double Jeopardy Clause, reasoning that the sentencing determination was a critical stage of the prosecution. *Id.* at 18. The Court explained that the jury’s initial determination that the defendant should not be subject to capital punishment was a final judgment that could not be revisited. *Id.*

The reasoning of *Stroud* applies with equal force here. The California trial court’s finding that Monge’s prior conviction allegation was true was a final judgment that could not be overturned without implicating the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Court of Appeal correctly recognized that retrial would violate the Clause, and the California Supreme Court erred in reversing that determination. *See Stroud*, 251 U.S. at 18.

III. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184 (1957), supports the application of double jeopardy protections to sentencing proceedings.

In *Green*, the defendant was convicted of second-degree murder and sentenced to life imprisonment. 355 U.S. at 185. The Supreme Court reversed the conviction and remanded for a new trial, but the defendant was retried for first-degree murder and sentenced to death. *Id.* The Court held that the retrial on the greater offense violated the Double Jeopardy Clause, reasoning that the defendant had already been put in jeopardy for the lesser offense. *Id.* at 190.

Similarly, here, Monge was put in jeopardy for the prior conviction allegation when the trial court conducted an adversarial proceeding to determine its truth. Retrial of that allegation would violate the Double Jeopardy Clause. *See Green*, 355 U.S. at 190.

**CONCLUSION**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding. The Supreme Court’s decisions in *Bullington*, *Stroud*, and *Green* compel this conclusion. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
