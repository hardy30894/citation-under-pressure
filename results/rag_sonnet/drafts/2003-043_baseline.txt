# ARGUMENT

## I. The Internal Revenue Code Treats the Partnership and the Individual Partners as Separate "Taxpayers," Requiring Separate Assessment to Extend the Limitations Period Against Each

Under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), the government must assess a tax "against the taxpayer" within three years after the return is filed, and only a *timely assessment* triggers the extended ten-year collection period of § 6502(a). Both provisions incorporate § 7701(a)(1)'s definition of "person," which lists "an individual" and "a partnership" as separate categories. A partnership and its partners are therefore distinct taxpayers, each with its own assessment and its own limitations period.

This Court has long recognized that "[t]he partnership is a distinct thing from the partners themselves." Forsyth v. Woods, 78 U.S. 484, 486 (1871). In Forsyth, the Court refused to treat a joint promise by individual partners as automatically a "debt of the partnership," explaining that "debts of the firm are different in character from other joint debts of the partners." Id. The converse holds equally true here: an assessment against the firm does not, without more, become an assessment against the partners. The IRS assessed only the Partnership; it never separately assessed the Gallettis or the Briguglios as individual "taxpayers" under § 6203. Absent that individual assessment, only the ordinary three-year period of § 6501(a) governs collection from Debtors.

## II. This Court's Bankruptcy Precedents Confirm That Partnership and Partner Liabilities Must Be Separately Established

This Court's decisions construing the former Bankruptcy Act's parallel partnership structure confirm the same rule. In Francis v. McNeal, 228 U.S. 695 (1913), the Court explained that the statutory scheme assumed "that the partnership and individual estates both were to be administered," and that even where some partners are in bankruptcy while "the firm has remained out," the firm's separate status persists. Id. at 698. The Court warned of the "anomaly" in treating obligations as resolved against parties never brought within the relevant proceeding. Id.

Liberty Nat. Bank of Roanoke, Va. v. Bear, 276 U.S. 215 (1928), quoting General Order in Bankruptcy No. 8, likewise describes a trustee's duty to "keep separate accounts of the partnership property and of the property belonging to the individual partners," with proceeds allocated "in such proportions as the court shall determine," and permits "proof of the claim of the partnership estate against the individual estates, and vice versa." Id. at 217–18. That structure presumes a claim valid against the partnership is not automatically valid against an individual partner absent separate proof—precisely the rule applied below.

So too, in Meek v. Centre County Banking Co., 264 U.S. 499 (1924), and its companion decision, 268 U.S. 426 (1925), this Court held that a petition adjudicating a partnership bankrupt cannot bind non-consenting partners absent their own individual proceeding. 268 U.S. at 428–29. Partnership process does not substitute for partner-specific process. The same logic governs assessment: an assessment naming only the Partnership does not substitute for an individual assessment against, or judgment against, the partners themselves.

## III. This Court's Tax Decisions Confirm That Individual Partners Retain a Distinct Tax Identity From the Partnership

This Court's partnership tax decisions confirm that partners possess individually cognizable tax status apart from the entity. In Guaranty Trust Co. v. Commissioner, 303 U.S. 493 (1938), the Court analyzed a decedent partner's "share of the profits" under rules distinct from the partnership's own fiscal accounting. Id. at 495–96. Commissioner v. Tower, 327 U.S. 280 (1946), and Lusthaus v. Commissioner, 327 U.S. 293 (1946), likewise turned on whether a particular individual—not the partnership as an abstraction—bore the tax incidents of partnership income. And Commissioner v. Culbertson, 337 U.S. 733 (1949), reaffirmed that "a wife and a husband may ... become partners for tax ... purposes," 337 U.S. at 738 (quoting Tower, 327 U.S. at 290), because individual partner status carries independent tax consequences not subsumed within the partnership's own.

Finally, Begier v. Internal Revenue Service, 496 U.S. 53 (1990), shows that when Congress wished to grant the government special collection advantages for withheld taxes, it did so expressly, through the trust-fund provisions of 26 U.S.C. § 7501. Id. at 55–57. Congress could have provided that an assessment against a partnership automatically extends the limitations period against its partners. It did not. This Court should decline to read such an extension into the statute where Congress chose not to provide one, and where this Court's own precedents consistently treat the partnership and its partners as separate juridical and tax entities requiring separate process.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, and those stated by the court below, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
