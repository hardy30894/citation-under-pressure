# ARGUMENT

## I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews a district court's imposition of discovery sanctions, including the exclusion of expert testimony for failure to comply with a scheduling order, for abuse of discretion. *National Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club, Inc.*, 427 U.S. 639, 642 (1976). The decision to admit or exclude expert testimony under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), is likewise reviewed for abuse of discretion. *Christophersen v. Allied-Signal Corp.*, 939 F.2d 1106, 1109 (5th Cir. 1991) (en banc). A grant of summary judgment, however, is reviewed de novo, applying the same standard employed by the district court. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986).

## II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Striking Plaintiffs' Expert Testimony as a Discovery Sanction

Striking a party's experts as a sanction for a discovery violation is the functional equivalent of dismissal when, as here, it deprives the plaintiff of the evidentiary basis for the claim. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that dismissal-type sanctions are "a severe sanction that implicates due process," and are appropriate "only when the failure to comply with the court's order results from bad faith or willful misconduct" and lesser sanctions would not achieve the desired deterrent effect. *Batson v. Neal Spelce Assocs., Inc.*, 765 F.2d 511, 514 (5th Cir. 1985). Similarly, dismissal-type sanctions require "a clear record of delay or contumacious conduct" before the district court may resort to the most severe sanction available. *Rogers v. Kroger Co.*, 669 F.2d 317, 320 (5th Cir. 1982).

The record here does not support a finding of willfulness or bad faith rising to that level. Plaintiffs' counsel — representing hundreds of pro se litigants who had only recently been severed into this action and assigned new counsel — sought a protective order because their experts had not yet formulated final opinions. While the motion was belated, and the subsequent failure to meet the July 30, 1993 deadline was a serious lapse, the district court did not consider, on the record, whether a lesser sanction — such as a further extension conditioned on payment of Defendants' costs, or reopening a narrow discovery window limited to the specific deficiencies identified — would have "substantially achieve[d] the desired deterrent effect." *Batson*, 765 F.2d at 514. Because the district court's order effectively terminated Plaintiffs' claims without exploring these alternatives, it exceeded the bounds of permissible discretion. *National Hockey League*, 427 U.S. at 642.

## III. The District Court Erred in Excluding Dr. Schroeder's and Dr. Watson's Testimony Under *Daubert*

Under *Daubert*, the trial court's gatekeeping role requires it to "ensure that any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable." 509 U.S. at 589. That inquiry is a "flexible one," id. at 594, and its "focus, of course, must be solely on principles and methodology, not on the conclusions that they generate." Id. at 595. The Court identified nonexclusive factors — testability, peer review and publication, known or potential rate of error, and general acceptance — as guideposts, not a rigid checklist. Id. at 593-94.

The district court's exclusion of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson conflated the reliability inquiry with an assessment of the ultimate correctness of their conclusions regarding causation between the chemical exposures at the superfund sites and Plaintiffs' injuries. That approach is precisely what *Daubert* forbids: a court may not exclude an opinion merely because it finds the expert's conclusions unpersuasive, so long as the methodology underlying those conclusions is scientifically sound. 509 U.S. at 595. Dr. Schroeder's and Dr. Watson's opinions were grounded in recognized methodologies for assessing chemical exposure and toxicological causation, including reliance on site sampling data and epidemiological literature — methodologies routinely credited by courts applying *Daubert*. See *Christophersen*, 939 F.2d at 1110 (recognizing that an expert's reliance on epidemiological and exposure data may satisfy the reliability threshold even where the expert's ultimate conclusion is contested). By instead weighing the persuasiveness of the experts' conclusions, the district court substituted its own judgment for that of the jury and abused its discretion.

## IV. Because the Expert Testimony Was Improperly Excluded, Summary Judgment Must Be Reversed

Summary judgment is proper only where "there is no genuine issue as to any material fact." *Celotex*, 477 U.S. at 322; Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c). In ruling on such a motion, the court must view the evidence "in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion." *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). The district court's summary judgment ruling rested entirely on the predicate exclusion of Plaintiffs' expert testimony establishing exposure and causation. Because that exclusion was error, as demonstrated above, the district court's summary judgment order — which depended on the absence of any competent evidence of causation — cannot stand. Once the improperly excluded testimony is restored to the record, genuine issues of material fact regarding exposure and causation preclude judgment as a matter of law. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 248 ("a dispute about a material fact is 'genuine' … if the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party").

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
