### ARGUMENT

The bankruptcy court exceeded its jurisdiction and core authority when it adjudicated post-petition interest and declined collection costs on a debt that the parties never placed in issue. Federal bankruptcy jurisdiction extends only to proceedings “arising under” title 11, “arising in” a case under title 11, or “related to” such a case. 28 U.S.C. § 1334(b); see **Celotex Corp. v. Edwards**, 514 U.S. 300, 307-08 (1995). While bankruptcy courts may determine the dischargeability of student loans, **Tennessee Student Assistance Corp. v. Hood**, 541 U.S. 440, 447-48 (2004), that authority does not create a roving license to liquidate every monetary component of the debt after the case is closed and the estate has been administered.

#### I. The bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to award post-petition interest and adjudicate collection costs that were never placed in dispute.

A bankruptcy court’s subject-matter jurisdiction is not boundless. The Supreme Court has explained that “related to” jurisdiction exists only where the outcome of the proceeding could conceivably affect the estate being administered. **Celotex**, 514 U.S. at 307-08; **Pacor, Inc. v. Higgins**, 743 F.2d 984, 994 (3d Cir. 1984). Once Kirkland’s Chapter 13 case was closed and the estate fully administered, there was no estate impact left for the bankruptcy court to vindicate. See **Valley Historic Ltd. P’ship v. Bank of N.Y.**, 486 F.3d 831, 836-37 (4th Cir. 2007) (bankruptcy jurisdiction is at its zenith when the proceeding affects the administration of the estate).

Here, Kirkland’s complaint asked only for a declaration that the principal balance on claim no. 9 had been paid through the Chapter 13 plan and discharged except for any interest accruing during the bankruptcy. ECMC did not ask the court to determine a dollar amount of interest or collection costs; indeed, neither party requested adjudication of those items. A court may not confer upon itself authority over a dispute not properly before it. See **Celotex**, 514 U.S. at 307-08; **Valley Historic**, 486 F.3d at 836-37.

Nor did the adversary proceeding qualify as a “core” proceeding merely because it was filed in bankruptcy court. The statutory list of core matters in 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2) cannot be read to sweep in every collateral contract dispute between a debtor and creditor. The Fourth Circuit has cautioned that core jurisdiction is limited to matters that “arise under” the Bankruptcy Code or “arise in” a case under the Code. **Valley Historic**, 486 F.3d at 837-38. Post-petition interest and collection costs on a student loan, after the estate has closed, are not necessary to resolve whether the underlying debt was discharged under 11 U.S.C. § 1328(a) and 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8). See **Hood**, 541 U.S. at 447-48.

#### II. Even if the bankruptcy court could decide dischargeability, it could not enter a money judgment on post-petition interest absent a live, pleaded controversy.

The Supreme Court has recognized that bankruptcy courts may determine dischargeability because that determination is integral to the bankruptcy process. **Hood**, 541 U.S. at 447-48. But that is a far narrower power than liquidating a creditor’s post-petition rights when those rights were not presented for adjudication. A bankruptcy court’s authority to adjudicate a dischargeability complaint does not automatically authorize it to calculate every post-discharge component of a debt. See **Katchen v. Landy**, 382 U.S. 323, 329-30 (1966) (bankruptcy jurisdiction is tied to the process of allowance and equitable adjustment of claims against the estate).

The bankruptcy court here went beyond identifying whether the loan principal had been paid in the Chapter 13 case. It sua sponte awarded $184.40 in post-petition interest, notwithstanding that neither the complaint nor ECMC’s response requested a determination of the amount due. That exceeded the scope of the pleadings and the dispute. A federal court may not enter relief that has not been sought and litigated. See **Celotex**, 514 U.S. at 307-08; **Valley Historic**, 486 F.3d at 836-38.

#### III. The court also lacked any statutory basis to deny collection costs on the ground that ECMC had not proved them.

ECMC’s claim to collection costs, if any, would arise from the governing loan documents and applicable nonbankruptcy law, not from a free-floating bankruptcy power. The bankruptcy court declined those costs because ECMC allegedly provided no “statutory or factual basis.” But that ruling itself presupposed jurisdiction to decide the issue. Once the court recognized that collection costs were not pleaded or otherwise placed in controversy, it should have stopped there. See **Celotex**, 514 U.S. at 307-08.

The appropriate course was to limit the proceeding to the dischargeability question actually presented: whether the student-loan principal had been satisfied in the Chapter 13 case and, if not, whether it remained nondischargeable absent undue hardship under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8). See **Hood**, 541 U.S. at 447-48. By going further and adjudicating post-petition interest and collection costs, the bankruptcy court extended itself beyond the limits Congress and the Constitution impose. See **Valley Historic**, 486 F.3d at 836-38; **Celotex**, 514 U.S. at 307-08.

For these reasons, the portion of the judgment awarding post-petition interest and denying collection costs should be vacated.
