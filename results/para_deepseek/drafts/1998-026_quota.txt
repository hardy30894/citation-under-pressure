**ARGUMENT**

**Respondent’s awareness of his appeal right rendered any Rule 32 error harmless and insufficient for habeas relief.**

The sole question is whether a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his appeal right under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32 provides grounds for habeas relief when the defendant undisputedly knew of that right and elected not to appeal. It does not. The Third Circuit correctly held that any Rule 32 violation was harmless because respondent’s actual knowledge satisfied the rule’s purpose.

**I. Rule 32 violations are subject to harmless-error review.**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) commands that “[a]ny error, defect, irregularity, or variance that does not affect substantial rights must be disregarded.” This harmless-error standard applies to all nonstructural errors, including failures to comply with Rule 32. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734-35 (1993). The omission of a Rule 32 appeal advisement is not a structural error requiring automatic reversal; structural errors are “a limited class of fundamental constitutional errors” that “defy analysis by ‘harmless error’ standards.” *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 309-10 (1991). The failure to give a Rule 32 advisement does not render a trial fundamentally unfair or affect the framework of the proceeding. *See United States v. Paladino*, 401 F.3d 471, 483 (7th Cir. 2005). Accordingly, harmless-error review applies.

**II. The error did not affect respondent’s substantial rights because he knew of his appeal right.**

Rule 32’s advisement requirement exists to ensure that defendants are informed of their appeal right so they can make a knowing decision about whether to appeal. *See Peguero v. United States*, 526 U.S. 23, 28 (1999). When a defendant already knows of that right, the rule’s purpose is satisfied, and any omission is harmless. *Id.* at 28-29. The district court found—after an evidentiary hearing—that respondent knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That factual finding is entitled to deference. *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 575 (1985) (findings of fact shall not be set aside unless clearly erroneous).

Because respondent knew of his right and elected not to appeal, he cannot show that the court’s omission “affected his substantial rights.” *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 73 (2002) (error harmless where defendant already knew information omitted from Rule 11 colloquy). Indeed, *Peguero* itself holds that where a defendant “was aware of his right to appeal” and “cannot show prejudice,” habeas relief is unavailable. *Peguero*, 526 U.S. at 29. The failure to advise does not per se violate due process. *United States v. Broughton-Jones*, 71 F.3d 1143, 1147 (4th Cir. 1995).

**III. Respondent cannot show prejudice from the omission.**

A habeas petitioner must demonstrate “actual prejudice” from the alleged error. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984). For a Rule 32 violation, the prejudice inquiry focuses on whether the defendant was unaware of his appeal right and lost the opportunity to appeal. *Roe v. Flores-Ortega*, 528 U.S. 470, 484-85 (2000). Respondent cannot show prejudice because he knew his right and chose not to appeal. “Where a defendant has actual knowledge of his right to appeal, he cannot establish prejudice from the court’s failure to advise him.” *United States v. Sanchez*, 88 F.3d 1243, 1247 (D.C. Cir. 1996). Courts consistently hold that harmless error applies to Rule 32 omissions when the defendant had independent knowledge. *United States v. Drummond*, 128 F.3d 1310, 1313 (9th Cir. 1997); *United States v. Dukes*, 104 F.3d 891, 893 (6th Cir. 1997).

**IV. Respondent’s knowledge precludes habeas relief under *Brecht* and *Kotteakos*.**

Even assuming constitutional error, habeas relief requires showing that the error “had substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the jury’s verdict.” *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 623 (1993); *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946). The omission of a redundant advisement had no effect on respondent’s decision not to appeal. He cannot satisfy this standard.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
