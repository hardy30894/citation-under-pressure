# ARGUMENT

## THE RULE ANNOUNCED IN JOHNSON v. UNITED STATES DOES NOT APPLY RETROACTIVELY TO CASES ON COLLATERAL REVIEW

### A. Retroactivity Is Governed by a Balancing Test That Weighs the Purpose of the New Rule, Reliance on the Old Rule, and the Effect on the Administration of Justice.

This Court has long recognized that "the Constitution neither prohibits nor requires retrospective effect" for a new constitutional rule. *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 629 (1965). Rather than treat retroactivity as automatic, the Court has weighed three factors: "(a) the purpose to be served by the new standards, (b) the extent of the reliance by law enforcement authorities on the old standards, and (c) the effect on the administration of justice of a retroactive application of the new standards." *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293, 297 (1967). That framework governed this Court's refusal to give retroactive effect to *Griffin v. California* in *Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott*, 382 U.S. 406 (1966), to *Escobedo* and *Miranda* in *Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. 719 (1966), to the *Wade*-*Gilbert* identification rules in *Stovall* itself, to *Duncan v. Louisiana*'s jury-trial guarantee in *DeStefano v. Woods*, 392 U.S. 631 (1968), and to *Katz v. United States*'s search-and-seizure rule in *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244 (1969). As this Court explained, "the retroactivity or nonretroactivity of a rule is not automatically determined by the provision of the Constitution on which the dictate is based." *Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. at 728. That a rule vindicates due process, as does the vagueness holding of *Johnson v. United States*, therefore does not itself compel retrospective application.

### B. The Purpose of the Residual-Clause Vagueness Rule Does Not Concern the Integrity of the Fact-Finding Process.

Under *Tehan*, *Stovall*, and *DeStefano*, the pivotal inquiry is whether the new rule "went to the very integrity of the fact-finding process" at trial. *Tehan*, 382 U.S. at 416. Rules that merely regulate the procedure by which guilt is determined, without bearing on the accuracy of that determination, have consistently been denied retroactive effect. *DeStefano*, 392 U.S. at 633–34 (jury-trial right not retroactive though "fundamental," because "the values implemented by the right to jury trial would not measurably be served" by retroactive application); *Desist*, 394 U.S. at 249 (declining to extend *Katz* backward because doing so "would not significantly increase the reliability" of prior fact-finding).

The rule of *Johnson v. United States* operates at sentencing, not at trial, and concerns only how a court classifies an already-adjudicated prior conviction under the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act. It says nothing about whether Welch is guilty of the offense of conviction, nor does it cast doubt on the accuracy of the process by which his guilt was determined. Because the rule does not touch "the very integrity of the fact-finding process," *Tehan*, 382 U.S. at 416, the first *Stovall* factor weighs against retroactivity, just as it did for the trial-related rules at issue in *Tehan*, *Stovall*, and *DeStefano*.

### C. Courts and Prosecutors Reasonably Relied on the Residual Clause for Decades.

The second *Stovall* factor asks whether law enforcement reasonably relied on the superseded rule. 388 U.S. at 297. For years, sentencing courts across the country applied the residual clause exactly as its text instructed, sentencing recidivist offenders such as Welch to mandatory minimum terms based on that clause. This reliance mirrors the reliance this Court credited in *Stovall* itself, where "law enforcement authorities fairly relied" on then-prevailing identification procedures, 388 U.S. at 299, and in *DeStefano*, where the States had "relied" on the pre-*Duncan* understanding that the jury-trial guarantee did not bind them, 392 U.S. at 633. Disturbing final sentences imposed under a since-invalidated but, at the time, unremarkable statutory clause would penalize exactly the sort of good-faith reliance that *Stovall* and *DeStefano* protected.

### D. Retroactive Application Would Unduly Burden the Administration of Justice and Disturb Final Judgments.

The third factor—effect on the administration of justice—also counsels against retroactivity. *Stovall*, 388 U.S. at 297. This Court has long cautioned that collateral review is not meant to serve as a second appeal: "the writ of habeas corpus should not do service for an appeal." *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 182 (1947). Extending *Johnson* to final judgments would invite exactly that result, reopening a large class of long-settled sentences.

Moreover, a court may limit the operation of even a constitutional ruling to prospective effect without offending due process. As Justice Cardozo explained for the Court, "a state in defining the limits of adherence to precedent may make a choice for itself between the principle of forward operation and that of relation backward." *Great Northern Ry. Co. v. Sunburst Oil & Refining Co.*, 287 U.S. 358, 364 (1932). Likewise, the "actual existence of a statute, prior to such a determination [of invalidity], is an operative fact and may have consequences which cannot justly be ignored." *Chicot County Drainage Dist. v. Baxter State Bank*, 308 U.S. 371, 374 (1940). The residual clause's application to Welch's 1996 Florida robbery conviction was such an operative fact at the time of his sentencing, and the settled expectations built upon it should not now be justly ignored.

### Conclusion

Weighing the purpose of the rule, the reasonable reliance of sentencing courts, and the disruptive effect on final judgments, the balance struck in *Linkletter*, *Tehan*, *Johnson v. New Jersey*, *Stovall*, *DeStefano*, and *Desist* forecloses retroactive application of *Johnson v. United States* to Welch's collateral challenge.
