### Argument

The district court erred in denying the asserted claims the benefit of the February 6, 1997 filing date of PowerOasis’s original application. Under 35 U.S.C. § 120, a later-filed application is entitled to the benefit of the filing date of an earlier application when the earlier disclosure reasonably conveys possession of the later-claimed invention to a person skilled in the art. *Crown Cork & Seal Co. v. Ferdinand Gutmann Co.*, 304 U.S. 159, 170-71 (1938); *Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co. v. Radio Corp. of America*, 306 U.S. 86, 94 (1939). The statute requires continuity of disclosure, not verbatim repetition of every later claim limitation. *Muncie Gear Works, Inc. v. Outboard Marine & Mfg. Co.*, 315 U.S. 759, 768-69 (1942).

The original 1997 application disclosed the same invention later claimed in the ’658 and ’400 patents: a vending machine for vending telecommunications channel access, including a customer interface, payment mechanism, telecommunications access circuit, and control unit. The fact that the later claims were prosecuted through a continuation-in-part does not forfeit the original priority date for subject matter already present in the parent disclosure. *Mackay Radio*, 306 U.S. at 94; *Muncie Gear Works*, 315 U.S. at 768-69. The district court therefore erred by treating the 2000 continuation-in-part as though it were the only disclosure relevant to priority.

The court’s construction of “customer interface” supplied the basis for its invalidity ruling, but that construction was itself infected by reliance on later-added material rather than the original disclosure. Claim construction must be rooted in the language of the claims and the patent record as understood by those skilled in the art. *United States v. Adams*, 383 U.S. 39, 48-49 (1966); *Graver Tank & Mfg. Co. v. Linde Air Prods. Co.*, 339 U.S. 605, 609-10 (1950). Here, the parties agreed that a “customer interface” is “an interface that enables information to be passed between a human user and hardware or software components of a system.” Nothing in that agreed meaning requires the interface to be physically mounted on the vending machine itself. The district court’s contrary premise improperly narrowed the claim by importing a location limitation that the claims do not recite and that the original specification did not exclude. *Adams*, 383 U.S. at 48-49.

The consequence of that error was dispositive. With the customer interface properly construed, the asserted claims are not shown to be anticipated by the MobileStar Network as a matter of law. Anticipation requires that a single prior art reference or system disclose every element of the claim, arranged as claimed. *Tilghman v. Proctor*, 102 U.S. 707, 711 (1881); *Crawford v. Heysinger*, 123 U.S. 589, 602 (1887). Summary judgment is improper where the record permits a finding that the claimed combination was not previously known in the same form and arrangement. *Tilghman*, 102 U.S. at 711; *Crawford*, 123 U.S. at 602.

T-Mobile relied on the MobileStar Network, but the undisputed evidence showed only that MobileStar provided wireless Internet access. That is not enough. The asserted claims require a vending machine that begins and ends telecommunications channel access in response to a vending transaction, with a control unit and customer interface as claimed. *Tilghman*, 102 U.S. at 711. A prior system anticipating one feature of a later combination does not anticipate the combination itself. *Muncie Gear Works*, 315 U.S. at 768-69. Because T-Mobile’s anticipation case depended on an artificially narrow construction and on disregarding the priority chain back to 1997, the grant of summary judgment cannot stand.

This Court should reverse and hold that the asserted claims are entitled to the benefit of the February 6, 1997 filing date, or at minimum vacate the summary judgment and remand for application of the correct priority date and claim construction. *Crown Cork & Seal*, 304 U.S. at 170-71; *Muncie Gear Works*, 315 U.S. at 768-69.
