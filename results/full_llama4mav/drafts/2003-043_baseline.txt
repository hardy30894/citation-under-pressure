The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing those partners or obtaining a judgment against them is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess a tax against a "taxpayer" within three years of the filing of the taxpayer's return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, both a partnership and its individual partners are separate "taxpayers" under the Code. United States v. Galletti, 298 F.3d 1107, 1112 (9th Cir.2002).

The IRS's assessment against the partnership does not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners. The Code's assessment provision, 26 U.S.C. § 6203, requires the IRS to record the liability of the "taxpayer" in its records. The regulation interpreting this provision states that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer." 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. This language indicates that the assessment must be made against a specific taxpayer, and an assessment against a partnership does not satisfy this requirement with respect to its individual partners.

The government's reliance on Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir.1960), is misplaced. In Young, the district court held that a partner was not entitled to a refund of taxes paid because he was liable for the partnership's tax debt under state law. 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D.Cal.1959). However, the court also acknowledged that the IRS would have had to assess the tax against the partner or bring a court action to collect it if the partner had not paid voluntarily. Id. at 76,050. The Ninth Circuit's affirmance of the district court's decision did not reject this interpretation. Galletti, 298 F.3d at 1114.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir.1961), also supports the respondents' position. In Coson, the court held that an assessment against a partnership was insufficient to create a lien on the property of an individual partner. Id. at 458. Although the court ultimately affirmed on other grounds, the district court's reasoning that an assessment against a partnership does not satisfy the assessment requirement with respect to individual partners remains persuasive. Coson v. United States, 169 F.Supp. 671, 676 (S.D.Cal.1958).

Under California law, a creditor cannot collect a partnership debt from an individual partner without first obtaining a judgment against the partner. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c). The IRS has not obtained such a judgment against the respondents, and the statute of limitations for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Therefore, the bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the IRS's claims against the respondents.

In conclusion, the IRS's assessment against the partnership does not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners. The respondents are separate "taxpayers" under the Code, and the IRS must assess them individually or obtain a judgment against them to collect the tax debt. The Ninth Circuit's decision in Galletti is consistent with the Code and relevant case law, and this Court should affirm it.
