## ARGUMENT

### I. The 1988 release does not bar Bancorp’s claims because it is limited to claims “arising from or with respect to the decision” to close the Bank, not post-closing misconduct by the FDIC as receiver.

A release is interpreted according to ordinary contract principles, and its scope is confined to the claims its language actually covers. *Mangini v. McClurg*, 24 N.Y.2d 556, 563-64 (1969). Under New York law, a release may not be expanded beyond its clear terms, and “a general release is to be construed in the light of the controversy being settled.” *Cahill v. Regan*, 5 N.Y.2d 292, 299 (1959). Here, Bancorp released claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” That language reaches challenges to the OCC’s closure decision itself, not separate claims that the FDIC later breached fiduciary duties, wasted estate assets, or unjustly enriched itself after the receivership began.

This Court and New York courts repeatedly hold that releases do not sweep in claims outside the subject matter of the dispute. *Centro Empresarial Cempresa S.A. v. América Móvil, S.A.B. de C.V.*, 17 N.Y.3d 269, 276-77 (2011); *W.W.W. Assocs., Inc. v. Giancontieri*, 77 N.Y.2d 157, 162-63 (1990). The claims pleaded here concern the FDIC’s conduct after the Bank had been closed: charging fees to the receivership, paying itself post-insolvency interest, and allegedly selling assets below value. Those are distinct from a claim that the OCC wrongly closed the Bank in the first place. A claim based on later receiver misconduct does not “arise from” the closure decision merely because the receivership would not have existed without that decision.

The district court’s contrary reading effectively rewrites the release to cover all future disputes connected to the Bank’s failure. New York law forbids such expansion. *Shklovskiy v. Khan*, 273 A.D.2d 371, 372 (2d Dep’t 2000) (“a release may not be read to cover matters not specifically mentioned”), and *Mangini*, 24 N.Y.2d at 564, requires that any ambiguity be resolved in light of the dispute actually settled. Because the release does not expressly mention post-closing receivership misconduct, summary judgment should not have been granted on that basis.

### II. The six-year limitations period did not begin until the receivership terminated in 1995, when Bancorp’s claims accrued.

Claims for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste are governed by New York’s six-year period under C.P.L.R. 213. *Loengard v. Santa Fe Indus., Inc.*, 70 N.Y.2d 262, 266-67 (1987); *Kaufman v. Cohen*, 307 A.D.2d 113, 118 (1st Dep’t 2003). Under New York law, a fiduciary’s duty continues while the fiduciary relationship persists, and the statute does not ordinarily begin to run until the fiduciary openly repudiates the relationship or the relationship ends. *Golden Pac. Bancorp v. FDIC*, 273 F.3d 509, 517-18 (2d Cir. 2001); *Westchester Religious Inst. v. Kamerman*, 262 A.D.2d 131, 131-32 (1st Dep’t 1999).

That rule is especially apt here. The FDIC acted as receiver for the Bank until November 1, 1995. While the receivership continued, the FDIC controlled the Bank’s estate, paid itself, and managed the assets. Because the challenged conduct was undertaken in the course of that ongoing fiduciary administration, Bancorp’s claims did not accrue until the FDIC’s receivership ended and the fiduciary relationship terminated. See *Golden Pac. Bancorp*, 273 F.3d at 518; *In re Estate of Witbeck*, 245 A.D.2d 848, 849 (3d Dep’t 1997).

The district court’s view that limitations began earlier would force Bancorp to sue before it could know the full extent of the FDIC’s self-dealing and asset mismanagement. New York does not impose such a result where a fiduciary relationship is ongoing. *IDT Corp. v. Morgan Stanley Dean Witter & Co.*, 12 N.Y.3d 132, 140 (2009); *Matter of Barabash*, 31 N.Y.2d 76, 80-81 (1972). Bancorp filed this action on October 31, 1995, one day before termination of the receivership, well within six years of accrual.

### III. The FDIC’s continuing control over the receivership also supports accrual at termination under federal accrual principles.

Even apart from New York fiduciary principles, federal accrual doctrine looks to when the plaintiff can first sue on a completed injury. *United States v. Kubrick*, 444 U.S. 111, 120 (1979). A claim does not accrue while the defendant’s continuing conduct remains part of an ongoing course of administration that prevents final injury from being fixed. *AQ v. United States*, 860 F.2d 672, 673-74 (6th Cir. 1988). Here, the FDIC’s alleged wrongs were not isolated historical acts; they were continuing decisions made in its role as receiver until the estate closed. Bancorp therefore had a ripe claim, at the earliest, when the receivership ended and the FDIC’s accounting and distributions became final. See *FDIC v. Henderson*, 61 F.3d 421, 425-26 (5th Cir. 1995).

Because neither the release nor the statute of limitations bars this suit, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
