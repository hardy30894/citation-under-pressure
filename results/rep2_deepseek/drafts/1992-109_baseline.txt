**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Requires a Single Competency Standard for All Phases of a Criminal Proceeding**

This Court has long held that the constitutional standard for competency to stand trial is whether the defendant “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). That standard ensures that a defendant can “understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense.” *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975). No decision of this Court has ever held that a different, heightened standard applies when a defendant chooses to plead guilty or waive counsel.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard finds no support in this Court’s precedents. The Court has consistently treated competency as a unitary concept. *See, e.g., Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385-86 (1966) (applying *Dusky* standard to trial competency). When a defendant pleads guilty, he waives several constitutional rights, but the competency inquiry remains whether he possesses the capacity to understand the proceedings and the rights he is forgoing. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969) (guilty plea must be “voluntary and intelligent”); *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver of counsel must be “intelligent and competent”). The “reasoned choice” requirement conflates competency with the wisdom of the defendant’s decision. A defendant may make a self-destructive choice and still be competent. Due process demands only that he have the capacity to appreciate the consequences of his choice, not that he exercise that capacity in a particular manner.

**II. The Ninth Circuit’s Heightened Standard Lacks Foundation in This Court’s Decisions**

The court of appeals relied on its own precedent to require a “reasoned choice” standard, citing *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987), and *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973). Those cases, however, misinterpreted this Court’s holding in *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966). *Westbrook* merely recognized that “the constitutional right of a defendant to conduct his own defense” may require a separate inquiry into the “knowing and intelligent” nature of a waiver. *Id.* at 150. It did not impose a heightened competency standard. A defendant’s decision to waive counsel or plead guilty must be voluntary, knowing, and intelligent, but the competency component of that inquiry is the same *Dusky* standard. *See, e.g., Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975) (defendant must be “competent to stand trial” to waive counsel). The Ninth Circuit’s approach erroneously requires a trial court to assess whether the defendant can make a “reasoned choice” among alternatives—a subjective judgment that invites a court to second-guess a defendant’s decisions on the merits.

**III. The Trial Court Properly Applied the Correct Standard and Found Moran Competent**

The record demonstrates that the state court had no good faith doubt about Moran’s competency under the *Dusky* standard. Two court-appointed psychiatrists examined Moran and concluded that he understood the charges, could assist counsel, and was aware of the penalties. *See* O’Gorman Psychiatric Evaluation at 2; Jurasky Psychiatric Evaluation. The trial court personally questioned Moran, who responded coherently and acknowledged the rights he was waiving. Although Moran was on medication, the court asked about it, and Moran’s responses were lucid. The state post-conviction court, after an evidentiary hearing, found that Moran was competent. The Ninth Circuit erred by requiring a higher showing of “reasoned choice” and by discounting the psychiatric reports as irrelevant simply because they were phrased in terms of trial competency. The reports directly addressed Moran’s rational understanding and ability to assist—precisely the factors relevant under *Dusky*.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Would Create Unworkable Burdens on Trial Courts**

A “reasoned choice” standard would require trial courts to evaluate the quality of a defendant’s decision-making, not merely his capacity. This would transform competency hearings into inquiries into the wisdom of a defendant’s choices, undermining the autonomy of competent defendants to make their own decisions. *See Drope*, 420 U.S. at 171 (competency is a “present ability” inquiry). The *Dusky* standard already provides ample protection by ensuring that a defendant understands the proceedings and the consequences of his actions. The court of appeals’ judgment should be reversed.
