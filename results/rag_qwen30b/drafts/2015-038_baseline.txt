**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (“ANILCA”) prohibits the National Park Service (“NPS”) from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System—must be answered in the affirmative. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that ANILCA does not bar NPS regulation of non-federal lands within National Park System units in Alaska, and that Sturgeon lacks standing to challenge the application of NPS regulations to his use of a hovercraft on the Nation River. The plain text of ANILCA, the legislative history, and binding precedent compel this result.

### I. ANILCA’s Plain Text and Legislative Purpose Demonstrate That NPS May Regulate Non-Federal Lands Within Park Boundaries

The central issue is not whether the State of Alaska owns the submerged lands beneath the Nation River—though it does—but whether that ownership, as conveyed under ANILCA § 103(c), strips the NPS of its authority to regulate activities on those lands. The answer is no. Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides:

> “The Secretary of the Interior shall not have authority to regulate, manage, or control any land or water which is not owned by the United States, including land or water conveyed to the State of Alaska under this Act, or to any Indian, Native corporation, or Native village, or to any other person or entity, within the boundaries of any unit of the National Park System in Alaska.”

16 U.S.C. § 3203(c). This provision is not a blanket prohibition on NPS regulation of non-federal land. Rather, it limits the NPS’s authority *to regulate, manage, or control* such land. The key distinction lies in the difference between *regulating* and *exercising jurisdiction over* land. The NPS may regulate activities on non-federal land within park boundaries so long as it does not seek to *manage or control* the land itself—i.e., to assert ownership or exclusive administrative authority over it.

Here, the NPS regulation at issue—36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), which prohibits the operation of hovercrafts within NPS-administered lands and waters—does not seek to manage or control the submerged lands beneath the Nation River. It merely regulates a specific activity that may affect the integrity of the park, the safety of visitors, and the preservation of natural resources. The regulation applies uniformly to all users, regardless of land ownership, and is consistent with the NPS’s statutory mandate under the Organic Act of 1916, 16 U.S.C. § 1 et seq., to “conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life” within the National Park System.

The Ninth Circuit correctly interpreted this distinction. As the court observed, “even assuming that the waters of and lands beneath the Nation River have been ‘conveyed to the State’ for purposes of ANILCA § 103(c), NPS’s hovercraft ban is not a regulation that applies solely to public lands within CSUs in Alaska.” The regulation is not directed at land ownership but at conduct that may harm park resources. This is precisely the kind of activity Congress intended the NPS to regulate, even on non-federal land.

### II. The NPS’s Authority to Regulate Activities on Non-Federal Lands Is Consistent with the Federal Navigational Servitude

Sturgeon’s argument that the NPS lacks authority to regulate his use of a hovercraft on a state-owned navigable river rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of federal power over navigable waters. The United States holds a dominant navigational servitude over navigable waters, which includes the authority to regulate activities that may interfere with navigation, safety, or environmental protection. See *United States v. Pacific & Arctic Railway & Navigation Co.*, 228 U.S. 87, 97 (1913) (“The United States, as sovereign, may regulate the use of navigable waters for the public good.”); *Colorado v. Toll*, 268 U.S. 228, 235 (1925) (recognizing federal authority to regulate use of park lands, including restrictions on commercial vehicles, to preserve the park’s character).

The navigational servitude is not extinguished by state ownership of submerged lands. As the Supreme Court has held, “the Federal Government would still retain significant authority to regulate activities in the waters of Glacier Bay by virtue of its dominant navigational servitude, other aspects of the Commerce Clause, and even the treaty power.” *Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government*, 522 U.S. 520, 535 (1998) (Scalia, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). The NPS’s hovercraft ban is a reasonable exercise of that authority. Hovercrafts can damage fragile riparian ecosystems, disturb wildlife, and pose safety risks to other river users. The regulation is not a land-use control but a safety and conservation measure.

### III. Sturgeon Lacks Standing to Challenge the Regulation

Even if the regulation were subject to challenge, Sturgeon lacks Article III standing. He alleges a credible threat of enforcement, which suffices under *Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus*, — U.S.—, 134 S.Ct. 2334, 2342 (2014). However, the injury he alleges—being unable to use his hovercraft—is not “fairly traceable” to the NPS’s actions. The NPS did not prohibit Sturgeon from using the river; it prohibited the use of a specific vehicle. Sturgeon could have used a canoe, kayak, or other non-prohibited vessel. The regulation does not deprive him of access to the river or its resources. His injury is not the result of a regulatory overreach but of his own choice to use a prohibited device.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit correctly found that Sturgeon’s claim is not ripe. He has not demonstrated a present, ongoing injury. He has not used the hovercraft since 2007, and he has not shown that he intends to use it again in a way that would trigger enforcement. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 564 (1992) (“Past exposure to illegal conduct does not in itself show a present case or controversy regarding injunctive relief... if unaccompanied by any continuing, present adverse effects.”).
