**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not bar the use of Michael Bryant’s prior tribal-court convictions as predicates under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Those convictions were obtained in proceedings to which the Sixth Amendment does not apply. *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (“the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes”). They therefore “do not violate the Sixth Amendment.” *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 746-47 (1994) (citing *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367 (1979)).

Section 117(a) is a recidivist statute that “penaliz[es] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747. It does not impose additional punishment for the predicate offenses; it merely “increases the penalty for a crime” already proved beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 747 (quoting *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948)). Because the predicates are valid under the Sixth Amendment, their use to establish the element of a subsequent federal offense raises no constitutional infirmity. *See Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967) (uncounseled conviction may not be used only when it “is invalid for purposes of imposing a sentence of imprisonment for the subsequent conviction”).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule rests on *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), which treated tribal-court judgments as “constitutionally infirm” whenever they lack appointed counsel. That premise is irreconcilable with *Nichols*. *Nichols* expressly permits enhancement based on an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction that itself complied with the Sixth Amendment. 511 U.S. at 746-47. The reliability concerns that animated *Baldasar v. Illinois*, 446 U.S. 222 (1980), were rejected in *Nichols* precisely because the prior conviction remained valid. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 743-46.

*Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654 (2002), confirms the distinction. There the Court invalidated only the suspended sentence, leaving the underlying conviction intact. *Id.* at 661-62. The same result follows here: the tribal convictions are valid; only their federal use as predicates is at issue. Every other circuit to address the question has so held. *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011).

Comity principles reinforce the point. Federal courts must respect tribal judgments rendered in compliance with the Indian Civil Rights Act. *See Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987); *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810-11 (9th Cir. 1997). Conditioning recognition on the provision of counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment would impose “judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts,” contrary to settled doctrine. *Wilson*, 127 F.3d at 810.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
