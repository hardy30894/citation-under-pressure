**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act provides an intelligible principle that satisfies the nondelegation doctrine. The nondelegation doctrine asks whether Congress has supplied a reasonably clear standard to guide the exercise of delegated authority. As this Court long ago explained: "If Congress shall lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to fix such rates is directed to conform, such legislative action is not a forbidden delegation of legislative power." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Temporary Scheduling Provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), easily meets this standard.

**I. The Temporary Scheduling Provision Provides an Intelligible Principle Channeling the Attorney General's Discretion.**

The Act itself provides that temporary scheduling may issue only to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). That standard is more exacting than other delegations this Court has sustained. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 420 (1944), this Court upheld a delegation to the Price Administrator to fix prices under the Emergency Price Control Act. In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), this Court upheld a delegation to the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to prescribe regulations for savings and loan associations. If those broad authorizations satisfied the intelligible principle test, the statutory "imminent hazard" standard does as well.

The Act further confines the Attorney General by requiring consideration of enumerated factors: the drug's history and current pattern of abuse; the scope, duration, and significance of abuse; and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4)-(6). Requiring consideration of specified factors is a hallmark of a lawful delegation. See *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-79 (1948); *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952) (upholding discretionary authority in the Attorney General).

The Temporary Scheduling Provision is also narrowly limited in duration: one year, with a possible extension of six months. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). This temporal limit is a defining boundary, unlike the open-ended delegations invalidated in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). In *Panama Refining*, Congress gave the President authority to prohibit interstate transportation of "hot oil" without statutory criteria. 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, Congress authorized the President to approve codes of fair competition but failed to supply sufficient standards to confine that authority. 295 U.S. at 537-38. Here, by contrast, Congress defined the hazard, prescribed the factors, and imposed a sunset. No *Panama Refining* or *Schechter Poultry* problem exists.

**II. This Delegation Involves Criminal Sanctions, but the Intelligible Principle Standard Applies.**

The Toubys argue that a stricter test applies because temporary scheduling creates criminal prohibitions. But this Court has never held that delegating authority to define crimes requires greater specificity than delegating economic or regulatory authority. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911), this Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to make regulations governing use of national forests, violations of which were criminal. The Court explained that Congress had established a policy and prescribed a standard to guide the Secretary. Id. at 517. The Temporary Scheduling Provision is far more concrete. The Secretary in *Grimaud* had general authority to adopt rules and regulations for forest reservations. Id. Here, the Attorney General must make a specific finding of imminent hazard to the public safety and consider three enumerated factors. This is not the kind of unconfined authority that was invalidated in *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 538.

**III. The Attorney General Properly Subdelegated to the DEA.**

The Controlled Substances Act authorizes the Attorney General to promulgate and enforce any rules, regulations, and procedures which he may deem necessary. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This Court has long recognized that officers to whom executive power is delegated may subdelegate that power unless Congress expressly prohibits it. *United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc.*, 272 U.S. 1, 11 (1926) (upholding subdelegation by the President under the Trading with the Enemy Act); *Runkle v. United States*, 122 U.S. 543, 557 (1887) (recognizing implied authority to subdelegate). Here, Congress vested the temporary scheduling power in the "Attorney General," not in a specific subordinate or bureau. The Attorney General's regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), subdelegating to the DEA Administrator, is a routine administrative arrangement.

The Toubys' challenge would require this Court to hold that every executive officer must personally exercise every power Congress confers. That proposition has no support in precedent. *Runkle*, 122 U.S. at 557; *Chemical Foundation*, 272 U.S. at 11. It would paralyze law enforcement.

**IV. Conclusion**

The Temporary Scheduling Provision provides an intelligible principle—imminent hazard to public safety—coupled with specific factors and a fixed duration. This easily satisfies the nondelegation doctrine as applied in *J.W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409; *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 420; *Fahey*, 332 U.S. at 250; *Lichter*, 334 U.S. at 778-79; *Carlson*, 342 U.S. at 542-44; and *Grimaud*, 220 U.S. at 521. The Attorney General's subdelegation to the DEA is well within the authority recognized in *Chemical Foundation*, 272 U.S. at 11, and *Runkle*, 122 U.S. at 557. The judgment below should be affirmed.
