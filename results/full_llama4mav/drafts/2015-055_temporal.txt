The issue before this Court is whether the rule announced in Johnson v. United States, regarding what constitutes a violent offense, applies retroactively to cases on collateral review, such as Gregory Welch's. 

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) imposes a minimum 15-year sentence on defendants with three prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses. At the time of Welch's sentencing, the ACCA defined a "violent felony" as an offense that has as an element the "use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another," or is "burglary, arson, or extortion, involves use of explosives, or otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another." The residual clause, which includes action that "otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another," was held to be unconstitutionally vague in Johnson.

In determining whether Johnson applies retroactively, we look to the principles established by this Court in Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618 (1965). In Linkletter, the Court held that the retroactivity of a new constitutional rule is determined by considering the purpose of the rule, the extent of reliance on the prior rule, and the effect of retroactivity on the administration of justice. Applying these principles, we argue that Johnson should apply retroactively.

First, the purpose of the rule in Johnson is to ensure that defendants are not sentenced under a vague and arbitrary statute. This purpose is rooted in the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment, as established in cases such as Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451 (1939), where the Court held that a penal statute must be sufficiently definite to give fair notice to individuals of the prohibited conduct.

Second, the extent of reliance on the prior rule is significant, as many defendants were sentenced under the ACCA's residual clause. However, this reliance is not sufficient to overcome the importance of ensuring that defendants are not sentenced under a vague and arbitrary statute.

Finally, the effect of retroactivity on the administration of justice is a consideration. While applying Johnson retroactively may result in the re-sentencing of some defendants, it is a necessary step to ensure that the ACCA is applied in a constitutional manner.

In United States v. Katz, 271 U.S. 354 (1926), the Court held that a statute must be construed in a manner that avoids constitutional doubts. Applying Johnson retroactively is consistent with this principle, as it ensures that the ACCA is applied in a manner that is consistent with the Constitution.

Therefore, based on the principles established in Linkletter v. Walker and other cases, we argue that the rule announced in Johnson v. United States should apply retroactively to cases on collateral review, including Gregory Welch's.
