The Eleventh Circuit properly consulted the full record, including matters outside the evidence presented to the jury, in assessing whether the Rehaif error affected Greer’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. Longstanding precedent confirms that appellate courts may examine the entire record when reviewing claims of error.

In determining whether a conviction rests on an invalid prior judgment, this Court has examined the complete proceedings, including evidence and objections developed outside the jury’s presence. Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109, 110-11 (1967). The same principle governs review of guilty pleas, where the Court looks to the full arraignment and sentencing record to assess voluntariness. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 242-44 (1969). When a double-jeopardy violation is asserted, the Court has remanded for further exploration of the trial record rather than limiting itself to the jury’s evidence. Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 787 (1969).

Appellate examination of sentencing records has likewise extended beyond the jury’s view to determine whether misinformation or procedural irregularity affected the outcome. Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728, 730-31 (1948). In collateral attacks on prior convictions, the Court has considered the entire documentary record of the earlier case. In re Belt, 159 U.S. 95, 96-97 (1895). Technical defects in arraignment or plea entry are evaluated against the full proceedings, not merely the evidence adduced at trial. Garland v. Washington, 232 U.S. 642, 644-45 (1914).

Where an indigent defendant challenges the adequacy of appellate review, the Court has required production of the complete transcript to permit assessment of prejudice. Eskridge v. Washington State Bd. of Prison Terms & Paroles, 357 U.S. 214, 215-16 (1958). Even after reversal of an erroneous sentence, the Court has permitted the appellate court to consult the original record to impose a corrected judgment. Murphy v. Massachusetts, 177 U.S. 155, 158-59 (1900). These authorities establish that plain-error review is not confined to the trial evidence; the reviewing court may consider the entire record to determine whether any error was prejudicial or undermined the integrity of the proceedings.

The Eleventh Circuit’s examination of Greer’s prior convictions and the full trial record was therefore consistent with this Court’s longstanding practice.
