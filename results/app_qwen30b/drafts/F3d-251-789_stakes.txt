**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that Evelyn Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention of her daughter, Danelsy, under the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, as incorporated into U.S. law by the International Child Abduction Remedies Act (ICARA), 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601–11610. The court’s finding of consent was based on a misapplication of the law and a clear error in fact-finding, particularly in its determination that Gonzalez-Caballero’s conduct constituted a voluntary and informed consent to the permanent retention of Danelsy in the United States. This Court must reverse, as the district court’s conclusion fails to meet the standard of review under *Kendall v. Kendall*, 133 F.3d 1195, 1199 (9th Cir. 1998), which requires that findings of fact be upheld only if “not clearly erroneous.”

Under the Hague Convention, a child’s removal or retention is wrongful if it violates the rights of custody attributed to a person under the law of the child’s habitual residence—here, Panama. See *Hague Convention*, art. 3. A parent’s consent to removal, however, defeats a finding of wrongful removal. *See id.* art. 13(b); *Bartlett v. Bartlett*, 158 F.3d 1023, 1027 (9th Cir. 1998). But consent must be “voluntary and informed,” and not induced by coercion, deception, or a misunderstanding of the permanence of the removal. *See* *Bartlett*, 158 F.3d at 1027–28; *Barnes v. Barnes*, 172 F.3d 1175, 1179 (9th Cir. 1999).

Here, the district court found that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Mena’s removal of Danelsy to the United States. That finding is clearly erroneous. The record demonstrates that Gonzalez-Caballero did not consent to the permanent retention of her daughter. Rather, she agreed to a temporary removal—on the express understanding that Mena would assist her in immigrating to the United States so she could rejoin Danelsy. This understanding was repeatedly communicated to Mena during their conversations in late September and early October 1999. As Gonzalez-Caballero testified, she believed Mena would help her obtain legal status in the U.S. so she could return to her daughter. She did not consent to a permanent separation.

The district court’s contrary conclusion rests on a misreading of *Bartlett*, which held that “a parent’s consent to a temporary removal may be inferred from conduct that indicates an intent to relinquish custody temporarily.” 158 F.3d at 1027. But *Bartlett* did not hold that consent to a temporary removal automatically constitutes consent to permanent retention. Indeed, the Ninth Circuit has made clear that “a parent’s consent to a temporary removal does not constitute consent to permanent retention unless the parent clearly and unambiguously agrees to such retention.” *Barnes*, 172 F.3d at 1179. The record here shows no such clear and unambiguous agreement. On the contrary, Gonzalez-Caballero repeatedly sought to recover Danelsy after Mena’s arrival in the United States, contacting Panamanian authorities, the U.S. military liaison, the Children’s Commission, and even the office of the Panamanian President. Her actions were those of a parent seeking to reclaim her child, not a parent who had consented to permanent separation.

Moreover, the district court failed to consider the critical fact that Gonzalez-Caballero provided Mena with Danelsy’s legal documents—birth certificates, Consular Report, passport, and military ID—not as a sign of consent to permanent retention, but as a necessary step to facilitate Danelsy’s travel and Mena’s efforts to assist Gonzalez-Caballero’s immigration. The transfer of documents is not, in itself, evidence of consent to permanent retention. *See* *Kendall*, 133 F.3d at 1200 (holding that “the mere handing over of documents does not establish consent” where the parent’s intent was to facilitate temporary travel). The court’s reliance on this conduct as evidence of consent ignores the context and the express understanding between the parties.

Finally, the district court’s finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal is undermined by her immediate and persistent efforts to recover Danelsy. Within days of the departure, she contacted police and child welfare authorities. She sought assistance from the U.S. Judge Advocate General Corps and the Institute for Children. She traveled to the office of the Panamanian President and engaged media outlets. These actions are inconsistent with a parent who has voluntarily and knowingly consented to the child’s permanent removal. *See* *Barnes*, 172 F.3d at 1179 (noting that “a parent’s efforts to recover the child after removal are strong evidence that the removal was not consented to”).

The district court’s finding of consent is therefore not supported by the evidence and constitutes a clear error. Under *Kendall*, 133 F.3d at 1199, this Court must reverse. The Hague Convention is designed to protect children from the trauma of international abduction and to ensure prompt return to the country of habitual residence. *See* *Hague Convention*, Preamble. To hold otherwise would reward a parent who exploits a temporary removal as a vehicle for permanent retention, under the guise of consent, while undermining the very purpose of the Convention.

For these reasons, the district court’s finding of consent must be reversed. The case should be remanded for a determination of whether Danelsy’s return to Panama is appropriate under the Hague Convention, including consideration of the child’s best interests under Article 13(b), which permits a court to decline return if the child would face a grave risk of physical or psychological harm.

The judgment of the district court is reversed.
