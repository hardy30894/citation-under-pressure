**ARGUMENT**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent obtained by” limits the exception to the value actually received through the fraud. Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 282 n.2 (1991) (describing the provision as excepting “debts for money … obtained by … actual fraud”). Treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are not money obtained by fraud; they are a statutory multiple imposed after the fact as a penalty. They therefore fall outside the statutory text.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that exceptions to discharge are construed narrowly to effectuate the fresh-start policy. Kelly v. Robinson, 479 U.S. 36, 47 (1986) (“[E]xceptions to discharge are to be narrowly construed.”). In Pennsylvania Department of Public Welfare v. Davenport, 495 U.S. 552 (1990), the Court held that restitution obligations constitute “debts” under § 101(11) yet remain dischargeable under Chapter 13 because they are not among the enumerated nondischargeable categories. The same textual discipline applies here: only the compensatory sum actually obtained by the debtor’s misrepresentation satisfies the “obtained by” requirement.

Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. 59 (1995), confirms that § 523(a)(2)(A) reaches only the money or property traceable to the fraud itself. The Court there parsed the identical language and required proof that the creditor’s loss was proximately caused by the debtor’s misrepresentation. Id. at 66. Statutory trebling does not meet that test; it is imposed by operation of state law irrespective of the amount the debtor pocketed.

The lower courts’ contrary view—that the entire judgment, including the punitive component, is nondischargeable—imports into the federal statute a breadth unsupported by its text. As the Ninth Circuit correctly observed in a parallel context, “only the amount of compensatory damages which were actually obtained by fraud could not be discharged.” In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir. 1991). Because the $31,382.50 in overcharges alone was obtained by the implied misrepresentation, only that sum is excepted from discharge. The remaining $62,765 in treble damages is dischargeable.

The judgment below should be reversed to the extent it declares the treble damages nondischargeable.
