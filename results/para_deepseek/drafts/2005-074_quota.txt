## ARGUMENT

The reinstatement statute, INA § 241(a)(5), 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), does not apply to aliens who reentered the United States before its effective date in 1997, and even if it did, applying it retroactively to petitioner would violate the presumption against retroactivity and due process.

### I. The Statute Does Not Reach Pre-Enactment Reentries

The plain language of § 241(a)(5) authorizes reinstatement of a prior deportation order for “an alien who has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed.” The statute speaks in the present tense—“has reentered”—which, under ordinary principles of statutory construction, indicates a reentry occurring after the statute’s enactment. See *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 438 (1987) (courts must “give effect to the plain meaning of the language used”). Congress knows how to draft statutes with explicit retroactive effect; its failure to do so here invokes the “presumption against retroactive legislation [that] is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence.” *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 265 (1994). The absence of any express statement that the statute applies to prior reentries confirms that it reaches only reentries occurring after 1997. See *Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital*, 488 U.S. 204, 208-09 (1988) (“Congressional enactments . . . will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result”).

### II. Applying the Statute Retroactively Is Impermissible

Even if the statutory text could be read to encompass pre-enactment reentries, such application would be impermissibly retroactive under the framework set forth in *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. That framework asks whether the statute “would impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party’s liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” *Id.* at 280. Here, petitioner reentered the United States in 1982, fifteen years before the reinstatement statute existed. At the time of his reentry, he had the right to seek adjustment of status based on a qualifying relative. The 1997 statute stripped away that right, attaching a new disability—a permanent bar to relief—to his completed act of reentry. That is precisely the sort of retroactive effect *Landgraf* forbids.

This Court has repeatedly applied *Landgraf* to invalidate retroactive immigration provisions. In *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 320-24 (2001), the Court held that a statute eliminating discretionary relief from deportation could not be applied retroactively to an alien who pleaded guilty before the statute’s enactment, because the alien had relied on the “settled expectations” that relief would remain available. Similarly, petitioner had a “protectable expectation” that by reentering and establishing family ties, he could one day adjust his status. See *id.* at 322. The reinstatement statute “attache[d] a new disability” to that past act, making it a “prime example of retroactive legislation.” *Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer*, 520 U.S. 939, 947 (1997) (internal quotation marks omitted).

The *Landgraf* inquiry also considers whether the new statute governs “continuing conduct” or instead applies to “completed” events. *Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 359 (1999). While remaining in the country after reentry is continuing conduct, the statute here does not penalize mere presence; it bars relief based on the *mode of entry*—the specific act of illegal reentry. That act had already been completed for fifteen years when the statute took effect. See *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70 (focusing on “whether the new provision attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment”). Reentry is a singular event, not an ongoing course of conduct.

Moreover, the presumption against retroactivity is “strong to the point of requiring clear congressional intent.” *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 316 (quoting *Lindh v. Murphy*, 521 U.S. 320, 326 (1997)). Congress provided no such clear statement here. The statutory text does not say “reentered at any time” or “regardless of when the reentry occurred.” Compare *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 317-18 (finding no clear intent where statute used “present tense” and lacked “express command”). The absence of any directive to apply the statute retroactively dictates that it be given only prospective effect. See *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 286-87.

Finally, applying the statute retroactively would raise serious due process concerns. Retroactive legislation that “deprive[s] a person of a right or interest that was previously protected” can violate the Due Process Clause. *Eastern Enterprises v. Apfel*, 524 U.S. 498, 529 (1998) (plurality opinion). Petitioner had a substantial interest in the ability to adjust his status based on his marriage to a U.S. citizen—an interest he had relied on for years. The government’s sudden imposition of a permanent bar to all relief based on conduct that was already years old when the statute enacted would be “harsh and oppressive.” *Id.* at 527 (internal quotation marks omitted). This Court should construe the statute to avoid such constitutional doubt. See *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 299-300 (citing the canon of constitutional avoidance).

For these reasons, the reinstatement statute does not apply to petitioner’s pre-1997 reentry, and even if it did, that application would be impermissibly retroactive. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
