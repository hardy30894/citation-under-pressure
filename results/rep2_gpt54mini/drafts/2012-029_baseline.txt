**Argument**

Federal courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction over every case that merely involves a patent issue. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), federal jurisdiction exists only if a well-pleaded claim either is created by federal patent law or necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of patent law. *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that a federal issue embedded in a state-law claim does not automatically confer federal jurisdiction. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 313-14 (2005); *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936); *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912).

That is especially true where the patent question is only an element of a state malpractice claim. Legal malpractice is a creature of state law, and a plaintiff must prove duty, breach, causation, and damages. *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., Inc.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). The “case within a case” method is simply the means by which a plaintiff proves causation; it does not transform the underlying state claim into a patent action. *Schaeffer v. O’Brien*, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied). As the Nebraska Supreme Court has explained, “when patent issues are merely implicated incidentally in a cause of action, however, federal courts do not have jurisdiction of the case pursuant to § 1338.” *New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 270 Neb. 264, 702 N.W.2d 336, 344 (2005).

The correct framework is *Grable*: federal jurisdiction lies only when a state-law claim necessarily raises a federal issue that is actually disputed and substantial, and when hearing the case will not disturb the congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. This Court applied that test in *Minton v. Gunn* and correctly recognized that the patent issue in a malpractice case does not satisfy it. 355 S.W.3d 634, 637-43 (Tex. 2011). The court explained that although the patent issue may be necessary and disputed, it is not substantial in the relevant sense because its resolution would not control patent law in future cases; it would matter only to the parties in that malpractice action. *Id.* at 642-43. That limitation follows the Supreme Court’s teaching that the substantiality inquiry looks to the importance of the issue “to the federal system as a whole,” not merely to the litigants. *Gunn v. Minton*, 568 U.S. 251, 260 (2013) (quoting *Gunn*, 568 U.S. at 260).

The Supreme Court’s decision in this case confirms the point. It held that Minton’s malpractice claim did not arise under federal patent law because, although the patent issue was “necessary” and “disputed,” it was not substantial in the relevant sense and federal jurisdiction would upset the state-federal balance. *Gunn v. Minton*, 568 U.S. 251, 258-63 (2013). The Court emphasized that the hypothetical patent ruling in a malpractice suit would have “no broader significance” for the federal system, because state courts’ answers would not bind future patent cases. *Id.* at 261-62. The Court also explained that states have a strong interest in regulating attorneys and adjudicating malpractice claims, while Congress did not intend to shift such ordinary state disputes into federal court. *Id.* at 264-65.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach in cases such as *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), cannot override the Supreme Court’s jurisdictional framework. *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 819 (lower courts are bound by Supreme Court precedent construing § 1338). Nor does the fact that patent law may be consulted, analyzed, or applied in a malpractice case make federal jurisdiction exclusive. The Supreme Court has squarely rejected the notion that a federal issue embedded in a state claim is enough. *Gunn*, 568 U.S. at 258-59; *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314.

Accordingly, the answer to the question presented is no: federal courts do not possess exclusive jurisdiction merely because a state-law case involves a patent issue. Where, as here, the patent question arises only as part of a state malpractice claim and its resolution has no systemic effect on patent law, jurisdiction remains with the state courts. *Gunn*, 568 U.S. at 258-65; *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808-09.
