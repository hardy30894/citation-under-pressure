The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one" where the plaintiff did not allege membership in a class or group. The Court of Appeals' holding that a plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective" is an unwarranted extension of the Equal Protection Clause.

The Equal Protection Clause is traditionally understood to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group. As the Court held in Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), the clause prohibits state action that discriminates against individuals based on their membership in a particular class. The Court has consistently applied this principle in cases such as Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303 (1880), and Truax v. Raich, 239 U.S. 33 (1915), where it struck down state laws that discriminated against particular groups.

In Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the Court held that the Equal Protection Clause is violated when state action intentionally discriminates against an individual based on their membership in a particular class. The Court stated that "the unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination." Snowden, 321 U.S. at 8. This principle is grounded in the idea that the Equal Protection Clause is intended to protect against arbitrary and discriminatory state action.

In contrast, the Court of Appeals' holding in this case would allow a plaintiff to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause without alleging membership in a class or group. This is inconsistent with the traditional understanding of the clause. As the Court held in Williamson v. Lee Optical Co., 348 U.S. 483 (1955), the Equal Protection Clause does not prohibit state action that is merely arbitrary or capricious; rather, it prohibits state action that discriminates against particular groups or classes.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that the Equal Protection Clause is not a "generalized grievance" provision. In Cotting v. Godard, 183 U.S. 79 (1901), the Court held that a plaintiff must demonstrate that they have been treated differently than others who are similarly situated in order to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. Olech has not made such a showing here.

Additionally, the Court's decision in Skinner v. Oklahoma, 316 U.S. 535 (1942), emphasizes that the Equal Protection Clause is concerned with protecting against discriminatory state action that is based on an individual's membership in a particular class or group. The Court stated that "the guaranty of equal protection of the laws is a pledge of the protection of equal laws." Skinner, 316 U.S. at 541. Olech's claim does not involve discriminatory state action based on class membership.

The Court of Appeals' holding is also inconsistent with the principle that the Equal Protection Clause is not a license for courts to review the wisdom or fairness of state action. As the Court held in Ferguson v. Skrupa, 372 U.S. 726 (1963), the clause is not a "judicial review" provision that allows courts to second-guess the decisions of state officials. Rather, it is a limited provision that prohibits state action that discriminates against particular groups or classes.

In conclusion, the Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one" where the plaintiff did not allege membership in a class or group. The Court of Appeals' holding to the contrary is an unwarranted extension of the clause and is inconsistent with the traditional understanding of the Equal Protection Clause as established in Yick Wo v. Hopkins, Strauder v. West Virginia, Truax v. Raich, Snowden v. Hughes, Williamson v. Lee Optical Co., Cotting v. Godard, and Skinner v. Oklahoma.
