The Due Process Clause does not require a special “reasoned choice” test beyond the settled constitutional requirements governing competency and waiver.

This Court has long held that a defendant may not be tried unless he has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). That standard, reaffirmed in Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), and Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), is the constitutional measure of competence. Nothing in those decisions creates a second, heightened competency inquiry for guilty pleas or waiver of counsel.

To the contrary, the Court has treated the validity of a guilty plea and waiver of counsel as turning on whether the waiver is knowing, voluntary, and intelligent, not on whether the defendant made the “best” or most “reasoned” choice. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), held that guilty pleas require an affirmative showing of understanding because a plea entails waiver of the privilege against self-incrimination, the right to trial by jury, and the right to confront accusers. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), likewise requires that waiver of counsel be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” But neither case imposes a separate competency standard more demanding than the constitutional test for competence itself.

That is why the Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” formulation is unsound. The Constitution asks whether the defendant was competent and whether the waiver was knowing and voluntary; it does not ask whether the defendant, in hindsight, made a wise decision. As this Court has explained in the closely related context of self-representation, the right to conduct one’s own defense belongs to a defendant who is mentally competent under the ordinary constitutional standard. Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975). Later, in Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993), this Court rejected the very notion that competency to plead guilty or waive counsel demands a higher level of functioning than competency to stand trial. The Court held that “the competency standard for pleading guilty or waiving the right to counsel is the same as the competency standard for standing trial.” Id. at 398. It further explained that the focus is on whether the defendant has the capacity to understand the proceedings and make a choice, not whether the choice is prudent. Id. at 400-01.

Petitioner also relies on the trial court’s ability to protect defendants through inquiry and colloquy. But this Court has never converted those procedural safeguards into a substantive “reasoned choice” test. A court must ensure that a plea is voluntary and that waiver is knowing and intelligent; it need not conduct a separate search for whether the defendant’s reasons are sensible. Boykin, 395 U.S. at 243-44; Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742, 748-49 (1970). The Constitution “does not require complete appreciation of all the consequences of a guilty plea,” only that the defendant understand the nature of the right and the consequences of abandoning it. Brady, 397 U.S. at 757.

Nor does the presence of mental illness, medication, or emotional distress alter the rule. Competence is a threshold question of capacity, and due process is satisfied so long as the defendant meets the constitutional standard and the record supports a knowing and voluntary waiver. Drope, 420 U.S. at 172-73; Godinez, 509 U.S. at 399-400. A court may inquire further when facts create a bona fide doubt about competence, Pate, 383 U.S. at 385; Drope, 420 U.S. at 180-81, but that obligation does not transform the standard itself.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed. The Due Process Clause requires application of the settled competency standard, not a heightened “reasoned choice” test. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993).
