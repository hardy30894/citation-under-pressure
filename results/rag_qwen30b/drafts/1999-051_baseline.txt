**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The Court has long held that procedural default bars federal habeas review unless the petitioner demonstrates *cause* for the default and *prejudice* from the error, or shows that a fundamental miscarriage of justice would result. See *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 485 (1986). The claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, standing alone, cannot serve as *cause* to excuse a procedural default unless that claim itself is not procedurally defaulted and is cognizable in federal habeas.

Here, petitioner Carpenter’s claim that his appellate counsel was ineffective in failing to raise a sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge on direct appeal is itself procedurally defaulted. The Ohio courts rejected this claim as untimely, and the Ohio Supreme Court affirmed. See *Edwards v. Carpenter*, 493 U.S. 1 (1989) (per curiam). The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that this defaulted claim could nonetheless serve as *cause* to excuse the default of the underlying sufficiency claim is contrary to settled precedent.

The Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that a defaulted ineffective assistance claim can function as *cause* to excuse another default. In *Diaz-Albertini v. United States*, 498 U.S. 1061 (1991), the Court denied certiorari in a case where the Court of Appeals held that a petitioner was barred from raising an ineffective assistance claim on collateral review because he had been represented by new counsel on direct appeal and thus could have raised the claim then. The Court’s denial of certiorari, while not a holding, reflects the understanding that a defaulted ineffective assistance claim cannot serve as *cause* to excuse a procedural default in another claim. Similarly, in *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991), the Court emphasized that “a procedural default does not bar federal habeas review unless the state court’s decision rests on an independent and adequate state ground.” But even where a federal claim is not barred by such a ground, the petitioner must still show *cause* and *prejudice* to overcome the default. The Court has never held that a defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can itself constitute *cause*.

The Sixth Circuit’s reasoning—that the ineffective assistance claim can excuse the default of the sufficiency claim regardless of whether it is itself defaulted—would eviscerate the procedural default doctrine. It would allow a prisoner to circumvent state procedural rules by asserting a claim of ineffective assistance that is itself barred by those same rules. Such a rule would invite abuse and undermine the finality of state court judgments. As the Court stated in *Murray v. Carrier*, “the procedural default doctrine is not a mere technicality; it is a necessary component of the federal habeas corpus system.” 477 U.S. at 485. To allow a defaulted claim to serve as *cause* for another default would render the doctrine meaningless.

Moreover, the Court has consistently required that *cause* be independent of the underlying claim. In *Stewart v. LaGrand*, 526 U.S. 115, 126 (1999), the Court held that a claim of cruel and unusual punishment based on a method of execution could not be raised in federal habeas until the prisoner had chosen that method. The Court found that the claim was not ripe until then, and thus not procedurally defaulted. But even there, the Court did not hold that a defaulted claim could excuse another default. Rather, it found that the claim was not yet cognizable. The same principle applies here: the claim of ineffective assistance of counsel is not *ripe* for federal habeas review because it is itself procedurally defaulted.

Finally, the Court has recognized that the doctrine of procedural default serves to preserve the integrity of the state court system. See *Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. 255, 263 (1989). Allowing a defaulted claim to excuse another default would undermine that principle. The state courts have already determined that Carpenter’s claim of ineffective assistance was untimely. To permit federal habeas review on that basis would be to disregard the state’s procedural rules and to treat them as mere formality.

For these reasons, the Court should affirm the judgment of the Sixth Circuit only to the extent that it concluded that the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was procedurally defaulted. The Court should reject the Sixth Circuit’s holding that a defaulted ineffective assistance claim can serve as *cause* to excuse another default. The procedural default doctrine must be respected, and the Court has never held otherwise.

**The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.**
