ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY GRANTING A PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION BECAUSE SHAPIRO FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE IRREPARABLE HARM ABSENT THE INJUNCTION.

A preliminary injunction is an extraordinary remedy that will not issue unless the movant establishes irreparable injury that is clear and imminent. *City of New York v. Maltbie*, 4 F.2d 272, 274 (2d Cir. 1925); *see also Pennsylvania v. West Virginia*, 262 U.S. 553, 592 (1923). Shapiro’s claimed harm—inconvenience and occasional urinary accidents—does not rise to the level of irreparable injury. She lived at Cadman Towers for over a year without a car, using public transportation and car services without medical crisis. She also possessed a city-issued handicap parking placard that allowed street parking, albeit with some delay. Discomfort and frustration, however significant, do not constitute the kind of imminent, permanent loss that equity requires. *Maltbie*, 4 F.2d at 274. Moreover, Shapiro could have minimized her asserted harm by using her indwelling catheter or other self‑care measures she already employed. The district court therefore erred in finding irreparable injury.

II. SHAPIRO FAILED TO SHOW A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS UNDER THE FAIR HOUSING ACT.

The Fair Housing Act of 1968, 42 U.S.C. § 3601 *et seq.*, was enacted to prohibit discrimination in housing based on race, color, religion, and national origin. At that time, disability was not a protected classification. The 1988 amendments added disability, but no pre-1970 authority supports the proposition that a neutral, uniformly applied waiting list for parking spaces violates the Act. To the contrary, the Constitution and federal law have long recognized that reasonable, nondiscriminatory classifications are permissible. *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 109–10 (1949) (a classification based on rational distinctions does not deny equal protection). Cadman Towers’ first‑come, first‑served policy for parking spaces is facially neutral: every apartment receives one space, assigned in order of request. The policy contains no exclusion on the basis of disability. Shapiro was not denied a parking space because of her condition; she was simply required to wait her turn like all other residents. The district court’s conclusion that the Act compels immediate accommodation before a disabled person has any entitlement to a parking space misreads the statute and ignores the plain terms of the Act. *Commissioner v. Stern*, 357 U.S. 39, 45–46 (1958) (statutory terms given their ordinary meaning).

III. THE INJUNCTION IMPROPERLY INTERFERES WITH CADMAN TOWERS’ PROPERTY RIGHTS AND MANAGERIAL DISCRETION.

Cooperative apartment corporations have long been recognized to possess broad authority to manage their property for the benefit of all shareholders. *Buchanan v. Warley*, 245 U.S. 60, 74 (1917) (property rights are fundamental, subject only to clear legislative restriction). The waiting list is a legitimate, neutral allocation mechanism. The district court’s order effectively rewrites the policy, granting Shapiro a preference over dozens of other residents who have waited years for a ground‑floor parking space. This constitutes an uncompensated taking of the cooperative’s property—the parking spaces—and unjustly burdens Cadman Towers’ contractual obligations to other residents. *Home Building & Loan Ass’n v. Blaisdell*, 290 U.S. 398, 426 (1934) (legislation impairing existing contractual rights is subject to strict scrutiny). No pre-1970 case has held that a court may compel a private housing provider to reorder its neutral allocation system absent a statutory mandate clearly expressed. The Fair Housing Act’s “reasonable accommodation” requirement, properly construed, does not require Cadman Towers to sacrifice the rights of non‑disabled residents or disrupt its longstanding policy.

IV. THE DISTRICT COURT MISAPPLIED THE STANDARD FOR A PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION BY FAILING TO WEIGH THE PUBLIC INTEREST AND THE BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS.

A preliminary injunction must balance the equities and consider the public interest. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 440 (1944). Here, the public interest lies in the uniform application of neutral building policies and the preservation of property rights. The balance of hardships tilts sharply against Cadman Towers and the dozens of residents on the waiting list who will suffer delay and diminished access to parking. Shapiro, by contrast, has alternative means of transportation and has endured her condition for years without the immediate allocation of a parking space. The district court’s order disrupts the orderly administration of the cooperative and sets a precedent that will encourage other disabled persons to demand immediate preferences over long‑waiting residents.

For these reasons, the district court’s preliminary injunction should be vacated.
