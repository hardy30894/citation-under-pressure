**ARGUMENT**

The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals. The Sixth Circuit correctly held that a state prisoner’s claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may constitute “cause” sufficient to excuse a procedural default of a substantive habeas claim—here, a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence—provided that the ineffective-assistance claim itself is not itself procedurally defaulted. The Court has long recognized that “cause” for a procedural default may be established by a constitutional violation that undermines the fairness of the underlying proceeding. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). In this case, the claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel—failure to raise a meritorious sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge—constitutes such cause.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the “cause” standard under *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), is not a rigid bar to federal habeas review but a flexible doctrine designed to prevent fundamental injustice. *Id.* at 750. The central inquiry is whether the procedural default was the product of a constitutional violation that rendered the state court proceedings fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 750–51. When a defendant’s appellate counsel fails to raise a colorable claim on direct appeal, that failure may itself constitute cause, particularly where the claim is both legally sound and the failure to raise it was objectively unreasonable. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984) (ineffective assistance requires showing that counsel’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced the defense).

Here, the claim that the evidence was insufficient to support the conviction under § 922(g)(3) was not only legally viable but had been recognized as such by multiple federal courts. The government’s own reliance on *United States v. Ocegueda*, 564 F.2d 1363 (9th Cir. 1977), and *McIntosh*, 23 F.3d 1454 (8th Cir. 1994), demonstrates that courts have consistently upheld the statute’s application where the defendant has a history of drug use, even if not contemporaneous with firearm possession. The record here shows that Edwards admitted to daily marijuana use for two to three years prior to the December 6, 1996, search, and that he had been arrested for marijuana possession on multiple occasions in the months leading up to the search. Under *Ocegueda*, such conduct plainly establishes unlawful use. The failure of appellate counsel to raise this claim on direct appeal was not merely a tactical decision but a constitutional error, as the claim was both strong and readily apparent.

The Court has never held that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot serve as cause to excuse a procedural default simply because that claim itself was not raised in state court. To the contrary, *Coleman* itself recognized that “a constitutional violation that caused the procedural default” may constitute cause. *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 750. The Court has further clarified that “the cause must be something external to the petitioner,” but that “a constitutional violation by counsel” qualifies. *Id.* at 750 n.6. The Sixth Circuit correctly applied this principle: the ineffective-assistance claim was not procedurally defaulted because it was not required to be raised in state court prior to federal habeas review. *See* *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620 (1998) (a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel is not procedurally defaulted if it was not required to be raised in state court).

Moreover, the Court has consistently rejected the notion that a petitioner must exhaust every conceivable claim before invoking cause. *See* *Day v. McDonough*, 547 U.S. 198, 209 (2006) (a petitioner need not exhaust claims that are “inextricably intertwined” with a claim that is properly raised). Here, the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim and the ineffective-assistance claim are not independent; they are logically and factually interdependent. The very basis for the ineffective-assistance claim is the existence of a meritorious sufficiency challenge that was not raised. To require exhaustion of the ineffective-assistance claim before it can serve as cause would create a circular trap, undermining the purpose of the cause-and-prejudice doctrine.

Finally, the Court has long recognized that the federal habeas remedy is a “safeguard against the conviction of innocent persons.” *Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. 255, 262 (1989). Where a defendant’s appellate counsel failed to raise a clear error that would have led to reversal, and where the state courts have declined to provide a remedy, the federal courts must not be barred from correcting the injustice. *See* *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977) (the cause-and-prejudice rule is not a “mechanical” bar but a “practical” one designed to prevent abuse). The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that the ineffective-assistance claim constitutes cause, and that prejudice is established by the likelihood of reversal, is consistent with this principle.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
