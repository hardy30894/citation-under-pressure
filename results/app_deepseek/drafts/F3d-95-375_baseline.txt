ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Striking Plaintiffs’ Expert Witnesses as a Discovery Sanction.

A district court’s discovery sanction must be “just and proportional” to the offending conduct. *FDIC v. Conner*, 20 F.3d 1376, 1380 (5th Cir. 1994). Here, the court struck the testimony of four experts—a case-dispositive sanction—without considering less drastic alternatives. The record shows that Plaintiffs’ experts failed to appear for depositions only because they lacked sufficient time to form opinions, not because of bad faith or willful disobedience. When the court later ordered depositions completed by July 30, 1993, Plaintiffs attempted to comply but were unable to produce all experts within the truncated timeframe. The court never offered a continuance, monetary sanctions, or an order compelling compliance before resorting to the extreme remedy of exclusion. This Court has held that “[dismissal is a drastic sanction that should be imposed only when the failure to comply is clear and the party has been given a second chance.” *Poulis v. State Farm Fire & Cas. Co.*, 747 F.2d 863, 868 (3d Cir. 1984) (cited approvingly in *Conner*, 20 F.3d at 1380). The district court gave Plaintiffs no such second chance; it struck the experts after a single missed deadline, despite the Plaintiffs’ prior good-faith efforts to identify them. The sanction was disproportionate and constituted an abuse of discretion.

II. The District Court Erred in Excluding the Testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson Under *Daubert*.

The district court conducted a flawed *Daubert* analysis. *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 113 S.Ct. 2786, 125 L.Ed.2d 469 (1993). The court excluded Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson without holding a hearing or allowing Plaintiffs to supplement their experts’ reports. The court appears to have applied too stringent a standard, requiring certainty rather than reliability. In *General Elec. Co. v. Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136, 143, 118 S.Ct. 512, 139 L.Ed.2d 508 (1997), the Supreme Court clarified that a district court abuses its discretion when it “applies the wrong legal standard” in a *Daubert* ruling. Here, the court’s summary conclusion that the opinions were “not based on reliable methodology” was unsupported by any analysis of the scientific principles underlying the experts’ causation theories. *Daubert* requires a flexible inquiry, not a rigid checklist. *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 150, 119 S.Ct. 1167, 143 L.Ed.2d 238 (1999). The district court failed to give Plaintiffs a meaningful opportunity to establish admissibility, thereby abusing its discretion.

III. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Existed.

The district court granted summary judgment solely because it struck or excluded Plaintiffs’ expert testimony. Without that testimony, Plaintiffs could not prove causation. But because the sanctions and *Daubert* rulings were erroneous, the court should have considered the experts’ opinions as competent evidence. Once those opinions are admitted, genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether Plaintiffs’ exposure to chemicals at the superfund sites caused their injuries and property damage. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322, 106 S.Ct. 2548, 91 L.Ed.2d 265 (1986) (summary judgment only appropriate if no genuine dispute of material fact). The court’s error in excluding expert testimony directly led to an improper grant of summary judgment.

IV. This Court Has Jurisdiction Over the Premature Notice of Appeal.

Plaintiffs filed their notice of appeal on October 19, 1994, before the district court entered final judgment on March 1, 1995. However, a premature notice of appeal is effective if “a final judgment has been entered before the appeal is decided.” *FirsTier Mortgage Co. v. Investors Mortgage Ins. Co.*, 498 U.S. 269, 273, 111 S.Ct. 648, 112 L.Ed.2d 743 (1991). Here, the final judgment was entered while the appeal was pending, and the notice of appeal should be treated as an appeal from that judgment. This Court should therefore consider the merits of Plaintiffs’ claims.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s orders striking Plaintiffs’ expert witnesses, excluding the testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson, and granting summary judgment, and remand for further proceedings.
