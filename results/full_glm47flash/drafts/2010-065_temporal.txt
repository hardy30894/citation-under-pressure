**Argument**

**I. Bond Lacks Standing to Challenge the Statute on Tenth Amendment Grounds**

The Tenth Amendment is a structural limitation on federal power, not a private right of action. Consequently, private parties lack standing to challenge federal statutes on Tenth Amendment grounds absent state action. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, the Supreme Court held that private utility companies lacked standing to challenge the federal government’s sale of electricity on Tenth Amendment grounds. The Court explained that "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment." 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939). This principle applies with equal force to Bond. As a private citizen, she cannot assert a Tenth Amendment challenge to a valid exercise of federal power. The remedy for a Tenth Amendment violation lies with the political branches or the states, not with a private litigant.

**II. The Statute Is a Valid Exercise of the Treaty Power**

Even if Bond possessed standing, the statute is a valid exercise of Congress’s Article I, Section 8 powers. The Constitution grants Congress the power to make treaties, and the Necessary and Proper Clause authorizes Congress to enact laws to execute those treaties. In *Missouri v. Holland*, the Supreme Court recognized that the treaty power is an independent power, not dependent on the legislative power. 252 U.S. 416, 433 (1920). The Court further noted that "it is obvious that there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could." Id. at 433. Congress enacted 18 U.S.C. § 229 to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention, a valid treaty. Under *McCulloch v. Maryland*, Congress may enact laws "necessary and proper" to execute the powers of the Government. 17 U.S. 316, 421 (1819). Therefore, the statute is a valid exercise of federal authority.
