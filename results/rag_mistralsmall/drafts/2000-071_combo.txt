**ARGUMENT**

I. Milford’s Denial of Access Did Not Violate the First Amendment Because It Was Based on Subject, Not Viewpoint, Discrimination.

The Second Circuit correctly held that Milford’s denial of the Good News Club’s application was constitutional subject discrimination, not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. *See Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) (“[T]he government violates the First Amendment when it denies access to a speaker solely to suppress the point of view he espouses.”). The Club’s proposed activities—singing religious songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing Scripture, and praying—were quintessentially religious worship, not merely moral or character development from a religious perspective. *Compare Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) (noting that “singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles” are forms of speech that may be religious in nature), *with Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313 (1952) (upholding released-time programs where religious instruction occurred off school grounds and did not involve school sponsorship of worship).

Milford’s policy explicitly excluded religious worship from its limited public forum. *See* N.Y. Educ. Law § 414 (McKinney 1990) (authorizing use of school facilities for “social, civic and recreational meetings” but prohibiting “religious purposes”). The Club’s activities fell outside this permissible scope. *See Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 405 (1953) (upholding permit denial for religious services in a public park where the ordinance was “content-neutral” and applied equally to all religious groups). The Club sought to use school facilities for religious instruction and prayer, not secular moral instruction. *See Abington School Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 224 (1963) (distinguishing between permissible “secular education” and impermissible “religious indoctrination” in public schools).

II. Milford’s Policy Did Not Violate the Establishment Clause Because It Excluded All Religious Worship, Not Just Christianity.

Milford’s policy was neutral toward religion and applied equally to all faiths. *See Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 70 (1953) (holding that a municipality may not “prefer[] some religious groups over this one” but may enforce neutral laws of general applicability). The Club’s proposed activities—prayer, hymn-singing, and proselytization—were indistinguishable from worship. *See Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ. of Sch. Dist. No. 71*, 333 U.S. 203, 211 (1948) (invalidating released-time programs where religious instruction occurred *on* public school grounds). Milford’s policy avoided the constitutional infirmities identified in *McCollum* by excluding all religious worship, not just Christian worship. *See Zorach*, 343 U.S. at 315 (upholding released-time programs where religious instruction occurred *off* school grounds and did not involve school sponsorship).

III. The Club’s Proposed Activities Were Not Merely “Moral Instruction” but Religious Worship.

The Club’s activities were not merely moral instruction from a religious perspective but religious worship. *See Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 269 n.6 (recognizing that “singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles” are religious in nature). The Club’s meetings included prayer, proselytization, and scriptural memorization—activities that are inherently religious. *See Schempp*, 374 U.S. at 224 (distinguishing between permissible “secular education” and impermissible “religious indoctrination”). The Club’s own materials described its mission as teaching children to “give God first place in your life” and inviting children to “believe on the Lord Jesus Christ” for salvation. *See* App. 10-15. These are not secular moral lessons but religious worship.

IV. Milford’s Policy Was a Valid Time, Place, and Manner Restriction.

Even if the Club’s activities were deemed speech, Milford’s policy was a valid time, place, and manner restriction. *See Heffron v. Int’l Soc’y for Krishna Consciousness*, 452 U.S. 640, 648 (1981) (upholding content-neutral restrictions on speech in a limited public forum). Milford’s policy was viewpoint-neutral and applied equally to all religious groups. *See Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806 (upholding content-neutral restrictions on speech in a limited public forum). The policy served the important government interest of avoiding the appearance of school endorsement of religion. *See Schempp*, 374 U.S. at 224 (recognizing the government’s interest in avoiding “sponsorship, financial support, and active involvement of the sovereign in religious activity”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Milford’s denial of the Good News Club’s application did not violate the First Amendment, and its policy was justified by the Establishment Clause. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.
