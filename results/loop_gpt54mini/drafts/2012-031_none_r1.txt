**ARGUMENT**

Rule 23(b)(3) requires more than a plausible story about classwide damages. A district court may certify a class only after determining, on competent evidence, that common questions predominate and that the proposed method will measure the legally relevant injury on a classwide basis. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 42-43 (1940); *Cohen v. Beneficial Indus. Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 551-52 (1949). That requirement cannot be satisfied by a damages model untethered to the sole theory of liability the court accepted. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264-66 (1946); *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563-64 (1931).

The Third Circuit allowed certification even though the class’s expert model did not isolate damages caused by the one antitrust theory the district court certified. That was error. A class damages methodology must “fit” the theory of injury that will be tried. If the model measures the effect of rejected theories as well as the certified one, it does not establish classwide damages for the actual claim. *Miller v. Mackey Int’l, Inc.*, 452 F.2d 424, 429 (5th Cir. 1969); *Brown v. Cole*, 198 F.2d 967, 971 (5th Cir. 1952). A court cannot assume predominance from an expert’s label if the substance of the model sweeps too broadly. *West v. Radio-Keith-Orpheum Corp.*, 70 F.2d 621, 626 (2d Cir. 1934).

This Court’s pre-1970 precedents require actual proof, not speculation. Findings supporting certification must rest on evidence with a rational relationship to the proposition certified. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948); *Morrison v. Texas Co.*, 289 U.S. 501, 507 (1933). And class treatment is unavailable where individual questions dominate or the purported common proof does not genuinely bind the class together. *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 122 (1934); *Sam Fox Publ’g Co. v. United States*, 366 U.S. 683, 689 (1961). The district court therefore had to determine whether Plaintiffs’ proof would actually establish damages common to the class, not merely whether an economist had produced a formula. *Moore v. New York Cotton Exch.*, 270 U.S. 593, 610 (1926); *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 303 (1853).

That was especially important here because antitrust damages must be causally linked to the challenged restraint. While uncertainty in the amount of damages does not defeat recovery, the plaintiff still must show that the damages flowed from the proven violation. *Southern Pac. Co. v. Darnell-Taenzer Lumber Co.*, 245 U.S. 531, 533-34 (1918); *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66. The class’s model here did not make that showing for the class claim as certified. Instead, it bundled together effects from multiple theories, some of which the district court excluded from the certification order. A damages model that includes injury from uncertified theories cannot support classwide predominance on the surviving theory. *Brenham v. Southern Pac. Co.*, 328 F.2d 1, 5 (5th Cir. 1964); *Union Pac. R.R. Co. v. Eisenmenger*, 256 F.2d 246, 248 (8th Cir. 1958).

The premise of representative litigation does not rescue the certification order. Class treatment is proper only when the members’ claims are sufficiently aligned that a representative judgment will decide a common dispute. *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) at 303; *Hansberry*, 311 U.S. at 42-43. Where the proposed damages proof does not correspond to the certified theory, the action will require individualized proof of injury and causation despite the class label. That defeats predominance and renders certification improper. *Cohen*, 337 U.S. at 551-52; *Miller*, 452 F.2d at 429.

The judgment below should be reversed. A district court may not certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages can be measured and quantified on a classwide basis for the theory actually certified. Here, the record did not supply that proof, and the court of appeals erred in affirming certification nonetheless. *Hansberry*, 311 U.S. at 42-43; *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66; *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395.
