**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that a plaintiff’s subjective unawareness—stemming from a failure to read or recall disclosed information—can defeat the three-year statute of limitations in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). That interpretation conflicts with settled principles that “actual knowledge” includes knowledge fairly chargeable when a defendant provides clear, accessible information. This Court should reverse.

**I. Actual Knowledge Does Not Require Subjective Awareness When Information Is Provided**

A party who receives a document containing relevant facts is charged with knowledge of its contents. *Upton v. Tribilcock*, 91 U.S. 45, 48 (1875) (“It will not do for a man to enter into a contract, and, when called upon to respond to its obligations, to say that he did not read it.”). Similarly, service of a notice “was equivalent to actual knowledge.” *The “Steamer” John H. Pearson*, 121 U.S. 469, 474 (1887). An insured “is bound to know the contents of his policy.” *Hagan v. Scottish Union & National Ins. Co.*, 186 U.S. 423, 432 (1902). Here, Intel provided Sulyma with Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and website disclosures detailing the alternative investments and their risks. Under these authorities, Sulyma had actual knowledge of the information those documents contained, regardless of whether he read or remembered them.

**II. Knowledge of the Facts, Not Their Legal Significance, Triggers the Limitations Period**

The statute requires “actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” not knowledge that the conduct was imprudent. *United States v. Patten*, 226 U.S. 525, 535 (1913) (“Knowledge of the facts is sufficient; it is not necessary that the party should know that they constitute a crime.”). In fraud cases, the limitations period runs from discovery of the facts, not from appreciation of their legal effect. *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 140 (1879) (statute begins when “the party has reasonable ground to suspect” the fraud); *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. 342, 348 (1874) (concealment prevents running until “discovery of the facts”). Sulyma knew the facts—that his retirement funds were invested in hedge funds and private equity—from the disclosures. That he did not also know that those investments were imprudent is irrelevant.

**III. A Plaintiff Cannot Avoid Limitations by Claiming Ignorance of Disclosed Information**

A person is charged with knowledge of obvious facts. *Texas & Pacific Ry. Co. v. Behymer*, 189 U.S. 468, 470 (1903) (plaintiff “must be held to have known” what was “open and obvious”). Every person “is presumed to know” what is publicly available. *The “City of New York”*, 147 U.S. 72, 82 (1893). Sulyma’s self-serving declaration that he was “unaware” and did “not recall” reading the documents cannot create a genuine dispute when the undisputed record shows Intel provided him with clear, repeated disclosures. To hold otherwise would allow plaintiffs to defeat summary judgment by simply averring ignorance, undermining the statute’s purpose of ensuring repose.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Rule Conflicts with the Purpose of § 1113(2)**

Congress eliminated the constructive-knowledge provision in 1987, but it did not intend to allow plaintiffs to avoid limitations by willful blindness. The rule that a party is bound by what he had the opportunity to know is deeply rooted. *Upton*, 91 U.S. at 48; *John H. Pearson*, 121 U.S. at 474. The Ninth Circuit’s requirement of subjective awareness invites gamesmanship and contradicts the principle that statutes of limitations “are to be strictly construed.” *Wood*, 101 U.S. at 139. Intel’s full disclosure gave Sulyma actual knowledge as a matter of law.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
