**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE "TRUE THREAT" EXCEPTION TO THE FIRST AMENDMENT REQUIRES PROOF OF THE DEFENDANT’S SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN.**

The First Amendment protects speech unless it falls within the narrow exception for "true threats." *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). The Supreme Court has consistently held that the "true threat" exception requires the government to prove the defendant subjectively intended to threaten. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707 (1969) ("The First Amendment does not protect speech that is intended to be taken as a threat."). This requirement ensures that the government does not punish speech simply because it is offensive or violent, but only when the speaker actually intends to communicate a threat. *Cohen v. California*, 403 U.S. 15, 20 (1971) (holding the government may not prohibit the use of force except where advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action).

The Third Circuit’s objective standard, which looks at what a reasonable speaker would foresee, conflates the speaker’s intent with the listener’s reaction. *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991). This standard is inconsistent with Supreme Court precedent, which emphasizes that the government must prove the defendant’s specific intent to threaten. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 707. Under the objective standard, a speaker could be convicted for statements that were merely hyperbolic or artistic expression, even if they did not intend to threaten anyone. *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (establishing that the government may not punish abstract advocacy of law violation except where such advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action).

**II. THE OBJECTIVE STANDARD IS INCOMPATIBLE WITH THE FIRST AMENDMENT’S PROTECTION OF ARTISTIC EXPRESSION.**

Elonis’s statements were rap lyrics intended as artistic expression. *Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Ass’n*, 564 U.S. 786, 799 (2011) (recognizing the First Amendment protects the right to receive and view information and ideas, including those that are offensive). The Supreme Court has held that the government cannot ban speech based on its content unless it falls within a narrow exception, such as the "true threat" exception. *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 389 (1992) (stating the First Amendment does not permit a government to prohibit the expression of an idea because society finds the idea itself offensive or disagreeable). Because Elonis’s posts were artistic, the government must prove he subjectively intended to threaten, not that a reasonable person might misinterpret them as threats.

**III. STATUTORY INTERPRETATION REQUIRES A SUBJECTIVE INTENT STANDARD.**

The statute criminalizes the transmission of threats. *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 71 (1994) (emphasizing that statutory interpretation requires looking at the defendant’s intent). The government must prove that the defendant intended to communicate a threat. The Third Circuit’s objective standard effectively creates a new crime of "reckless speech," which is not authorized by the statute. *X-Citement Video*, 513 U.S. at 71. Therefore, the conviction must be reversed because the government failed to prove Elonis’s subjective intent to threaten.

**IV. THE THIRD CIRCUIT’S STANDARD IS INCONSISTENT WITH SUPREME COURT PRECEDENT.**

The Third Circuit’s decision in *Kosma* conflicts with the Supreme Court’s modern approach to the "true threat" doctrine. *Kosma*, 951 F.2d at 557. The Supreme Court has moved toward a standard that requires proof of the defendant’s intent. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 707. The Third Circuit’s objective standard would allow the government to punish speech based on the reaction of the listener, rather than the intent of the speaker. This is precisely what the First Amendment forbids. *R.A.V.*, 505 U.S. at 389. Accordingly, the conviction must be reversed.
