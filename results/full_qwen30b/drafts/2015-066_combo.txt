**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not prohibit the use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), provided the conviction is valid under the applicable constitutional standards governing tribal sovereignty and due process. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Bryant, Jr.*, 769 F.3d 671 (9th Cir. 2014), and its subsequent refusal to rehear the matter en banc, rest on a fundamental misreading of the constitutional framework governing tribal courts and the nature of recidivist statutes. The Court should reverse, for the following reasons.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings, and Thus Cannot Render an Uncounseled Tribal Conviction Inadmissible as a Predicate Offense.**

The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies only to federal and state criminal proceedings. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978), reaffirmed that “the sovereign power to punish tribal offenders” is “the continued exercise of retained tribal sovereignty.” This principle is rooted in the long-standing recognition that Indian tribes are distinct, self-governing political communities with inherent authority to govern their members and administer justice within their territories. *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 384 (1886). The Bill of Rights, including the Sixth Amendment, does not apply to tribal courts. *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (citing *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 323); *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) (noting that “the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes”).

Because the Sixth Amendment does not govern tribal court proceedings, a conviction obtained in such a forum—even without appointed counsel—cannot violate the Sixth Amendment. The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that such a conviction is “constitutionally infirm” because it lacks a Sixth Amendment right to counsel is therefore legally untenable. *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389, 1394–95 (9th Cir. 1989), relied on a flawed premise: that tribal court proceedings must conform to the Sixth Amendment. That premise is contrary to settled precedent. The Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968, 25 U.S.C. § 1302, explicitly provides that tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment, but instead are required to afford “procedural due process” under the ICRA. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 323–24. The ICRA’s due process standard is not coextensive with the Sixth Amendment, and Congress has not mandated that tribal courts provide counsel in all cases. *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004) (Congress has plenary power over Indian tribes); *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 323–24.

**II. The Use of Valid Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses in Recidivist Statutes Is Consistent with the Principles of Comity and Respect for Tribal Sovereignty.**

Federal courts have long recognized the legitimacy of tribal court judgments and extended comity to them. *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997). Comity does not require federal courts to impose federal procedural standards on tribal courts. *Iowa Mutual Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987). The Supreme Court has emphasized that “federal courts must also be careful to respect tribal jurisprudence along with the special customs and practical limitations of tribal court systems.” *Wilson*, 127 F.3d at 811 (citing *Hilton v. Guyot*, 159 U.S. 113, 205 (1895)). To hold that a tribal court conviction is inadmissible in a federal prosecution simply because it did not afford counsel under the Sixth Amendment is to impose a federal procedural standard that is not constitutionally required and that undermines tribal self-governance.

The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that tribal court convictions be “coextensive” with the Sixth Amendment right to counsel imposes “judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts,” a practice the Supreme Court has warned against. *Wilson*, 127 F.3d at 810. Such judicial paternalism “intrudes needlessly on tribal self-government,” *Santa Clara Pueblo*, 436 U.S. at 71, and contradicts the principle that “the federal courts should not interfere with tribal self-government unless there is a clear violation of federal law or constitutional rights.” *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 323.

**III. The Use of Prior Convictions in Recidivist Statutes Does Not Require the Same Burden of Proof as the Guilt Phase of a Criminal Trial.**

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), to elevate the burden of proof in the context of predicate offenses is misplaced. *Winship* established that the government must prove each element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. But *Winship* did not address the use of prior convictions in recidivist statutes. The Supreme Court has long recognized that recidivist statutes “penalize only the last offense committed by the defendant,” not the prior convictions themselves. *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008); *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 747 (1994) (citing *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. at 386). The punishment under § 117(a) is for the current domestic assault, not for the prior offenses.
