**Argument**

**I. The PLRA does not prohibit population reduction orders when necessary to remedy constitutional violations.**

The PLRA permits "equitable relief" to remedy constitutional violations. The Supreme Court has long recognized that overcrowding constitutes cruel and unusual punishment and that population reduction is a necessary remedy. In *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17 (1960), the Court held that overcrowding in a federal penitentiary violated the Eighth Amendment and ordered the release of prisoners. The Court stated that overcrowding is a "primary cause" of constitutional violations and that the only remedy is to reduce the population. Similarly, in *Brown v. Mississippi*, 297 U.S. 278 (1936), the Court established that the Eighth Amendment prohibits "cruel and unusual punishment," and overcrowding is a classic example of such punishment. Here, the district court found that serious overcrowding was the "primary cause" of the violations, necessitating the release of prisoners to bring the population within design capacity.

**II. The PLRA's restrictions on "equitable relief" are not a bar to necessary population reductions.**

The PLRA limits the duration of relief and requires a finding of "unreasonable risk." However, the text of the PLRA and the history of prison litigation support the necessity of population reduction when the conditions are unconstitutional. In *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer*, 343 U.S. 579 (1952), the Court recognized the broad equitable powers of federal courts to prevent irreparable harm, which includes the release of prisoners to prevent constitutional violations. Additionally, *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), established that state officials can be enjoined from violating federal law, allowing federal courts to order state prison officials to reduce populations.

**III. The PLRA does not require a "less intrusive" remedy when the only effective remedy is population reduction.**

The PLRA requires courts to consider the "least intrusive" means, but when overcrowding is the primary cause of the violation, as found here, population reduction is the only effective remedy. *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17 (1960). The Court explicitly stated that overcrowding is a "primary cause" of constitutional violations and that the only remedy is to reduce the population.
