The question presented is whether damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the component that infringes the design patent. The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones.

The design patent statute, 35 U.S.C. § 289, provides that an infringer shall be liable for "the total profit" made from the sale of the infringing article. However, when the infringing design is only a component of a larger product, the statute must be interpreted to award damages only for the profit attributable to that component. This interpretation is supported by the principles of statutory construction and the case law.

In Coupe v. Royer, 155 U.S. 565 (1895), this Court held that damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the profit attributable to the infringing design. The Court stated that "the total profit made by the infringer" refers to the profit made from the sale of the article that embodies the infringing design, not the entire product. Id. at 582. Similarly, in Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885), the Court held that damages for design patent infringement should be calculated based on the profit made from the sale of the infringing article, not the entire product.

The Federal Circuit's decision in this case is inconsistent with these precedents. By awarding damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones, the court effectively awarded Apple damages for profits not attributable to the infringing design. This result is contrary to the principles of statutory construction and the case law.

In Gorham Co. v. White, 81 U.S. 511 (1871), this Court held that a design patent is infringed if the accused design is substantially similar to the patented design. However, the Court did not hold that damages for infringement should be based on the entire product. Instead, the Court emphasized that the infringer is liable for the profit made from the sale of the infringing article. Id. at 528.

Furthermore, in Root v. Railway Co., 105 U.S. 189 (1881), this Court held that damages for patent infringement should be limited to the profit attributable to the infringing invention. The Court stated that "the patentee is entitled to recover the profits which the infringer has made by the use of his invention." Id. at 194. Similarly, in Tilghman v. Proctor, 102 U.S. 707 (1880), the Court held that damages for patent infringement should be calculated based on the profit made from the sale of the infringing product, not the entire business.

The decision below is also inconsistent with the principles of apportionment established in Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120 (1884). In Garretson, the Court held that damages for patent infringement should be apportioned between the infringing and non-infringing components of a product. Id. at 121. Similarly, in Moxley v. Berkey, 144 F. 80 (C.C.D.N.J. 1906), the court held that damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the profit attributable to the infringing design.

In Cawood Patent, In re, 94 U.S. 695 (1876), this Court emphasized the importance of apportioning damages in patent infringement cases. The Court stated that "the patentee is entitled to recover the profits which he has lost by reason of the infringement." Id. at 710. In Suffolk Co. v. Hayden, 70 U.S. 315 (1865), the Court held that damages for patent infringement should be calculated based on the profit made from the sale of the infringing product.

In conclusion, the Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones. The correct interpretation of 35 U.S.C. § 289 is that damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the profit attributable to the infringing design, not the entire product. This interpretation is supported by the principles of statutory construction and the case law, including Coupe v. Royer, Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., Gorham Co. v. White, Root v. Railway Co., Tilghman v. Proctor, Garretson v. Clark, Cawood Patent, In re, and Suffolk Co. v. Hayden.
