**Argument**

The district court should have suppressed the handgun because the officers lacked the particularized, objective basis required by the Fourth Amendment to frisk Michael Soares. A pat-down search is not justified by a hunch, nor by generalized concern arising from a traffic stop. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer may frisk a person only when he can point to specific and articulable facts that, taken together with rational inferences, warrant a belief that the person is armed and presently dangerous. *Id.* at 21, 27. The government bears the burden of showing that standard was met. *Id.* at 27; *Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 64 (1968).

That burden was not carried here. The stop began as a routine traffic stop for headlights and taillights. Standing alone, such a stop does not authorize a frisk of a passenger. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 23-24. Soares was not seen brandishing a weapon, making a threatening gesture, or attempting to flee. The officers observed only that, after being ordered to remain still, he became agitated, moved his hands, and verbally protested the stop. But *Sibron* squarely rejected the notion that nervousness, association with others, or mere presence in a setting viewed by police as suspicious can substitute for the individualized suspicion needed for a frisk. 392 U.S. at 62-64. Likewise, in *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1969), the Court held that a person’s proximity to suspected wrongdoing does not justify a pat-down absent reasonable belief that that particular person is armed and dangerous. *Id.* at 91-94. Soares’s conduct, while perhaps annoying or disrespectful, did not rise to the level of specific evidence that he was armed.

Nor did the late hour, high-crime location, or prior movements in the car supply the missing justification. The Supreme Court has repeatedly made clear that presence in an area thought to be dangerous cannot by itself authorize a frisk. *Brown v. Texas*, 443 U.S. 47, 52 (1969); *Sibron*, 392 U.S. at 62-63. The officers here relied in part on “furtive movements” before the car stopped. But ambiguous movement, especially inside a vehicle during a traffic stop, is too slender a reed to support a frisk of every passenger. *Terry* requires more than an officer’s inchoate and unparticularized suspicion or “hunch.” 392 U.S. at 27. The record does not show that Soares reached toward a waistband, concealed an object, or made any specific motion indicating that he was armed.

The government may argue that officer safety concerns during a traffic stop justify the frisk. But *Terry* does not dispense with individualized suspicion. 392 U.S. at 24, 27. Even where officers reasonably fear danger, the Fourth Amendment still requires a particularized basis for believing the person to be frisked is armed. *Sibron*, 392 U.S. at 63-64. The officers here had several alternatives short of a frisk: they could have maintained control of the scene, continued verbal commands, or monitored Soares without physically searching him. The constitutional inquiry is not whether a frisk might have been prudent in hindsight, but whether the officers had a reasonable basis before the search. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21-22. On this record, they did not.

The government also cannot justify the frisk as incident to a lawful arrest, because Soares was frisked before arrest and the record shows no probable cause to arrest him at that point. A search incident to arrest requires a valid arrest supported by probable cause. *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752, 762-63 (1969). Here, the gun was discovered only after the frisk and struggle, so the search cannot be bootstrapped into validity by the evidence it produced. *Sibron*, 392 U.S. at 63-64.

Finally, suppressing the gun is the proper remedy because the firearm was the direct product of the unconstitutional frisk. Evidence obtained by exploiting an illegal search must be excluded. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484-88 (1963). The handgun was found only because officers removed Soares from the car, attempted to pat him down, and recovered the gun during the resulting confrontation. Without the unlawful frisk, there would have been no seizure of the firearm and no felon-in-possession case.

The Fourth Amendment does not permit the police to frisk a passenger merely because he is in a car stopped late at night in a high-crime area and reacts angrily to police commands. The Constitution demands “specific reasonable inferences” pointing to the person as armed and dangerous. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27. Those facts were absent here. The suppression order should therefore be reversed.
