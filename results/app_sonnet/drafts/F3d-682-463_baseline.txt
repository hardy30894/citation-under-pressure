ARGUMENT

I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, drawing all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party. Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986); Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) (“[T]he evidence of the non-movant is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in his favor.”). Summary judgment is proper only where “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact.” Anderson, 477 U.S. at 247–48. Because the record here permits a reasonable jury to find that Ottobre’s conduct was sexual in nature and that Arrow Energy failed to remedy it, the district court’s judgment must be reversed.

II. A Reasonable Jury Could Find That Ottobre Harassed Wasek “Because of Sex.”

Title VII prohibits discrimination “because of . . . sex,” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), and the Supreme Court has squarely held that this prohibition reaches same-sex harassment. Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc., 523 U.S. 75, 79 (1998) (“Title VII’s prohibition of discrimination ‘because of . . . sex’ . . . [protects] male victims [from] discriminat[ion] . . . in the terms and conditions of their employment [by] male co-workers.”). The plaintiff need only show that the conduct “constituted discrimination because of sex,” and Oncale identified several evidentiary routes for making that showing, including a showing that “the harasser was homosexual” and acted out of “sexual desire.” Id. at 80.

Wasek’s evidence fits squarely within this framework. Ottobre repeatedly grabbed Wasek’s buttocks, poked him in the rear with a hammer handle and a sucker rod, and told Wasek that he had “a pretty mouth,” “pretty lips,” and that he “know[s] you like it sweetheart.” This is not generic workplace hazing; it is physical touching of an intimate nature accompanied by comments evincing sexual interest. A reasonable jury, crediting Wasek’s testimony as it must on summary judgment, could conclude that Ottobre’s conduct was sexual in nature and directed at Wasek because of his sex, precisely the inference Oncale contemplated. See Oncale, 523 U.S. at 80–81 (explaining that “[t]he objective severity of harassment should be judged from the perspective of a reasonable person in the plaintiff’s position” and that same-sex harassment claims are to be evaluated “without artificial limits”). The district court’s conclusion that no jury could find sexual motivation improperly resolved a credibility question that belongs to the factfinder. Anderson, 477 U.S. at 255.

Michigan’s Elliott-Larsen Civil Rights Act tracks this same analysis. Michigan courts look to federal Title VII precedent for guidance on hostile-work-environment claims, Radtke v. Everett, 442 Mich. 368, 381–82, 501 N.W.2d 155, 162 (1993), and require proof that the harassment was based on sex, Corley v. Detroit Bd. of Educ., 470 Mich. 274, 278, 681 N.W.2d 342, 345 (2004). Because a jury could find that Ottobre’s conduct was sexually motivated under the Oncale standard, Wasek’s ELCRA claim must proceed alongside his Title VII claim.

III. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on Employer Liability.

To prevail on a hostile-work-environment claim, a plaintiff must show: (1) he is a member of a protected class; (2) he was subjected to unwelcome sexual harassment; (3) the harassment was based on sex; (4) the harassment created a hostile work environment; and (5) a basis exists for employer liability. Williams v. Gen. Motors Corp., 187 F.3d 553, 560–61 (6th Cir. 1999). Where, as here, the harassment is perpetrated by a coworker rather than a supervisor, the employer is liable if it “knew or should have known of the charged sexual harassment and failed to implement prompt and appropriate corrective action.” EEOC v. Harbert-Yeargin, Inc., 266 F.3d 498, 520 (6th Cir. 2001) (quoting Blankenship v. Parke Care Ctrs., Inc., 123 F.3d 868, 872 (6th Cir. 1997)).

The record establishes actual notice at multiple levels of Arrow Energy’s management. Rig boss Tripp personally observed Ottobre’s conduct and laughed rather than intervening. When Wasek complained directly, Tripp threatened to put him on a “starvation schedule” and told him not to “make waves.” Wasek then reported the sexual touching and comments to Director of Operations Smillie, who responded by asking, “Why don’t you just kick his ass?” Wasek escalated further to Director of Operations Strong, who told Wasek he was “whining” and suggested the two men “duke it out.” No corrective action was ever taken; Ottobre’s conduct continued unabated until Wasek was forced to leave the job site.

This is precisely the kind of employer inaction that defeats summary judgment. See Faragher v. City of Boca Raton, 524 U.S. 775, 787–88 (1998) (recognizing employer liability may rest on negligence in “failing to take remedial action”); Burlington Indus., Inc. v. Ellerth, 524 U.S. 742, 758–59 (1998) (same). Rather than take prompt and effective action, Arrow Energy’s supervisory chain dismissed, mocked, and threatened Wasek. On this record, a reasonable jury could find that Arrow Energy knew of the harassment and failed to remedy it.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
