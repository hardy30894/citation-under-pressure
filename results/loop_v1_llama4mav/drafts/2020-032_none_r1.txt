The petitioners' constitutional challenge to the school policy is rendered moot by the revision of that policy during litigation, and their claim for nominal damages is insufficient to save their otherwise moot claims. The mootness doctrine, as established by the Court, dictates that a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. See Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895). The revision of the "Freedom of Expression Policy" and the removal of the challenged portion of the "Student Code of Conduct" have eliminated the live controversy regarding the constitutionality of the superseded policies, thereby rendering the petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot.

The claim for nominal damages does not revive the moot claims. As the Court has held, a claim for damages is not sufficient to save a case from mootness when the underlying controversy has been resolved. See Barker v. Twp. of West Norriton, 302 U.S. 644 (1937) (per curiam). The principle that a case becomes moot when the plaintiff's personal stake in the outcome is eliminated, even if the plaintiff seeks damages, is well-established. Atherton Mills v. Johnston, 259 U.S. 13, 15-16 (1922). In this case, the petitioners' personal stake in the outcome was eliminated when the school revised its policies, and their claim for nominal damages does not provide a sufficient basis to continue litigating the constitutionality of the superseded policies.

Furthermore, the petitioners' complaint did not adequately plead a claim for compensatory damages. The Court has emphasized the importance of proper pleading, requiring that a complaint provide sufficient notice of the claims being asserted, including any claims for damages. Nashville, C. & St. L. Ry. v. Wallace, 288 U.S. 249, 262 (1933). The petitioners' complaint failed to meet this standard, and their attempt to amend their complaint to add a claim for compensatory damages was not properly presented to the district court, as it was embedded in a response to a motion to dismiss rather than being presented in a properly filed motion. See Cummings v. Missouri, 71 U.S. 277, 318 (1866) (noting the importance of proper pleading); California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R., 149 U.S. 308, 314 (1893) (emphasizing the need for a live controversy).

The district court's dismissal of the petitioners' complaint as moot was therefore proper. The Court has consistently held that a district court's decision to dismiss a case as moot is reviewed de novo. United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). In this case, the district court correctly applied the relevant law, including the principles set forth in Mills and Atherton Mills, and properly concluded that the petitioners' claims were moot.

The petitioners' attempt to distinguish their case from the relevant precedents is unpersuasive. The Court has made clear that a case is moot when the underlying controversy has been resolved, regardless of any potential distinctions between the facts of the case and those of prior precedents. San Pablo & Tulare R.R., 149 U.S. at 314. The petitioners' argument that their case involves an as-applied challenge, rather than a facial challenge, does not provide a basis for distinguishing their case from the relevant precedents.

In conclusion, the petitioners' constitutional challenge to the school policy is moot, and their claim for nominal damages is insufficient to save their otherwise moot claims. The district court's dismissal of their complaint was proper, and this Court should affirm that decision. Commercial Cable Co. v. Burleson, 250 U.S. 360, 362-63 (1919).
