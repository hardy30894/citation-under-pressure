**Argument**

I. Milford Did Not Violate the Good News Club’s Free Speech Rights Because It Excluded a Form of Speech That Fell Outside the School’s Limited Public Forum.

A public school that opens its property for limited use may reserve the forum for the subjects and speakers it chooses, so long as it does not discriminate on the basis of viewpoint within the forum it has created. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802-06 (1985). Milford’s community-use policy opened school facilities after hours for social, civic, recreational, and other community welfare uses, but expressly excluded “religious purposes.” That exclusion was content-based, not viewpoint-based. The school did not ban the Club because it espoused a Christian perspective on an otherwise permitted subject; it denied access because the proposed meetings consisted of religious instruction, prayer, Bible memorization, and evangelism. A school may draw such a subject-matter line in a limited public forum. *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829-30 (1995); *Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 393-94 (1993).

The Court of Appeals erred in treating Milford’s decision as viewpoint discrimination. In *Rosenberger*, the Court explained that viewpoint discrimination occurs when government targets “the specific motivating ideology or the opinion or perspective of the speaker,” while subject-matter discrimination excludes a category of speech altogether. 515 U.S. at 829. Milford’s policy did the latter. It did not open a forum for moral instruction and then exclude only Christian morals; it excluded religious worship and instruction in all forms. That is a permissible subject limitation in a limited public forum. *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806.

II. The Club’s Activities Were Properly Characterized As Religious Worship and Instruction, Not Merely Secular Moral Instruction From a Religious Viewpoint.

The record shows that the Club’s meetings included prayer, Bible lessons, Scripture memorization, songs about Jesus Christ, invitation to trust “the Lord Jesus” as Savior, and private prayer with children after the meeting. Those activities are not simply a religious perspective on the topic of child development; they are religious exercise and evangelism. The distinction matters because a forum opened for moral and character development does not thereby become a forum for worship services. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, the Court recognized that “singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles” are speech, but also acknowledged that the State may avoid appearing to sponsor worship. 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981). Milford reasonably concluded that the Club’s proposed meetings crossed the line from discussion into worship and religious instruction.

Nor does *Lamb’s Chapel* compel the opposite result. There, the school excluded a film series about child-rearing and family issues merely because the films were presented “from a religious standpoint.” 508 U.S. at 393-94. Here, by contrast, the Club sought to conduct devotional exercises with young children in a school setting: prayer, Bible memory work, and a salvation invitation. The school did not exclude a religious viewpoint on a permitted subject; it excluded religious exercise itself. That distinction is constitutionally significant. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831-32.

III. Milford’s Exclusion Was Independently Justified By the Establishment Clause.

Even if Milford’s policy burdened the Club’s expressive activity, the exclusion was justified by the school’s obligation to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. Public schools occupy a uniquely sensitive position because compulsory attendance and the impressionability of young students can make religious activity on school premises appear school-sponsored. *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592-93 (1992); *Edwards v. Aguillard*, 482 U.S. 578, 584-85 (1987). The Establishment Clause prohibits not only direct religious coercion but also governmental endorsement of religion. *County of Allegheny v. ACLU Greater Pittsburgh Chapter*, 492 U.S. 573, 592-94 (1989).

Milford reasonably feared that allowing an after-hours children’s club devoted to prayer, Bible lessons, and conversion invitations in school classrooms would convey official endorsement of Christian worship. The Court has repeatedly held that government may take steps to avoid that appearance. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271-75; *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395. At a minimum, Milford was entitled to proceed cautiously in light of its duty to maintain religious neutrality in a public-school setting. *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 509-11 (2d Cir. 2000).

The judgment should therefore be affirmed. Milford excluded the Club because the Club proposed to use school facilities for religious worship and instruction, not because the school disfavored a particular religious viewpoint. And even if the exclusion implicated speech interests, Milford’s Establishment Clause concerns furnished a constitutionally sufficient justification. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46; *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829-30.
