# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text and Structure of the Railroad Retirement Act Confirm That a Denial of Reopening Is a Reviewable "Final Decision."

Section 355(f) authorizes judicial review of "any final decision" of the Railroad Retirement Board. Nothing in that text confines review to a claimant's first encounter with the agency. When the Board denies a request to reopen, it conclusively determines—for a second and final time—that the claimant is not entitled to the annuity he seeks. That determination "impose[s] an obligation, den[ies] a right or fix[es] some legal relationship as a consummation of the administrative process," which is precisely the touchstone of finality this Court has long applied. Chicago & S. Air Lines v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 333 U.S. 103, 113 (1948). Nothing about the label "reopening" strips the decision of that practical finality; it is the last word the agency will speak on Salinas's 2006 claim, and the administrative process with respect to that claim has fully run its course. See Fed. Commc'ns Comm'n v. Pottsville Broad. Co., 309 U.S. 134, 143 (1940) (recognizing that judicial review attaches once the administrative process has "run its course" and produced an order with legal consequence).

## II. A Strong Presumption Favors Judicial Review, and Congress Did Not Clearly Foreclose Review of Reopening Denials.

This Court has repeatedly instructed that statutory review provisions are to be read hospitably, not restrictively, and that Congress's intent to preclude review of agency action will not be inferred absent clear indication. In Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 140 (1967), the Court explained that "judicial review of a final agency action by an aggrieved person will not be cut off unless there is persuasive reason to believe that such was the purpose of Congress." That presumption traces to Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro, 349 U.S. 48, 51 (1955), where the Court gave the review provisions at issue a "hospitable" interpretation rather than a grudging one, and to Rusk v. Cort, 369 U.S. 367, 379–80 (1962), which reaffirmed that only clear congressional intent can overcome the "well-settled" presumption of reviewability. Likewise, in Leedom v. Kyne, 358 U.S. 184, 190 (1958), the Court refused to read statutory silence as foreclosing judicial correction of agency action, emphasizing the importance of ensuring that rights conferred by Congress are not left "unprotected" by the courts.

Section 355(f) contains no language singling out reopening decisions for exclusion from review. Under Abbott Laboratories, Shaughnessy, Rusk, and Leedom, that silence cannot be transmuted into a jurisdictional bar. The Fifth Circuit's rule—and that of the majority of circuits it followed—effectively manufactures an exception to reviewability that Congress never wrote into the statute.

## III. The Second Circuit Correctly Held in Szostak That Reopening Denials Are Reviewable Under the Analogous Statutory Scheme.

The Second Circuit confronted this precise question in Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), and concluded that the Board's refusal to reopen a prior benefits determination is a "final decision" subject to review under the very review provision at issue here. That court recognized that nothing in the statutory text or in the structure of the Board's administrative process suggested that Congress meant to insulate reopening decisions from judicial scrutiny while permitting review of every other final determination affecting a claimant's benefits. Szostak's reading gives full effect to the statutory grant of jurisdiction and avoids the anomaly of treating identical agency action—denial of an annuity—differently depending on the procedural label attached to the claimant's request.

The reasoning that later carried the day among a majority of circuits rests on a mistaken analogy to cases holding that reopening decisions under the Social Security Act are committed to agency discretion. But this Court has consistently required that the Railroad Retirement Act, no less than its sister statute, be read to protect the substantive rights of claimants rather than to erect procedural barriers to their vindication. See Soc. Sec. Bd. v. Nierotko, 327 U.S. 358, 364 (1946) (construing analogous social insurance legislation in light of its remedial purpose). A construction of § 355(f) that leaves a claimant with no judicial recourse whenever the agency chooses to process his request as one for "reopening" rather than as an original claim cannot be reconciled with that remedial purpose, nor with the presumption favoring review discussed above.

## IV. Denying Review Here Would Leave Meritorious Claims Unreviewable Based Solely on Agency Nomenclature.

This Court has recognized that jurisdiction to review must not be made to depend on the form an agency chooses for its decision rather than its substance. In Stark v. Wickard, 321 U.S. 288, 309 (1944), the Court emphasized that statutory review provisions must be construed to ensure that persons aggrieved by administrative action have a forum in which to test the legality of that action, rather than being turned away on formalistic grounds. The distinction the Board and the Fifth Circuit draw—between an "original" denial and a denial "on reopening"—is exactly the kind of formalism Stark warned against. In both instances, the Board tells the claimant, finally and irrevocably, that he will receive no annuity. The label attached to that determination should not determine whether a court may examine its lawfulness.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that the Board's denial of Salinas's request to reopen his 2006 application constitutes a "final decision" reviewable under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), and reverse the judgment of the court of appeals.
