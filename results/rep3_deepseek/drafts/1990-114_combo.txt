## ARGUMENT

### I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges Because They Are “Appointee[s] on the Policymaking Level.”

Congress did not clearly and unequivocally intend to interfere with a state’s traditional power to prescribe the qualifications and tenure of its own judges. *United States v. Rumely*, 345 U.S. 41, 46–47 (1953). When legislation “affect[s] the federal balance,” this Court requires a clear statement that Congress “has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved.” *Kent v. Dulles*, 357 U.S. 116, 130 (1958). The ADEA’s definition of “employee” excludes “any person chosen” by an elected official “to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Appointed state judges fall within that exception.

Judges are “appointee[s] on the policymaking level” because judging inherently involves making law. This Court long ago recognized that judges “do and must legislate.” *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205, 221 (1917) (Holmes, J., dissenting). Judicial decisions create binding precedent that governs future conduct. *Erie R.R. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78–79 (1938) (acknowledging that state courts make law). The common law tradition vests judges with discretion to fill interstices in statutes and constitutions. *Swift v. Tyson*, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 1, 18–19 (1842) (noting federal courts’ power to declare general common law). That discretion places judges squarely within the policymaking exception.

Missouri’s appointed judges also exercise supervisory authority over the court system. The state’s Supreme Court adopts rules governing judicial conduct and the bar and resolves administrative disputes. See *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc). Such rulemaking is a quintessential policymaking function. Because the exception must not be read “to deprive the States of authority over their judicial systems,” *Prentis v. Atlantic Coast Line Co.*, 211 U.S. 210, 227 (1908), the ADEA cannot be construed to sweep appointed judges into its coverage.

### II. Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause.

The Fourteenth Amendment permits a state wide latitude in drawing classifications so long as they bear a rational relationship to a legitimate government interest. *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911). A classification is constitutional if “any state of facts reasonably may be conceived to justify it.” *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 426 (1961).

Missouri rationally concluded that mandatory retirement at age 70 serves several legitimate goals: preserving public confidence in judicial competence, avoiding the spectacle of removing aging judges through cumbersome proceedings, and ensuring turnover to infuse the bench with fresh perspectives. *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949) (a classification need not be scientifically accurate; it suffices that the legislature “could have believed” it would promote the objective). Age 70 is not an arbitrary line. *Williamson v. Lee Optical Co.*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955) (“the law need not be in every respect logically consistent with its aims to be constitutional”). Other states and the federal government have long applied similar retirement ages to judges. *See, e.g.*, *Carmichael v. Southern Coal & Coke Co.*, 301 U.S. 495, 511–12 (1937) (legislatures may rely on common experience).

Appellants’ claim that the mandatory retirement provision irrationally treats judges differently from other public officials fails. *Goesaert v. Cleary*, 335 U.S. 464, 466–67 (1948) (a classification may be “narrower than it might be.”) A state may “take one step at a time, addressing itself to the phase of the problem which seems most acute to the legislative mind.” *Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm’rs*, 330 U.S. 552, 564 (1947). The unique demands of judging—requiring mental acuity, stamina, and the respect of litigants—provide ample rational basis for treating judges differently from other state employees. *See* *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 68 (1941) (state regulation of its own officers is entitled to deference).

Because Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision is supported by rational reasons and Congress has not clearly included appointed judges within the ADEA, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
