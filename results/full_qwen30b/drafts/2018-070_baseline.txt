**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the precise language of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), which governs the choice of law on the outer Continental Shelf. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that state wage and hour laws may apply on the OCS so long as they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, misreads the statute and undermines the comprehensive federal regulatory scheme Congress designed for offshore operations. The correct interpretation is that state law applies only when federal law is silent or inadequate—i.e., when there is a gap in federal coverage. The Court should reverse.

### I. THE TEXT AND STRUCTURE OF OCSLA DEMANDS THAT STATE LAW FILL GAPS, NOT SUPPLANT FEDERAL LAW

The OCSLA’s choice of law provision states: “To the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted, the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State... are declared to be the law of the United States for that portion of the subsoil and seabed...” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (emphasis added). The phrase “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent” does not mean “whenever applicable and not inconsistent.” Rather, it reflects a legislative intent to supplement federal law only where federal law fails to provide a complete framework.

This interpretation is consistent with the Act’s foundational purpose: to create a coherent legal regime for the OCS, which lies beyond state jurisdiction but not beyond federal governance. As the Supreme Court explained in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355, 89 S.Ct. 1835, 23 L.Ed.2d 360 (1969), the OCSLA was enacted “to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as drilling platforms on the outer Continental Shelf.” The Act was not designed to create a patchwork of overlapping state and federal rules, but to ensure that federal law governs the OCS while allowing state law to fill gaps where federal law is silent.

The legislative history confirms this. Congress rejected the idea of extending state law to the OCS in a blanket fashion, fearing it would amount to an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power. See S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 33 (1953). To allay those concerns, Congress limited the initial scope of § 1333(a)(2)(A) to state laws *then in existence*. Only in 1975, after *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 78 S.Ct. 291, 2 L.Ed.2d 282 (1958), clarified that Congress could constitutionally incorporate state law, did Congress expand the provision to include *future* state laws. But even then, Congress did not intend for state law to supplant federal law. The 1975 amendment was meant to ensure consistency between the OCS and deepwater ports—*not* to allow state law to override federal statutes where federal law already governs.

Thus, the phrase “not inconsistent” does not mean “not in conflict with a federal statute that is otherwise fully operative.” It means “not in conflict with a federal law that is actually governing the matter.” When federal law is comprehensive and fully applicable, as it is here under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), state law cannot be applied as a substitute. The FLSA is not a mere floor; it is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wages, hours, and overtime for all employees, including those on the OCS. See 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq. The FLSA’s savings clause—29 U.S.C. § 218—explicitly permits states to impose *more protective* standards, but only where federal law does not already provide such protection. That is, the savings clause is a *safety net*, not a *substitute*.

### II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S INTERPRETATION UNDERMINES CONGRESSIONAL DESIGN AND CREATES UNINTENDED CONFLICT

The Ninth Circuit’s holding—that California’s wage and hour laws apply on the OCS so long as they are not inconsistent with the FLSA—creates a regime in which state law governs even when federal law is fully operative. This is contrary to the settled principle that “federal law preempts state law when the two are in conflict, or when Congress has occupied the field.” *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67, 61 S.Ct. 399, 85 L.Ed. 581 (1941). The FLSA is not merely a floor; it is a complete statutory framework. The Department of Labor has issued detailed regulations interpreting the FLSA’s overtime and meal period requirements, including for offshore workers. See 29 C.F.R. § 778.101 (exempting certain offshore workers from overtime, but only under strict conditions). These regulations are binding and reflect Congress’s intent to govern the OCS through a uniform federal standard.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach would allow a worker on a drilling platform in the Gulf of Mexico to claim under Texas law, while a worker in the Santa Barbara Channel could claim under California law—despite identical working conditions and federal regulatory oversight. This undermines the uniformity Congress sought. As the Supreme Court has held, “Congress may not have intended to leave the field open to state regulation where it has itself acted comprehensively.” *Fidelity Federal Savings & Loan Ass’n v. de la Cuesta*, 458 U.S. 141, 153, 102 S.Ct. 3014, 73 L.Ed.2d 664 (1982). The FLSA is such a comprehensive scheme.
