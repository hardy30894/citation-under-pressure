I. The Supplemental Instruction Erroneously Defined “Shielding.”

A district court abuses its discretion when a supplemental instruction misstates the elements of the offense. Bollenbach v. United States, 326 U.S. 607, 612 (1946). The statute requires proof that the defendant acted “for the purpose of commercial advantage or private financial gain,” 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(B)(i), yet the court’s definition of “shielding” omitted any requirement that the conduct be undertaken to prevent detection by authorities. See United States v. Kim, 193 F.3d 567, 574 (2d Cir. 1999) (harboring demands “conduct tending substantially to facilitate the alien remaining in the United States illegally”). By adopting a non-statutory formulation drawn from other circuits, the instruction permitted conviction on proof of mere employment or housing, thereby relieving the government of its burden to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970); United States v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506, 510 (1995).

The error was not harmless. The jury specifically requested definitions of the statutory terms, demonstrating that the instruction shaped its deliberations. Because the definition was both vague and overbroad, reversal is required. United States v. Santos, 553 U.S. 507, 514 (2008) (ambiguity in criminal statutes resolved in favor of the defendant).

II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Intent to Prevent Detection.

A conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) cannot stand unless the government proves the defendant acted with the specific intent to shield the aliens from detection. Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). The record shows only that Ye hired illegal aliens, paid them in cash, and provided housing and transportation—conduct equally consistent with ordinary employment. United States v. Cantu, 557 F.2d 1173, 1177 (5th Cir. 1977) (mere employment insufficient). No evidence established that Ye took affirmative steps to conceal the workers from immigration authorities or that his actions were motivated by a desire to evade detection. United States v. Varkonyi, 645 F.2d 453, 459 (5th Cir. 1981). At most, the proof demonstrated knowledge of unlawful status, not the requisite intent to shield. Because no rational trier of fact could find this element beyond a reasonable doubt, the conviction must be reversed. Jackson, 443 U.S. at 319.
