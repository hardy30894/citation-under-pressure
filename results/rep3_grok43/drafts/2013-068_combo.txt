The Massachusetts statute constitutes a permissible time, place, and manner regulation of speech in a traditional public forum. Such regulations are sustained when they are content-neutral, serve significant governmental interests, and leave open ample alternative channels of communication. Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (recognizing "the authority of a municipality to impose regulations in order to assure the safety and convenience of the people in the use of public highways"); Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77, 87 (1949) (upholding restrictions that "do not abridge the constitutional liberty" when they address "the hours and place" of expression).

The Act is content-neutral on its face. It applies equally to all speakers within the designated zones and does not target particular viewpoints. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940) (noting that the First Amendment "embraces two concepts,—freedom to believe and freedom to act," the latter subject to regulation); Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 571-572 (1942) (affirming that "certain well-defined and narrowly limited classes of speech" may be regulated without violating constitutional guarantees).

The statute advances significant interests in public safety, orderly traffic flow, and unimpeded access to medical facilities. Feiner v. New York, 340 U.S. 315, 320 (1951) (sustaining conviction where speech created "a clear danger of riot, disorder, interference with traffic"); Cox v. Louisiana, 379 U.S. 536, 554 (1965) (acknowledging "a legitimate public interest in preventing" obstruction of streets and sidewalks).

The fixed buffer zones are narrowly drawn to those ends. They prohibit only knowing presence within thirty-five feet of entrances, exits, and driveways and exempt several categories of persons whose presence serves the statute's purposes. Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395, 405 (1953) (approving licensing schemes that "regulate the use of the streets" without suppressing expression); Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966) (upholding trespass convictions on jail grounds because "the State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated").

Ample alternative channels remain. Speakers may communicate from beyond the zones by voice, sign, literature, or amplified sound, and may approach patients before they enter the zones. Shuttlesworth v. City of Birmingham, 394 U.S. 147, 152 (1969) (invalidating only those ordinances that "confer upon administrative boards a degree of uncontrolled discretion" while recognizing valid place restrictions); Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 271 (1951) (stressing that regulation must not operate as "a prior restraint" but may control "the time and place").

The exemptions do not render the Act viewpoint-discriminatory. They are justified by the distinct functions performed by exempted persons and do not favor one side of any debate. Kunz v. New York, 340 U.S. 290, 293 (1951) (condemning only "unbridled discretion" while permitting "reasonable" limits on public expression).

Because the Act satisfies these settled criteria, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
