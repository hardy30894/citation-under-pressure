**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE EXEMPT AS “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL”

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. § 621–634, does not extend to appointed state court judges because they fall within the statutory exemption for “appointees on the policymaking level.” Congress enacted this exception not merely to exclude high-level executive and legislative officials, but to preserve the integrity of the entire tripartite structure of government—especially the judiciary—by recognizing that judges, like other senior officials, exercise substantial discretion in shaping the law and administering justice. The plain language of 29 U.S.C. § 630(f) unambiguously covers appointed state judges, and no “clear statement” of congressional intent is required to override the constitutional balance of federalism and state sovereignty in this domain.

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office… or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The petitioners, though appointed under Missouri’s Non-Partisan Court Plan, are not “elected” and thus do not fall under the first exemption. But they are unquestionably “appointees on the policymaking level.” The term “policymaking” is not confined to the executive or legislative branches; it encompasses any official whose decisions have broad, enduring consequences for the law and public order. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, “the judicial function is not merely the mechanical application of rules to facts, but the exercise of judgment in resolving disputes that often involve unsettled legal questions.” *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954). Judges, particularly in appellate and supreme courts, routinely “make policy” by interpreting constitutions, statutes, and precedents—filling “interstices or lacunae” in the law. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988).

This is not a theoretical distinction. Missouri’s appointed judges exercise significant policymaking authority. The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, “is responsible for adopting the rules governing the professional conduct of the Missouri judiciary and the Missouri Bar,” and for resolving internal disputes within the judicial hierarchy. *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985); *In re Rules of Circuit Court of Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 757 S.W.2d 590 (Mo. 1988). These are not administrative tasks; they are foundational to the functioning of the entire state judiciary. The decisions of the Missouri Supreme Court establish binding precedent across the state, as demonstrated by *Gustafson v. Benda*, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983), where the Court overruled a long-standing tort doctrine and adopted comparative negligence—a decision with profound implications for civil litigation statewide. Similarly, *Cruzan v. Harmon*, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988), involved a life-or-death decision with sweeping ethical and legal consequences, ultimately reaching the U.S. Supreme Court. Such decisions are quintessential policymaking.

The petitioners’ argument that judges are merely “applying” law ignores the reality of judicial decisionmaking. As the First Circuit observed, “judges… must exercise the same sort of discretion in decisionmaking, temper their rulings with the same sort of self-restraint, and engage in the same sort of thoughtful judgment that is required of ‘appointee[s] on the policymaking level’ in the executive and legislative branches.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55. To hold otherwise would be to deny that judges, like cabinet officers or agency heads, shape the law through their interpretations and rulings. The legislative history cited by the petitioners—while not dispositive—confirms that Congress intended the exemption to apply broadly to “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments.” Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers, H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180. The judiciary is a “department” of state government, and its senior officials are no less policymaking than those in the executive or legislative branches.

Moreover, the Court has repeatedly emphasized that Congress does not lightly displace state authority over its own judiciary. In *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971), the Court held that “in traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved in the judicial decision.” Here, Congress did not clearly intend to override Missouri’s constitutional authority to set age limits for its judges. The ADEA’s broad language does not, by itself, override the structural and functional realities of the judiciary. The exemption for “appointees on the policymaking level” is not a narrow technicality—it is a recognition that the judiciary, like the other branches, must be insulated from the kind of employment regulation that would undermine its independence and effectiveness.

II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SURVIVES EQUAL PROTECTION REVIEW

Even if the ADEA applied, Missouri’s mandatory retirement at age 70 does not violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The standard of review is rational basis: “a classification must be rationally related to a legitimate state interest.” *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). Missouri’s interest in ensuring judicial competence, efficiency, and public confidence in the judiciary is both legitimate and substantial.
