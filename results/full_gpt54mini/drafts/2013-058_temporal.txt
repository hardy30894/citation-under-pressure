Respondent’s inherited IRA is exempt only if the Bankruptcy Code’s phrase “retirement funds” is read to include assets that are no longer held for retirement at all. It should not be so read. The better reading follows the Code’s text, the structure of exemption law, and this Court’s longstanding admonition that courts must not rewrite statutes to advance a perceived policy beyond what Congress enacted. See Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) (explaining that legislation does not “pursue[] its purposes at all costs”); cf. United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, 310 U.S. 534, 542–44 (1940) (statutory terms are read in context and in light of the statute as a whole).

The Bankruptcy Code exempts only “retirement funds” held in a qualifying tax-favored account. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). That language is naturally read to refer to money held for retirement, not money that once belonged to a retirement account but, by operation of law, may be withdrawn immediately and used for present consumption. As this Court has said, “[t]here is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes.” United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Here, Congress chose the word “retirement,” not merely “tax-deferred” or “inherited.”

That distinction matters. Inherited IRAs are subject to special restrictions that make them fundamentally unlike retirement accounts maintained for the debtor’s own future support: no additional contributions are permitted, the account cannot be rolled into a new IRA, and required distributions begin immediately. See 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B); 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(11)(A). Assets subject to immediate distribution are not funds preserved for the owner’s retirement. A statute exempting “retirement funds” does not naturally encompass a vehicle designed for earlier distribution and current use.

The broader structure of exemption law confirms that exemptions turn on the debtor’s present interest and present use. In White v. Stump, 266 U.S. 310, 313–15 (1924), the Court held that a bankruptcy exemption is fixed as of the filing and cannot be created afterward by the debtor’s later acts. Likewise, in Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622, 626–27 (1943), the Court emphasized that exemption rights arise from the debtor’s legally cognizable interest at bankruptcy. Those cases reflect the settled principle that exemption statutes protect specified property in specified circumstances; they do not preserve whatever label an asset once bore in someone else’s hands.

Respondent’s rule would also disregard the settled meaning of exempt property as property the debtor himself uses in the protected manner the statute contemplates. In Rousey v. Jacoway, 544 U.S. 320, 329–32 (2005), the Court held that IRA funds qualified because they were objectively “retirement funds,” tied to the debtor’s future retirement needs. The same feature is absent here. An inherited IRA forces distributions before the beneficiary’s retirement, so it is not a retirement fund in the beneficiary’s hands.

Appellants’ contrary reading would expand the exemption beyond Congress’s design. The Court has repeatedly declined to stretch exemption provisions by implication. See Kokoszka v. Belford, 417 U.S. 642, 647–49 (1974) (narrowly construing exemption for tax-refund-like property and emphasizing Congress’s limited policy choice); but cf. In re Conklin, 114 F. 986, 988 (C.C.A. 6th Cir. 1902) (exemption laws are not to be enlarged by construction). Although exemptions are sometimes construed liberally, see Forsyth v. Vehmeyer, 177 U.S. 177, 180–81 (1900), liberal construction cannot override clear statutory limits. A court “is not at liberty to add to the words of a statute” under the guise of interpretation. United States v. Goldenberg, 168 U.S. 95, 103 (1897).

The judgment below should be affirmed because an inherited IRA is not “retirement funds” within the meaning of 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). It is inherited property, available for present consumption, not a fund reserved for the debtor’s retirement.
