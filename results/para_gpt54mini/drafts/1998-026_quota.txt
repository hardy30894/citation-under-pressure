## Argument

Peguero is not entitled to habeas relief because the District Court’s omission did not prejudice him. Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) required the court to advise a defendant of the right to appeal, but a failure to give that advisement does not itself warrant collateral relief when the defendant already knew of the right and chose not to appeal. Habeas relief lies only for a constitutional or jurisdictional defect, or for an error that results in a “complete miscarriage of justice.” *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). A purely technical violation of a procedural rule, standing alone, does not satisfy that standard. *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 783-85 (1979).

This Court has long treated Rule 32’s appeal-notice requirement as designed to ensure that a defendant is informed of a right he might otherwise miss. But where the defendant already knows of that right, the rule’s purpose is fulfilled and no prejudice follows. In *Rodriquez v. United States*, 395 U.S. 327 (1969), the Court granted relief because the district court’s failure to advise the defendant deprived him of a requested appeal; the Court emphasized that the omission had prevented the defendant from pursuing appellate review. Id. at 329-30. Likewise, in *Wilkerson v. United States*, 366 U.S. 429 (1961), the Court vacated where the defendant had not been advised of appellate rights and thus lost the opportunity to appeal. Those cases do not help Peguero, because the District Court here found as a fact that he knew of his right to appeal at sentencing and elected not to pursue it.

The Court’s post-*Rodriquez* decisions confirm that collateral relief is unavailable absent actual prejudice. In *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780 (1979), the Court rejected § 2255 relief for a Rule 11 defect, explaining that “collateral relief is not available when all that is shown is a failure to comply with the formal requirements” of a rule. Id. at 785. The Court reasoned that § 2255 is not a substitute for direct appeal and does not reach every procedural error. Id. at 784. *Peguero* is the same kind of case: the alleged violation concerns a formal advisement rule, and the record shows no injury because respondent already knew of his appellate rights.

The Court has also made clear that harmless-error principles apply to procedural mistakes that do not affect substantial rights. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946), the Court held that a conviction should not be reversed for errors that had no substantial influence on the outcome. And in *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 637-38 (1993), the Court held that habeas relief is limited to errors that had a “substantial and injurious effect or influence” on the proceeding. Because Peguero knew of his right to appeal, the omission had no effect on his ability to seek review; at most, it was harmless.

The same principle appears in the Court’s decisions on appellate-rights claims more generally. In *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), the Court explained that due process protects the right to a first appeal as of right, but the right is violated only when the defendant is actually deprived of appellate review through state action. Id. at 393-94. In *Roe v. Flores-Ortega*, 528 U.S. 470 (2000), the Court held that where counsel fails to file a notice of appeal, a defendant must show prejudice, namely a reasonable probability that, but for the deficiency, he would have appealed. Id. at 484, 486. That prejudice requirement is fatal to Peguero’s claim, because the District Court found that he knew of his right and chose not to appeal.

Nor does the Rules Enabling Act convert every Rule 32 violation into a basis for habeas relief. The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure are important, but they do not themselves create automatic collateral remedies. *Bank of Nova Scotia v. United States*, 487 U.S. 250, 254-55 (1988), held that nonconstitutional errors in criminal procedure do not require dismissal absent prejudice. The same is true here. Peguero identifies no lost appeal, no meritorious claim forfeited by ignorance, and no unfairness in the sentencing proceeding itself.

In short, the District Court correctly denied relief. The undisputed finding that Peguero knew of his right to appeal means the court’s Rule 32 omission caused no prejudice and no miscarriage of justice. Under *Hill*, *Timmreck*, *Rodriquez*, *Wilkerson*, *Kotteakos*, *Brecht*, *Evitts*, *Flores-Ortega*, and *Bank of Nova Scotia*, habeas relief is unwarranted. The judgment should be affirmed.
