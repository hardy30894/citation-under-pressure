The IRS may collect a tax only from the taxpayer against whom it has made a timely assessment. An assessment is valid only if it identifies the taxpayer whose liability is recorded. Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960); United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961). A partnership and its individual partners are distinct taxpayers. Id. at 462. Consequently, an assessment against the partnership alone does not extend the limitations period for collection from the partners. Young, 283 F.2d at 910.

In Young, the court held that a partner who had voluntarily paid taxes assessed only against the partnership could not obtain a refund, but it expressly recognized that collection without an individual assessment or judgment would require resort to court action. Id. The same principle precludes collection here. Because the IRS made no assessment against the individual partners within three years of the returns, the ordinary limitations period bars any action against them. Coson, 286 F.2d at 458.

The Ninth Circuit reached the identical conclusion in Coson, where the government sought to enforce a lien against a partner’s property based solely on an assessment naming the partnership. The court held the lien invalid because “no lien exists without such an assessment” against the individual. Id. at 462. The same requirement applies when the IRS seeks to collect through bankruptcy claims rather than liens.

State partnership law does not alter the federal assessment rule. Even where partners are jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, federal tax collection requires either an assessment against the partner or a judgment against him. Young, 283 F.2d at 910; Coson, 286 F.2d at 458. No such judgment was obtained, and the time to do so has expired.

The timely assessment against the partnership therefore extended the limitations period only as to the partnership itself. The IRS’s claims against the individual partners were properly disallowed.
