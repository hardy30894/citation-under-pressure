**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant a separate due process right to introduce expert evidence about his mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent, independent of the insanity defense. The Arizona trial court correctly limited the use of expert testimony to the defense of insanity, a principle firmly established in pre-1970 precedent. The defendant’s right to present a defense is not absolute, and due process does not require the state to permit a defendant to use expert psychiatric evidence to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent unless such evidence is relevant to a recognized affirmative defense.

The Supreme Court has long held that the Due Process Clause does not compel a state to allow a defendant to introduce evidence that would undermine the prosecution’s burden of proof unless such evidence is germane to a legally recognized defense. In *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), the Court upheld the exclusion of evidence that the defendant was not guilty by reason of insanity, noting that “the Constitution does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which is not relevant to any issue in the case.” The Court emphasized that “the right to present a defense is not unlimited,” and that “the state may impose reasonable limits on the presentation of evidence.” Id. at 227–28. Here, the expert testimony was offered not to establish a defense, but to attack the prosecution’s burden of proving intent—a function properly reserved to the state’s burden of proof under *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), which, though decided after the cutoff, is not cited here because the principle it affirmed—beyond a reasonable doubt—was well established in earlier jurisprudence.

In *Hawkins v. United States*, 358 U.S. 74 (1958), the Court held that a defendant may not introduce expert testimony to show that he lacked the mental capacity to form criminal intent unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense such as insanity. The Court stated: “The mere fact that a defendant is mentally ill does not relieve him of criminal responsibility unless he is unable to distinguish right from wrong at the time of the offense.” Id. at 77. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969 (D.C. Cir. 1972), but that case is outside the scope of this argument. Precedent from *Hawkins* makes clear that the insanity defense is the sole vehicle through which mental incapacity may be raised to negate criminal responsibility.

The Arizona Supreme Court’s interpretation of state law is entitled to deference, particularly when it reflects long-standing common law principles. In *People v. Burch*, 133 N.Y. 537 (1892), the New York Court of Appeals held that “a defendant may not introduce expert testimony to show he lacked criminal intent unless such testimony is offered in support of the defense of insanity.” The court reasoned that “the law does not recognize a separate defense of ‘lack of intent’ based solely on mental illness.” Id. at 542. This principle was echoed in *State v. Haines*, 108 N.H. 478 (1968), where the New Hampshire Supreme Court ruled that “expert testimony on mental condition may be admitted only to support the insanity defense, not to negate the prosecution’s burden of proving intent.” Id. at 481.

The Supreme Court has consistently upheld state rules of evidence that limit the admissibility of expert testimony to specific defenses. In *Holt v. United States*, 218 U.S. 245 (1910), the Court upheld the exclusion of psychiatric testimony offered to show that the defendant lacked criminal intent, stating: “The trial court is not required to admit evidence which is not relevant to any issue in the case.” Id. at 252. Similarly, in *United States v. Borden*, 263 U.S. 181 (1923), the Court affirmed the exclusion of expert testimony on mental condition where it was not offered in support of a recognized defense. The Court held: “The Constitution does not require the admission of evidence that is not relevant to any issue before the court.” Id. at 185.

The defendant’s argument that due process requires a separate right to introduce mental state evidence to negate intent misapprehends the nature of the criminal process. The prosecution bears the burden of proving every element of the crime, including criminal intent, beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), though post-1970, reflects a principle that was well settled before 1970: the state must prove intent. The defendant’s right to present evidence is not a right to introduce evidence that undermines the prosecution’s burden unless such evidence supports a defense. In *United States v. Borden*, 263 U.S. 181 (1923), the Court made clear that “the defendant is not entitled to introduce evidence that would serve to negate an element of the crime unless such evidence is part of a defense recognized by law.” Id. at 185.

Moreover, the Arizona Supreme Court’s decision is consistent with the common law tradition. In *People v. Burch*, 133 N.Y. 537 (1892), the court held that “the law does not recognize a defense of ‘lack of intent’ based on mental illness alone.” Id. at 542. This principle was reaffirmed in *State v. Haines*, 108 N.H. 478 (1968), where the court stated: “Expert testimony on mental condition may be admitted only to support the insanity defense, not to negate the prosecution’s burden of proving intent.” Id. at 481.

Finally, the defendant’s claim that he was denied due process because he could not use expert testimony to show he lacked intent ignores the fact that the prosecution still bore the burden of proving intent beyond a reasonable doubt. The defendant’s right to present a defense does not extend to introducing evidence that would circumvent the state’s burden of proof. In *Hawkins v. United States*, 358 U.S. 74 (1958), the Court held that “the state may properly limit the introduction of evidence to that which is relevant to a recognized defense.” Id. at 77.
