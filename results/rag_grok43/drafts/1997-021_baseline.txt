ARGUMENT

I. The Board's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling is irrational and inconsistent with the Act.

An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). An employer may overcome that presumption with "objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status." Id. at 41 n.8; NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). The Board applies the identical standard to three distinct employer actions: withdrawal of recognition, petition for an RM election, and internal polling. Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-63 (1989).

This equivalence renders polling useless. An employer that already possesses evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal has no need to poll; it may simply withdraw. The Board itself acknowledges that polling serves to obtain "more certain, precise information" than the reasonable-doubt threshold supplies. Id. at 1063. Yet the Board simultaneously insists that polling is "potentially disruptive and unsettling." Id. at 1061. These positions cannot be reconciled. If the statutory goal is stable bargaining relationships, the Board should either raise the withdrawal standard or lower the polling standard. Maintaining the same threshold for both actions while disparaging polling as inferior to Board-supervised elections is arbitrary.

The three courts of appeals that rejected the Board's rule correctly recognized that polling is a "useful and legitimate tool" only when the evidentiary bar is lower than for withdrawal. The Board's refusal to adjust its standard in light of this logic exceeds the deference owed under the Act. See Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978).

II. Substantial evidence established Allentown's reasonable doubt.

The Board found that only seven employees had repudiated the union. That finding ignored competent evidence. Statements by employees hired after the January 25, 1991 cutoff date were properly excluded, but the Board also discounted contemporaneous expressions of dissatisfaction by Ridgick and Marsh on the ground that they occurred during job interviews. Board precedent treating such statements as inherently insincere does not compel that result when the employer has not created a coercive atmosphere. See Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc., 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978).

Bloch's report that the entire night shift opposed the union, and steward Mohr's statement that the union would lose a vote, supplied additional objective grounds for doubt. The Board discounted these reports for lack of corroboration, yet the Act does not require an employer to verify every employee's sentiment before polling. The record as a whole demonstrates that Allentown possessed a good-faith reasonable doubt. The Board's contrary determination is not supported by substantial evidence.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
