**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING THE EXPERT OPINION OF AMERICAN STEAMSHIP’S WITNESS REGARDING THE CAUSE OF THE LINE’S PARTING.**

The district court abused its discretion in admitting the testimony of American Steamship’s expert, as it failed to satisfy the foundational requirements for expert testimony under Rule 702 of the Federal Rules of Evidence. *See Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923) (establishing the general acceptance test for expert testimony). The expert’s opinion was not based on specialized knowledge but rather on speculation, as he could not definitively explain why the line parted. *See Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993) (though decided later, the principle that expert testimony must be reliable is rooted in earlier cases like *Frye*). The admission of this testimony was prejudicial, as it improperly bolstered the defendant’s case without proper evidentiary foundation.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ISSUE OF UNSEAWORTHINESS.**

The district court erred in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. Under maritime law, a vessel is unseaworthy if it is not reasonably fit for its intended purpose. *See Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 550 (1960). The evidence presented at trial established that the boom was not properly maintained, the grease in the bearings had not been changed for winter conditions, and the line used for the bos’n’s chair was improperly rigged. *See The Osceola*, 189 U.S. 158, 175 (1903) (holding that a vessel must be reasonably fit for its intended use). The district court’s denial of Cook’s motion was contrary to established maritime law.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ISSUE OF COMPARATIVE NEGLIGENCE.**

The district court properly submitted the issue of comparative negligence to the jury. Under maritime law, comparative negligence is a question of fact for the jury to determine. *See Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn*, 346 U.S. 406, 413 (1953). The evidence presented at trial showed that Cook may have contributed to his own injury by failing to properly inspect the line or the boom before using them. The jury’s finding of 50% comparative negligence was supported by the evidence and was not erroneous.

**IV. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK’S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS.**

The district court did not err in admitting evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments. Under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence, evidence of a person’s character is admissible to prove a relevant trait when it is probative of a material issue in the case. *See Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 475-76 (1948) (holding that evidence of a defendant’s prior arrests was admissible to show knowledge of the law). Cook’s alcohol-related ailments were relevant to his ability to perform his duties safely and were properly admitted to show his potential contribution to the accident. The district court’s ruling was within its discretion and was not erroneous.
