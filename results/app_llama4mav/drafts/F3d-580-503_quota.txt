## Argument

I. The District Court Erred in Denying Winbush's Motion for a Fingerprint Identification Expert

The district court's denial of Winbush's motion for a fingerprint identification expert was an abuse of discretion. The Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to present a defense, which includes the right to expert assistance. See Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68, 76-77 (1985). In determining whether a defendant is entitled to expert assistance, courts consider whether the expert's testimony is necessary to present a viable defense. See United States v. Cravens, 275 F.3d 637, 639 (7th Cir. 2001).

Here, Winbush's defense relied heavily on challenging the government's evidence, particularly the lack of fingerprint evidence linking him to the contraband. The government's own expert, Michael Shay, was expected to testify that no latent fingerprints of value were discovered on the physical evidence. Winbush's requested expert would have provided crucial testimony to interpret this evidence and potentially rebut the government's case. See United States v. Frazier, 387 F.3d 1244, 1262 (11th Cir. 2004) (holding that a defendant is entitled to an expert to challenge the government's expert testimony).

The district court's denial of Winbush's motion was based on the untimeliness of the request, citing that it was filed "on the Friday before trial in a case that's been pending for over two years." However, the government's Notice of Expert Witnesses was filed on October 3, 2007, and Winbush filed his motion on November 8, 2007, which is not an unreasonable delay given the complexity of the case and the need to respond to the government's expert testimony. See United States v. Morrison, 946 F.2d 484, 498 (7th Cir. 1991) (holding that a district court's denial of a motion for expert assistance based on untimeliness must be balanced against the defendant's right to present a defense).

Furthermore, the district court's denial of Winbush's motion without considering the potential prejudice to his defense was error. See United States v. Gresham, 118 F.3d 258, 264 (5th Cir. 1997) (holding that a district court must consider the potential prejudice to a defendant's defense when denying a motion for expert assistance). The lack of fingerprint evidence was a critical aspect of the government's case, and Winbush's expert could have provided testimony to challenge the government's interpretation of this evidence.

II. The District Court Erred in Denying Winbush's Motion to Continue

The district court's denial of Winbush's motion to continue was also an abuse of discretion. A district court's decision to deny a continuance is reviewed for abuse of discretion, and will be reversed only if the denial resulted in prejudice to the defendant. See United States v. Robbins, 197 F.3d 829, 846 (7th Cir. 1999).

Here, Winbush's motion to continue was based on the need to secure a fingerprint identification expert. As discussed above, this expert was necessary to present a viable defense. The denial of the continuance resulted in prejudice to Winbush, as he was unable to present expert testimony to challenge the government's evidence. See United States v. Santos, 201 F.3d 953, 958 (7th Cir. 2000) (holding that a district court's denial of a continuance resulted in prejudice to the defendant where the defendant was unable to present a key witness).

The district court's denial of Winbush's motion to continue without considering the potential prejudice to his defense was error. See United States v. Issacs, 593 F.3d 596, 605 (7th Cir. 2010) (holding that a district court must consider the potential prejudice to a defendant's defense when denying a motion to continue). The denial of the continuance, combined with the denial of Winbush's motion for a fingerprint identification expert, effectively denied Winbush the right to present a defense.

In sum, the district court's denial of Winbush's motion for a fingerprint identification expert and motion to continue was an abuse of discretion, and resulted in prejudice to Winbush's defense. This court should reverse Winbush's conviction and remand for a new trial.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, Winbush respectfully requests that this court reverse his conviction and remand for a new trial.
