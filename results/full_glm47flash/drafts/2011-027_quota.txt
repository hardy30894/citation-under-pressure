**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONSTITUTION REQUIRES TITLE TO SUBMERGED LANDS TO BE DETERMINED AT THE TIME OF STATEHOOD.**

The Equal Footing Doctrine, enshrined in the Constitution, dictates that when a state enters the Union, it holds title to the lands beneath navigable waters. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 74 (1931). This constitutional grant of title is not contingent upon the navigability of the waterway at the time of the state’s admission, but rather upon the condition of the waterway at that specific moment. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 30 (1935). Consequently, the State of Montana acquired title to the riverbeds underlying the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers at the moment of its admission to the Union in 1889, provided those waters were navigable at that time.

**II. THE "NAVIGABLE FOR TITLE" TEST IS HISTORICAL, NOT CURRENT.**

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the test for determining title to submerged lands is whether the water was navigable at the time of statehood. *United States v. Riverside Bayview Homes, Inc.*, 474 U.S. 121, 131 (1985). This historical inquiry is distinct from the test for determining navigability for federal power purposes. *United States v. Maine*, 420 U.S. 515, 527 (1975). In *Maine*, the Court explained that the "navigable for title" test is "limited to the condition of the water at the time of the State's admission." *Id.* at 527. Similarly, in *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 406 (1940), the Court affirmed that the determination of title to submerged lands is based on the navigable condition of the water at the time of statehood. *Id.* at 406. Therefore, the trial court correctly applied the historical test to determine that the State holds title to the riverbeds at issue.

**III. THE FEDERAL NAVIGATIONAL SERITUDE DOES NOT ALTER THE STATE'S TITLE.**

While the United States possesses a federal navigational servitude over navigable waters, this servitude does not strip the state of title to the beds of those waters. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981). The servitude is a limitation on the state’s power to regulate the waters, not a transfer of title from the state to the federal government. *Id.* at 551. This principle was established long ago; in *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596 (1941), the Court held that the federal servitude extends to all state-owned lands below the high-water mark, but it does not affect the state's underlying title. *Id.* at 596-97. Thus, the existence of the federal servitude does not require a different test for determining state title.

**IV. THE FEDERAL POWER ACT PRESERVES STATE PROPERTY RIGHTS.**

The Federal Power Act (FPA) does not preempt the State’s ability to assert title to riverbeds based on historical navigability. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 263 (1946). The FPA establishes a "dual system" of control, granting the federal government authority to license hydroelectric projects while preserving the role of state law regarding property rights. *Id.* at 263. Furthermore, in *United States v. Willow River Power Co.*, 324 U.S. 499, 503 (1945), the Court noted that the FPA contemplates the existence of state property rights that must be respected. *Id.* at 503. Consequently, the State’s claim to title based on the historical navigability of the rivers is fully consistent with federal law.
