**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF.**

The plain text of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") limits its extension of Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") coverage to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The statute’s use of the phrase "on the outer Continental Shelf" modifies the entire clause—"operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf"—and thus requires that the operations themselves occur on the shelf. *See* *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) ("[W]ords in a statute should be given their ordinary meaning unless a contrary intent is clearly manifested."). This interpretation is consistent with the OCSLA’s purpose of extending federal jurisdiction over submerged lands beyond state boundaries. *See* *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 50 (1894) (noting that federal jurisdiction over submerged lands is limited to the area beyond state waters).

The Ninth Circuit’s "substantial nexus" test—under which an injury occurring onshore may be covered if it is sufficiently connected to shelf operations—finds no support in the statutory text. *See* *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) ("Where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning the duty of interpretation does not arise."). The statute does not merely require that an injury *result from* shelf operations; it requires that the injury occur *as the result of operations conducted on the shelf*. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This distinction is critical. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutory coverage provisions must be strictly construed in favor of the employer. *See* *Sun Ship, Inc. v. Pennsylvania*, 447 U.S. 715, 719 (1980) (citing *Voris v. Eikel*, 346 U.S. 328, 333 (1953)).

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), correctly applies this principle. The court held that an injury must occur on the shelf itself to trigger OCSLA coverage, reasoning that the statute’s language "clearly limits coverage to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf." *Id.* at 362. This interpretation aligns with the OCSLA’s structure, which extends federal jurisdiction only to the shelf and adjacent waters. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a) (defining the outer Continental Shelf as "submerged lands lying seaward and outside the area of lands beneath navigable waters").

The Third Circuit’s contrary decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is unpersuasive. The court relied on a "but for" test, under which an injury is covered if it would not have occurred but for shelf operations. *Id.* at 811. However, this test ignores the statute’s explicit geographic limitation. *See* *Bifulco v. United States*, 444 F.2d 124, 126 (3d Cir. 1971) ("A court may not rewrite a statute to include provisions not found in the clear language of the enactment."). The Supreme Court has cautioned against such expansive interpretations of workers’ compensation statutes. *See* *Nardone v. United States*, 302 U.S. 379, 384 (1937) ("[S]tatutes which impose liability without fault are to be narrowly construed.").

**II. THE SUPREME COURT’S DICTUM IN *OFFSHORE LOGISTICS, INC. v. TALLENTIRE* SUPPORTS A SITUS-OF-INJURY REQUIREMENT.**

In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Supreme Court noted in dicta that § 1333(b) "superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement." *Id.* at 219 n.2. While the Court did not directly address the issue, its observation underscores that OCSLA coverage is fundamentally tied to the shelf’s geographic boundaries. *See* *United States v. Dickerson*, 310 U.S. 554, 562 (1940) (noting that judicial exposition of a statute should not be deemed controlling where the scope of the statute is determined by the words employed by Congress).

The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to dismiss this dictum as unconsidered is unavailing. *See* *Valladolid*, 6937 F.3d at 6942. The Supreme Court’s statements, even if not dispositive, are entitled to "respectful consideration" and should not be "blandly shrugged off." *Zal v. Steppe*, 968 F.2d 924, 935 n.17 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc) (quoting *Humphrey’s Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935)). The Court’s observation that § 1333(b) imposes a "situs requirement" aligns with the statute’s text and purpose.

**III. THE LHWCA’S MARITIME SITUS REQUIREMENT FURTHER CONFIRMS THAT OCSLA COVERAGE IS LIMITED TO INJURIES ON THE SHELF.**

The LHWCA itself requires that an injury occur "upon the navigable waters of the United States" to trigger coverage. 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Supreme Court has held that this requirement is strictly construed. *See* *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 424 (1985) ("The coverage provisions of the Act are ‘strictly construed in favor of the employer.’") (quoting *Voris v. Eikel*, 346 U.S. 328, 333 (1953)). The OCSLA’s extension of LHWCA coverage does not alter this requirement. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (limiting coverage to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf").

The Ninth Circuit’s decision conflates the OCSLA’s "operations on the shelf" requirement with the LHWCA’s maritime situs requirement. *See* *Valladolid*, 6937 F.3d at 6959. However, the two are distinct. The OCSLA extends LHWCA coverage to shelf operations, but the LHWCA’s situs requirement remains unchanged. *See* *Pfeiffer v. Ford Motor Co.*, 444 U.S. 69, 83 (1979) (holding that the LHWCA covers workers engaged in "intermediate steps of moving cargo between ship and land transportation"). The court’s reliance on *Pfeiffer* is misplaced, as that case involved injuries occurring on a maritime situs. *Id.* at 71.

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA’s extension of LHWCA coverage is limited to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The Ninth Circuit’s "substantial nexus" test is inconsistent with the statute’s text and purpose. The Supreme Court’s dictum in *Tallentire* supports a situs-of-injury requirement, and the LHWCA’s maritime situs requirement further confirms this interpretation. The judgment below should be reversed.
