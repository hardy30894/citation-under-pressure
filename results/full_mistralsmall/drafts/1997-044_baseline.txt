**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Debt Arising from Fraud Is Dischargeable Under the Bankruptcy Code.**

The Bankruptcy Code permits discharge of all debts except those excepted by statute. 11 U.S.C. § 727(b). Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … actual fraud.” The Third Circuit correctly held that debts arising from fraud are nondischargeable, but erred in extending that rule to statutorily imposed treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act. Those awards are punitive in nature and not “obtained by” fraud within the meaning of §523(a)(2)(A).

A. **The Debt for Overcharged Rent Is Dischargeable.**

The Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge only those debts “obtained by … actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. §523(a)(2)(A). To establish nondischargeability under this provision, a creditor must prove five elements by a preponderance of the evidence: (1) the debtor obtained money or property through a material misrepresentation; (2) the debtor knew the representation was false or made it with gross recklessness; (3) the debtor intended to deceive; (4) the creditor reasonably relied on the misrepresentation; and (5) the creditor sustained damages proximately caused by the misrepresentation. *In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992). The Bankruptcy Court found all five elements satisfied, but its findings rest on an erroneous legal premise: that silence alone can constitute a “false representation” under §523(a)(2)(A).

This Court has never held that mere nondisclosure constitutes a false representation for purposes of §523(a)(2)(A). To the contrary, the Supreme Court has cautioned that “[t]he nondischargeability provisions of the Bankruptcy Code are to be construed strictly in favor of the debtor and against the creditor.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286 (1991). The Third Circuit has likewise emphasized that §523(a)(2)(A) requires a “false representation,” not merely a failure to disclose. *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118 (3d Cir.1995) (discussing §523(a)(2)(B), but noting the parallel requirement of a false statement). The Bankruptcy Court’s conclusion that Cohen’s silence in setting rents constituted a false representation thus misapprehends the statute.

Even assuming arguendo that Cohen’s conduct could be deemed a false representation, the record does not support a finding of reckless indifference or intent to deceive. The Bankruptcy Court found that Cohen “believed he was permitted to charge the fair market value for each apartment but could only increase the rent of existing tenants by six percent,” and that he “was not aware that the rent control ordinance set limits on the amount of rent that could be charged for a vacant apartment.” *In re Cohen*, 185 B.R. 171, 175 (Bankr.D.N.J.1994). Such negligence, even if gross, does not rise to the level of reckless indifference or intent to deceive required under §523(a)(2)(A). *See In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr.E.D.Va.1991) (“Recklessness is usually accompanied by a conscious indifference to the consequences … [and] must exceed negligence and rise to the level of reckless disregard for truth.”).

B. **Statutorily Imposed Treble Damages, Attorney’s Fees, and Costs Are Not Dischargeable as Debts “Obtained by” Fraud.**

The Third Circuit held that the treble damages award, attorney’s fees, and costs are nondischargeable because they “were obtained by” Cohen’s fraud. *In re Cohen*, 1996 WL 10690, at *5 (3d Cir. Jan. 24, 1996). This conclusion misconstrues the statutory text. Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only those debts “obtained by … actual fraud.” The treble damages award, attorney’s fees, and costs were not “obtained by” fraud; they were imposed by statute as punishment for Cohen’s conduct.

The Ninth Circuit has held that punitive damages are dischargeable under §523(a)(2)(A) because they are not “obtained by” fraud. *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 198, 201 (9th Cir.1991). The Eleventh Circuit reached the opposite result, but only because the punitive damages in that case were discretionary and imposed by a jury. *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 678 (11th Cir.1993). Here, by contrast, the treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs are mandatory and imposed by statute. They are not “obtained by” fraud; they are penalties imposed *after* the fraud occurred.

The Supreme Court has instructed that the Bankruptcy Code’s exceptions to discharge “must be confined to those plainly expressed.” *Kawaauhau v. Geiger*, 523 U.S. 57, 62 (1998). The Third Circuit’s expansive reading of §523(a)(2)(A) to encompass statutorily imposed penalties conflicts with this principle. The Court should reverse the judgment insofar as it excepts the treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs from discharge.

**II. The Bankruptcy Court’s Findings Are Not Entitled to Deference.**

The Third Circuit reviewed the Bankruptcy Court’s legal conclusions de novo, but mischaracterized the standard of review for the Bankruptcy Court’s “ultimate fact” findings. *In re Cohen*, 1996 WL 10690, at *3. The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Universal Minerals, Inc. v. C.A. Hughes & Co.*, 669 F.2d 98 (3d Cir.1981), is misplaced. *Universal Minerals* addressed the standard of review for the district court’s review of the bankruptcy court’s findings, not the standard of review for the court of appeals. *Id.* at 102. The court of appeals owes no deference to the bankruptcy court’s legal conclusions, which are reviewed de novo. *Id.*

The Bankruptcy Court’s conclusion that Cohen’s silence constituted a false representation is a legal error, not a factual finding. The Court should correct that error and hold that the debt for overcharged rent is dischargeable, and that the statutorily imposed treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs are not excepted from discharge under §523(a)(2)(A).
