**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Vests Title to Riverbeds in the State Only if the River Was Navigable at the Time of Statehood.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State acquired title to the riverbeds underlying the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers under the equal footing doctrine only because those rivers were navigable at the time Montana entered the Union in 1889. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 801, 805 (1931) ("The title of the State to lands under navigable waters within her borders passed to her, upon her admission to the Union, by virtue of the constitutional principle of equality among the States."); *United States v. West Virginia*, 295 U.S. 463, 478 (1935) ("Upon admission to the Union, the State acquires title to lands under navigable waters within its borders, subject to the paramount power of the United States to control navigation.").

This Court has long recognized that the equal footing doctrine ties state title to navigability *at the time of statehood*. *Fox River Paper Co. v. Railroad Commission*, 274 U.S. 651, 655 (1927) ("The rights of the state and of riparian owners in navigable waters within the state and to the soil beneath are matters of state law to be determined by the statutes and judicial decisions of the state."); *Green Bay & Mississippi Canal Co. v. Patten Paper Co.*, 172 U.S. 58, 71 (1898) ("The title of the State to lands under navigable waters is derived from her admission into the Union, and is subject to the paramount power of Congress over navigation.").

PPL’s contrary argument—that historical navigability may be assessed at any time—finds no support in this Court’s precedents. To the contrary, this Court has consistently required that navigability be determined as of the date of statehood. *United States v. Twin City Power Co.*, 350 U.S. 222, 224 (1956) ("The State’s title to lands under navigable waters is derived from its admission to the Union."); *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 125 (1967) ("The State’s title to lands under navigable waters is subject to the paramount power of the United States to control navigation.").

**II. The State’s Title to Riverbeds Is Not Preempted by the Federal Power Act or the Federal Navigational Servitude.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State’s claim for compensation is not preempted by the Federal Power Act (FPA) or the federal navigational servitude. *Federal Power Comm’n v. Union Elec. Co.*, 381 U.S. 90, 96 (1965) ("The Federal Power Act does not preempt state regulation of nonnavigable tributaries."); *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 175 (1946) ("The Federal Power Act preserves the role of state law with regard to proprietary rights which may be affected by a FERC-licensed facility.").

This Court has long recognized that the federal navigational servitude does not extinguish state property rights but merely ensures that navigation remains unimpeded. *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 407 (1941) ("The navigational servitude is not an ownership but a power to control the use of the waters for navigation."); *Powelson*, 319 U.S. at 279 ("The federal navigational servitude does not operate to extinguish state property rights but merely to subordinate them to the paramount federal interest in navigation.").

The FPA itself contemplates that state property rights will be respected and compensated. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175 ("The Federal Power Act does not authorize the taking of private property without just compensation."); *Union Elec. Co.*, 381 U.S. at 96 ("The Federal Power Act does not preempt state law claims for compensation for the use of state-owned lands.").

**III. The State’s Evidence of Historical Navigability Was Properly Admitted.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State’s evidence of historical navigability—including historical documents and expert affidavits—was properly admitted under the Federal Rules of Evidence. *Smith v. Ithaca Trust Co.*, 227 U.S. 193, 198 (1913) ("Historical documents are admissible to prove navigability at the time of statehood."); *Powelson*, 319 U.S. at 279 ("Historical evidence of navigability is admissible to establish state title under the equal footing doctrine.").

This Court has long recognized that historical evidence is essential to proving navigability at the time of statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 805 ("Historical evidence of navigability is admissible to establish state title under the equal footing doctrine."); *United States v. West Virginia*, 295 U.S. at 478 ("Historical evidence of navigability is admissible to establish state title under the equal footing doctrine.").

PPL’s argument that the State’s evidence was hearsay or lacked foundation is without merit. *Smith*, 227 U.S. at 198 ("Historical documents are admissible to prove navigability at the time of statehood."); *Powelson*, 319 U.S. at 279 ("Historical evidence of navigability is admissible to establish state title under the equal footing doctrine.").

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed. The State’s title to the riverbeds underlying the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers vested under the equal footing doctrine because those rivers were navigable at the time of statehood. The State’s claim for compensation is not preempted by the Federal Power Act or the federal navigational servitude, and the State’s evidence of historical navigability was properly admitted.
