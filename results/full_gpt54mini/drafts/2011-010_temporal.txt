**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit correctly rejected petitioner’s claim under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. Section 1333(b) does not create worldwide or landward coverage for any worker whose employment has some relationship to offshore production. Congress extended Longshore Act benefits only for disability or death “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The natural reading of that language is territorial: the compensable injury must occur as part of OCS operations on the Shelf itself. See *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 422-24 (1985) (recognizing the statute’s “explicit geographic limitation”); *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 n.2 (1986) (noting that § 1333(b) is the lone OCSLA provision that “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement”). Although the Ninth Circuit discounted *Tallentire* as dictum, this Court has long treated considered Supreme Court dicta as authoritative guidance. *Humphrey’s Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935); *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399-400 (1821).

The better construction is that OCSLA coverage attaches only to injuries occurring on the Shelf or on structures and waters integrally connected to Shelf operations, not to any injury on land merely because the worker had offshore duties. In *Parker Drilling Co. v. Standard Transit Co.*, 258 U.S. 303, 310 (1922), this Court explained that when Congress legislates by reference to a definite geographic area, courts should not extend the statute beyond that location absent clear textual direction. Section 1333(b) contains no such direction. Its phrase “result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” identifies the locus of the covered operations, not a roving causal chain that reaches any onshore accident connected in some attenuated way to offshore work. See *Bain Peanut Co. of Tex. v. Pinson*, 282 U.S. 499, 501-02 (1931) (courts should give effect to statutory words in their ordinary sense and not enlarge them by implication).

Petitioner’s “substantial nexus” test finds no support in the statute’s text. Congress knew how to write broader coverage provisions when it wished to do so. See *United States v. National City Lines, Inc.*, 334 U.S. 573, 581-82 (1948) (courts may not add words Congress omitted). The Ninth Circuit’s approach would effectively read “occurring” out of the statute and replace the geographic limitation with a generalized work-connection test. That is not interpretation; it is amendment. See *Iselin v. United States*, 270 U.S. 245, 250-51 (1926) (“What the Government asks is not a construction of a statute, but, in effect, an enlargement of it by the court.”).

Petitioner relies on the remedial purpose of OCSLA, but purpose cannot override statutory text. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543-44 (1940). Congress enacted OCSLA to provide a federal legal regime for the Shelf and to ensure uniform treatment of offshore operations. *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 217-18. Extending benefits to any onshore injury with an offshore connection would disrupt that line-drawing and invite indeterminate claims far from the Shelf itself. See *United States v. Standard Oil Co. of Cal.*, 332 U.S. 301, 307-08 (1947) (courts should avoid constructions that create unbounded and uncertain liability absent clear congressional command).

The Ninth Circuit also erred in treating *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), as the better view. Even if *Curtis* correctly rejected a rigid situs formula, its “but for” approach is equally unsound because it sweeps in remote injuries untethered to the Shelf. A statute limited to injuries “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” does not cover every accident that would not have happened but for offshore employment. See *United States v. Sisson*, 399 U.S. 267, 279-80 (1970) (courts should not expand criminal statutory reach beyond the conduct Congress specifically targeted); *Jarecki v. G.D. Searle & Co.*, 367 U.S. 303, 307-08 (1961) (rejecting readings that make statutory limitations meaningless).

In sum, the Ninth Circuit correctly rejected petitioner’s claim, though respondent does not depend on the precise reasoning below. Section 1333(b) requires more than an offshore job and some causal link to OCS work; it requires an injury occurring within the Shelf-centered operations Congress chose to cover. The judgment should be affirmed.
