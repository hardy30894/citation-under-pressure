### Argument

The School District violated the First Amendment when it denied Lamb’s Chapel access to school premises because the proposed films were “church related.” Once the District opened its facilities for community use after hours, it could not exclude speech on the basis of its religious viewpoint. The Constitution forbids that form of discrimination. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939); *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 160 (1939); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-11 (1940).

The government may of course regulate the use of its property, but when it opens property for expressive activity it may not engage in content discrimination. *Hague*, 307 U.S. at 515-16, explained that public places used for assembly and communication are subject to First Amendment protection. *Jamison v. Texas*, 318 U.S. 413, 416-17 (1943), likewise held that the State may not suppress advocacy merely because it disapproves of the speaker’s message. And *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 537 (1945), rejected administrative discretion that burdened expression on matters of public concern. Those cases establish the core point here: when government opens a forum for speech, it may not close it to a disfavored viewpoint.

That principle applies with special force where the exclusion is based on religion. In *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-11, the Court held that the First Amendment protects religious proselytizing as speech. In *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 111-12 (1943), the Court explained that spreading religious views is not a lesser form of expression. And in *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943), the Court made clear that official orthodoxy is forbidden: government may not prescribe what shall be orthodox in matters of opinion. Excluding a film series because it presents family values from a Christian perspective is precisely that kind of orthodoxy.

The District’s policy was not neutral. It did not deny access because the films would cause disruption, interfere with scheduling, or exceed reasonable time limits. It denied access because the films were religious in nature. That is content discrimination at the very least, and viewpoint discrimination in substance. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 551-58 (1965), and *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 559, 563-65 (1965), invalidated selective suppression of expression based on official disagreement with the message. *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 235 (1963), likewise rejected punishment of peaceful expression because authorities disliked what was being said. The District’s refusal here rests on the same unconstitutional premise.

Nor can the District justify its exclusion by invoking the fact that the speech was religious. The First Amendment does not allow government to favor secular discussion over religious discussion. *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 15-18 (1947), held that the State may not prefer one faith over another or religion over nonreligion; neutrality is the rule. But neutrality is not served by excluding religious speech from an otherwise open forum. To the contrary, *Murdock*, 319 U.S. at 111-12, and *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-11, make clear that religious speech stands on equal constitutional footing with other advocacy.

The District also cannot recast its exclusion as a permissible forum restriction. Even in settings that are not traditional public fora, the government may not enforce exclusions that are unreasonable or that suppress a speaker because of disfavored ideas. *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501, 506-09 (1946), held that government-controlled property used as a place of public interaction is subject to First Amendment limits. *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949), emphasized that speech does not lose protection because it provokes controversy. And *Feiner v. New York*, 340 U.S. 315, 320-21 (1951), confirms that restrictions must be justified by genuine necessity, not official hostility. Here, the District identified no neutral basis for excluding Lamb’s Chapel beyond the religious nature of its message.

Finally, the Establishment Clause does not support the District’s refusal. The District’s position rests on the mistaken premise that permitting religious speech would amount to state endorsement of religion. But *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-04, and *Everson*, 330 U.S. at 17-18, require neutrality, not hostility. Allowing equal access to a religious speaker in an open forum does not establish religion; excluding the speaker because of religion does the opposite by preferring the secular over the religious.

Because the District denied Lamb’s Chapel access solely on the ground that its proposed films were religiously oriented, the judgment below should be reversed. The First Amendment forbids a school district from opening its facilities to the public and then excluding one speaker because its viewpoint is religious.
