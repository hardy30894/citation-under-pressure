# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEXT OF SECTION 1333(b) CONTAINS NO SITUS-OF-INJURY REQUIREMENT

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage to any "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The statute's geographic limitation attaches to "operations," not to the situs of injury. Petitioners ask this Court to read into the statute a requirement Congress did not write—that the injury itself occur on the Shelf. This Court "must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says." *Conn. Nat'l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992). Congress required only a causal relationship—"resulting from"—between the injury and OCS operations. It did not require that the injury be geographically situated on the Shelf.

This reading is reinforced by contrast with the OCSLA choice-of-law provision, § 1333(a)(2)(A), which expressly applies "to the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon." Congress knew how to draft a situs-specific provision when it wanted one. Section 1333(b) contains no comparable locational restriction. "Where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another," this Court has held, "it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely." *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983).

## II. THE THIRD CIRCUIT'S "BUT-FOR" CAUSATION TEST FAITHFULLY IMPLEMENTS THE STATUTE

The Third Circuit in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), correctly held that § 1333(b) requires only that the claimant establish that the injury would not have occurred "but for" operations on the OCS. That court explained that the "operative language of § 1333(b)... focuses on the cause of the injury, not on the place of the injury." *Id.* at 810. Applying that test, the Third Circuit awarded benefits to a worker injured in a car accident on his way to a helicopter that would transport him to an offshore platform. *Id.* at 806, 811. The Ninth Circuit's "substantial nexus" formulation, adopted below, is simply a refinement of this causation-based approach, ensuring the required nexus is not too attenuated while remaining faithful to the statutory text's focus on causation rather than location.

By contrast, the Fifth Circuit's contrary situs-of-injury rule in *Mills v. Director, OWCP*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), rests on a misreading of dicta in this Court's decision in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986). *Mills* denied benefits to a welder injured during onshore construction of a platform destined for the Shelf, reasoning that OCSLA claimants must show injury "on the situs of an OCS platform" in addition to satisfying the but-for test. 877 F.2d at 362. But as the court below correctly recognized, the *Tallentire* footnote on which *Mills* relies is "textbook dictum." The issue in *Tallentire* was whether the § 1333(a)(2)(A) choice-of-law provision extended to a helicopter crash over Shelf waters—the Court expressly stated that § 1333(b) "has no bearing on this case." 477 U.S. at 219 n.2. A footnote addressing a provision the Court itself deemed irrelevant to its disposition cannot bear the interpretive weight *Mills* places upon it. This Court has cautioned that "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used," and their "possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." *Humphrey's Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935) (quoting *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399-400 (1821)).

## III. THE PURPOSE OF OCSLA CONFIRMS THAT COVERAGE TURNS ON CAUSATION, NOT LOCATION

Congress enacted OCSLA's compensation scheme to ensure that workers engaged in the extraction of natural resources from the Shelf receive compensation for injuries arising from that hazardous work, regardless of the fortuity of where the injury manifests. A rule denying coverage merely because an employee happened to be onshore when injured—notwithstanding that the injury arose from Shelf operations—would produce arbitrary results untethered to any statutory purpose. A roustabout injured while performing maintenance duties integral to the platform's operations, or while traveling to or from the platform, should not lose coverage based solely on which side of an invisible three-mile line his body happened to occupy at the moment of injury.

This Court has recognized that OCSLA's compensation scheme should be read consistently with its remedial purpose. In *Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), the Court observed that OCSLA's coverage was designed with attention to the "explicit geographic limitation" concerning where *operations* occur—not an independent requirement that the injury itself be so located. *Id.* at 427 (Blackmun, J., dissenting) (characterizing majority's discussion of § 1333(b) coverage). The statute's geographic focus is on the situs of *operations*, ensuring OCSLA benefits are tethered to genuine Shelf-related extractive activity, while the causation requirement—"resulting from"—guards against limitless liability by requiring a substantial connection between the injury and those operations.

## IV. RESPONDENT SATISFIES THE SUBSTANTIAL NEXUS TEST

Juan Valladolid spent 98% of his working time aboard the Hogan platform on the outer Continental Shelf. His death occurred while consolidating scrap metal generated by platform operations—a maintenance task directly connected to, and arising from, the OCS extraction operations that constituted his employment. This is not a case of an employee with only incidental or fortuitous connection to Shelf operations; it is a case where an employee's entire employment was devoted to OCS extractive activity, and his death arose directly from duties integral to that employment. Under either the Third Circuit's but-for test or the substantial-nexus formulation adopted below, Mrs. Valladolid has established the required causal connection.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
