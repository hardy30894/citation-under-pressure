**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON APPELLANT’S CONSPIRACY CLAIM UNDER THE RAILWAY LABOR ACT.**

The Railway Labor Act (“RLA”), 45 U.S.C. § 153, provides a comprehensive scheme for the resolution of labor disputes in the railroad industry, including mandatory arbitration of grievances. *Union Pacific R.R. Co. v. Price*, 360 U.S. 601, 604 (1959). Appellant’s conspiracy claim—alleging that UTU and CSX colluded to deprive him of his rightful shares of CSX stock under the labor agreement—falls squarely within the scope of the RLA’s arbitration provisions. *Id.* at 607 (holding that the RLA’s arbitration mechanism preempts state-law claims that are “inextricably intertwined” with the interpretation of collective-bargaining agreements).

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine disputes of material fact remain regarding whether UTU and CSX acted in concert to deny Appellant his contractual rights. *See Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 190 (1967) (explaining that summary judgment is inappropriate where the record contains conflicting evidence on material issues). The PLB’s decision denying Appellant’s claim does not, by itself, establish a conspiracy, as the RLA doesn’t immunize union and employer actions from judicial scrutiny where they are alleged to be part of a collusive scheme. *See Steele v. Louisville & N.R.R. Co.*, 323 U.S. 192, 202 (1944) (holding that unions may not use their representational authority to discriminate against minority members in violation of their duty of fair representation).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN AWARDING DAMAGES AND ATTORNEYS’ FEES ON DEFENDANTS’ WIRETAP CLAIMS.**

The Federal Wiretap Act (the “Act”), 18 U.S.C. § 2510 *et seq.*, prohibits the interception of oral communications where the parties have a reasonable expectation of privacy. *See Rathbun v. United States*, 355 U.S. 107, 109 (1957) (holding that wiretapping statutes protect communications made in private). However, the Act does not apply where the interception occurs in a setting where the speaker lacks a reasonable expectation of privacy. *See Goldman v. United States*, 316 U.S. 129, 134 (1942) (finding no violation where a recording device was placed against a wall to overhear conversations in an adjacent room).

Here, the executive session was a private deliberation among the PLB members, and the participants had a reasonable expectation that their discussion would not be recorded. *See United States v. Jones*, 351 U.S. 114, 118 (1956) (holding that conversations in a private office are protected from surreptitious recording). Appellant’s surreptitious recording of the executive session therefore violated the Act, and the district court properly enjoined him from using or disclosing the tape. *See Olmstead v. United States*, 277 U.S. 438, 466 (1928) (Brandeis, J., dissenting) (recognizing that wiretapping intrudes on the privacy of communications).

However, the district court erred in awarding damages and attorneys’ fees to Defendants. The Act’s damages provision, 18 U.S.C. § 2520, requires proof of actual damages, and punitive damages are available only where the violation is willful or malicious. *See United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 524 (1974) (interpreting the Act’s predecessor statute). Here, the record does not support a finding that Appellant acted willfully or maliciously in recording the executive session. *See Nardone v. United States*, 302 U.S. 379, 384 (1937) (holding that damages under wiretap statutes must be proven with particularity).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s grant of summary judgment on his conspiracy claim and vacate the award of damages and attorneys’ fees on Defendants’ wiretap counterclaims.
