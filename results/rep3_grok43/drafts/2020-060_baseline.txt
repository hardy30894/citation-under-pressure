**ARGUMENT**

Section 113(f)(3)(B) of CERCLA provides that a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” may seek contribution. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The 2004 consent decree resolved only Clean Water Act violations and therefore did not trigger that provision.

CERCLA distinguishes sharply between cost-recovery actions under section 107(a) and contribution actions under section 113(f). *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 161 (2004). As originally enacted, CERCLA “contained no provision expressly providing for a right of action for contribution.” *Id.* at 162. Congress added section 113(f) in 1986 to supply that remedy, but limited it to parties that have resolved CERCLA liability. *Id.* at 167. The statute of limitations for contribution is three years from entry of the settlement; the limitations period for a remedial cost-recovery action is six years. 42 U.S.C. §§ 9613(g)(2)(B), (g)(3).

The 2004 decree was entered in litigation brought solely under the Clean Water Act. It required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and install a cover system to eliminate untreated leachate discharges. The decree states that it is “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3. Because the decree did not resolve liability “for some or all of a response action” under CERCLA, it did not satisfy the statutory predicate for section 113(f)(3)(B). *See Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167.

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that substantive obligations alone can trigger contribution rights—contradicts the text. Section 113(f)(3)(B) requires resolution of CERCLA liability, not merely performance of work that might incidentally address hazardous substances. *See United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (CERCLA imposes liability only for “hazardous substances” as defined). The decree’s disclaimer of liability reinforces that no CERCLA liability was resolved. *See Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States*, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009) (emphasizing that CERCLA liability turns on statutory elements, not equitable considerations).

Guam therefore retains its timely section 107(a) claim. The judgment below should be reversed.
