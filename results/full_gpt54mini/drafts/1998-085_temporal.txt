Respondent’s argument is straightforward: punitive damages under Title VII require more than a bare showing that the employer acted with discriminatory animus. Congress used the punitive-damages standard to reach only especially blameworthy conduct, not every intentional violation. That reading is consistent with the traditional common-law rule that punitive damages are reserved for conduct exhibiting “actual malice” or its equivalent, and it follows from the Supreme Court’s repeated insistence that exemplary damages are not the ordinary remedy for statutory wrongs. See Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851); Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550, 563-64 (1886); Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489, 493 (1875).

At common law, punitive damages were never automatic upon a finding of liability. The Court has long held that such awards are “for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant,” and only where the wrongdoing is accompanied by aggravating circumstances. Day, 54 U.S. (13 How.) at 371. In Arms, the Court explained that exemplary damages are appropriate only where the defendant’s conduct is “wanton” or “malicious,” not merely unlawful. 91 U.S. at 493. Barry likewise emphasized that punitive damages require a showing of conduct “wanton, or reckless, or malicious.” 116 U.S. at 563-64. That settled principle matters here because Congress legislated against the background of ordinary common-law punitive-damages doctrine. See Day, 54 U.S. (13 How.) at 371; Barry, 116 U.S. at 563-64.

Nothing in Title VII’s text displaces that background rule. A statute silent on the quantum of reprehensibility needed for exemplary damages should not be read to authorize punishment for any intentional violation. See Tullis v. Lake Erie & W.R. Co., 175 U.S. 348, 352-53 (1899) (punitive damages depend on the nature of the wrong and the circumstances of aggravation). Nor should “malice” be treated as a talisman divorced from its punitive-damages setting. In the common-law cases, malice in this context has meant a conscious disregard of rights coupled with aggravating conduct, not simply the existence of discriminatory intent. See Barry, 116 U.S. at 563-64; Arms, 91 U.S. at 493.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would collapse the distinction between liability and punishment. But the Court has repeatedly recognized that the law may impose liability for intentional wrongdoing while reserving punishment for conduct that is independently egregious. See Tullis, 175 U.S. at 352-53; Barry, 116 U.S. at 563-64. That distinction is especially important in employment cases, where liability can rest on pretext or improper motive, yet punitive damages are meant to deter only the most culpable violations. See Arms, 91 U.S. at 493. If every intentional Title VII violation supported punitive damages, then the statutory standard would do no work beyond the liability standard. Courts should avoid reading a damages statute to erase the line between compensatory relief and punishment. See Day, 54 U.S. (13 How.) at 371.

Nor is there any unfairness in requiring proof of egregiousness beyond discriminatory intent. Punitive damages are exceptional, and the Court has long treated them as such. See Barry, 116 U.S. at 563-64; Tullis, 175 U.S. at 352-53. A defendant who intentionally discriminates may still have acted under a mistaken understanding of the law or in a manner lacking the kind of wantonness that justifies punishment. The common law’s insistence on aggravating circumstances preserves that separation. See Arms, 91 U.S. at 493.

Accordingly, this Court should hold that punitive damages under Title VII are available only when the employer’s conduct is independently egregious or outrageous, beyond proof of intentional discrimination alone. That rule best fits the traditional doctrine governing exemplary damages and prevents punitive awards from becoming automatic in every case of intentional disparate treatment. See Day, 54 U.S. (13 How.) at 371; Barry, 116 U.S. at 563-64; Arms, 91 U.S. at 493.
