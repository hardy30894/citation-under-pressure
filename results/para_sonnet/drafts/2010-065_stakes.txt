# ARGUMENT

## I. A Private Individual Convicted Under a Federal Criminal Statute Lacks Standing to Assert That the Statute Exceeds Congress's Enumerated Powers Under the Tenth Amendment.

This Court has already resolved the question presented. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies challenged the federal Tennessee Valley Authority's sale of electric power as exceeding Congress's enumerated powers and violating the Tenth Amendment. This Court held that the private companies had "no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." *Id.* at 144. The Court explained that Tenth Amendment claims belong to the States, not to private litigants: absent the States or their officers asserting the claim, private parties may not invoke the Amendment on the States' behalf. *Id.*

That holding controls here. The Tenth Amendment provides that "[t]he powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." U.S. Const. amend. X. By its own terms, the Amendment protects the States as sovereign entities in our federal system—it is, as this Court has recognized, a structural limitation reserved to sovereigns, not a font of individual constitutional rights. *See New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 181 (1992) (Tenth Amendment "confirms" that constitutional limits on federal power "protect[ ] the sovereignty of the States"). Because the Amendment protects state sovereignty rather than individual liberty, it follows that the right to complain of its violation runs to the States, not to private citizens prosecuted under an assertedly ultra vires federal statute.

The courts of appeals that have followed *Tennessee Electric Power* have reasoned soundly. The First Circuit, in *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25 (1st Cir. 2005), *cert. denied*, 548 U.S. 904 (2006), explained that when a State "refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state's policy of acquiescence." *Id.* at 35. The Tenth, Ninth, Second, and Eighth Circuits have likewise held that private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims. *See United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004), *cert. denied*, 543 U.S. 874 (2004); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006), *cert. denied*, 552 U.S. 1097 (2008); *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009).

Petitioner will contend that the relevant language in *Tennessee Electric Power* is dicta. It is not. As the First Circuit correctly observed, this Court "dismissed the Tenth Amendment claim after analyzing both the standing issue and the merits, and hence, the former holding is an alternative ground, rather than obiter dictum." *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 34 (citing *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978)). Moreover, because the standing question in *Tennessee Electric Power* concerned this Court's constitutional jurisdiction under Article III, its resolution necessarily predominates over any subsequent discussion of the merits. *See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't*, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95 (1998) (jurisdictional questions must be resolved before reaching the merits).

Nor can petitioner escape *Tennessee Electric Power* by characterizing her claim as one of individual liberty rather than state sovereignty. The Tenth Amendment "is not a font of individual rights"; it is, instead, a reservation of power to the States as sovereign entities within the federal system. A criminal defendant's assertion that Congress lacked authority to enact a given statute is, at bottom, a separation-of-powers argument belonging to the sovereign whose authority was allegedly usurped—the States. This is confirmed by the structure of the Tenth Amendment itself, which speaks in terms of powers "reserved to the States," not personal liberties retained by individuals. Where, as here, no State has intervened to assert that § 229 intrudes upon its reserved powers, and where the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania has not disclaimed the prosecution of conduct within its own borders, petitioner cannot manufacture standing to litigate a sovereign's prerogative on the sovereign's behalf.

This rule works no injustice. Petitioner remains free to raise—and did raise below—every other constitutional challenge available to a criminal defendant, including vagueness and overbreadth under the Due Process Clause. What she may not do is commandeer the Tenth Amendment, a provision designed to protect the States' role in the federal system, to advance what is in substance a personal defense to a federal indictment. The proper party to complain that Congress trenched upon reserved state authority is a State itself, or one of its officers acting in that capacity—not a private citizen whose only interest is avoiding conviction.

## II. Even If Standing Existed, 18 U.S.C. § 229 Is a Valid Exercise of Congress's Power Under the Necessary and Proper Clause to Implement the Chemical Weapons Convention.

Should this Court reach the merits, § 229 is plainly constitutional. This Court held in *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), that "[i]f the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government." *Id.* at 432. The Court further recognized that "there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could." *Id.* at 433. Section 229 implements the Chemical Weapons Convention, a valid treaty ratified by two-thirds of the Senate, and Congress's implementing legislation is therefore a proper exercise of the Necessary and Proper Clause regardless of whether the statute also satisfies some independent enumerated power. *See United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004).

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
