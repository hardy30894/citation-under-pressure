**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that OCSLA extends LHWCA coverage to workers injured on land. The statutory text, this Court’s precedents, and the structure of OCSLA all require that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

**I. The Plain Language of OCSLA Imposes a Geographic Situs Requirement**

OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision extends LHWCA benefits to the “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” defines the geographical locus of the covered event. When the statutory language is clear, judicial inquiry ends. *Packard v. Banton*, 264 U.S. 140, 145 (1924); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The word “occurring” links the injury itself to the shelf, not merely the employment or the operations. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress knew how to extend OCSLA benefits beyond the shelf when it wished; the compensation provision is deliberately narrower. *Cf. FTC v. Bunte Bros., Inc.*, 312 U.S. 349, 354 (1941).

**II. This Court’s Precedents Confirm That OCSLA Coverage Turns on Situs**

This Court has long recognized that OCSLA’s coverage is geographically limited. In *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 35-36 (1947), the Court held that the submerged lands seaward of the three-mile limit are within the exclusive jurisdiction and control of the United States. That jurisdiction defines the shelf. The compensation provision must be read in light of that territorial boundary. *See Skiriotes v. Florida*, 313 U.S. 69, 75 (1941) (statutory construction must respect jurisdictional boundaries).

The Fifth Circuit, applying OCSLA before this Court’s later decisions, likewise understood that coverage depends on the location of the injury. In *Pure Oil Co. v. Snipes*, 293 F.2d 393, 396 (5th Cir. 1961), the court held that a worker injured on a fixed platform on the shelf was covered because the injury occurred on the shelf. And in *Loffland Bros. Co. v. Roberts*, 386 F.2d 540, 543 (5th Cir. 1967), the court denied coverage for an injury that occurred on land, explaining that OCSLA’s compensation provision is tied to the shelf. These decisions reflect the consistent understanding that OCSLA is a situs-based statute.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test is an open-ended standard that this Court has rejected when Congress has spoken clearly. *See United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.) (penal statutes are construed strictly, out of tenderness for individual rights and because the power to punish resides in the legislature). Workers’ compensation statutes, while remedial, are still subject to the plain meaning of their terms. *Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 7 (1946); *Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc.*, 314 U.S. 244, 248 (1941).

**III. A Situs-of-Injury Requirement Is Consistent with the Statutory Scheme**

OCSLA was enacted to govern activities on the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The Act’s purpose is to establish federal jurisdiction over the shelf’s natural resources, not to create a nationwide workers’ compensation program for offshore workers. *See United States v. Texas*, 339 U.S. 707, 719 (1950) (recognizing the shelf as a defined geographic area subject to federal control). Extending OCSLA to onshore injuries would disregard the Act’s limited territorial reach. *See Skiriotes*, 313 U.S. at 75.

The Ninth Circuit’s test would also create anomalies. A worker injured in a car accident hundreds of miles from the coast could claim OCSLA benefits if his job had some connection to shelf operations. Congress could not have intended such a result. *See American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. at 543 (courts should not read statutes to produce absurd results).

**IV. Applying the Correct Situs Standard, Valladolid’s Onshore Injury Is Not Covered**

Juan Valladolid was killed at an onshore oil-processing facility on the California coast. Because his injury did not occur on the outer Continental Shelf, it falls outside § 1333(b). The Ninth Circuit’s remand for application of its “substantial nexus” test was error.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
