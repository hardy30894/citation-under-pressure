**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Indictment Was Deficient Because Mrs. Parker Lacked the Authority to Perform the Acts Alleged, and the Government Failed to Allege Any Breach of an Official Duty.**

A. Mrs. Parker’s Acts Were Not “Official Acts” Under 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3) Because She Had No Authority to Grant or Deny Benefits.

The indictment charged Mrs. Parker with public bribery under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C), which prohibits a public official from being induced to “do or omit any act in violation of *[her] official duty.”* The term “official act” is defined by statute as “any decision or action on any question . . . which may at any time be pending, or which may by law be brought before any public official, in such official’s official capacity.” 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). This definition, while broad, is not limitless. It requires that the act be one that the official is lawfully empowered to perform in her official capacity. *See United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 230 (1914) (holding that bribery statutes require an act “which by law is an official act, or which *by law* is required to be performed by the official”). The legislative history confirms that Congress intended to cover “any decision or action taken by a public official *in his capacity as such.*” S. Rep. No. 87-2213, at 3856 (1962).

Here, Mrs. Parker was a clerk whose duties were limited to assisting ALJ Aronson, taking notes, and typing and mailing decisions. The record establishes that she was *not* authorized to approve benefits or sign the ALJ’s name. Because she lacked any authority to grant or deny SSI benefits, her forgery and alteration of letters was not an “official act” within the meaning of Section 201. An act performed wholly outside the scope of one’s official duties—and in direct contravention of those duties—cannot constitute an “official act” for bribery purposes. *See United States v. Hood*, 343 U.S. 148, 151 (1952) (holding that bribery requires an act “in relation to” an official proceeding, not a private act of deception). The government’s contrary interpretation would convert every unauthorized act by a low-level employee into a bribe, a result Congress could not have intended.

B. The Government Failed to Allege an Act in Violation of Mrs. Parker’s Official Duty.

Even if Mrs. Parker’s acts could be considered “official,” the indictment must allege that she acted “in violation of *[her] official duty.*” 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C). This element is not satisfied by showing that an act is merely prohibited by law; it requires a breach of a duty *arising from the official position itself.* *See Birdsall*, 233 U.S. at 229-31 (distinguishing between duties imposed by law and duties imposed by custom or “settled practice,” but requiring that the duty be “official” in nature). The government alleged only that Mrs. Parker forged documents and approved benefits without authority. But her duty as a clerk was to *refrain* from such conduct, not to perform it. An act that is *ultra vires*—entirely outside the scope of her employment—cannot, by definition, be an act within her official duty. *See United States v. Kenneally*, 126 F.2d 857, 860 (2d Cir. 1942) (holding that a customs employee’s theft of goods was not an “official act” because it was “wholly outside the scope of his employment”).

Mrs. Parker is not a policymaker who could be corrupted by performing a lawful act for an unlawful purpose. She was a clerk whose “official duty” was to perform her ministerial tasks honestly. The government’s theory—that she violated her duty by *exceeding* her authority—confuses the crime of bribery with the crime of fraud or forgery. Congress did not intend Section 201 to sweep so broadly. *See Birdsall*, 233 U.S. at 229-30 (limiting bribery to acts that “concern a matter which may be brought before the official in his official capacity”).

For these reasons, the indictment should have been dismissed. Mrs. Parker’s conviction must be reversed.
