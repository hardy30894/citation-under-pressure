**ARGUMENT**

The Board erred in granting Harari the benefit of the 1989 filing date of the '566 application. The intervening '838 and '768 applications failed to incorporate the '579 application by reference with the clarity required by 35 U.S.C. § 120. This Court must reverse.

**I. The Board Applied an Incorrect Legal Standard**

The Board held that the incorporation language in the '838 and '768 applications was sufficient because a reasonable examiner could discern the reference. That standard is too lax. This Court’s prior decision in *Harari I* articulated a narrow rule for the “initial filing stage” of a single application. *Harari v. Hollmer*, 602 F.3d 1348, 1352-53 (Fed. Cir. 2010). But the question here—whether intervening applications in a priority chain satisfy § 120—requires a stricter test.

Section 120 demands that each application in the chain “contain a specific reference to the earlier filed application.” 35 U.S.C. § 120. The purpose is to provide “clear notice” to the public and the Patent Office of the priority claim. *See* *In re Lund*, 376 F.2d 982, 988 (CCPA 1967). This Court has long held that incorporation by reference must be “unambiguous” and “explicit.” *In re Hawkins*, 331 F.2d 876, 878 (CCPA 1964). A “reasonable examiner” standard invites speculation and undermines the statutory requirement of certainty.

**II. The Incorporation Language Was Ambiguous**

The '566 application’s incorporation statement referred to a copending application “filed on the same day as the present application.” J.A. 1204. That language was copied verbatim into the '838 and '768 applications. But those applications were filed years later, on different dates. The phrase “filed on the same day” became false and misleading when transposed into later filings. A reference that is accurate in one context may be “wholly insufficient” in another. *Ex parte Krug*, 1941 Dec. Comm’r Pat. 1, 2 (1941). The Board ignored this temporal mismatch.

The Supreme Court has instructed that statutory requirements for patent applications must be “strictly construed.” *Permutit Co. v. Graver Corp.*, 284 U.S. 52, 60 (1931). Here, the copied language did not “specifically refer” to the '579 application by serial number or filing date, as § 120 requires. *See* *In re Henriksen*, 399 F.2d 253, 256 (CCPA 1968) (holding that a reference must “clearly identify” the earlier application). The Board’s reliance on extrinsic evidence—the transmittal sheet and preliminary amendment—cannot cure an intrinsic defect. *See* *In re Borkowski*, 422 F.2d 904, 909 (CCPA 1970) (holding that incorporation must be “apparent from the face of the application”).

**III. The Board’s Decision Conflicts with Precedent**

This Court has consistently required that incorporation by reference be “clear and unequivocal.” *In re Lund*, 376 F.2d at 988. In *Lund*, the court rejected an incorporation statement that referred to a “copending application” without identifying it by number. *Id.* at 989. Similarly, the statement here—referring to an application “filed on the same day”—is too vague to satisfy § 120. *See also* *In re Hawkins*, 331 F.2d at 878 (holding that “mere reference to a copending application” is insufficient).

The Board’s approach would allow applicants to “bootstrap” priority through ambiguous language, contrary to the public notice function of the patent system. *See* *Permutit Co.*, 284 U.S. at 60. The '838 and '768 applications did not incorporate the '579 application with the requisite clarity. Therefore, the '880 application is not entitled to the 1989 filing date, and Hollmer’s '601 patent should prevail.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the Board’s decision and reinstate judgment in favor of Hollmer.
