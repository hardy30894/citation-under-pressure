ARGUMENT

I. NPS's authority to regulate conduct within the boundaries of a conservation system unit does not depend on who holds title to the land or water beneath the regulated activity.

For over a century this Court has recognized that the National Park Service's regulatory authority attaches to conduct occurring within park boundaries, without regard to whether the particular parcel is federally owned. In Colorado v. Toll, 268 U.S. 228 (1925), the Court considered regulations of the Director of the National Park Service governing automobile traffic and residency within Rocky Mountain National Park even though, as the Court observed, "[t]here are many thousands of acres in the park owned by private persons, and there are houses and hotels that were built before the park was laid out." 268 U.S. at 229. The existence of private ownership within the park's exterior boundaries did not remove those lands, or the conduct occurring on them, from the reach of the Park Service's regulatory scheme; the dispute concerned only the scope of the Director's authority over highways, not whether private inholdings placed conduct beyond regulation altogether.

This Court reached the same conclusion in Curtin v. Benson, 222 U.S. 78 (1911). There, the plaintiff owned patented lands within Yosemite National Park and objected to the Superintendent's regulation restricting the driving of stock "over the park lands." Id. at 79–80. The regulation the Court reviewed expressly acknowledged that "[o]wners of patented lands within the park limits are entitled to the full use and enjoyment thereof," yet subjected the manner of access to and passage over the surrounding park lands to the Superintendent's supervision. Id. Nothing in that case suggests that the boundary of federal regulatory power tracks the boundary of federal title parcel-by-parcel; rather, the regulation of the manner of conduct within the park as a whole was upheld as a valid incident of park administration. See also Collins v. Yosemite Park & Curry Co., 304 U.S. 518, 528 (1938) (recognizing that jurisdiction ceded to the United States over Yosemite extended to "all of the territory which is now or may hereafter be included" within the park, subject only to specific reservations the state chose to retain).

II. Federal authority over navigation and activity on Alaska's waters has never been treated as defeated by state or territorial title to the submerged lands or the surrounding banks.

Sturgeon's theory—that state ownership of the riverbed forecloses federal regulation of the manner in which persons navigate the water above it—cannot be squared with this Court's longstanding treatment of federal authority over navigation in Alaska. In United States v. Pacific & Arctic Railway & Navigation Co., 228 U.S. 87 (1913), the Court held that a combination controlling transportation routes operating "in our territory" was subject to federal law "as we undoubtedly may control our own citizens and our own corporations," id. at 106, regardless of the character of the route's ownership. Similarly, in Interstate Commerce Commission v. United States ex rel. Humboldt Steamship Co., 224 U.S. 474 (1912), the Court examined the scope of federal regulatory jurisdiction over carriers "operating in Alaska," treating the reach of that jurisdiction as a question of statutory text and federal purpose, not land title. And in Territory of Alaska v. Troy, 258 U.S. 101 (1922), Congress's assertion of control over coastwise and river transportation, extending even to "the Yukon river," id. at 108 (reproducing 41 Stat. 999), confirms that federal regulatory power over navigation-related conduct in Alaska has never been thought to stop at the water's edge of state-owned submerged lands.

III. Alaska's statehood disclaimers and the retention of state title to lands and waters do not divest the United States of authority to regulate activity in furtherance of federal programs occurring on or over those lands.

This Court's decisions construing the Alaska Statehood Act confirm that state title does not automatically oust federal regulatory authority exercised for a valid federal purpose. In Organized Village of Kake v. Egan, 369 U.S. 60 (1962), the Court explained that the Act's provisions retaining federal "absolute jurisdiction and control" over certain property demonstrate that "'absolute' federal jurisdiction is not invariably exclusive jurisdiction," and that "[t]he disclaimer of right and title by the State was a disclaimer of proprietary rather than governmental interest." Id. at 66, 68. That same Term, in Metlakatla Indian Community v. Egan, 369 U.S. 45 (1962), the Court sustained the Secretary of the Interior's regulation of fish traps in Alaska waters notwithstanding a contrary state conservation law, underscoring that federal regulatory schemes tied to a legitimate federal interest may operate concurrently with, and even override, state authority over the same waters. See also Hynes v. Grimes Packing Co., 337 U.S. 86, 95–96 (1949) (recognizing the Secretary's authority to "set apart and reserve fishing areas in any of the waters of Alaska over which the United States has jurisdiction" and to regulate "the manner...of...fishing" therein).

IV. Sturgeon's construction of ANILCA § 103(c) cannot be reconciled with this settled framework.

The regulation of the manner of conduct within a conservation system unit—here, a ban on hovercraft use—is precisely the kind of activity-based regulation this Court has repeatedly sustained without regard to subsurface title. Just as "[t]he power of a State to control and regulate the taking of game cannot be questioned," Ward v. Race Horse, 163 U.S. 504, 507 (1896), so too the Park Service's power to control activity within park boundaries, exercised for the protection of park resources and visitors, does not evaporate merely because Alaska holds title to the bed of the Nation River. Section 103(c) should not be read to work such a result.
