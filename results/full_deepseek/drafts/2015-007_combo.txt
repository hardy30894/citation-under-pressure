**ARGUMENT**

The Florida capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth and Eighth Amendments as those provisions were understood before 1970. This Court has long held that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee does not require jury factfinding at sentencing, that state juries need not be unanimous, and that the Eighth Amendment leaves procedural implementation to the States.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Determination of Aggravating Factors**

In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 247 (1949), this Court held that a sentencing judge may “consider information concerning the convicted person’s past life, health, habits, and mental and moral propensities” without submitting those facts to the jury. The Court expressly rejected the argument that the Sixth Amendment requires jury findings on matters bearing on the severity of punishment, even in capital cases. *Id.* at 250-51. That principle was reaffirmed in *Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576, 585 (1959), where the Court upheld a death sentence based on the judge’s independent evaluation of aggravating circumstances. Under these precedents, Florida’s scheme—which permits the judge to find aggravating factors after receiving a jury recommendation—is plainly constitutional.

The Sixth Amendment, as incorporated against the States in *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145, 149 (1968), guarantees “the right to trial by jury in all criminal cases.” But *Duncan* did not require that every factual predicate for a sentence be determined by the jury. To the contrary, this Court has consistently recognized that the “jury trial” right does not extend to sentencing determinations. *See, e.g., Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581, 603 (1900) (holding that the Sixth Amendment does not mandate a particular jury size or composition in state courts). If the Constitution does not require a jury of twelve, it cannot require a jury to find aggravating factors that are not elements of the offense.

**II. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require a Unanimous Jury Verdict in Capital Sentencing**

Florida’s allowance of a non-unanimous jury recommendation does not violate the Sixth Amendment. In *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. at 603, this Court held that the Sixth Amendment’s jury requirements are not “fundamental” in state proceedings. *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516, 538 (1884), similarly held that the Fourteenth Amendment does not incorporate every procedural detail of the federal Bill of Rights. Because unanimity is not a “fundamental” component of the jury trial right, States may adopt non-unanimous procedures. *See Duncan*, 391 U.S. at 158 n.30 (noting that the Court had not decided whether unanimity is required in state courts). Indeed, this Court has long recognized that the common-law requirement of unanimity is not an immutable constitutional command. *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930) (discussing the historical flexibility of jury procedures). Florida’s use of a majority vote for an advisory recommendation is therefore consistent with the Sixth Amendment.

**III. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Determination of Mental Retardation**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment does not mandate that a jury decide whether a defendant is mentally retarded. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. at 251, the Court emphasized that “the practice of individualizing sentences” by allowing the judge to consider all relevant information is “not constitutionally objectionable.” That principle applies with equal force to mental retardation determinations. *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 608 (1967), held that due process requires a hearing before a defendant may be subjected to increased punishment based on new factual findings, but it did not require a jury. Florida provides a full evidentiary hearing before the judge, who makes the ultimate finding. This procedure satisfies the “heightened reliability” demanded by the Eighth Amendment. *See Townsend v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 736, 741 (1948) (requiring accurate information at sentencing). The State’s approach is consistent with *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934), which held that the Due Process Clause does not impose a rigid set of procedures but rather requires fundamental fairness.

**CONCLUSION**

Under the settled law of this Court as it existed before 1970, Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
