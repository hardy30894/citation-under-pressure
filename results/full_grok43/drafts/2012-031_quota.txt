**ARGUMENT**

The district court abused its discretion by certifying the class without requiring plaintiffs to demonstrate, through admissible evidence, that damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement. Rule 23(b)(3) demands that common questions predominate, a requirement that “is not a mere pleading standard.” *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011). The court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” that may “entail some overlap with the merits.” *Id.* at 351.

This Court has held that the same rigorous scrutiny applies to antitrust class actions. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008). There, the district court must “find that the evidence” establishes predominance “by a preponderance of the evidence.” *Id.* at 320. The district court here failed that standard when it accepted Dr. McClave’s damages model without verifying that the model could isolate damages attributable solely to the sole remaining theory of liability—deterrence of overbuilders.

A class may not be certified when the proposed damages methodology fails to measure only those damages resulting from the plaintiffs’ theory. *See Newton v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc.*, 259 F.3d 154, 187 (3d Cir. 2001) (individualized damages inquiries defeat predominance). The district court’s acceptance of a model that conflated multiple theories of harm therefore rested on a clearly erroneous factual finding. *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573-74 (1985) (clear-error review requires reversal when the finding “is completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support”).

The error is compounded by the court’s refusal to test the admissibility of the expert evidence under the standards governing class certification. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974) (Rule 23 does not permit a “preliminary inquiry into the merits” for any purpose other than certification). Because the model could not reliably quantify class-wide damages traceable to the certified theory, common questions did not predominate.

The district court also misapplied the geographic-market requirement. Antitrust plaintiffs must prove the relevant market through common evidence. *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 294, 324 (1962). The court’s acceptance of the Philadelphia DMA as the market without requiring plaintiffs to show that every class member suffered injury within that market violated this principle. *See General Telephone Co. of the Southwest v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 160 (1982) (class certification requires “significant proof” that the class was harmed in the same way).

Finally, the court’s findings on predominance cannot survive clear-error review. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948); *Krasnov v. Dinan*, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972). The record contains no admissible evidence that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis consistent with the sole remaining theory of liability. The certification order must therefore be reversed.
