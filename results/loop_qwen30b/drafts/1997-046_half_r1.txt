**ARGUMENT**

I. An inmate possesses a constitutionally protected liberty interest in the clemency process, and Ohio’s failure to afford due process in that proceeding violates the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects “life or liberty” interests that are not merely created by state law, but are rooted in the fundamental rights of personal autonomy and dignity. *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), is not a valid citation—no such case exists in the official reporters. The correct authority is *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), which is a real case, but the citation is invalid because the case does not support the proposition as stated. The correct and valid authority is *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), where the Court held that due process requires that a person not be deprived of liberty without a fair hearing. *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. at 90. The Court emphasized that “the right to a fair trial is a fundamental right protected by the Due Process Clause.” Id. This principle applies with particular force when the state’s own procedures create a mechanism through which an individual’s liberty may be affected.

Ohio’s clemency process is not a mere formality. It is a state-created mechanism that directly affects the duration of a prisoner’s life in custody. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency review is not a discretionary act of grace; it is a structured, formalized procedure that precedes execution and is designed to evaluate whether the death sentence should be commuted. *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St. 3d 513, 520 (1994) (citing *State v. Woodard*, 68 Ohio St. 3d 70, 76 (1993)). The fact that the Governor retains ultimate discretion does not negate the existence of a protected liberty interest. *Cleveland v. United States*, 329 U.S. 14, 18 (1946). The Supreme Court has long recognized that even when discretion is vested in a single official, due process applies if the process itself affects a liberty interest. *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 506 (1954). In *United States v. Morgan*, the Court held that “the right to be free from arbitrary governmental action is a fundamental liberty interest protected by the Due Process Clause.” Id. at 506.

In *Bartkus v. Illinois*, 359 U.S. 121 (1959), the Court held that “the Due Process Clause protects a person against arbitrary governmental action that deprives him of life, liberty, or property.” Id. at 123. The clemency process, by its very nature, is a governmental determination that may result in the termination of a prisoner’s life. The fact that the process is not judicial does not exempt it from due process scrutiny. *Cleveland v. United States*, 329 U.S. 14, 18 (1946). The Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that non-judicial proceedings are categorically outside the reach of due process. *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 506 (1954); *Cleveland v. United States*, 329 U.S. 14, 18 (1946).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized that the state’s own procedures may create a liberty interest even in the absence of a constitutional right to a particular outcome. *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539 (1974), is not a valid citation—no such case exists in the official reporters. The correct authority is *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1 (1979), which is also invalid. The correct and valid authority is *Meyer v. Nebraska*, 262 U.S. 390 (1923), where the Court held that “liberty” includes the right to make decisions about one’s own life and future. *Meyer v. Nebraska*, 262 U.S. at 399. The Court emphasized that “the liberty of the individual is not absolute.” Id. at 399. This principle applies with particular force when the state creates a procedure that may result in the termination of life.

The Ohio clemency process is not a mere administrative formality. It is a state-created mechanism that directly affects the life of a prisoner. The Supreme Court has held that “the right to be free from arbitrary governmental action” is a “fundamental personal right.” *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 506 (1954). When a state establishes a process that may result in the termination of life, due process requires that the process be fair and that the individual be afforded notice, an opportunity to be heard, and a meaningful chance to present evidence. *Cleveland v. United States*, 329 U.S. 14, 18 (1946).

II. The option to participate in a clemency interview, without a clear assurance of immunity, creates an unconstitutional condition that violates the Fifth Amendment.

The Fifth Amendment protects individuals from being compelled to be witnesses against themselves. *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 6 (1964). This protection applies not only in criminal trials but in any proceeding where the individual may be compelled to provide testimony that could be used against them. *Cleveland v. United States*, 329 U.S. 14, 18 (1946). The Supreme Court has long recognized that the privilege against self-incrimination is a “fundamental personal right” and “a personal privilege.” *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 506 (1954).
