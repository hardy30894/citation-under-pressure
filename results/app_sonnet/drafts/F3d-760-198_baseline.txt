# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING MOLL'S HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT CLAIM BY REFUSING TO CONSIDER FACIALLY SEX-NEUTRAL CONDUCT AS PART OF THE TOTALITY OF CIRCUMSTANCES

To establish a hostile work environment, a plaintiff must show that her workplace was "permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult" sufficiently "severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim's employment." *Meritor Sav. Bank v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 65, 67 (1986) (quoting *Rogers v. EEOC*, 454 F.2d 234, 238 (5th Cir. 1971)). That inquiry is not a checklist of discrete, facially offensive acts; it requires "looking at all the circumstances," including "the frequency of the discriminatory conduct; its severity; whether it is physically threatening or humiliating, or a mere offensive utterance." *Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 23 (1993).

This Court has expressly held that facially sex-neutral incidents must be considered alongside overtly sexual conduct when evaluating a hostile work environment claim, so long as a reasonable jury could link the neutral incidents to the plaintiff's sex. *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002). In *Alfano*, this Court reversed a grant of summary judgment where the district court had "erroneously excluded" incidents that were not overtly sexual, holding that such incidents may "contribute to the overall hostility of the work environment" once there is some basis for inferring that the sex-neutral incidents "were, in fact, based on sex." *Id.* This principle recognizes that discriminatory hostility rarely announces itself exclusively through sexual remarks; exclusion from professional opportunities, disparate scheduling accommodations, and unequal social inclusion can be as probative of a hostile, sex-based environment as an offensive comment.

The district court here committed the precise error condemned in *Alfano*. Rather than considering the totality of Moll's allegations—the intrusive and unwelcome notes and phone calls from Irving, his subsequent refusal to communicate with her except in person, his denial of her promotion while advancing two male colleagues, his revocation of her ability to work from home while her male counterparts retained that privilege, the denial of her vacation request while junior male colleagues received theirs, and the exclusion of Moll and other women from workplace social events—the district court isolated only the conduct it deemed "sexually offensive" and evaluated that narrow subset against the statute of limitations. Because none of the isolated "sexually offensive" acts fell within the limitations period, the court dismissed the claim in its entirety.

That approach cannot be reconciled with *Alfano* or with the Supreme Court's instruction that hostile work environment claims must be assessed by "the totality of the circumstances." *Harris*, 510 U.S. at 23. It also conflicts with *National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Morgan*, 536 U.S. 101 (2002), which recognized that "[a] hostile work environment claim is composed of a series of separate acts that collectively constitute one 'unlawful employment practice,'" *id.* at 117, such that a court may consider the "entire time period of the hostile environment" so long as an act contributing to that environment falls within the limitations period, *id.* at 118. The district court never asked whether any act—sexual or otherwise—fell within the limitations period as part of a continuing pattern; it truncated the analysis by excising the sex-neutral conduct altogether. Had the court properly considered Irving's differential treatment of Moll regarding promotions, telework, and vacation requests, and the broader pattern of exclusion, in conjunction with the earlier sexually charged notes and calls, a reasonable jury could find an actionable hostile work environment extending into the limitations period. This Court should therefore vacate the judgment and remand for the district court to evaluate Moll's hostile work environment claim under the correct, totality-based standard.

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN APPLYING THE "SHAM ISSUE OF FACT" DOCTRINE TO DISREGARD A NON-PARTY WITNESS'S SUBSEQUENT TESTIMONY

Where a party attempts to defeat summary judgment by submitting an affidavit that flatly contradicts her own prior sworn testimony without explanation, courts may disregard the later, self-serving statement as a "sham." *Perma Research & Dev. Co. v. Singer Co.*, 410 F.2d 572, 578 (2d Cir. 1969); *see also Trans-Orient Marine Corp. v. Star Trading & Marine, Inc.*, 925 F.2d 566, 572 (2d Cir. 1991) (disregarding an affidavit that was "flatly contradictory" to prior deposition testimony absent a satisfactory explanation for the change). That doctrine rests on concern that a litigant who is a party to the action may be tempted to manufacture a factual dispute to survive summary judgment.

That concern is materially diminished, and the rationale for disregarding contradictory testimony correspondingly weaker, when the witness is a non-party with no personal stake in the outcome of the litigation. A non-party witness does not have the same incentive to fabricate testimony that a party does, and credibility determinations concerning such conflicts are properly reserved for the jury rather than resolved by the court on summary judgment. Because genuine disputes of credibility among witnesses—particularly disinterested ones—are for the trier of fact, the district court erred by treating the non-party witness's later testimony here as a sham and disregarding it wholesale rather than permitting the resulting factual conflict to go to a jury. The judgment granting summary judgment on this basis should likewise be vacated.
