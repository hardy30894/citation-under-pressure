# ARGUMENT

## I. Due Process Permits Specific Jurisdiction Where Claims "Arise Out of or Relate to" the Defendant's Forum Contacts—A Standard That Does Not Require Strict Causation.

Since *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), this Court has held that a State may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant whose activities within the forum give rise to "obligations" connected to the litigation, so long as maintaining the suit does not offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *Id.* at 316. The Court has consistently phrased the specific-jurisdiction inquiry in the disjunctive: jurisdiction is proper when the litigation "arises out of *or* relates to" the defendant's forum contacts. *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984); *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1780 (2017). That formulation is not surplusage. It reflects that due process protects against being "haled into a jurisdiction" with which a defendant has no meaningful connection, *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958), not against suit in a forum where the defendant has purposefully and extensively cultivated the very market in which the injury occurred.

Ford asks this Court to convert "relate to" into "caused by," collapsing the disjunctive test into a single, but-for causation requirement. Nothing in this Court's precedent supports that collapse. In *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770 (1984), the Court sustained jurisdiction in New Hampshire over a libel claim even though the plaintiff's connection to the forum was minimal, because the defendant's regular circulation of magazines in the State gave it "fair warning" that it could be sued there on claims connected to that circulation. *Id.* at 781. The causal nexus between New Hampshire sales and the plaintiff's particular reputational injury was attenuated at best; what mattered was the relationship between the ongoing forum activity and the claim.

## II. Ford's Continuous and Purposeful Contacts With Montana Relate Directly to Gullett's Claims.

Ford does not merely sell cars nationally and hope Montanans buy them elsewhere. Ford deliberately markets, advertises, and sells Ford Explorers—the very model at issue—in Montana; it maintains dealerships and authorized service centers there; and it supplies replacement parts, including tires and components for Explorers, into the Montana market. The Montana Supreme Court correctly found a "connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue," Pet. App., because Ford's Montana-directed conduct is not incidental—it is the deliberate result of Ford's decision to exploit the Montana market for this product line.

This is precisely the "purposeful availment" this Court's precedents require. In *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286 (1980), the Court explained that a manufacturer who "delivers its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State" may reasonably anticipate being sued there. *Id.* at 297–98. Ford's expectation is not hypothetical; Ford *actually* markets and sells Explorers in Montana, unlike the New York retailer in *World-Wide Volkswagen* who sold a single car that fortuitously ended up in Oklahoma. *Id.* at 295. Ford's contacts with Montana are not "random, fortuitous, or attenuated," *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985); they are the product of Ford's own market strategy.

Because Gullett's claims allege that the very type of vehicle Ford chose to sell and service in Montana—the Explorer—was defectively designed and inadequately warned against, those claims "relate to" Ford's Montana activity even though the particular car changed hands before arriving in the State. The design-defect and failure-to-warn theories concern the Explorer model generally, the model Ford placed into the Montana market through its own purposeful conduct. Ford's contacts and the plaintiff's injury thus share the necessary relationship contemplated by *Helicopteros* and *Bristol-Myers Squibb*.

## III. Exercising Jurisdiction Here Comports With Fair Play and Substantial Justice.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that "the constitutional touchstone" remains "whether the defendant purposefully established 'minimum contacts' in the forum State," evaluated alongside considerations of fairness. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 474. Ford, a sophisticated multinational manufacturer that profits enormously from the Montana market, can hardly claim surprise or unfairness in defending a products-liability suit there concerning the very product line it sells and services in that State. *Cf. Asahi Metal Indus. Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 114 (1987) (fairness factors include the forum's interest and the plaintiff's interest in convenient relief). Montana has a substantial interest in adjudicating claims brought by its own residents injured on its own highways involving products actively marketed within its borders. *Keeton*, 465 U.S. at 776.

Ford's reliance on *Bristol-Myers Squibb* is misplaced. There, nonresident plaintiffs sued over conduct and injuries occurring entirely outside the forum, severing any relationship between the claims and the defendant's California contacts. 137 S. Ct. at 1781–82. Here, by contrast, a Montana resident suffered her injury in Montana, and Ford's Montana contacts are directly bound up with the product line at issue. Nor does *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117 (2014), or *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915 (2011), assist Ford; those cases addressed general jurisdiction, a distinct inquiry from the specific-jurisdiction analysis at issue here.

## CONCLUSION

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford's purposeful, ongoing contacts with Montana relate to Gullett's claims, satisfying due process. The judgment should be affirmed.
