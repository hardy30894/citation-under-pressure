# ARGUMENT

## I. The Bankruptcy Code Exempts Only Funds Objectively Characterized as Retirement Savings, and Inherited IRAs Lack Those Characteristics

Sections 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code exempt "retirement funds" held in an account "exempt from taxation" under specified Internal Revenue Code provisions. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). This Court has instructed that whether an account qualifies for exemption turns on the "objective characteristics" of the funds themselves—not merely on labels or on the funds' origin. Rousey v. Jacoway, 544 U.S. 320, 331 (2005) (exemption for IRAs turns on whether the account bears the "characteristics" of funds "set aside for the day when they stop working"). In Rousey, this Court held ordinary IRAs exempt precisely because the tax code's penalties for early withdrawal ensure that the funds are actually preserved until retirement. Id. at 325–26. The inquiry is therefore functional: does the account, by its legal terms, operate to reserve wealth for the owner's post-employment years?

Inherited IRAs fail that test. As the Seventh Circuit correctly explained, an inherited IRA "must begin distributing its assets within a year of the original owner's death," and "[p]ayout must be completed in as little as five years." Pet. App. 3. There is no penalty for early withdrawal, no floor protecting the funds from immediate use, and no ability to add new contributions or roll the account into another retirement vehicle. Id. In short, "an inherited IRA is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner's retirement." Id. Under Rousey's objective-characteristics approach, that is decisive: a fund that the owner is required to consume before retirement, and that carries none of the disincentives to early withdrawal that define retirement savings, is not "retirement funds" within the meaning of § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12).

## II. The Statute's Reference to "Retirement Funds," Without a Temporal Qualifier, Does Not Extend the Exemption to Assets That Are No One's Retirement Savings at the Time of Bankruptcy

Petitioners, echoing In re Chilton, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and In re Nessa, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), argue that because § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) do not use the phrase "the debtor's retirement funds," any account that was ever anyone's retirement account remains exempt forever, regardless of its current character. That argument proves too much. Other exemptions in § 522 are written to depend on the property's function at the time of filing. Section 522(b)(3)(A), for example, exempts a homestead only if it has been the debtor's domicile for a specified period before filing; a debtor who inherits a parent's house and rents it out cannot claim the homestead exemption merely because the house was once the parent's home. Pet. App. 6–7. The retirement-funds exemption works the same way: it "does not (necessarily) depend on whether an asset is a retirement fund . . . as the debtor uses it," but it does depend on whether the asset functions as retirement savings at all by the time of bankruptcy. Pet. App. 4. Once Ruth Heffron died, the $300,000 "became no one's retirement funds." Pet. App. 4. Had she withdrawn the money herself before her death and given it to her daughter, no one would suggest the gift remained "retirement funds" in the daughter's hands. The result cannot differ simply because the transfer occurred through inheritance rather than an inter vivos gift. Id.

## III. Petitioners' Reading Would Convert a Retirement Exemption into a Vehicle for Sheltering Assets from Creditors for Ordinary Consumption

Extending the exemption to inherited IRAs would not serve the purpose Congress identified—protecting funds "set aside for retirement," Rousey, 544 U.S. at 325—but would instead shield from creditors a "pot of money that can be freely used for current consumption." Pet. App. 4. Petitioners may respond that broader exemption would encourage account owners to save more, anticipating that their heirs could inherit free of creditor claims. But as this Court has cautioned, "no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs," and it "frustrates rather than effectuates legislative intent simplistically to assume that whatever furthers the statute's primary objective must be the law." Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) (emphasis omitted). Congress balanced the goal of protecting retirement savings against the interests of creditors by limiting the exemption to funds that retain the objective features of retirement accounts—continued tax-deferred growth without mandatory pre-retirement drawdown. Inherited IRAs, by statutory design, lack those features. See 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C) (barring rollover or additional contributions); id. § 401(a)(9)(B) (mandating distribution beginning within one year of the original owner's death). To read the exemption otherwise would allow "capital held in IRAs [to] pass down through the generations without ever being subject to income tax," Pet. App. 3, and without ever serving the purpose that justified the exemption in the first place.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
