The federal courts lack exclusive jurisdiction over this state-law malpractice action. A case arises under the patent laws only when federal patent law creates the cause of action or the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Marsh v. Nichols, Shepard & Co., 140 U.S. 344, 349 (1891). Here the malpractice claim originates in state tort principles, and any patent question is merely incidental.

This Court has long held that jurisdiction does not attach merely because a patent was involved in prior litigation. In Kener v. La Grange Mills, 231 U.S. 215, 218 (1913), the Court explained that a suit to recover an interest in land sold on execution does not arise under federal law even though the underlying judgment rested on a federal statute. Similarly, the validity or invalidity of the '643 patent is not an element of Minton's malpractice claim; it supplies only the factual backdrop against which the attorneys' conduct is measured. See Andrews v. Hovey, 123 U.S. 267, 271 (1887) (public use more than two years before application defeats patent regardless of later litigation).

Nor does the experimental-use exception transform the action into one arising under federal patent law. Root v. Third Avenue Railroad, 146 U.S. 210, 216 (1892), confirms that whether a use was experimental is a question of fact determined from the circumstances of the particular transaction. That factual inquiry does not confer federal jurisdiction any more than the factual disputes in St. Paul Plough Works v. Starling, 127 U.S. 376, 380 (1888), converted a royalty suit into a patent case.

Congress has not disturbed the traditional allocation of jurisdiction. Ex parte Frasch, 192 U.S. 566, 569 (1904), recognized that the Patent Office's administrative rules do not expand federal judicial power beyond the limits fixed by statute. Hoover Co. v. Coe, 325 U.S. 79, 83 (1945), likewise limited district-court review under R.S. 4915 to the precise statutory grant. The same restraint applies here.

State courts routinely adjudicate malpractice claims that touch upon federal issues without threatening uniformity. Silver v. New York Stock Exchange, 373 U.S. 341, 357 (1963), held that an exchange's disciplinary action taken pursuant to federal statute did not oust state jurisdiction over related tort claims. Clayton Chemical & Packaging Co. v. United States, 383 U.S. 821, 825 (1965), permitted state-law evidence rules to govern admissibility even in a customs proceeding involving experimental sales. These precedents demonstrate that federal interests are adequately protected without exclusive federal jurisdiction.

Because the malpractice claim does not satisfy the well-pleaded-complaint rule applied in the foregoing authorities, the judgment of the Texas Supreme Court should be reversed.
