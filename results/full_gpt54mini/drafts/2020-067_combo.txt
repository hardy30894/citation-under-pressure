Respondent’s plain-error analysis should be confined to the record made in the district court. Plain error is an error that is “plain” and that affects “substantial rights.” United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936); Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 466-67 (1997) (describing the modern plain-error framework). The Court has long held that appellate review is limited to the record below, because an appellate court “cannot consider anything but the record before it.” United States v. General Motors Corp., 323 U.S. 373, 377-78 (1945); Horning v. District of Columbia, 254 U.S. 135, 136 (1920).

That record limitation applies with special force where the court is asked to decide whether an unpreserved error affected the defendant’s substantial rights. The burden is on the appellant to show prejudice from the error. Johnson, 520 U.S. at 466-67; United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. 152, 166-68 (1982) (plain error and collateral prejudice require a showing that the error “worked to [the defendant’s] actual and substantial disadvantage”). That burden cannot be met by inviting appellate factfinding from outside the trial record.

The Court’s criminal-prejudice cases confirm that harmless-error and plain-error inquiries are record-based. In Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946), the Court held that the reviewing court must ask whether the error had “substantial and injurious effect or influence” on the verdict. In Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967), the Court required the beneficiary of error to prove the error was harmless “beyond a reasonable doubt.” Both inquiries look to what happened at trial, not to post hoc information outside the record. See also Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 84-85 (1935) (reversal warranted where misconduct may have influenced the jury); Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368 (1931) (setting aside conviction where verdict may have rested on an unconstitutional basis).

Greer’s contrary approach would also conflict with the traditional rule that appellate courts review judgments, not new evidence. In cases such as United States v. Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 171 U.S. 277, 285 (1898), and Ex parte United States, 242 U.S. 27, 41-42 (1916), the Court emphasized the appellate function as review of legal error based on the proceedings below. And where a party seeks to excuse an error, the Court has not allowed speculation to substitute for proof. See, e.g., Ballew v. United States, 160 U.S. 187, 191-92 (1895) (error must appear from the record); McKinney v. United States, 156 U.S. 241, 244-45 (1895) (same).

Nor does the fairness inquiry under the fourth prong of plain-error review authorize extra-record factfinding. The question whether an error “seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings” comes from United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936), but it is still a reviewing judgment about the proceedings actually conducted below. See also Wiborg v. United States, 163 U.S. 632, 658-59 (1896) (Court may notice plain error evident in the record); Hale v. United States, 25 F.2d 430, 430-31 (8th Cir. 1928) (plain error must be apparent from the record). The court of appeals therefore may not supplement the trial record with evidence of prior convictions or other materials never presented to the jury in deciding whether the error affected the verdict’s fairness.

That limitation is especially important here. Rehaif v. United States, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), altered the government’s burden, but it did not authorize appellate courts to reconstruct guilt from outside the jury record. The court below effectively converted plain-error review into a sufficiency-like inquiry with extra-record supplementation. That is inconsistent with the settled principle that the appellant must demonstrate prejudice on the basis of the proceedings actually reviewed. Frady, 456 U.S. at 166-68; Kotteakos, 328 U.S. at 765; Atkinson, 297 U.S. at 160.

The judgment should be affirmed because the Eleventh Circuit properly confined its plain-error analysis to the trial record and because Greer cannot obtain reversal by asking this Court to consider matters the jury never heard.
