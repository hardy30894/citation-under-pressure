ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to the FDIC on the Grounds that the Release Bars Bancorp's Claims

The district court incorrectly concluded that the release executed by Bancorp on June 6, 1988, bars its claims against the FDIC. The language of the release does not cover the claims asserted by Bancorp in the instant action. The release states that Bancorp releases the FDIC "from any and all claims which Bancorp ... has or may have arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985." The claims asserted by Bancorp, however, relate to the FDIC's actions as receiver, including its payment of post-insolvency interest to itself, its sale of Bank assets, and its charging of legal and office expenses to the receivership estate. These claims do not "arise from or with respect to" the OCC's decision to close the Bank.

Courts have consistently held that releases are to be construed narrowly, and that they only bar claims that are explicitly covered by their terms. See, e.g., S.E.C. v. Levine, 881 F.2d 1165, 1181 (2d Cir. 1989) ("A release is a contract, and its construction is governed by contract law."); see also In re Ivan F. Boesky Sec. Litig., 948 F.2d 1358, 1366 (2d Cir. 1991) ("A release that is clear and unambiguous on its face will be enforced according to its terms."). Here, the release does not explicitly cover the claims asserted by Bancorp, and therefore, it does not bar those claims.

II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to the FDIC on the Grounds that the Statute of Limitations Bars Bancorp's Claims

The district court also erred in concluding that New York's six-year statute of limitations for claims of unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste bars Bancorp's claims. The statute of limitations for these claims runs from the termination of the receivership, not from the date of the OCC's decision to close the Bank.

Under New York law, a claim for unjust enrichment or breach of fiduciary duty accrues when the plaintiff possesses a complete and present cause of action. See, e.g., Kaufman v. Cohen, 307 A.D.2d 113, 124, 760 N.Y.S.2d 157, 167 (1st Dep't 2003). In the context of a receivership, a claim against the receiver does not accrue until the receivership is terminated. See, e.g., FDIC v. Hirsch, 635 F. Supp. 1343, 1346 (S.D.N.Y. 1986) ("A claim against a receiver does not accrue until the receivership is terminated, because until that time, the receiver's actions are not final and the claimant's rights are not fixed."). Here, the receivership was terminated on November 1, 1995, and Bancorp commenced its action on October 31, 1995. Therefore, its claims are timely.

The FDIC's reliance on N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7) is misplaced, as this provision does not govern the accrual of claims against a receiver. Rather, it simply provides a six-year statute of limitations for certain claims, including unjust enrichment and breach of fiduciary duty. See N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7). The district court's conclusion that the statute of limitations bars Bancorp's claims is therefore erroneous.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
