The First Circuit properly upheld the Massachusetts 35-foot buffer zone as a valid time-place-manner regulation. Under the standard established in *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941), a regulation is content-neutral if it is "justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech." The statute regulates the physical presence of persons, not the message they convey. It applies equally to all individuals, regardless of their viewpoint, and is therefore content-neutral.

Furthermore, the state has a significant interest in ensuring safety and access to healthcare facilities. As recognized in *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 559 (1965), the state has a compelling interest in preventing violence and ensuring the orderly conduct of public affairs. The buffer zone is a reasonable measure to achieve these goals without infringing on the right to free speech.

Finally, the regulation is narrowly tailored to serve that interest. In *Shuttlesworth v. City of Birmingham*, 394 U.S. 147 (1969), the Supreme Court held that a restriction must be "narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest." The 35-foot buffer zone is the least restrictive means to prevent obstruction and violence without banning speech entirely. As the Court stated in *Cox v. New Hampshire*, "the right of free speech does not include the right to use the highways of the state for the purpose of engaging in a campaign of noise disturbance." The petitioners retain ample alternative channels for communication, such as the public sidewalks outside the buffer zone, ensuring their speech remains protected.
