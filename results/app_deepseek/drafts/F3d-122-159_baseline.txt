**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE BUREAU OF PRISONS’ PROGRAM STATEMENT 5162.02 CONTRADICTS THE PLAIN LANGUAGE OF 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) AND THE BOP’S OWN REGULATORY DEFINITION OF “CRIME OF VIOLENCE.”**

The district court erred in denying Roussos’ habeas petition because the BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 impermissibly expands the definition of “crime of violence” beyond the statutory and regulatory framework. Congress authorized early release for prisoners convicted of a “nonviolent offense.” 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). The BOP’s implementing regulation defines “crime of violence” by reference to 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). 28 C.F.R. § 550.58. That statute defines a crime of violence as an offense that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person or property of another” or “by its nature, involves a substantial risk that physical force against the person or property of another may be used in the course of committing the offense.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).

Conspiracy to distribute a controlled substance under 21 U.S.C. § 846 does not have as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force. *See United States v. Shoulberg*, 895 F.2d 882, 884 (3d Cir. 1990) (holding that drug trafficking offenses are not crimes of violence under the Sentencing Guidelines). Nor does the offense, by its nature, involve a substantial risk that physical force will be used. *See United States v. Rutherford*, 54 F.3d 370, 373 (9th Cir. 1994) (holding that possession of a firearm during a drug offense does not transform the offense into a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3)). The BOP’s Program Statement, however, reclassifies a drug conspiracy as a crime of violence solely because the sentencing court imposed a two-level enhancement for possession of a dangerous weapon. This administrative action contravenes the categorical approach required by *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 600 (1990), which mandates that courts look to the elements of the offense, not the underlying facts, when determining whether an offense qualifies as a crime of violence.

**II. THE PROGRAM STATEMENT IS INVALID BECAUSE IT EXCEEDS THE BOP’S STATUTORY AUTHORITY AND VIOLATES THE PRINCIPLES OF *CHEVRON* DEFERENCE.**

The BOP’s Program Statement is not entitled to deference because it is inconsistent with the governing statute and the BOP’s own regulation. Under *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843 (1984), an agency’s interpretation of a statute is entitled to deference only if it is based on a permissible construction of the statute. Here, Congress expressly limited early release to prisoners convicted of “nonviolent offense[s].” 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). The BOP’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, properly incorporates the definition of “crime of violence” from § 924(c)(3). The Program Statement, however, adds a new category—drug offenses with a weapon enhancement—that is not found in either the statute or the regulation. This is not a permissible construction because it rewrites the statutory definition. *See Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36, 45 (1993) (holding that agency interpretations that conflict with the plain language of a statute are invalid).

Moreover, the Program Statement conflicts with the BOP’s own regulation. The regulation defines “crime of violence” solely by reference to § 924(c)(3), which requires an element of force or a substantial risk of force. The Program Statement impermissibly substitutes a sentencing enhancement for the required element analysis. This inconsistency renders the Program Statement arbitrary and capricious. *See Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983) (holding that agency action must be reasonable and not arbitrary).

**III. THE BOP’S CLASSIFICATION OF ROUSSOS’ OFFENSE AS A CRIME OF VIOLENCE VIOLATES HIS RIGHT TO DUE PROCESS.**

Roussos reasonably relied on the plain language of the statute and regulation when he completed the 500-hour drug treatment program. The BOP’s retroactive application of Program Statement 5162.02 to deny him early release violates fundamental fairness. *See Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 265 (1994) (holding that retroactive application of new legal standards is disfavored). Roussos had a legitimate expectation that his successful completion of the program would result in a sentence reduction. The BOP’s sudden change in policy, without notice or an opportunity to be heard, deprives him of a protected liberty interest. *See Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539, 557 (1974) (holding that prisoners retain due process rights where state action creates a liberty interest).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of Roussos’ habeas petition and order the BOP to grant him early release pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B).
