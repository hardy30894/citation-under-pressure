ARGUMENT

The Internal Revenue Code requires the government to assess a tax against each separate “taxpayer” within three years of the return’s filing. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). An assessment “shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer,” 26 U.S.C. § 6203, and must “provide identification of the taxpayer,” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The Code defines “taxpayer” to include both a “partnership” and an “individual,” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). Because each Debtor is a distinct taxpayer with a separate identification number, the assessments against the Partnership left the three-year period applicable to Debtors untouched. See United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (lien invalid where assessment named only partnership and “an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers”).

The timely assessment against the Partnership therefore extended the collection period only for the Partnership. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) (“such tax may be collected … only if the … proceeding [is] begun … within 10 years after the assessment of the tax”). No assessment having been made against Debtors, the government could not invoke the ten-year extension to file proofs of claim in their bankruptcy cases. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).

The government’s reliance on state-law joint-and-several liability is misplaced. California law provides that “[a] judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner” and that a partner’s personal assets may not be reached “unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The government obtained no such judgment, and the three-year period for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), does not alter this result. There the partner had already paid the tax; the court merely held that he could not recover a refund because he was substantively liable under state law. Id. at 910. The opinion expressly recognized that collection without an individual assessment or judgment would require “resort[] to court action.” Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). No such action was timely commenced here.

The government’s proofs of claim were therefore properly disallowed.
