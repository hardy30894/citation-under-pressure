**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE PAT-FRISK OF SOARES VIOLATED THE FOURTH AMENDMENT BECAUSE IT WAS NOT SUPPORTED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION.**

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures, and a pat-frisk constitutes a "seizure" within the meaning of the Amendment. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 16-17 (1968). A pat-frisk is permissible only if the officer has "reasonable suspicion" that the suspect is armed and dangerous. *Id.* at 27. Reasonable suspicion requires "specific, articulable facts" that, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, justify the intrusion. *United States v. Arvizu*, 534 U.S. 266, 273 (2002) (quoting *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21). The government bears the burden of proving reasonable suspicion by a preponderance of the evidence. *Ornelas v. United States*, 517 U.S. 690, 699 (1996).

Here, the government failed to meet its burden. The officers’ initial justification for the stop—failure to use headlights—was lawful, but the pat-frisk occurred *after* the stop’s purpose was fulfilled. *See United States v. McKoy*, 428 F.3d 25, 29 (1st Cir. 2005) (noting that a pat-frisk must be justified by separate reasonable suspicion). The government relies on Soares’s "erratic behavior" and "furtive movements" to justify the frisk, but these factors, without more, are insufficient. *See United States v. Young*, 105 F.3d 1, 7 (1st Cir. 1997) (holding that nervousness alone does not establish reasonable suspicion); *United States v. Lender*, 986 F.2d 1, 2 (1st Cir. 1993) (same).

Moreover, the officers’ subjective concerns about Soares’s agitation do not amount to objective reasonable suspicion. *See United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 7 (1989) (requiring an objective basis for suspicion). The government cites *United States v. Alvarez*, 895 F.2d 833 (1st Cir. 1990), for the proposition that erratic behavior can support reasonable suspicion, but *Alvarez* involved a suspect who fled upon seeing police, a far more probative factor than mere agitation. *Id.* at 835. Here, Soares’s verbal abuse and profanity, while unwise, do not create reasonable suspicion of armedness. *See United States v. Thame*, 846 F.2d 201, 205 (1st Cir. 1988) (holding that "belligerence" alone is insufficient).

The government also argues that the officers’ concern was heightened by the high-crime area and the passengers’ initial movements. However, the First Circuit has cautioned against overreliance on high-crime areas as a basis for reasonable suspicion. *See United States v. O’Brien*, 444 F.3d 32, 36 (1st Cir. 2006) (noting that "high-crime area" alone is insufficient without additional factors). The passengers’ initial movements occurred *before* the stop and were not observed by the officers until after the stop was effectuated. *See United States v. Suarez*, 553 F.3d 1, 4 (1st Cir. 2009) (requiring that reasonable suspicion arise *after* the stop to justify a frisk). Thus, these factors do not support the pat-frisk.

Finally, the government’s reliance on *United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. 221 (1985), is misplaced. *Hensley* involved a *Terry* stop based on a flyer describing a suspect in a robbery, not a pat-frisk. *Id.* at 223. The pat-frisk here was not supported by reasonable suspicion and therefore violated the Fourth Amendment.

II. **THE FRISK EXCEEDED THE SCOPE OF A VALID *TERRY* STOP.**

Even if the initial stop were justified, the pat-frisk exceeded the permissible scope of a *Terry* stop. A *Terry* frisk is limited to a pat-down of the outer clothing for weapons. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 29. Here, the officers removed Soares from the car and conducted a more intrusive search, including a scuffle during which Soares’s waistband was exposed. *See United States v. Taylor*, 716 F.2d 701, 705 (1st Cir. 1983) (holding that a frisk must be limited to a pat-down for weapons). The government’s reliance on *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983), is unavailing, as *Long* involved a search of the passenger compartment of a vehicle, not a pat-frisk of a person. *Id.* at 1049.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress. The pat-frisk violated the Fourth Amendment because it was not supported by reasonable suspicion and exceeded the scope of a valid *Terry* stop. The judgment below should be reversed, and the evidence obtained from the unlawful frisk should be suppressed.
