**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Requires Only That a Defendant Be Competent Under the Standard of *Dusky v. United States*, Not Under a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Test.**

This Court has long defined the constitutional floor of competency in a single, uniform way. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that the test is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. *Dusky* did not purport to create a floor applicable only to standing trial; it announced the constitutional measure of mental competency itself. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), reaffirmed that due process forbids the conviction of a person who lacks the mental capacity to understand and participate in the proceedings against him, and that a court's failure to conduct a hearing where the evidence raises a genuine doubt on that score offends the Fourteenth Amendment. *Id.* at 385.

Nothing in the competency inquiry changes when the defendant, rather than proceeding to trial, chooses to plead guilty or to represent himself. The Ninth Circuit's demand for a distinct "reasoned choice" standard has no textual or doctrinal source in this Court's due process jurisprudence. It rests instead on the mistaken premise that the *decision* to waive rights requires greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But the competency inquiry has always asked whether the defendant possesses the mental capacity to understand and participate—not whether the choice he makes is wise, or is the choice a rational and counseled defendant would make. As this Court explained in *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), competency doctrine safeguards a defendant against being subjected to proceedings he cannot comprehend or participate in; it does not superintend the soundness of the particular decisions a competent defendant elects to make. *Id.* at 108.

**II. The Standards Governing Waiver of Counsel and Guilty Pleas Concern the Voluntariness and Knowledge of the Waiver, Not a Separate, Elevated Mental-Capacity Threshold.**

The decisions of this Court addressing waiver of counsel and guilty pleas confirm that the constitutional inquiry is whether the waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent—not whether the defendant met some heightened capacity beyond *Dusky*. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court instructed that courts must indulge every reasonable presumption against the loss of the constitutional right to counsel and must ask whether the accused understood and intended to relinquish that right. *Id.* at 464. That test asks about the defendant's knowledge and intent respecting his rights, a question distinct from the *Dusky* capacity inquiry, and one that in no way implies a separate, more demanding competency standard. *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required that a waiver of counsel be assessed through "a penetrating and comprehensive examination of all the circumstances" under which the accused proceeds without counsel, *id.* at 724, emphasizing the defendant's awareness of the risks of self-representation—again a voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry, not a mental-capacity inquiry.

*Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), confirms that the focus is on whether the record shows an informed choice, explaining that the absence of counsel at arraignment does not vitiate proceedings where the record demonstrates that the accused understood the nature and consequences of his plea. *Id.* at 174–75. And *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), recognized that "the Constitution does not force a lawyer upon a defendant," *id.* at 279, because he may competently elect to conduct his own defense—an election this Court treated as a matter of intelligent waiver, not of superior mental competence. Similarly, *Kercheval v. United States*, 274 U.S. 220 (1927), explained that a plea of guilty "is itself a conviction," *id.* at 223, and operates as a waiver of the trial rights that a conviction after trial would likewise extinguish; the inquiry the Court applied concerned whether the plea was understandingly and voluntarily made, not whether the defendant possessed some elevated capacity beyond that required to stand trial.

**III. This Court's Waiver-of-Counsel Cases Do Not Establish a Substantive Competency Standard Higher Than *Dusky*.**

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard cannot be traced to this Court's waiver-of-counsel jurisprudence. In *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), the Court held that a waiver of counsel may not be presumed from a silent record, explaining that "the record must show, or there must be an allegation and evidence which show, that an accused was offered counsel but intelligently and understandingly rejected the offer. Anything less is not waiver." *Id.* at 516. *Carnley* thus confirms that the inquiry is whether the record affirmatively demonstrates an intelligent and understanding rejection of counsel—a voluntariness-and-knowledge question resolved by examining what the defendant was told and what he understood. Nothing in *Carnley*, or in any decision of this Court, suggests that waiver of counsel demands a quantum of rationality beyond *Dusky*'s "reasonable degree of rational understanding." 362 U.S. at 402. The competency standard and the knowing-and-voluntary-waiver standard serve distinct functions and must not be conflated into a single, heightened "reasoned choice" test untethered from this Court's precedents.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard that governs whether a defendant may stand trial, articulated in *Dusky* and *Pate*, likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional inquiries required by *Zerbst*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, *Kercheval*, and *Carnley* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
