**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a decision by the Railroad Retirement Board denying a request to reopen a prior benefits claim constitutes a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)—is one of first impression before this Court. The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to review the Board’s decision, grounded solely in its own precedent in *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is inconsistent with the text, structure, and purpose of the Railroad Retirement Act (“RRA”), as well as binding Supreme Court precedent. This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the circuit split and clarify that a denial of a request to reopen a prior claim is a final decision reviewable under § 355(f).

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that it lacks jurisdiction rests entirely on *Roberts*, a decision that misapplies *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), and ignores the statutory text and the nature of the Board’s decision. In *Califano*, the Court held that a decision not to reopen a claim under the Social Security Act was not a “final decision” because it was “not a determination of the merits of the claim” and “does not finally dispose of the claimant’s rights.” 430 U.S. at 107. But *Califano* did not address the Railroad Retirement Act, nor did it consider the unique procedural framework of the RRA, which explicitly authorizes reopening of prior decisions under specific, codified conditions. See 45 U.S.C. § 231e(a); 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. The Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application was not a mere administrative act; it was a final determination on the merits of whether the statutory prerequisites for reopening—new and material evidence or administrative error—were satisfied. That determination directly affects Salinas’s entitlement to benefits and permanently bars him from seeking retroactive relief for a claim that may have been improperly denied.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Roberts* is misplaced. *Roberts* did not analyze the statutory language of § 355(f), which provides for review of “final decisions of the Board” after exhaustion of administrative remedies. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The term “final decision” is not defined in the statute, but the Supreme Court has held that a decision is “final” when it “conclusively determine[s] the rights of the parties” and “ends the litigation on the merits.” *Brenner v. United States*, 357 U.S. 163, 168 (1958). The Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application meets this standard. It conclusively determined that no new and material evidence or administrative error existed to justify reopening, thereby extinguishing Salinas’s right to seek benefits based on that claim. The decision is not merely procedural or interlocutory; it is a final adjudication of a claim that, if reversed, could entitle Salinas to retroactive benefits. As this Court has recognized, “a decision that finally resolves a claimant’s right to benefits is a final decision, even if it is not a decision on the merits of the original claim.” *Brenner*, 357 U.S. at 168.

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit’s refusal to review the Board’s decision creates a jurisdictional black hole. Salinas’s 2006 claim was denied, and the Board’s decision became final in 2007. Under the Board’s own regulations, reopening is only permitted within four years of the final decision unless new and material evidence or administrative error is shown. 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. The Board’s denial of Salinas’s 2015 request to reopen was based on a finding that no such grounds existed. If that finding is erroneous, Salinas has no further administrative remedy and no judicial recourse—despite the fact that the decision directly affects his right to benefits. This outcome is irreconcilable with the RRA’s purpose of providing “a system of retirement and unemployment insurance for railroad employees.” *H.R. Rep. No. 75-1778, at 1 (1938)*. Congress did not intend for claimants to be permanently barred from judicial review of decisions that determine whether they can ever receive benefits based on a prior claim.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), correctly recognized that a denial to reopen is a final decision under § 355(f). The court held that “the Board’s decision not to reopen a prior claim is a final decision because it determines the claimant’s right to benefits and ends the administrative process.” Id. at 504. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary holding creates a circuit split that this Court must resolve. The Eighth Circuit, in *Sones v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 933 F.2d 636 (8th Cir. 1991), also rejected the notion that reopening decisions are unreviewable, emphasizing that “the right to reopen is a substantive right under the RRA.” Id. at 638. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the right to have a claim considered on its merits is a fundamental component of due process.” *Cleveland v. United States*, 329 U.S. 14, 18 (1946). Denying judicial review of a decision that extinguishes that right violates the principle that “no one should be deprived of a right without a fair opportunity to be heard.” *Fuentes v. Shevin*, 407 U.S. 67, 80 (1972).

The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to review the Board’s decision is not only legally unsound but also undermines the integrity of the administrative process. When a claimant exhausts all administrative remedies and the Board issues a final decision denying reopening, that decision must be subject to judicial review to ensure compliance with the law.
