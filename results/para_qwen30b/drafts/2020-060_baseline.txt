**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether Guam or the Navy is financially responsible for the environmental hazards arising from the Ordot Dump—turns on a single, dispositive legal issue: whether Guam’s 2004 Consent Decree with the EPA triggered its right to seek contribution under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby precluding it from pursuing a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The D.C. Circuit correctly concluded that it did. The district court erred in holding otherwise, and its decision conflicts with binding precedent and the plain text of CERCLA. We respectfully ask this Court to reverse.

### I. The 2004 Consent Decree “Resolved” Guam’s Liability for a Response Action, Triggering § 113(f)(3)(B)

CERCLA’s structure is clear: a party that has resolved its liability to the United States or a State in an administrative or judicially approved settlement may seek contribution under § 113(f)(3)(B), but may not bring a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). See 42 U.S.C. § 113(f)(3)(B). The key inquiry is whether the settlement “resolved” liability for a response action. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004). The Supreme Court has made clear that “a settlement resolves liability when it ‘ends the dispute’ and ‘bars further claims’ on the same subject matter.” *Id.* at 168.

The 2004 Consent Decree did precisely that. It required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a cover system to prevent further discharges of leachate into the Lonfit River and ultimately the Pacific Ocean. Consent Decree at 5–12, J.A. 94–101. These obligations were not merely technical; they were *response actions* within the meaning of CERCLA. Section 101(24) defines “remedial action” as “the cleanup or removal of hazardous substances from the environment.” The installation of a cover system to contain leachate is a quintessential remedial action. *See also* 40 C.F.R. § 300.5. The Decree’s terms were not aspirational—they were binding, enforceable, and fully executed. Guam closed the landfill in 2011 in compliance with the Decree. The EPA’s enforcement action under the Clean Water Act was not a mere regulatory demand; it was a judicially approved settlement that resolved the dispute over Guam’s obligation to address the environmental harm caused by the Ordot Dump.

The D.C. Circuit correctly held that “the Consent Decree’s substantive provisions” resolved Guam’s liability for a response action. *Guam v. United States*, 947 F.3d 1, 16 (D.C. Cir. 2020). The district court erred in focusing on the Decree’s disclaimer of liability—“without any finding or admission of liability”—as dispositive. But as this Court has long recognized, “a party may disclaim liability while still assuming obligations that are consistent with a finding of liability.” *Asarco Inc. v. United States*, 866 F.3d 1119, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017). The disclaimer is not a magic shield that immunizes a settlement from § 113(f)(3)(B)’s reach. *See also* *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (disclaimer of liability “does not preclude a finding that liability was resolved”).

### II. The Statute of Limitations for Contribution Claims Began to Run When the Decree Was Entered

Section 113(g)(3) provides that a contribution claim must be brought within three years of the “entry of a settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 113(g)(3). The Decree was entered in 2004. Guam’s 2017 suit was therefore barred by the statute of limitations. The D.C. Circuit correctly applied this rule. The district court’s contrary conclusion—that the Decree did not “resolve” liability—was legally untenable. If a settlement does not resolve liability, then § 113(f)(3)(B) does not apply, and the three-year clock does not start. But here, the Decree *did* resolve liability. The only remaining question was *who* would pay for the cleanup. That question was settled: Guam would pay, and it could seek contribution from others.

The district court’s reasoning would render § 113(f)(3)(B) meaningless. If every settlement with the EPA could be avoided by a mere disclaimer of liability, then the statute’s three-year limitation would be a nullity. Congress could not have intended such a result. *See* *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (CERCLA enacted “to ensure that responsible parties foot the bill”). The structure of CERCLA—providing a clear, time-limited path to contribution for settling parties—depends on the timely triggering of § 113(f)(3)(B). To allow Guam to bypass this mechanism would undermine the statutory scheme and create uncertainty for all PRPs.
