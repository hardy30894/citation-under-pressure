The petitioners' challenge to Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges is without merit. The Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) does not apply to appointed state court judges because they are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f).

The ADEA's exemption for "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" is not limited to the executive and legislative branches. The term "policymaking level" is not defined in the statute, but it is clear that judges exercise significant policymaking authority. As the Court noted in Springer v. Philippine Islands, 277 U.S. 189, 202 (1928), "legislative power is the authority to make laws" and "judicial power is the authority to apply them." Judges, therefore, are not simply adjudicators, but also play a crucial role in shaping the law.

The petitioners argue that judges do not make policy, but rather apply settled law to specific facts. However, this view is too narrow. As the Court observed in Hurtado v. California, 110 U.S. 516, 532 (1884), "the law is administered by the courts of justice... according to the course of the common law." Judges, therefore, have significant discretion in interpreting the law and applying it to new and complex situations.

Moreover, the Missouri Supreme Court has significant supervisory power over the state's judiciary, and its decisions have far-reaching implications for the development of state law. See, e.g., Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. 87, 114-15 (1925) (noting the importance of judicial discretion in applying the law). The Court's decision in Tumey v. Ohio, 273 U.S. 510, 523 (1927), also highlights the significance of judicial independence and discretion.

The petitioners' equal protection challenge is also without merit. The rational basis test, as articulated in Williamson v. Lee Optical Co., 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955), is the appropriate standard of review. Under this test, a state law is constitutional if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest. Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement is rationally related to the state's interest in ensuring that its judiciary is composed of judges who are capable of performing their duties effectively. See, e.g., McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420, 426 (1961) (noting that a state law is constitutional if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest).

The Court has consistently held that age is not a suspect classification, and that mandatory retirement requirements are constitutional as long as they are rationally related to a legitimate state interest. See, e.g., United States v. Carolene Products Co., 304 U.S. 144, 152-53 (1938) (noting that a state law is entitled to a presumption of constitutionality). The state's interest in maintaining a capable and effective judiciary is a legitimate one, and the mandatory retirement requirement is a rational means of achieving that interest.

In conclusion, the petitioners' challenge to Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement is without merit. The ADEA does not apply to appointed state court judges, and the equal protection challenge fails under the rational basis test. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
