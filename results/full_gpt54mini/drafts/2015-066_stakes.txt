Bryant’s conviction under 18 U.S.C. 117(a) cannot stand because the Government used uncounseled tribal-court misdemeanors to transform a later assault into a federal felony. That application of the statute violates the Sixth Amendment.

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is a safeguard of reliability in criminal adjudication. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970); Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 344-45 (1963). The Court has held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment may not be used to support imprisonment in a later proceeding. Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967); Alabama v. Shelton, 535 U.S. 654, 662-63 (2002). And although Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), permitted use of an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction at sentencing, Nichols emphasized that the conviction there was used only to calculate criminal-history points in a sentencing proceeding, not to prove an element of a new offense. Id. at 746-48.

That distinction is dispositive. Section 117(a) does not authorize a sentence enhancement after conviction; it makes prior domestic-violence convictions an element of a separate felony. The Government must prove that element beyond a reasonable doubt. Winship, 397 U.S. at 364. The Court has repeatedly warned that recidivist provisions may enhance punishment for the latest offense, but they do not erase the constitutional requirements governing proof of the offense itself. Almendarez-Torres v. United States, 523 U.S. 224, 230-35 (1998); Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000); United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 385-86 (2008). Nichols therefore cannot be stretched beyond sentencing to authorize use of an uncounseled conviction as an element of guilt.

The tribal-court setting makes the constitutional problem sharper, not weaker. It is true that the Sixth Amendment does not directly apply to Indian tribes. United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978); Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). But Congress has not provided that a conviction constitutionally valid in tribal court may be repurposed as an element of a federal felony irrespective of the absence of counsel. The Indian Civil Rights Act does not make tribal convictions equivalent to federal convictions for all purposes. Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 56-57, 71-72 (1978). And this Court has long recognized that federal use of tribal judgments must respect tribal sovereignty and comity, not override them with a rule that treats tribal convictions as if they satisfied the Constitution’s own counsel guarantee. Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 14-15 (1987); Wilson v. Marchington, 127 F.3d 805, 810-11 (9th Cir. 1997).

The Eighth and Tenth Circuits correctly held that uncounseled tribal convictions may not be used to sustain federal recidivist prosecutions where those convictions would be unconstitutional if obtained in state or federal court. United States v. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d 592, 598-605 (8th Cir. 2011); United States v. Shavanaux, 647 F.3d 993, 996-1000 (10th Cir. 2011). Those courts recognized that using such convictions as elements of a later federal felony would do exactly what Nichols did not permit: convert a prior uncounseled misdemeanor into the basis for criminal liability. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d at 603-05; Shavanaux, 647 F.3d at 998-1000.

The contrary rule also collides with Shelton and Burgett. If an uncounseled conviction cannot support a suspended sentence that may lead to imprisonment, Shelton, 535 U.S. at 662-63, it cannot constitutionally serve as the predicate that turns later conduct into a federal felony. And if the constitutional infirmity of a prior conviction may not be used “to support guilt or enhance punishment,” Burgett, 389 U.S. at 115, then it may not be used to establish an element that increases the offense itself.

The Court should reverse. Section 117(a) may punish repeat domestic violence, but not by relying on an uncounseled tribal conviction to satisfy an element of the federal crime. Under the Sixth Amendment, that use is impermissible.
