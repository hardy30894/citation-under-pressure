# ARGUMENT

## I. A Claim for Nominal Damages Does Not, by Itself, Present the "Definite and Concrete" Controversy That Article III Demands.

The judicial power of the federal courts extends only to genuine "cases" and "controversies," and this Court has consistently refused to adjudicate disputes that have lost their live, adversarial character. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346 (1911). The exercise of that power presupposes a real and substantial dispute between parties with adverse legal interests, capable of specific relief through a decree of a conclusive character, rather than an abstract question detached from any actual stake between the parties. *Id.* at 361-62. Once Georgia Gwinnett College rescinded the Freedom of Expression Policy and the challenged provision of the Student Code of Conduct, no live dispute remained over the validity of those provisions; petitioners no longer face, and cannot show any reasonable prospect of again facing, enforcement of rules that no longer exist. A bare demand for a nominal sum does not resurrect that extinguished controversy, because it identifies no injury, no continuing relationship between the parties, and no specific relief beyond a symbolic label.

This limitation on judicial power is not a modern innovation. As early as *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), this Court held that "[t]he duty of this court, as of every other judicial tribunal, is limited to determining rights of persons or of property, which are actually controverted in the particular case before it." *Id.* at 653. Where, as here, the policy that gave rise to the dispute has been repealed and superseded, and where petitioners identify no actual, continuing injury flowing from that repealed policy, there is no "right actually controverted" left for a court to determine.

## II. This Court Has Long Held That Claims Too Insubstantial to Reflect a Genuine Stake in the Outcome Will Not Preserve Jurisdiction Once the Underlying Dispute Is Resolved.

In *California v. San Pablo & Tulare Railroad Co.*, 149 U.S. 308 (1893), the State sought to recover a small sum of back taxes from the railroad; while the suit was pending, the legislature released the claim entirely. This Court held the case moot notwithstanding the technically live claim for the residual sum, because the dispute no longer presented any matter of genuine consequence between the parties. *Id.* at 314. The same principle controls here: once GGC rescinded the Prior Policies, the only thing petitioners retained was a request for a token sum untethered to any pleaded actual injury—precisely the kind of residual, insubstantial claim that *San Pablo* held insufficient to sustain a federal court's continuing jurisdiction.

This Court has likewise dismissed a case as moot where an intervening change in the challenged rule extinguished the predicate for the controversy while suit was pending. In *Hall v. Beals*, 396 U.S. 45 (1969) (per curiam), the Court held that a challenge to a durational residency requirement for voting was moot because the State had shortened the requirement before the case reached this Court, so that the plaintiffs no longer had any stake in the outcome. The same logic applies here: the repeal of the Prior Policies eliminated any occasion for equitable relief and left only a nominal damages request incapable of restoring a live controversy over the constitutionality of provisions no longer on the books.

## III. The Voluntary Revision of the Challenged Policy Mooted This Case, and Petitioners Have Identified No Reasonable Expectation That the Same Controversy Will Recur.

Where a defendant voluntarily ceases the challenged conduct, the case becomes moot unless the plaintiff can show a reasonable expectation that the wrong will be repeated. *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953). This Court explained that the party asserting a live controversy bears the burden of demonstrating that the underlying dispute is not, in fact, over. *Id.* The Eleventh Circuit correctly held—and petitioners do not dispute—that GGC's revision of the Freedom of Expression Policy and Student Code of Conduct mooted the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief. Petitioners have identified no basis for concluding that GGC will revive the rescinded policies, and their claim therefore falls squarely within the rule of *W. T. Grant*, not within the narrow exception for controversies "capable of repetition, yet evading review." *Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. Interstate Commerce Commission*, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911). That exception applies only where the challenged action is, by its nature, too short in duration to be fully litigated before it ceases; the Prior Policies suffered no such inherent limitation, and GGC's abandonment of them was permanent and unequivocal. The voluntary, complete abandonment of the challenged provisions thus deprives the residual nominal-damages request of any controversy "actually controverted" for a court to resolve. *Mills*, 159 U.S. at 653.

*St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41 (1943), illustrates why a technical remnant of a claim cannot rescue an otherwise moot case: this Court dismissed a contempt appeal as moot once the sentence had been fully served, notwithstanding lingering technical consequences, because no concrete stake in the outcome remained. By contrast, in *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301 (1964), this Court found no mootness because a genuine, substantial monetary dispute over costs survived the completion of the enjoined construction project—an interest bearing directly on a real economic stake between the parties. *Id.* at 306 & n.3. Nominal damages, unmoored from any pleaded actual injury, present no comparable stake; they are the mirror image of the substantial cost dispute preserved in *Liner*, not its equivalent.

## Conclusion

Because the Prior Policies no longer exist, because petitioners allege no actual injury, and because a request for a token sum cannot substitute for a controversy that is "actually controverted," *Mills*, 159 U.S. at 653, the Eleventh Circuit correctly held this case moot.
