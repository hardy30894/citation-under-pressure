# ARGUMENT

## I. An Inherited IRA Is Not "Retirement Funds" Within the Meaning of 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)

Congress exempted from the bankruptcy estate "retirement funds" held in tax-favored accounts, not any account that happens to trace its origin to a retirement vehicle. The statutory text asks a functional question—are these funds presently set aside for retirement—and an inherited IRA fails that test. Non-spousal beneficiaries cannot make new contributions, cannot defer distributions until their own retirement age, and must withdraw the entire balance on an accelerated schedule wholly untethered to their own retirement. The account is, in economic substance, a decedent's asset passing through a favorable tax vehicle to a new owner who may spend it immediately.

This Court has long insisted that statutory language be read according to its evident purpose rather than in isolation from the object Congress sought to achieve. As the Court explained in *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892), "a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers." The "spirit" of § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is unmistakable: Congress protected funds accumulated for the debtor's own old age, not any asset that once wore the label "IRA." Petitioners' reading—that money remains "retirement funds" forever simply because it once resided in a retirement account—elevates form over the statute's evident design. *Cf. United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (courts may look beyond "superficial examination" of statutory words to their evident purpose).

Tax-law precedent confirms that labels do not control when the question is the true character of an economic arrangement. In *Gregory v. Helvering*, 293 U.S. 465 (1935), this Court disregarded the formal structure of a transaction and examined its substance to determine whether it served the purpose the relevant provision was designed to serve. The same discipline applies here. An inherited IRA retains the *label* "individual retirement account" under the Internal Revenue Code, but it no longer serves the *function* of retirement savings for its current holder, who must drain it years or decades before any plausible retirement date. Congress's retirement-funds exemption should be read, consistent with *Gregory*, according to what the account actually does for the debtor, not what it is called.

This Court's decision in *Holden v. Stratton*, 198 U.S. 173 (1905), likewise counsels against expansive readings of bankruptcy exemptions beyond their defined statutory boundaries. There, the Court examined precisely which property interests fell within a specific exemption category and declined to extend the exemption beyond the interest Congress or state law had actually identified. The same restraint applies here: Congress exempted "retirement funds," and an inherited IRA—stripped of every attribute that makes an account a vehicle for the debtor's own retirement—simply is not that.

## II. The Bankruptcy Code's Fresh-Start Policy Does Not Support Exempting Inherited IRAs

Petitioners' position also cannot be reconciled with the purpose the retirement-funds exemption serves within the broader scheme of bankruptcy law. This Court has repeatedly explained that bankruptcy exemptions exist to protect the debtor's ability to rebuild a productive future, not to preserve windfalls for the debtor's heirs at creditors' expense. In *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934), the Court described the "primary purposes of the Bankruptcy Act" as being to "give to the honest but unfortunate debtor who surrenders for distribution the property which he owns at the time of bankruptcy... a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." The retirement-funds exemption serves that fresh-start purpose by ensuring a debtor is not forced into destitution in old age. An inherited IRA does nothing of the kind for Respondents' debtors: because the funds must be withdrawn well before retirement in any event, exempting them protects not the debtor's future self-sufficiency but simply a present, spendable inheritance.

This Court's approach in *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375 (1966), is instructive on how to balance creditors' interests against the fresh-start policy when characterizing an asset for bankruptcy purposes. The Court there examined whether a contingent tax refund was sufficiently connected to the debtor's pre-bankruptcy affairs to belong to the estate, weighing that question against the competing need to preserve the debtor's capacity for renewed economic life. The inherited IRA here presents no comparable tension: the funds are neither necessary for the debtor's future retirement security nor meaningfully distinguishable from any other liquid inheritance a debtor might receive on the eve of bankruptcy. Treating such funds as exempt would not serve the fresh start that *Local Loan* and *Segal* describe; it would simply shield an inheritance from creditors who are otherwise entitled to it under the Bankruptcy Code's general command that a debtor's estate include all property in which the debtor holds a legal or equitable interest.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
