**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Satisfies the Prison Litigation Reform Act’s Requirements for Prospective Relief in Prison-Conditions Litigation.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”), 18 U.S.C. § 3626, permits a court to order prospective relief in prison-conditions cases only if such relief is “narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s population-reduction order satisfies each of these requirements.

**A. The Order Is Narrowly Drawn and Extends No Further Than Necessary to Remedy the Eighth Amendment Violation.**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that involve the “unnecessary and wanton infliction of pain.” *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 173 (1976). This Court has long recognized that inadequate medical care in prisons can constitute cruel and unusual punishment. *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 104 (1976). The three-judge court found that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of systemic Eighth Amendment violations in California’s prisons, including constitutionally inadequate medical and mental health care. That finding is entitled to substantial deference. *See Dayton Bd. of Educ. v. Brinkman*, 433 U.S. 406, 419 (1977).

The population cap of 137.5% of design capacity is the minimum reduction necessary to bring the prison system into constitutional compliance. The court considered less intrusive alternatives, including expanded use of early release credits and increased funding for community-based programs, but found those measures insufficient. *See Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ.*, 402 U.S. 1, 16 (1971) (remedial decrees must be “remedial in nature” and “designed to eliminate the constitutional violation”). The order does not require immediate release of any specific inmate; rather, it provides the State flexibility to determine which prisoners to release and when, so long as the population cap is met within two years.

**B. The Order Is the Least Intrusive Means Necessary to Correct the Violation.**

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be “the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order satisfies this standard. The court found that California had failed for over a decade to remedy the constitutional violations despite numerous court orders and the appointment of a receiver. *See Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978) (affirming population cap where State had “persistently and flagrantly” violated the Constitution). The court also found that no other remedy—including increased funding, expanded facilities, or improved management—would suffice to correct the violations within a reasonable time.

This Court has consistently held that population caps are an appropriate remedy for overcrowding that causes constitutional violations. *See, e.g., Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 687; *Bell v. Wolfish*, 441 U.S. 520, 543 n.27 (1979) (recognizing that overcrowding can violate the Eighth Amendment). The PLRA does not prohibit population caps; it merely requires that they be narrowly drawn and the least intrusive means available. The three-judge court’s order meets that standard.

**II. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Complies with the PLRA’s Requirement That Prospective Relief Be “Necessary to Correct a Current and Ongoing Violation.”**

The PLRA provides that prospective relief shall not be granted unless the court finds that such relief is “necessary to correct a current and ongoing violation of a Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A)(i). The three-judge court made extensive findings that California’s prisons were currently and ongoingly violating the Eighth Amendment due to overcrowding. These findings are supported by overwhelming evidence, including expert testimony, statistical data, and the State’s own admissions.

The State argues that the order is not “necessary” because the State has taken steps to reduce overcrowding. But the PLRA does not require that a court wait until a violation becomes irreparable before ordering relief. *See Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 511 (2011) (citing *Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 687). The court found that the State’s efforts were insufficient and that the constitutional violations would continue absent the population cap. That finding is not clearly erroneous. *See Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985).

**III. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s Requirement That Prospective Relief Be “Narrowly Drawn” to Remedy the Specific Violation Found.**

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be “narrowly drawn” to remedy the specific violation found. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order is precisely tailored to the violation: overcrowding. The court found that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of the Eighth Amendment violations, and the order directly addresses that cause by requiring a reduction in the prison population. The order does not impose any other requirements, such as specific staffing levels or facility improvements, that are not directly related to the overcrowding problem.

This Court has upheld similarly tailored remedies in other prison-conditions cases. *See, e.g., Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 687 (affirming population cap); *Bell*, 441 U.S. at 543 n.27 (recognizing that overcrowding can violate the Eighth Amendment). The PLRA does not require that a court adopt the least restrictive remedy imaginable; it requires only that the remedy be “narrowly drawn” to the violation. The three-judge court’s order satisfies that standard.

**IV. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s Requirement That Prospective Relief Be “the Least Intrusive Means Necessary to Correct the Violation.”**

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be “the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court considered and rejected less intrusive alternatives, including expanded use of early release credits, increased funding for community-based programs, and improved management of the prison system. The court found that these alternatives would not suffice to correct the constitutional violations within a reasonable time.

This Court has held that a court may order a population cap even when less intrusive alternatives exist, so long as those alternatives are inadequate to remedy the violation. *See Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 687. The three-judge court’s order is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation, given the State’s long history of noncompliance and the severity of the constitutional violations.

**V. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s Requirement That Prospective Relief Be “Necessary to Correct a Current and Ongoing Violation.”**

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be “necessary to correct a current and ongoing violation of a Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A)(i). **CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the three-judge court should be affirmed.
