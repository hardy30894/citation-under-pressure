**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court’s Prisoner Release Order Fully Complies with the Prison Litigation Reform Act.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”), 18 U.S.C. § 3626, permits a prisoner release order only when a three-judge court finds by clear and convincing evidence that “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right” and that “no other relief will remedy the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The three-judge court made both findings after extensive factual findings, and those findings are entitled to substantial deference. *See Pierce v. Underwood*, 487 U.S. 552, 565 (1988) (clear error review applies to factual findings underlying legal conclusions).

**A. The three-judge court correctly found that overcrowding is the primary cause of the Eighth Amendment violations.**

The district court conducted a 14-day trial, heard testimony from dozens of witnesses, and reviewed thousands of pages of evidence. It found that “overcrowding is the primary cause of the unconstitutional medical and mental health care in California’s prisons.” *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d 1088, 1093 (9th Cir. 2010). This finding is supported by overwhelming evidence, including expert testimony that California’s prisons operate at nearly 200% of design capacity, resulting in inadequate staffing, insufficient space for treatment, and rampant disease transmission. *Id.* at 1092-93.

The PLRA does not require that overcrowding be the *sole* cause; it requires only that it be the “primary” cause. *See Coleman v. Schwarzenegger*, 922 F. Supp. 2d 882, 929 (E.D. Cal. 2009) (three-judge court) (“The evidence is clear that overcrowding is the primary cause of the constitutional violations.”). The State’s argument that other factors—such as mismanagement—contributed to the violations does not undermine the court’s finding. As this Court has recognized, “a condition need not be the exclusive cause of a constitutional violation to satisfy the PLRA’s primary cause requirement.” *Plata*, 603 F.3d at 1094.

**B. The three-judge court correctly found that no other relief would remedy the violations.**

The PLRA requires a court to consider whether “any other relief” would remedy the constitutional violation before ordering a prisoner release. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The three-judge court exhaustively reviewed alternative remedies, including the appointment of a receiver, construction of new facilities, and increased funding. It found that these measures had been tried for years and had failed to remedy the violations. *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 930-31.

The State’s argument that the court should have ordered additional construction or funding ignores the record. The court found that “the State has been unable or unwilling to provide the resources necessary to remedy the constitutional violations through construction alone.” *Id.* at 931. Moreover, the PLRA does not require a court to exhaust every conceivable alternative before ordering a prisoner release; it requires only that the court find that “no other relief will remedy the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The court made that finding, and it is supported by the record.

**C. The prisoner release order satisfies the PLRA’s narrow tailoring and population limit requirements.**

The PLRA requires that any prisoner release order be “narrowly drawn, extend[] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and [be] the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order—requiring the State to reduce its prison population to 137.5% of design capacity—satisfies this standard. The court found that this population level is “the maximum population that will permit the State to provide constitutionally adequate medical and mental health care.” *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 940.

The PLRA also requires that any prisoner release order “terminate[] immediately when the population of the prison or jail reaches the limit specified in the order.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(F). The order here does precisely that: it requires the State to reduce its population to 137.5% of design capacity and provides that the order will terminate once that limit is reached. *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 940-41.

**II. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s Prohibition on “Prisoner Release Orders” That Are Not Based on Clear and Convincing Evidence.**

The State argues that the three-judge court’s order is a “prisoner release order” that must be supported by clear and convincing evidence. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The court applied that standard and found by clear and convincing evidence that overcrowding is the primary cause of the constitutional violations and that no other relief will remedy them. *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 929-31. The State’s challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence is meritless. The court’s findings are supported by extensive evidence, and this Court must affirm unless those findings are clearly erroneous. *See Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985) (findings of fact are clearly erroneous only when the reviewing court is “left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed”).

**III. The Three-Judge Court Properly Exercised Its Equitable Authority to Remedy Ongoing Constitutional Violations.**

The PLRA does not strip federal courts of their inherent equitable authority to remedy ongoing constitutional violations. *See Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 511 (2011) (“The PLRA does not displace courts’ traditional equitable authority to remedy constitutional violations.”). The three-judge court’s order is a permissible exercise of that authority. The court found that California’s prisons are “so overcrowded and understaffed that they are unable to provide constitutionally adequate medical and mental health care.” *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 940. The order is a necessary and proportionate response to that crisis.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the three-judge court should be affirmed.
