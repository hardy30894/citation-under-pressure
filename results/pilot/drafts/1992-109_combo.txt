**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and the judgment below must be reversed. The record in this case reveals substantial evidence of mental impairment at the time of the plea, including a recent suicide attempt, severe depression, and the use of four psychotropic medications—none of which prompted any inquiry by the trial court. Under settled precedent, such circumstances trigger a duty to conduct a competency hearing under the “reasoned choice” standard, which demands more than mere rational understanding of the proceedings. The trial court’s failure to do so violated due process.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the right to counsel and the right to a fair trial are not merely procedural protections but fundamental components of due process. In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), the Court held that “the Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.” Though decided in 1970, *Winship* builds upon a foundation of earlier decisions that affirm the centrality of due process in criminal proceedings. The Court’s earlier decisions in *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937), and *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86, 90 (1923), established that the Fourteenth Amendment incorporates fundamental rights essential to a fair trial, including the right to counsel and the right to be free from conviction based on unreliable evidence.

The right to waive counsel is not a mere procedural formality. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), the Court declared that “in a criminal prosecution, a defendant cannot be deemed to have waived his right to counsel unless the waiver is made with an understanding of the nature of the charges, the possible penalties, and the risks involved in proceeding without counsel.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Cuyler v. Sullivan*, 446 U.S. 335, 342 (1980), but its roots lie in earlier decisions that recognized the gravity of waiving counsel. The Court in *Johnson* emphasized that the waiver must be “knowing and intelligent,” a standard that demands more than a superficial understanding of legal rights.

The Supreme Court has further clarified that the standard for competency to waive counsel is higher than the standard for competency to stand trial. In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), the Court held that a defendant is competent to stand trial if he “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” This standard, derived from *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960), applies only to the ability to assist counsel. But the Court has consistently held that the waiver of counsel requires a higher threshold. In *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975), the Court stated that a defendant must “have the capacity to make a reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him.” This “reasoned choice” standard is not merely a matter of form; it is a constitutional requirement.

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this standard in *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1973), where the court held that “a defendant is competent to waive counsel only if he has the capacity for reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him.” The court emphasized that “the right to self-representation is not a right to be exercised in a state of mental incapacity.” Similarly, in *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973), the court stated that “a waiver of counsel must be made with full understanding of the nature of the charges and the consequences of the waiver,” and that “utmost solicitude” must be shown by the court. These decisions reflect a long-standing judicial recognition that the waiver of constitutional rights demands more than a mechanical understanding of legal terms.

The record here demonstrates that Moran was not capable of such a reasoned choice. He had attempted suicide just months before the plea hearing. He was taking four medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—all of which affect the central nervous system and can impair judgment, memory, and decision-making. The trial court, aware that Moran was on medication, made no inquiry into its effects. This failure is fatal. In *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1981), the court held that “a good faith doubt about a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights arises when there is substantial evidence of incompetence.” The record here contains such evidence.

Moreover, Moran’s conduct at the plea hearing was not consistent with a reasoned choice. His responses were monosyllabic, and he stated he wanted to fire counsel to prevent any mitigating evidence from being presented. This behavior suggests not a calculated decision, but a desire to avoid the consequences of his actions—precisely the kind of conduct that undermines the validity of a waiver. In *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969), the Court held that “a plea of guilty is not a mere formality” and that “the record must show that the plea was made voluntarily, knowingly, and intelligently.” The same principle applies to the waiver of counsel.
