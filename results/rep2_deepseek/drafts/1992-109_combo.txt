ARGUMENT

I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Require a Heightened “Reasoned Choice” Standard for Competency to Plead Guilty or Waive Counsel.

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Constitution demands a higher level of mental functioning for a defendant to waive counsel or plead guilty than for him to stand trial. This court has never articulated such a distinction. The standard for competency to stand trial—whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings”—is the constitutional floor for all phases of a criminal case. *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). That standard ensures that a defendant can make meaningful decisions about his defense, including whether to plead guilty or waive counsel.

In *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385–86 (1966), this Court held that a trial court must conduct a competency hearing whenever a “bona fide doubt” exists as to the defendant’s competence to stand trial. The Court did not suggest that a different, more demanding standard applies when the defendant seeks to plead guilty or waive counsel. To the contrary, the Court observed that the same due process concerns—the reliability of the proceedings and the voluntariness of the defendant’s actions—underlie all competency determinations. *Id.* at 378.

The Ninth Circuit relied on *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966), to support its heightened standard. But *Westbrook* held only that a trial court must make a separate inquiry into whether a defendant’s waiver of counsel is knowing and intelligent; it did not create a new competency standard. *Id.* at 150. The Court stated that “the trial judge, in determining whether the accused may waive counsel, must determine not only that he is competent to stand trial, but also that he is competent to waive his constitutional right to counsel.” *Id.* That language does not define “competent to waive” as requiring a higher level of mental capacity. Rather, it simply requires the court to ensure that the waiver is “intelligent and competent,” as *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464–65 (1938), had long required. *Johnson* held that waiver of counsel must be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege,” and that the determination depends on “the particular facts and circumstances surrounding that case, including the background, experience, and conduct of the accused.” *Id.* at 464. Nothing in *Johnson* suggests that a defendant must possess a capacity for “reasoned choice” beyond that required by *Dusky*.

Similarly, *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105, 108 (1954), held that a defendant who is “insane” cannot waive counsel, but the Court applied the same standard used for competency to stand trial. *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506, 516 (1962), reiterated that waiver of counsel must be “intelligent and competent,” but again did not articulate a higher standard. And *Bishop v. United States*, 350 U.S. 961 (1956) (per curiam), reversed a conviction where the defendant was not competent to plead guilty, but the standard applied was the same as for trial competency.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard finds no support in any pre-1970 decision of this Court. The Court has consistently treated competency as a unitary concept: a defendant who meets the *Dusky* standard is capable of making the basic decisions required in a criminal case. To impose a higher standard for guilty pleas or waivers of counsel would create an unwarranted distinction that the Due Process Clause does not require.

II. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Accepting Moran’s Guilty Pleas and Waiver of Counsel.

Even if a heightened standard were required, the record does not show that the trial court had a duty to hold a further competency hearing. Under *Pate*, a hearing is required only when there is a “bona fide doubt” as to competency. 383 U.S. at 385. Here, the trial court had before it two psychiatric reports concluding that Moran was competent to stand trial. The court questioned Moran, who responded monosyllabically but indicated he understood the charges and the consequences of his pleas. The court also noted that Moran was taking medication but did not observe any behavior suggesting incompetence. Nothing in the record created a bona fide doubt that Moran lacked the capacity to make a knowing and intelligent waiver.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on Moran’s suicide attempt and medication is misplaced. This Court has never held that such factors alone trigger a duty to hold a hearing. The trial court properly exercised its discretion in accepting Moran’s pleas and waiver.

III. The State Post-Conviction Court Applied the Correct Standard.

The Ninth Circuit assumed that the state court applied the *Dusky* standard rather than a higher standard. But the state court’s finding that Moran was competent to waive counsel was based on the same psychiatric evidence that supported his competency to stand trial. Even if the state court did not explicitly articulate a “reasoned choice” standard, its conclusion is consistent with the constitutional standard. This Court has never required state courts to use any particular formulation; it is enough that the court actually determined that the defendant was competent to make the waiver. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963) (state court findings are presumed correct absent evidence that an incorrect legal standard was applied).

Because the Due Process Clause does not require a heightened “reasoned choice” standard, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
