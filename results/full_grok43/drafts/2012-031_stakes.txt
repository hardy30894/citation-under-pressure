**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s certification order cannot stand because plaintiffs failed to demonstrate by a preponderance of admissible evidence that damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement. Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This Court has held that the predominance inquiry “tests whether proposed classes are sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation,” Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997), and demands “rigorous analysis” that may overlap with the merits, Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 351 (2011) (quoting Gen. Tel. Co. v. Falcon, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982)).

In In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), the Third Circuit correctly required district courts to “make findings” on each element of Rule 23, including whether a common methodology can quantify damages on a class-wide basis. Id. at 312, 320. The district court here purported to follow that standard yet accepted an expert model that measured damages flowing from four distinct theories of liability while the court itself had limited the class to a single theory—deterrence of overbuilders. Because the model did not isolate the damages attributable to the sole remaining theory, it supplied no evidence that damages could be measured class-wide through common proof.

This Court has emphasized that “a plaintiff cannot rely on mere speculation” to satisfy Rule 23. See Tyson Foods, Inc. v. Bouaphakeo, 577 U.S. 442, 454 (2016) (quoting earlier authorities). Here, the proffered methodology failed that test. It therefore could not discharge plaintiffs’ burden to show predominance by a preponderance of the evidence. The court of appeals’ affirmance rested on an erroneous view that any plausible damages formula suffices once antitrust impact is shown. That view conflicts with the rigorous-analysis requirement of Hydrogen Peroxide and Wal-Mart.

The certification order should be reversed.
