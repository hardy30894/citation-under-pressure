ARGUMENT

I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION.

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), provides that an alien “may be detained beyond the removal period” pending removal. This language does not authorize indefinite confinement. Rather, it must be read to contain an implicit limitation of reasonableness. *United States ex rel. Tisi v. Tod*, 264 U.S. 131, 133 (1924) (detention pending deportation is lawful only for a “reasonable time”); *see also* *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932) (courts construe statutes to avoid serious constitutional questions). A statute that permits the Attorney General to imprison a resident alien permanently, without any further justification, would raise grave constitutional concerns. This Court therefore must interpret § 1231(a)(6) to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to effect removal. *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283, 300 (1944) (reading statute to avoid “serious constitutional challenges”).

II. INDEFINITE DETENTION OF RESIDENT ALIENS VIOLATES DUE PROCESS.

Resident aliens are “persons” entitled to the protection of the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause. *The Japanese Immigrant Case (Yamataya v. Fisher)*, 189 U.S. 86, 100–01 (1903); *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896). Deportation proceedings are civil, not criminal. *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 237. But indefinite civil detention—imprisonment that may last a lifetime—is constitutionally permissible only when it serves a legitimate nonpunitive purpose and is closely tailored to that purpose. *Id.* at 237–38 (holding that Congress cannot impose “imprisonment at hard labor” in deportation proceedings without a judicial trial because such punishment exceeds regulatory authority). Here, the sole legitimate purpose of continued detention is to secure the alien’s presence for removal. *Tisi*, 264 U.S. at 133. When removal is not reasonably foreseeable, that purpose evaporates, and detention becomes punitive—a “prolonged confinement” that “bears no reasonable relation to the purpose for which the alien was committed.” *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 237; *cf.* *United States v. Brig Malek Adhel*, 43 U.S. (2 How.) 210, 236 (1844) (detention must be “limited to the exigency”).

This Court has repeatedly recognized that resident aliens, once admitted, have “the right to be at liberty” unless the government can justify detention. *Bridges v. Wixon*, 326 U.S. 135, 161 (1945) (Murphy, J., concurring). In *Tisi*, the Court held that a resident alien could not be detained indefinitely where “there was no reasonable ground to believe that deportation could be effected” within a reasonable time. 264 U.S. at 133. The same principle governs here. Petitioner Zadvydas has been in custody for years after every plausible avenue of removal has been exhausted. No country will accept him. Continued imprisonment serves no civil purpose; it is simply “imprisonment for a crime of which [he] has never been convicted.” *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 238.

*Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is not controlling. *Mezei* involved an excludable alien who was stopped at the border and had never effected an entry into the United States. *Id.* at 215–16. By contrast, Zadvydas is a resident alien who has been lawfully admitted and has resided here for decades. The constitutional protections due a resident alien are far greater than those due an alien at the threshold of entry. *See* *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953) (resident alien returning from abroad is entitled to procedural due process). The *Mezei* Court itself distinguished such cases. *Mezei*, 345 U.S. at 212.

III. THE FIFTH CIRCUIT’S “IMPOSSIBILITY” STANDARD IS TOO STRICT.

The Fifth Circuit required petitioner to show that deportation “will never be possible” before his detention could be challenged. That standard effectively sanctions indefinite detention as long as the government professes “good faith” efforts. But the Constitution does not permit indefinite imprisonment based on speculation about future removal. *Tisi*, 264 U.S. at 133. The proper inquiry is whether removal is reasonably foreseeable. When the government cannot demonstrate a realistic prospect of removal within a reasonable time, the statute no longer authorizes detention. *Tisi* so held; *Wong Wing* forbids a result where an alien is “imprisoned ... indefinitely” without judicial review. 163 U.S. at 237.

Because the lower court applied an erroneously strict standard, its judgment cannot stand. The district court correctly found that Zadvydas’s removal was not reasonably foreseeable. That finding is amply supported by the record and should be reinstated, with the appropriate conditions of release as set forth by the district court.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the district court’s order granting habeas relief should be affirmed.
