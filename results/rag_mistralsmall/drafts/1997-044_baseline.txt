**ARGUMENT**

**I. Debts Incurred Through Fraud Are Nondischargeable Under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code.**

The Bankruptcy Code explicitly excepts from discharge debts obtained by "actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). This provision reflects Congress’s intent to prevent debtors who engage in fraud from evading liability through bankruptcy. *Field v. Mans*, 516 U.S. 59, 69 (1995) ("[T]he Bankruptcy Code’s exceptions to discharge reflect a congressional judgment that certain debts are so tied to a debtor’s misconduct that they should remain with the debtor even after bankruptcy."). The Third Circuit correctly applied this principle in affirming the nondischargeability of Cohen’s debt. *Cohen v. de la Cruz*, 94 F.3d 67, 70 (3d Cir. 1996).

Petitioner does not dispute that his conduct violated New Jersey’s Consumer Fraud Act or that the Bankruptcy Court found his actions constituted "actual fraud." Instead, he argues that the treble damages award—including attorney’s fees and costs—should be dischargeable. This argument misapprehends the scope of § 523(a)(2)(A). The statute excepts from discharge *any debt* obtained by fraud, not merely the compensatory portion of a judgment. *Id.* at 71 ("The treble damage award under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is not dischargeable in bankruptcy."). The Third Circuit’s holding aligns with the Supreme Court’s interpretation of § 523(a)(2)(A) as encompassing all damages flowing from fraudulent conduct. *See Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 284 (1991) (holding that a debt obtained by fraud is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A)).

**II. The Treble Damages Award Is Nondischargeable as Part of the Debt Incurred Through Fraud.**

Petitioner contends that treble damages are punitive and thus dischargeable. This argument conflates state law labels with federal bankruptcy policy. The Supreme Court has made clear that the dischargeability of damages is a federal question governed by the Bankruptcy Code, not state characterizations. *Molzof v. United States*, 502 U.S. 301, 305 (1992) ("[T]he meaning of the term ‘punitive damages’ as used in [the Federal Tort Claims Act], a federal statute, is by definition a federal question."). Under § 523(a)(2)(A), the focus is on whether the debt was *obtained by* fraud—not whether the damages are labeled compensatory or punitive. *Cohen*, 94 F.3d at 71.

The Third Circuit correctly reasoned that the treble damages award was foreseeable to Petitioner at the time he committed fraud. *Id.* Because the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act mandates treble damages for violations, Petitioner’s liability for those damages was part of the "debt obtained by" his fraudulent conduct. *Id.* This interpretation comports with the Bankruptcy Code’s policy of ensuring that debtors who commit fraud do not receive a "fresh start" at the expense of their victims. *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 286-87.

**III. The Bankruptcy Court Properly Found Petitioner’s Conduct Constituted "Actual Fraud" Under § 523(a)(2)(A).**

Petitioner argues that he made no affirmative misrepresentation and thus cannot be liable for "actual fraud." This contention ignores the Bankruptcy Court’s finding that his silence in charging unlawful rents constituted an implied misrepresentation. *Cohen*, 94 F.3d at 70. The Third Circuit correctly deferred to this factual finding, as it was not clearly erroneous. *Id.* (citing *Universal Minerals, Inc. v. C.A. Hughes & Co.*, 669 F.2d 98, 102 (3d Cir. 1981)).

Moreover, the Bankruptcy Court’s determination that Petitioner acted with reckless indifference to the truth satisfies the scienter requirement for "actual fraud." *Id.* at 70-71. The Supreme Court has recognized that reckless disregard for the truth can establish the intent to deceive necessary for nondischargeability under § 523(a)(2)(A). *See Field*, 516 U.S. at 71 (citing *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118-19 (3d Cir. 1995)). The Third Circuit’s holding that Petitioner’s reckless conduct rendered his debt nondischargeable is fully supported by precedent.

**Conclusion**

The Bankruptcy Code’s clear text and the Supreme Court’s precedent compel the conclusion that debts obtained through fraud—including treble damages awards—are nondischargeable. The Third Circuit correctly applied these principles in affirming the Bankruptcy Court’s judgment. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
