**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT PROVIDE A CAUSE OF ACTION FOR A “CLASS OF ONE” WHERE THE PLAINTIFF ALLEGES NO MEMBERSHIP IN A PROTECTED CLASS OR GROUP.**

The text and history of the Equal Protection Clause confirm that its core purpose is to protect groups from discriminatory treatment. The Clause commands that no State shall “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. From its inception, this provision was understood to guard against laws that target discrete and insular minorities. *See United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 n.4 (1938). This Court has consistently applied the Equal Protection Clause to claims of discrimination based on membership in a class, such as race, *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), gender, *Craig v. Boren*, 429 U.S. 190 (1976), or national origin, *Korematsu v. United States*, 323 U.S. 214 (1944). The Clause does not, however, create a general cause of action for every arbitrary government action.

**A. This Court’s Precedent Requires a Showing of Class-Based Discrimination.**

In *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), this Court held that the Equal Protection Clause is violated only when a plaintiff proves that the government acted with a discriminatory purpose. *Id.* at 239-40. The Court emphasized that the Clause is not a “general source of substantive rights” but rather a prohibition on “invidious discrimination.” *Id.* at 248. Similarly, in *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256 (1979), the Court required proof that the government acted “because of,” not merely “in spite of,” the adverse effects on a particular group. *Id.* at 279. These cases underscore that the Equal Protection Clause is group-focused, not individual-focused.

The Court of Appeals erred by recognizing a “class of one” claim based solely on a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him.” This standard lacks any anchor in the Clause’s text or history. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), this Court rejected a similar claim, holding that the Equal Protection Clause does not guarantee “freedom from arbitrary action by state officials” unless the action is “based on a classification.” *Id.* at 8. The Court explained that the Clause “does not forbid discrimination as to one person as compared with others if the discrimination is not based on a classification.” *Id.* at 9.

**B. The “Class of One” Theory Conflicts with This Court’s Rational Basis Jurisprudence.**

This Court has long held that rational basis review applies to most equal protection challenges. *See, e.g., FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993). Under that standard, a law or action is constitutional if it is “rationally related to a legitimate state interest.” *Id.* at 314. The “class of one” theory would upend this deferential standard by requiring courts to second-guess every individualized government decision. In *Heller v. Doe*, 509 U.S. 312 (1993), the Court reaffirmed that rational basis review does not require the government to “prove that the classification is rationally related to a legitimate state interest.” *Id.* at 320. Instead, the challenger must “negative every conceivable basis” for the action. *Id.* at 320-21. A “class of one” claim, by contrast, would allow a plaintiff to survive dismissal merely by alleging ill will, a standard that is both unworkable and inconsistent with *Heller*.

**C. The Seventh Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with This Court’s Holding in *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*.**

Although *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*, 553 U.S. 591 (2008), was decided after the decision below, its reasoning confirms that “class of one” claims are not cognizable in the context of discretionary government actions. In *Engquist*, this Court held that the “class of one” theory does not apply in the public employment context because “the government is not constrained by the Equal Protection Clause to treat all employees alike.” *Id.* at 605. The Court explained that “the Equal Protection Clause is not a source of substantive rights or liberties,” but rather “a prohibition on discriminatory treatment.” *Id.* at 598. The same logic applies here: the Village’s decision to demand a 33-foot easement from Olech, rather than the 15-foot easement demanded of others, is a discretionary, individualized decision that does not implicate the Equal Protection Clause.

**D. Recognizing a “Class of One” Claim Would Flood the Federal Courts with Trivial Disputes.**

This Court has cautioned against expanding the Equal Protection Clause into a “general federal tort law.” *See Paul v. Davis*, 424 U.S. 693, 701 (1976). The “class of one” theory would invite federal litigation over every allegedly arbitrary government decision, from zoning variances to water connections. In *Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448 (1962), the Court held that the Equal Protection Clause does not require “the prosecution of all offenders against the law.” *Id.* at 456. Similarly, the Clause does not require the government to treat every citizen identically in every discretionary decision. The Seventh Circuit’s decision would transform the federal courts into “super-locals” reviewing routine municipal actions, a result this Court has consistently rejected. *See Rizzo v. Goode*, 423 U.S. 362, 378-79 (1976).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
