### Argument

Arizona’s rule does not violate due process. The Fourteenth Amendment requires that a defendant be allowed to present a meaningful defense, but it does not entitle him to introduce any and all evidence he labels “intent evidence” in whatever form he prefers. The Constitution permits States to define the elements of their crimes and to allocate the use of mental-disease evidence between an insanity defense and the mens rea element of the offense. See *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952); *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 749-51 (2006).

In *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 698-701 (1975), the Court held that due process prevents a State from shifting to the defendant the burden of disproving an element of the offense. But *Mullaney* did not constitutionalize any particular evidentiary theory of mental illness. Rather, the question remains whether the State has required the prosecution to prove criminal intent beyond a reasonable doubt. Arizona did that here. The State was still required to prove that Clark knowingly or intentionally shot the officer. Arizona simply restricted the defensive use of expert psychiatric testimony to the issue on which it was relevant and reliable: insanity. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 750-52, 779-80.

The Court has repeatedly held that due process does not prohibit States from limiting evidence that is speculative, confusing, or likely to mislead the jury. In *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 42-43, 56 (1996) (plurality opinion), a plurality upheld a state rule barring consideration of voluntary intoxication on the mens rea issue, emphasizing the States’ authority to define the relevance of mental-state evidence. Justice Ginsburg’s concurrence likewise recognized that the common-law tradition leaves States substantial room to regulate evidence bearing on culpability. *Id.* at 56-57 (Ginsburg, J., concurring). Arizona’s rule is narrower than the one sustained in *Egelhoff*: it did not exclude all mental-state evidence, only expert testimony offered to prove that a diagnosed mental illness prevented the formation of intent. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 770-71.

The Court’s decisions in *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973), and *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 690-91 (1986), are not to the contrary. Those cases involved the exclusion of reliable third-party confessions and the circumstances of a confession, not a State’s targeted limitation on expert psychiatric opinion. *Chambers* itself stressed that “the hearsay statements involved were originally made and subsequently offered at trial under circumstances that provided considerable assurance of their reliability.” 410 U.S. at 300. Arizona’s rule rests on the opposite concern: psychiatric opinion about whether a defendant actually formed a particular mental state is often of limited probative value because it depends on layers of clinical inference. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 770-77. The Constitution does not compel a State to admit expert testimony that it reasonably concludes will distract the jury from the real issue.

Nor does *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 83 (1985), help Clark. *Ake* held that due process requires access to psychiatric assistance when sanity is a significant factor at trial. But *Ake* did not hold that a defendant has a freestanding constitutional right to present psychiatric evidence on every issue to which it might be marginally relevant. To the contrary, *Ake* recognized that the State may structure the insanity defense and the admissibility of psychiatric evidence. *Id.* at 83-84. Arizona satisfied *Ake* by allowing Clark to present expert evidence in support of insanity.

Arizona’s rule also fits comfortably within the longstanding distinction between “insanity” and “mens rea.” In *Clark*, the Court explained that many jurisdictions have drawn that line to prevent expert testimony from collapsing the separate insanity inquiry into a broad and unreliable claim that mental disorder negated intent. 548 U.S. at 747-48, 752-54. That line is especially justified where, as here, the defendant had the burden to prove insanity by clear and convincing evidence. *Id.* at 748-49. Due process does not forbid a State from channeling mental-health evidence into the affirmative defense the State has created and from insisting that the prosecution’s burden on intent be tested by ordinary evidence, including the defendant’s conduct, statements, and surrounding circumstances.

Finally, the State’s rule is consistent with *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 206-07 (1977), which held that the Constitution allows States broad freedom to define affirmative defenses so long as the prosecution retains the burden to prove the statutory elements. Arizona did exactly that. Clark could argue insanity; the prosecution still had to prove intent; and Arizona merely limited one category of expert evidence whose use on the mens rea question it reasonably concluded would be misleading. Under *Patterson*, *Egelhoff*, *Ake*, and *Clark*, due process was not violated.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
