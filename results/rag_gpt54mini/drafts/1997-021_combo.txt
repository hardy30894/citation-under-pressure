I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” polling rule is rational and consistent with the Act.

The National Labor Relations Act does not specifically regulate employer polling of employees regarding incumbent-union support. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. National Labor Relations Board, 522 U.S. 359, 366-68 (1998). In that gap, the Board is entitled to formulate a policy so long as it reasonably accommodates the Act’s competing objectives, including industrial peace and employees’ freedom of choice. National Labor Relations Board v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786-87 (1990); Beth Israel Hospital v. National Labor Relations Board, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978). The Board’s polling rule does exactly that. It permits a poll only when objective circumstances raise a reasonable doubt about majority support, thereby limiting disruptive employer-initiated polling while preserving an avenue for testing genuine uncertainty. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, 522 U.S. at 367-69.

That judgment is fully consistent with the Act’s structure. An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of continuing majority status. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. National Labor Relations Board, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). The presumption promotes stability in bargaining relationships, a central policy of the Act. National Labor Relations Board v. Katz, 369 U.S. 736, 743 (1962). The Board has long been permitted to police employer conduct that destabilizes representation relationships even when the statute does not prescribe a single mechanical test. National Labor Relations Board v. J. Weingarten, Inc., 420 U.S. 251, 266-67 (1975) (recognizing Board’s policymaking role); Beth Israel Hospital, 437 U.S. at 500-01. Employer polling is especially fraught because it can chill employee choice and disrupt bargaining without the safeguards of a Board-conducted election. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, 522 U.S. at 368-69; Thomas Industries, Inc. v. National Labor Relations Board, 687 F.2d 863, 866-67 (6th Cir. 1982).

Petitioner’s contrary view—that the Act requires a looser polling standard—would invert the statutory preference for Board-supervised elections and bargaining stability. See National Labor Relations Board v. Savair Manufacturing Co., 414 U.S. 270, 277-78 (1973); Brotherhood of Railway & Steamship Clerks v. Association for the Benefit of Non-Contract Employees, 380 U.S. 650, 668-69 (1965). The Board reasonably concluded that if an employer lacks objective grounds to doubt majority status, it should not be free to initiate an unsupervised poll designed to supplant the Act’s preferred processes. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, 522 U.S. at 368-69. Courts do not rewrite that balance merely because another test might make polling more frequent. Curtin Matheson, 494 U.S. at 786-87; National Labor Relations Board v. Erie Resistor Corp., 373 U.S. 221, 236-37 (1963).

II. The Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt was not supported by substantial evidence.

Substantial evidence review requires more than a record that could support a different inference; the Board’s conclusion must be sustained if supported by relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept. Universal Camera Corp. v. National Labor Relations Board, 340 U.S. 474, 488-91 (1951); National Labor Relations Board v. Walton Manufacturing Co., 369 U.S. 404, 405-07 (1962). But the Board may not ignore probative evidence or apply its presumption in a manner detached from the record. Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 488; Allentown Mack Sales & Service, 522 U.S. at 370-75.

Here, the undisputed evidence showed a clear cluster of antiunion statements from employees in the unit, including expressions that the union was no longer wanted or that employees no longer saw value in it. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, 522 U.S. at 370-74. The Board discounted several statements on grounds that do not withstand scrutiny. Statements made during job interviews are not automatically incredible; the issue is whether they are probative in context. See National Labor Relations Board v. Walton Manufacturing Co., 369 U.S. at 408-09. Nor may the Board transform expressions of dissatisfaction with union performance into meaningless evidence of support for the union. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, 522 U.S. at 372-74; NLRB v. Columbia Enameling & Stamping Co., 306 U.S. 292, 299-300 (1939). Likewise, a supervisor’s or employee-leader’s report about unit sentiment cannot be dismissed without a reasoned explanation when the report is direct evidence bearing on majority status. Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 488-91.

Most importantly, the Board’s inference that only seven employees had repudiated the union was not fairly drawn from the whole record. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, 522 U.S. at 373-75. The Court has repeatedly held that when the record as a whole points in the opposite direction, enforcement cannot rest on speculation. Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 490-91; National Labor Relations Board v. Nevada Consolidated Copper Corp., 316 U.S. 105, 106-07 (1942). On this record, Allentown Mack had ample objective grounds to harbor reasonable doubt, and its carefully supervised poll confirmed that doubt. The Board’s contrary finding should therefore be set aside. Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 488-91; Thomas Industries, 687 F.2d at 868.
