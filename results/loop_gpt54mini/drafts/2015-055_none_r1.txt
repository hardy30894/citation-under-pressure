**Argument**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively on collateral review because it is substantive. Under the Court’s retroactivity framework, a new rule applies to cases on collateral review when it alters the class of persons or conduct the law punishes. See *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in judgments in part and dissenting in part). *Johnson* did exactly that. By holding the ACCA residual clause unconstitutionally vague, the Court removed that clause as a lawful basis for imposing the Act’s fifteen-year mandatory minimum. A rule that narrows the scope of a criminal statute in that way is substantive, not procedural. See *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880); *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874); *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971).

The distinction between substantive and procedural rules is longstanding. In *United States v. Schooner Peggy*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103 (1801), the Court explained that when the law changes, courts must decide cases under the law as it exists. That principle is especially compelling when the change removes legal authority to impose punishment. *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), and *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874), both reflect the same rule: a sentence imposed without lawful authority cannot stand. *Johnson* falls squarely within that tradition because it invalidated one of ACCA’s definitional clauses and thus eliminated a category of sentences previously imposed under it.

The government cannot recharacterize *Johnson* as merely procedural. A procedural rule regulates the manner of determining guilt or punishment; it does not change the range of punishment the law permits. See *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971). *Johnson* did more than alter sentencing procedure. It held that the residual clause’s indeterminacy denied due process and therefore could not serve as a basis for enhanced punishment at all. Once the clause was declared void, a prisoner sentenced under it was imprisoned for a punishment the statute no longer authorizes. That is the hallmark of a substantive rule. See *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880); *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874).

The Court’s older retroactivity decisions confirm that the answer turns on whether the new rule affects the legal reach of the criminal law. In *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965), *Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. 719 (1966), *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967), and *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244 (1969), the Court treated retroactivity as a function of the rule’s effect on criminal liability and punishment. But those decisions did not displace the fundamental proposition that rules invalidating the government’s authority to punish are retroactive. *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971), reaffirmed that distinction. *Johnson* is not a mere change in procedure; it is a determination that the residual clause cannot constitutionally support an ACCA sentence.

Retroactivity is also necessary to vindicate the Due Process principle that punishment may not rest on a vague law. A void statute is no law at all. See *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880). If a prisoner’s sentence was enhanced under a clause later held unconstitutionally vague, collateral relief is the only means of correcting a sentence the law does not permit. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874), teaches that a court may not continue to enforce punishment beyond its lawful authority, and *United States v. Schooner Peggy*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103 (1801), teaches that courts must apply the law as it stands when they decide a case.

Welch therefore is entitled to the benefit of *Johnson*. Because his ACCA sentence depended on the residual clause, and because that clause is void, his sentence rests on an invalid legal premise. The judgment below should be reversed.
