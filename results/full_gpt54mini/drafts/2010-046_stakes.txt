**ARGUMENT**

The order below does not violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act. Congress enacted the PLRA to curb overbroad prison injunctions, but it preserved federal courts’ authority to remedy ongoing constitutional violations with narrowly tailored prospective relief. See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The statutory text permits relief only if it is “narrowly drawn,” “extends no further than necessary to correct the violation,” and is the “least intrusive means necessary” to correct the violation. Id. The three-judge court’s population-reduction order satisfies that standard because it responds to an Eighth Amendment violation caused by overcrowding, a violation already found after extensive proceedings and supported by a detailed remedial record.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that prison overcrowding may violate the Eighth Amendment when it causes the deprivation of basic human needs or otherwise renders confinement unconstitutional. See Rhodes v. Chapman, 452 U.S. 337, 347-50 (1981); Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 502-03 (2011). Where state officials are unable or unwilling to cure unconstitutional conditions through lesser measures, federal courts may order structural relief that reaches the source of the violation. Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978); Milliken v. Bradley, 433 U.S. 267, 280-81 (1977). The PLRA codified that remedial principle by requiring courts to tailor relief to the constitutional harm, not to bar effective relief altogether. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A); Gilmore v. California, 220 F.3d 987, 999-1000 (9th Cir. 2000).

The three-judge court made precisely the findings the PLRA demands. It found, after considering the evidence, that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of the unconstitutional deficiencies in California’s medical and mental-health care systems. That factual determination matters, because the PLRA authorizes population limits when “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right” and “no other relief will remedy the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii). A population cap is therefore not an independent policy choice; it is the remedial mechanism Congress expressly permitted when overcrowding itself is the constitutional problem. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 511-12.

Nor is the order overbroad. The court set a ceiling of 137.5 percent of design capacity only after concluding that lesser measures had failed over many years. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 531-32. The Supreme Court in Plata upheld that same remedy, explaining that the PLRA “does not prevent a court from entering orders necessary to remedy constitutional violations,” id. at 530, and that “some relief will be denied only where it is impossible to fashion a remedy that meets the PLRA requirements,” id. at 526. The population-reduction order here falls well within that authority because it leaves California discretion over how to comply, including transfers, parole adjustments, and other administrative measures. Id. at 537-38.

The State’s contrary reading would collapse the PLRA into a rule against effective relief. That is not the statute Congress enacted, and it is not the law the Supreme Court has applied. See Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 531 (“The record demonstrates that the State cannot comply with the order without taking action to reduce prison population.”); Gilmore, 220 F.3d at 999-1000. When unconstitutional conditions persist and the State has failed repeatedly to cure them, a court may order the population reduction necessary to make the Constitution a reality. Hutto, 437 U.S. at 687; Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 511-12.

Accordingly, the order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions does not violate the PLRA. It is the narrow, necessary, and least intrusive means of curing an Eighth Amendment violation caused by overcrowding. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), (a)(3)(E); Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 530-38.
