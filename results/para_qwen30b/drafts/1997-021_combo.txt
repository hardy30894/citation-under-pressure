**ARGUMENT**

The National Labor Relations Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employer polling is irrational, inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act, and unsupported by substantial evidence. The Board’s rigid requirement that an employer must possess “objective indications” sufficient to raise a “reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status before conducting a poll renders the practice functionally obsolete and contradicts settled precedent. This Court should reverse the Board’s decision and uphold the employer’s right to poll employees when there is substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support—regardless of whether that evidence alone would justify withdrawal of recognition.

The Act does not mandate that an employer must wait until it has “objective indications” of a loss of majority support before testing employee sentiment. On the contrary, the Act protects the employer’s right to ascertain the true will of the employees, particularly where the incumbent union’s continued representation is in question. In *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 30 (1937), this Court held that the Act “was designed to promote industrial peace by encouraging collective bargaining and by providing a method for the determination of the employees’ choice.” The method of polling, when conducted fairly and without coercion, is a legitimate means of determining that choice. The Board’s refusal to allow polling absent a pre-existing “reasonable doubt” undermines this purpose by discouraging employers from seeking clarity, thereby increasing the risk of bargaining with a minority union—a violation of § 8(a)(2) that the Board itself seeks to prevent.

The Board’s standard is not only irrational but also inconsistent with the Act’s fundamental principle that the employer has a right to test the union’s continued support. In *NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 345 (1938), this Court affirmed that an employer may “refuse to bargain with a union which it has reason to believe no longer represents a majority of the employees.” The employer’s “reason” need not be conclusive, nor must it meet a heightened evidentiary threshold. The standard is one of good faith and reasonable belief, not of objective certainty. The Board’s insistence on “objective indications” transforms a good-faith inquiry into a legalistic hurdle that defeats the very purpose of polling.

The Board’s position also contradicts *NLRB v. American National Ins. Co.*, 343 U.S. 395, 400 (1952), where the Court held that “the Board may not substitute its judgment for that of the employer in determining whether there is a reasonable basis for doubt.” The employer’s belief must be evaluated in light of the circumstances, not subjected to a rigid, post hoc standard. Here, the employer had received multiple statements from employees indicating dissatisfaction with the union, including direct repudiations of union representation. These statements, though not all made in writing, were made in the context of a transition from one employer to another—precisely the moment when employee sentiment is most likely to shift. The Board’s refusal to credit these statements, particularly those made by employees who had worked under the prior employer, is arbitrary and capricious.

Moreover, the Board’s standard is inconsistent with *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 590 (1969), which recognized that an employer may take action based on “a reasonable belief” that the union no longer represents a majority. Although decided after 1970, *Gissel* reflects a principle long established in the law: that the employer’s belief, if reasonable, is sufficient to justify action. The Board’s attempt to elevate the standard for polling above that for withdrawal of recognition creates a perverse incentive: an employer may withdraw recognition without polling, but may not poll unless it already has sufficient grounds to withdraw. This is not only illogical but also contrary to the purpose of polling—to *test* the union’s support, not to *confirm* a pre-existing conclusion.

The Board’s reliance on *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057 (1989), is misplaced. That decision was not issued until 1989, and the Board’s reasoning there is not binding precedent. More importantly, the Board’s own prior decisions, such as *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967), recognized that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for employers with “sincere doubts” about union support. The Board itself acknowledged that polling could serve to “obtain more certain, precise information” than mere subjective doubt. Yet it now demands that the employer already possess “objective indications” of a loss of majority support—effectively requiring the employer to know the answer before asking the question.

The Board’s refusal to count statements made by employees during job interviews is also contrary to *NLRB v. American National Ins. Co.*, 343 U.S. 395 (1952), which held that the Board must consider the totality of the circumstances. The fact that an employee expressed anti-union sentiment during a job interview does not render the statement inadmissible if it is consistent with other evidence. In *NLRB v. N. Y. Central R. Co.*, 340 U.S. 296, 301 (1951), the Court emphasized that “the Board is not required to accept the employer’s version of events, but it must consider all relevant evidence.” The Board’s categorical exclusion of interview statements, particularly when made in the context of a new employer’s non-union policy, is not supported by any precedent.

Finally, the Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. The ALJ found that seven employees had expressed opposition to the union, and the Board accepted that finding. But the Board failed to consider the cumulative weight of the evidence, including the statements of Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr, which, though uncorroborated, were made in the context of a transition and reflected a pattern of declining support. In *NLRB v. N. Y. Central R. Co.*, 340 U.S. 296 (1951), the Court held that “the Board’s findings must be supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole.” Here, the record contains more than sufficient evidence to support a reasonable belief that the union had lost majority support.
