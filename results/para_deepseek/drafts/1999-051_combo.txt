**ARGUMENT**

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed. Under long-settled precedent of this Court, a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may serve as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim because the fundamental right to counsel, when effectively denied, vitiates any procedural default attributable to that denial.

**I. The Right to Effective Counsel Is Fundamental and Its Denial Cannot Constitute a Deliberate Bypass of State Procedures.**

This Court has repeatedly held that the right to counsel is essential to a fair trial. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963). That right extends to a first appeal as of right. *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353, 357 (1963). When a habeas petitioner’s failure to raise a claim on direct appeal results from constitutionally ineffective appellate counsel, that failure is not a “deliberate bypass” of state procedures. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 439 (1963). In *Fay*, this Court held that a federal habeas court may entertain a claim despite a state procedural default unless the petitioner “deliberately by-passed the orderly procedure of the state courts.” *Id.* A default caused by ineffective counsel cannot be deliberate because the petitioner—who lacked effective legal representation—cannot be charged with a knowing, voluntary waiver. *See Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443, 451 (1965) (Harlan, J., concurring) (waiver requires “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”). *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), similarly held that the right to counsel “is too fundamental” to permit an inference of waiver absent deliberate conduct by the defendant.

**II. A Procedural Default of the Ineffective Assistance Claim Itself Does Not Bar Its Use as Cause.**

The right to effective counsel is so fundamental that a procedural default of a claim alleging its denial must itself be excused when the petitioner had no meaningful opportunity to raise it earlier. In *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963), this Court recognized that federal habeas courts must consider claims where state processes were “inadequate.” Where a petitioner lacked counsel or had ineffective counsel during state post-conviction proceedings, the default of the ineffective assistance claim cannot be charged to the petitioner. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464–65 (lack of counsel renders subsequent proceedings invalid unless waived); *see also Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 113 (1935) (state may not use procedural rules to “shelter” a conviction obtained through constitutional violation). Moreover, *Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1, 15 (1963), instructs that habeas petitions should be denied only after “careful consideration” of the merits, not on the basis of procedural technicalities that obscure substantial constitutional claims.

**III. Federal Habeas Corpus Is a Flexible Remedy That Overrides State Procedural Rules When Fundamental Rights Are at Stake.**

This Court has long emphasized that federal habeas corpus is a “remedy available to a prisoner who has been deprived of a constitutional right.” *Fay*, 372 U.S. at 395. In *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 458 (1953), the Court held that “the jurisdiction of the federal courts on habeas corpus is not restricted by state court proceedings” and that the writ reaches “any detention in violation of constitutional right.” *Price v. Johnston*, 334 U.S. 266, 283 (1948), stated that habeas should be administered with “the utmost flexibility” to ensure justice. Requiring a petitioner to show that an ineffective assistance claim is itself free from procedural default before it can serve as cause would impose a rigid rule incompatible with the historic flexibility of the writ. *Cf. Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932) (holding that the duty to provide counsel includes a duty to provide effective representation, and that failure to do so denies the defendant a fair trial).

**IV. Adopting Respondent’s Position Avoids an Unconstitutional Catch-22.**

A rule that bars a petitioner from using a procedurally defaulted ineffective assistance claim as cause would create an insurmountable trap: the very counsel whose ineffectiveness caused the default of the underlying claim also failed to preserve the ineffective assistance claim itself. This Court has never countenanced such a result. In *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. at 71, the Court held that “the failure of the trial court to give the accused reasonable time and opportunity to secure counsel” required reversal. So too here, the state cannot rely on a procedural default that its own constitutional violation produced. *See Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. at 113.

For these reasons, the court of appeals correctly held that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. The judgment should be affirmed.
