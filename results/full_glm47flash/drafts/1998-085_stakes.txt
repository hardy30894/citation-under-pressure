**ARGUMENT**

**I. Punitive Damages Under Title VII Require a Higher Standard Than Simple Liability**

Punitive damages are not available for every violation of Title VII. To warrant such an award, an employer must act with "malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This standard requires conduct that is "substantially more blameworthy" than the intent required for liability. *Barber v. Time, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 428, 431 (1968). The Supreme Court has consistently held that "the availability of punitive damages is not a matter of right, but depends upon the circumstances of the case." *Barber*, 390 U.S. at 431. Consequently, a finding of intentional discrimination is insufficient to support a punitive damages award; the plaintiff must demonstrate that the employer's conduct rose to the level of "malice" or "reckless indifference."

**II. Congress Intended a High Threshold for Punitive Damages Under Title VII**

Congress intended to impose a high threshold for punitive damages under Title VII. The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 reflects a clear intent to limit punitive damages to "extraordinarily egregious cases." Senator Dole noted that punitive damages are available only in such cases. 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). This legislative history demonstrates that Congress did not intend to lower the bar for punitive damages to the level of simple liability. *See* *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 252 (1994) (legislative history is relevant to statutory interpretation). The requirement of "malice" or "reckless indifference" is a direct reflection of this congressional intent to ensure that punitive damages are reserved for the most egregious conduct.

**III. The Evidence Does Not Support a Finding of "Malice" or "Reckless Indifference"**

Here, the evidence does not support a finding of "malice" or "reckless indifference." While the jury found that the ADA's proffered reasons for the promotion were pretextual, *Barbour v. Merrill*, 48 F.3d 1270, 1277 (D.C. Cir. 1995), such a finding is insufficient to sustain a punitive damages award. The ADA's decision to promote Mr. Spangler was a business judgment based on the qualifications of the candidates, not a desire to harm Kolstad. The record shows that the ADA followed its internal procedures and that the decision was made prior to the formal posting of the position. There is no evidence that the ADA acted with the requisite "malice" or "reckless indifference" to Kolstad's federally protected rights. Therefore, the D.C. Circuit erred in remanding this case for a trial on punitive damages.
