**ARGUMENT**

**The Double Jeopardy Clause Bars Retrial of the Prior-Conviction Allegation**

The Double Jeopardy Clause was not written merely to prevent a second trial on the ultimate charge of guilt; it was written to prevent the government from making repeated attempts to prove the facts that expose a citizen to punishment. As this Court explained in *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 173 (1874), "the Constitution was designed as much to prevent the criminal from being twice punished for the same offence as from being twice tried for it." That principle controls here, for California seeks precisely what *Lange* forbids: a second opportunity to establish, through a second proceeding, the very fact—the prior conviction—on which an enhanced term of imprisonment depends.

The animating concern of the Clause is the one this Court identified in *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184 (1957): "the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity." *Id.* at 187–88. Petitioner Monge faces exactly that ordeal. Having once put on its proof of the 1992 assault conviction and lost on appeal for insufficiency of the evidence, the State now demands a second chance to marshal better evidence of the same historical fact. The anxiety, expense, and risk of erroneous conviction that *Green* condemned attach with equal force whether the fact to be proved determines guilt or determines the length of a sentence doubled and enhanced under the Three Strikes law.

That the Court of Appeal's ruling rested on insufficiency of the evidence, rather than on a jury verdict, does not diminish its double jeopardy consequence. This Court has long treated a determination that the evidence was legally insufficient as the functional equivalent of an acquittal, immune from a second attempt at proof. In *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), the Court held that "the verdict of acquittal was final, and could not be reviewed, on error or otherwise, without putting him twice in jeopardy, and thereby violating the Constitution." The same rule barred appellate reconsideration of an acquittal in *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100 (1904), and this Court reaffirmed, decades later, that even a legally erroneous judgment of acquittal forecloses further proceedings on the same factual question. *Fong Foo v. United States*, 369 U.S. 141 (1962). A finding that the prosecution failed to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Monge personally used a deadly weapon in 1992 is no different in kind from a finding that the prosecution failed to prove an element of a charged offense. Under *Ball*, *Kepner*, and *Fong Foo*, the government does not get a second turn at bat merely because the first swing missed.

Nor can California escape this result by labeling the proceeding a "sentencing" hearing rather than a "trial." This Court has already recognized that where a hearing to determine facts bearing on punishment carries the "hallmarks" of a trial, constitutional trial protections apply in full. In *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605 (1967), the Court refused to permit a sentence-enhancing determination to proceed without the basic procedural safeguards of confrontation, cross-examination, and the right to be heard, because such a proceeding was "a new finding of fact" that exposed the defendant to greater punishment. The California prior-conviction proceeding bears every one of the characteristics this Court has treated as trial-like: the prosecution must prove the allegation beyond a reasonable doubt, the rules of evidence apply, and the defendant enjoys the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses. Having chosen to clothe this proceeding in the trappings of a criminal trial, the State cannot simultaneously strip it of the trial's most fundamental protection—the bar against being twice placed in jeopardy for the same allegation.

This result follows naturally from *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784 (1969), which held the double jeopardy guarantee "fundamental to the American scheme of justice" and binding on the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *id.* at 796, and from *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711 (1969), which reiterated that the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." *Id.* at 717. Once a reviewing court has concluded that the State's proof was legally insufficient to establish the prior-conviction allegation, a second prosecution of that same allegation is a second prosecution "after acquittal" within the meaning of *Pearce*.

Respondent's reliance on *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), is misplaced. *Stroud* permitted a harsher sentence on retrial only because the defendant's own successful appeal reopened the entire proceeding on his motion, and the jury—not an appellate finding of evidentiary insufficiency—determined the facts anew. Here, Monge did not seek a new trial on the prior-conviction allegation; the Court of Appeal held the State's proof inadequate as a matter of law. That holding is the equivalent of an acquittal, and *Stroud* does not authorize the State to relitigate it.

For these reasons, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation, and the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
