**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state-based legal malpractice claims that require the application of federal patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between federal patent policy and the constitutional balance of state and federal judicial authority. The Supreme Court of Texas correctly concluded that federal jurisdiction is not exclusive in this case. The Court of Appeals erred in affirming dismissal based on the Federal Circuit’s precedents in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), which disregard the Supreme Court’s definitive framework for determining when a state-law claim “arises under” federal law. Under *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363, 162 L.Ed.2d 257 (2005), federal jurisdiction exists only if four conditions are met: (1) resolution of a federal issue is necessary to the state-law claim; (2) the federal issue is actually disputed; (3) the federal issue is substantial; and (4) federal jurisdiction will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. The Court of Appeals failed to apply this test with fidelity, and the Supreme Court of Texas correctly reversed.

**I. The Federal Issue Is Not Necessary to the Malpractice Claim**

The first prong of the *Grable* test requires that the resolution of a federal issue be “necessary” to the state-law claim. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. Here, the malpractice claim does not depend on the resolution of any federal patent law question. Minton’s claim is not that the patent is valid or invalid, but that his attorneys failed to raise a defense—specifically, the experimental use exception—that could have preserved the patent’s validity. The existence of that defense, however, is not a legal or factual prerequisite to the malpractice claim. As the Supreme Court has held, “a suit does not so arise [under federal law] unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends.” *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569, 32 S.Ct. 704, 56 L.Ed. 1205 (1912). The outcome of Minton’s malpractice suit depends not on whether the experimental use exception applies, but on whether Gunn’s failure to raise it was professionally negligent and caused harm. That inquiry is governed entirely by Texas common law of legal malpractice, which requires proof of duty, breach, causation, and damages. *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex.2004). The federal patent law issue is not a “necessary element” of the malpractice claim; it is merely a factual backdrop.

**II. The Federal Issue Is Not Actually Disputed**

The second *Grable* factor requires that the federal issue be “actually disputed.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. Here, the experimental use exception was never raised in the underlying patent litigation, and the Federal Circuit affirmed the district court’s denial of reconsideration precisely because the defense was not timely asserted. *Minton v. Nat’l Ass’n of Sec. Dealers, Inc.*, 336 F.3d 1373, 1379–81 (Fed. Cir. 2003). The issue was not “actually disputed” in the sense required by *Grable*—it was not litigated, contested, or even presented to the court. The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept* rests on the premise that the patent issue is “substantial and contested,” but that premise is false here. The experimental use exception was not a viable defense at the time of the original litigation, and Minton’s new counsel did not raise it until after the case was dismissed. The fact that the issue was later considered by the Federal Circuit does not transform it into a “disputed” issue for purposes of *Grable*. The Supreme Court has made clear that federal jurisdiction cannot be based on hypothetical or post hoc disputes. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313, 125 S.Ct. 2363.

**III. The Federal Issue Is Not Substantial**

The third *Grable* factor requires that the federal issue be “substantial.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. The experimental use exception is not a “substantial” federal issue in this context. It is a narrow, fact-intensive defense that applies only when the primary purpose of a sale is experimental, not commercial. *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Here, the lease agreement with Stark explicitly permitted Stark to use TEXCEN for commercial purposes—opening brokerage accounts and executing trades—and Minton himself touted its utility for business. The record shows no evidence that the lease was intended for experimental use. The issue is not one of broad national significance or uniformity in patent law enforcement. Rather, it is a narrow, fact-bound question that does not threaten the integrity of federal patent policy. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against expanding federal jurisdiction to cases where the federal issue is “merely incidental.” *Gully v.
