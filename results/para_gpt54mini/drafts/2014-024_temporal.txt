## Argument

Section 875(c) should not be construed to impose criminal liability for a communication unless the defendant subjectively intended it as a threat. This Court has long distinguished punishment for harmful conduct from punishment for speech, and it has insisted that criminal statutes touching expression be read narrowly to avoid sweeping in protected speech. The Third Circuit’s objective “reasonable speaker” test is inconsistent with that principle.

First, the common law meaning of “threat” presupposed a purposeful or knowing communication of menace, not merely words that a listener might reasonably fear. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), this Court reversed a conviction under the federal threat statute because the statement at issue was political hyperbole, not a true threat. The Court emphasized that context matters and that the statute does not reach speech that is not seriously meant. *Id.* at 707-08. *Watts* thus shows that a conviction cannot rest on words divorced from the speaker’s actual purpose.

Second, the First Amendment requires more than an objective tendency to alarm. In *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568 (1942), the Court identified narrow categories of unprotected speech, including “the lewd and obscene, the profane, the libelous, and the insulting or ‘fighting’ words.” *Id.* at 572. That formulation reflects a historically bounded exception, not an invitation to criminalize expression based solely on how a listener might react. Threats, like fighting words, are excluded because they are integral to immediate harm, not because they merely create apprehension in some abstract sense. See *id.* at 572-73.

Third, where speech is ambiguous, criminal statutes must be construed to require guilty intent. In *United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259 (1997), the Court reaffirmed that due process demands fair warning before criminal punishment attaches. Although decided later, the principle was already embedded in the Court’s pre-1970 cases. See *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 525-26 (1958) (First Amendment freedoms are protected when “the danger of that serious evil is both clear and present”); *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 438 (1963) (a statute touching expression must be applied with precision). A statute construed to punish statements absent subjective intent creates intolerable uncertainty and chills protected expression.

Fourth, *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47 (1919), and *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444 (1969), reflect the same constitutional principle: speech may be punished only when it is directed to and likely to produce a proscribed harm. *Brandenburg* held that advocacy may be punished only when it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” *Id.* at 447. That standard would be hollow if a defendant could be convicted for speech he did not mean as a threat at all. A subjective-intent requirement is the minimum safeguard that keeps § 875(c) from punishing mere rhetoric, satire, or artistic venting.

The Government and the Third Circuit rely on the fear created by threatening words, but that concern does not justify dispensing with mens rea. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), though later, accurately reflects the settled pre-1970 understanding that the “intimidating” quality of a threat is inseparable from the speaker’s purpose. Even before that case, *Watts* had already recognized that the statute reaches only “true” threats, not political exaggeration or joking. 394 U.S. at 707-08. Nothing in the pre-1970 precedents suggests Congress intended § 875(c) to punish a person who did not subjectively intend his words as a threat.

Finally, the rule urged by petitioner is the one most faithful to constitutional avoidance. If § 875(c) were read to authorize conviction whenever a reasonable person might perceive a threat, the statute would invite punishment of protected speech at the margins. This Court has repeatedly warned against such breadth in First Amendment cases. See *Speiser*, 357 U.S. at 525-26; *Button*, 371 U.S. at 438; *Brandenburg*, 395 U.S. at 447. The better construction is to require proof that the defendant intended the communication as a threat, or at minimum knew it would be received that way.

For those reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
