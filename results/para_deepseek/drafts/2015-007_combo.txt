ARGUMENT

I. Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee.

The Sixth Amendment, as applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, requires a jury to find every fact necessary to establish the elements of a criminal offense. *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145, 149 (1968). But this Court has long recognized that the jury’s role does not extend to every factual determination bearing on punishment. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 252 (1949), the Court held that a sentencing judge may consider “the widest possible scope of information” about a defendant’s life and background, even if that information was not presented to the jury. The judge’s independent factfinding at sentencing does not invade the jury’s province because the jury’s function is to determine guilt, not to dictate the precise sentence. *Id.* at 250–51.

Florida’s scheme follows this model. The jury renders an advisory verdict on whether death is appropriate, but the ultimate sentencing decision rests with the judge. The jury need not unanimously find aggravating circumstances or recommend death by a unanimous vote. This Court has never required jury unanimity on sentencing recommendations. In *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740, 752 (1948), the Court held that a federal capital jury’s recommendation of mercy “is not a verdict” and need not be unanimous. The same logic applies here: the jury’s advisory recommendation is not a verdict on an element of the offense, and the Constitution does not demand unanimity.

Moreover, the States retain broad discretion to structure their capital sentencing procedures. *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554, 564 (1967) (“The States are free to allocate functions between judge and jury as they see fit.”). Florida’s allocation—allowing the judge to find aggravating factors and to impose death after considering the jury’s non-unanimous recommendation—falls well within that discretion. The Sixth Amendment does not require that every fact bearing on punishment be found by a jury, let alone by a unanimous jury.

II. Florida’s scheme does not violate the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment.

The Eighth Amendment, as interpreted by this Court, prohibits punishments that are “barbaric” or “excessive” in relation to the crime. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 368–69 (1910). But the Amendment does not prescribe a particular method for determining whether a defendant is mentally retarded or otherwise ineligible for execution. In *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958), the Court recognized that the Eighth Amendment draws its meaning from “evolving standards of decency,” but it left to the States the task of implementing those standards through their own procedural rules.

Florida has done precisely that. The State requires a defendant claiming mental retardation to prove it by clear and convincing evidence to the trial court. The jury is not required to make that determination because mental retardation is not an element of the offense; it is a sentencing factor that the judge may evaluate. This Court has consistently held that the judge, not the jury, may decide such factual questions at sentencing. *Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576, 585 (1959) (sentencing judge may consider prior convictions not proven to the jury). The Eighth Amendment does not command a different result.

Nor does the Eighth Amendment require jury unanimity in the sentencing recommendation. The “heightened reliability” demanded in capital cases, *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932), is satisfied by the judge’s independent review of the evidence and the jury’s advisory input. Florida’s scheme provides multiple layers of judicial scrutiny: the trial court must weigh aggravating and mitigating factors, and the Florida Supreme Court conducts proportionality review. These safeguards ensure that the death penalty is imposed only in the most deserving cases, consistent with the Eighth Amendment.

Finally, the determination of mental retardation is a complex medical and legal question best resolved by the judge, who can consider expert testimony and make findings of fact. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966), recognized that competency determinations are for the judge, not the jury. The same reasoning applies to mental retardation. The Eighth Amendment does not require that a jury decide this issue.

III. Conclusion

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth and Eighth Amendments as understood by this Court’s precedents decided before 1970. The jury’s advisory role, the judge’s independent factfinding, and the absence of a unanimity requirement are all consistent with the Constitution. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
