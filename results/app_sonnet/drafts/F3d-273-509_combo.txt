**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE 1988 RELEASE DOES NOT REACH BANCORP’S CLAIMS BECAUSE THOSE CLAIMS DID NOT ARISE FROM THE DECISION TO CLOSE THE BANK**

The release Bancorp executed discharged the FDIC only "from any and all claims which Bancorp ... has or may have arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985." It says nothing about the FDIC's subsequent conduct *as receiver*—its self-payment of post-insolvency interest, its charging of legal and office expenses to the estate, or its accounting failures. A release, like any other negotiated instrument, must be read according to what the parties actually contemplated when they signed it, not expanded to cover matters neither party had in mind. The New York Court of Appeals made this precise point in *Cahill v. Regan*, 5 N.Y.2d 292 (1959), holding that a general release is not a bar to claims that were outside the contemplation of the parties at the time it was executed. Here, the 1988 release was negotiated to settle the *Chuang* and *May 1986* litigation—disputes over the OCC's insolvency determination and Chuang's law-office claims. Nothing in that settlement concerned the FDIC's later administration of the receivership, conduct which in large part had not yet even occurred (the FDIC's first self-payment of interest was not made until March 15, 1991, nearly three years after Bancorp signed the release). A release cannot discharge claims that had not yet accrued and that the parties could not have contemplated. The District Court therefore erred in construing the release to bar claims squarely outside its four corners.

**II. THE STATUE OF LIMITATIONS DOES NOT BAR BANCORP'S CLAIMS BECAUSE THE FDIC'S FIDUCIARY DUTY AS RECEIVER CONTINUED UNTIL THE RECEIVERSHIP TERMINATED**

Even apart from the release, the District Court erred in holding Bancorp's claims time-barred under N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7). The FDIC, as receiver, stood in a fiduciary relationship to the Bank's estate and to Bancorp as residual claimant, and it is a settled principle of equity that limitations do not run against a beneficiary while that fiduciary relationship subsists and has not been repudiated. In *Michoud v. Girod*, 45 U.S. (4 How.) 503 (1846), the Supreme Court held that a trustee cannot invoke the statute of limitations against the beneficiary so long as the trust relation continues and no open repudiation has occurred. The Court reaffirmed this rule in *Speidel v. Henrici*, 120 U.S. 377 (1887), explaining that where a trust is admitted to exist and has not been denied, "lapse of time is no bar" to the beneficiary's claim. The FDIC never repudiated its role as receiver of the Bank's estate; to the contrary, it continued administering the estate—collecting assets, paying itself interest, and charging expenses—until it formally terminated the receivership on November 1, 1995. Bancorp's claims for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and waste all concern the FDIC's conduct *during* that continuing receivership, and under *Michoud* and *Speidel* the limitations clock could not begin to run until the fiduciary relationship ended.

This conclusion is reinforced by the ordinary accrual rule that a cause of action does not accrue, and limitations do not begin to run, until the claim is complete. See *Kirby v. Lake Shore & Michigan Southern R.R. Co.*, 120 U.S. 130 (1887) (statute begins to run only when the right to bring suit is complete). Bancorp's right to a full and final accounting of the receivership estate—the predicate for evaluating whether the FDIC's self-payments and expense charges were proper—did not mature until the receivership closed. Bancorp filed this action on October 31, 1995, one day before that termination. Its claims were therefore timely no matter how the six-year period is measured from the events themselves, because the cause of action was not "complete" until the receivership's end.

To the extent the FDIC's self-dealing was not disclosed to Bancorp until late in the receivership, the limitations period was independently tolled. In *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342 (1874), the Supreme Court held that where a defendant's own conduct has kept a plaintiff in ignorance of a cause of action, the statute does not begin to run until discovery. The FDIC's decision to pay itself post-insolvency interest beginning in 1991, without notice to Bancorp, falls within this rule.

**III. BANCORP'S UNDERLYING CLAIMS ARE MERITORIOUS**

The FDIC's practice of charging its own legal and office expenses to the receivership estate for its own benefit, while paying itself interest ahead of any residual distribution to Bancorp, is precisely the sort of self-dealing that courts of equity have long condemned as waste of corporate or estate assets. In *Rogers v. Hill*, 289 U.S. 582 (1933), the Supreme Court held that corporate payments bearing no reasonable relation to the value received may constitute a waste of assets remediable by the shareholders on whose behalf the assets are held. The FDIC, administering the Bank's estate for the ultimate benefit of Bancorp as residual claimant, was bound by no less exacting a standard—one consistent with the duties long imposed on bank fiduciaries. See *Bank of the United States v. Dandridge*, 25 U.S. (12 Wheat.) 64 (1827). And the equitable principle underlying Bancorp's unjust enrichment claim is as old as the doctrine itself: one who has received money that in equity and good conscience belongs to another is "obliged by the ties of natural justice and equity to refund the money." *Moses v. Macferlan*, 2 Burr. 1005, 97 Eng. Rep. 676, 681 (K.B. 1760).

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
