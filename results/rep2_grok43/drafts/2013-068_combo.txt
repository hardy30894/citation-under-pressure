The judgment below should be affirmed. The challenged statute regulates the place of expressive activity on public sidewalks adjacent to reproductive health care facilities. Such regulation is permissible when it serves significant governmental interests in public safety and the orderly flow of traffic. Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (recognizing authority to impose “reasonable restrictions” on the time, place, and manner of speech “in order to conserve the public convenience”).

The statute is content-neutral on its face. It applies equally to all persons and does not turn on the ideas expressed. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940) (distinguishing regulations that “have no element of religious discrimination” from those that do). Its exemptions for clinic employees, patients, and emergency personnel are likewise neutral; they address conduct rather than viewpoint. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 571-572 (1942).

The thirty-five-foot fixed buffer zones are narrowly drawn to prevent obstruction at entrances and driveways. They leave open ample alternative channels, including oral advocacy, signs, literature, and prayer from positions immediately outside the marked arcs. Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77, 87 (1949) (upholding sound-truck restrictions that “do not forbid all” forms of communication); Feiner v. New York, 340 U.S. 315, 320 (1951) (sustaining limits that preserve “the opportunity to speak” elsewhere). Petitioners remain free to approach patients before they enter the zones and to display messages visible from within them.

These restrictions parallel measures long sustained to protect access to public buildings and to prevent congestion. Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395, 405 (1953); Cox v. Louisiana, 379 U.S. 536, 554-555 (1965); Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). The zones do not constitute a prior restraint; they operate only after enactment and require no discretionary licensing. Shuttlesworth v. Birmingham, 394 U.S. 147, 150-151 (1969).

Petitioners’ claim that the zones unduly burden their preferred method of “sidewalk counseling” does not render the statute invalid. The First Amendment does not guarantee the most effective means of expression in every location. Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District, 393 U.S. 503, 514 (1969). The statute leaves “the right to communicate” intact, Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939), while advancing the State’s interest in unobstructed clinic access. The First Circuit correctly held the Act constitutional as applied.
