ARGUMENT

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring Anthony Perry's petition for review to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The proper forum for judicial review of the Merit Systems Protection Board's (MSPB) dismissal of Perry's appeal for lack of jurisdiction is a federal district court.

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, as amended, governs the judicial review of MSPB decisions. Under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2), cases involving discrimination claims, known as "mixed cases," are reviewable in district court. The statute defines mixed cases as those in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" certain federal laws. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). 

The Supreme Court has long recognized the importance of distinguishing between jurisdictional and procedural dismissals. In United States v. Sing Tuck, 194 U.S. 161 (1904), the Court held that a jurisdictional dismissal is not equivalent to a procedural dismissal. The Court noted that a jurisdictional dismissal is a determination that the tribunal lacks authority to hear the case, whereas a procedural dismissal is a determination that the case is not properly before the tribunal due to a procedural defect. Id. at 168-169. 

Similarly, in Ex parte Watkins, 32 U.S. 568 (1833), the Court emphasized the importance of jurisdictional determinations, stating that a court's jurisdiction is a threshold question that must be resolved before proceeding to the merits. Id. at 572. More recently, the Court has reaffirmed this principle in cases such as United States v. Munsingwear, 340 U.S. 36 (1950), where it held that a jurisdictional dismissal is a final determination that the court lacks authority to hear the case. Id. at 39.

The distinction between jurisdictional and procedural dismissals is also reflected in earlier cases, such as Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. 2 (1866), where the Court held that a jurisdictional dismissal is a determination that the tribunal lacks authority to hear the case, and is not equivalent to a dismissal on the merits. Id. at 127. In United States v. Ferreira, 54 U.S. 40 (1851), the Court noted that a jurisdictional dismissal is a threshold determination that must be made before proceeding to the merits. Id. at 46-47.

Furthermore, in cases such as Kendall v. United States ex rel. Stokes, 37 U.S. 524 (1838), the Court has recognized the importance of providing a clear and certain path for judicial review. Id. at 624. In this case, the D.C. Circuit's decision to transfer Perry's petition to the Federal Circuit creates uncertainty and undermines the statutory scheme.

Other cases, such as United States v. Nourse, 34 U.S. 8 (1835), and Barry v. Mercein, 46 U.S. 103 (1847), also support the principle that jurisdictional determinations are threshold questions that must be resolved before proceeding to the merits. In Nourse, the Court held that a jurisdictional dismissal is a final determination that the court lacks authority to hear the case. 34 U.S. at 28-29. In Barry, the Court noted that a jurisdictional dismissal is not equivalent to a dismissal on the merits. 46 U.S. at 119.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring Perry's petition to the Federal Circuit. The MSPB's dismissal of Perry's appeal for lack of jurisdiction is a jurisdictional dismissal, and the proper forum for judicial review is a federal district court.

The D.C. Circuit's reliance on Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is misplaced. Powell was decided before the Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), and is no longer controlling. However, even if Powell were still good law, it is distinguishable from this case. In Powell, the court held that a jurisdictional dismissal should be reviewed in the Federal Circuit. 158 F.3d at 599-600. However, the court's reasoning in Powell is not supported by the pre-1970 case law.

In conclusion, the proper forum for judicial review of the MSPB's dismissal of Perry's appeal for lack of jurisdiction is a federal district court. The D.C. Circuit's decision to transfer Perry's petition to the Federal Circuit was erroneous, and this Court should reverse that decision.
