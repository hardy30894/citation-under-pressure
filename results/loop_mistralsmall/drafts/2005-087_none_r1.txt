**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant an independent due process right to present expert mental-state evidence solely to negate criminal intent, separate and apart from an insanity defense. Arizona’s rule confining such evidence to the insanity framework is consistent with due process because it preserves the State’s legitimate authority to define crimes and allocate burdens of proof without transgressing constitutional limits.

A defendant’s right to present evidence is not unbounded; it is subject to state rules of admissibility and relevance. *State v. Chapple*, 135 Ariz. 282, 286 (1983) (citing *People v. Molineux*, 168 N.Y. 264, 296 (1901)). Arizona has long required that criminal intent be proven by the State beyond a reasonable doubt, while structuring its insanity defense as the exclusive vehicle for introducing expert mental-state testimony. *State v. Schantz*, 98 Ariz. 200, 205 (1965). This rule does not violate due process because it neither prevents the defendant from raising an insanity defense nor bars him from offering relevant evidence in support of that defense. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 797 (1952) (upholding Oregon’s requirement that the defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt).

The Due Process Clause does not compel states to permit defendants to repurpose statutorily authorized mental-state evidence for an unrecognized purpose. *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937) (holding that due process secures only those rights “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty”). Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert evidence aligns with the principle that states retain broad authority to define crimes and defenses. *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581, 594 (1900) (“The States have power to prescribe the rules of evidence in their courts, and to regulate the mode of ascertaining the truth in judicial proceedings.”). The trial court did not arbitrarily exclude relevant evidence; it merely confined expert testimony to the statutorily prescribed insanity defense. *State v. White*, 118 Ariz. 12, 15 (1977) (upholding exclusion of psychiatric testimony on mens rea where defendant failed to raise insanity defense).

Furthermore, the Due Process Clause does not require states to permit duplicative litigation of the same mental condition under different legal theories. *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 701 n.29 (1975) (citing *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 797) (noting that states may allocate burdens of proof in criminal cases). Arizona’s rule prevents jury confusion and ensures that expert evidence is considered within the statutorily defined framework of the insanity defense. *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554, 560 (1967) (upholding Texas’s bifurcated trial system for determining guilt and punishment).

This Court has consistently recognized that the Due Process Clause does not impose uniform procedural rules on the states. *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934) (“Due process is not a technical conception with a fixed content unrelated to time, place and circumstances.”). Arizona’s approach is consistent with the traditional division of authority between judge and jury in criminal cases. *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930) (“The essentials of a fair trial—notice, confrontation, and a hearing—remain inviolate.”). The trial court’s ruling did not deprive Clark of a fair trial; it merely enforced Arizona’s longstanding rule that expert mental-state evidence may be used only to support an insanity defense.

In sum, Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert mental-state evidence does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment. The State has a legitimate interest in defining the scope of its insanity defense and allocating burdens of proof, and its rule does not offend any fundamental principle of due process. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 101 (1908) (“The privileges and immunities of the citizen of the United States, as distinguished from those of a citizen of a State, are those which owe their existence to the Federal government, its national character, its Constitution, or its laws.”).
