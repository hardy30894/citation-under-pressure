# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION OF THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT SATISFIES THE INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TEST

Article I vests "[a]ll legislative powers herein granted" in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. But this Court has long recognized that this vesting clause does not prevent Congress from enlisting the assistance of coordinate branches when it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The Court has explained that its application of this test has "been driven by a practical understanding that in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives." *Id.*

Section 811(h) readily satisfies this standard. Congress specified the precise goal the Attorney General must pursue—avoidance of "an imminent hazard to the public safety"—and enumerated the specific factors he must consider: the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). Congress further constrained the delegated authority by imposing procedural safeguards: notice must be published in the Federal Register, the Secretary of Health and Human Services must be consulted, and the temporary scheduling automatically expires after one year, extendable by only six months. *Id.* § 811(h)(2), (4). These constraints mirror those this Court found sufficient in *Mistretta*, where Congress specified "the goals the Commission must aim for, the final product of the delegation, specific factors the Commission must consider, and limits on the Commission's power." 488 U.S. at 374-79.

Petitioners' reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In *Panama Refining*, the statute "did not declare any policy as to the transportation of the excess production, did not qualify the President's authority, and did not establish any criterion to govern the President's course." 293 U.S. at 415. Section 811(h) does precisely what that statute failed to do: it declares a policy (preventing imminent hazards to public safety), qualifies the Attorney General's authority (limiting it to the enumerated factors), and establishes clear criteria to govern his course.

No heightened non-delegation standard applies merely because the delegated authority touches criminal enforcement. This Court has consistently upheld congressional delegations across a wide range of regulatory contexts touching public health and safety, including drug regulation itself. *See Minnesota ex rel. Whipple v. Martinson*, 256 U.S. 41, 45 (1921) (recognizing that regulation of "dangerous and habit-forming drugs" through delegated authority is "too firmly established to be successfully called in question"). The complexity and rapidly evolving nature of the designer-drug problem—where, as the record demonstrates, clandestine chemists exploit the "inevitable lag time" in permanent scheduling procedures to synthesize new analogs faster than Congress can legislate—is exactly the kind of "rapidity of changes in the matter being regulated" that justifies delegation. *See Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 424 (1944).

## II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL WAS AUTHORIZED TO SUBDELEGATE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA

Petitioners' subdelegation challenge fares no better. Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Act. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). Pursuant to that authority, the Attorney General promulgated 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), assigning to the DEA Administrator the "[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended." This regulation was properly promulgated through notice-and-comment rulemaking and falls squarely within the statutory grant of subdelegation authority.

Petitioners' argument that § 811(h)'s temporary scheduling function was somehow excluded from this general subdelegation authority finds no support in the statutory text. Where Congress has intended to withhold subdelegation authority for particular functions, it has said so explicitly, as this Court recognized in analyzing analogous statutory schemes. *See Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 363-64 (1942) (noting that "[t]he entire history of the legislation controlling the use of subpoenas by administrative officers indicates a Congressional purpose not to authorize by implication the delegation" of certain powers where Congress specifically enumerated which powers could be delegated and which could not). No comparable indication of congressional intent to withhold subdelegation exists here. Section 871(a)'s broad delegation authority, unqualified by any exception for temporary scheduling, demonstrates that Congress contemplated exactly this kind of administrative flexibility—entrusting the specialized expertise of the DEA, the agency charged with day-to-day enforcement of the Act, to make the time-sensitive determinations that temporary scheduling demands.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
