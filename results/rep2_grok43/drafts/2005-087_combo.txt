The Fourteenth Amendment does not afford a defendant a right, independent of an insanity defense, to introduce expert evidence of mental disease for the purpose of negating mens rea. States possess wide authority to prescribe the elements of offenses and the contours of affirmative defenses. Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 110-111 (1908); Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581, 598 (1900). Due process requires only that a criminal proceeding be fundamentally fair, a standard satisfied when the state permits a defendant to litigate insanity as a complete defense. Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937); Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934).

Arizona law channels evidence of mental incapacity exclusively to the insanity defense, on which the defendant bears the burden. This allocation is consistent with due process. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952). The same principle permits the state to define the mental element of murder without obliging the prosecution to disprove every species of mental abnormality. Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895); Hotema v. United States, 186 U.S. 413, 417 (1902).

A defendant has no constitutional right to present evidence in whatever form or for whatever purpose he chooses. Adamson v. California, 332 U.S. 46, 55 (1947). The trial court’s limitation therefore comports with the settled understanding that the Constitution does not constitutionalize every rule of evidence or procedure. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 68 (1932); Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455, 462 (1942). Because Clark received a full opportunity to litigate his insanity claim, the exclusion of the same evidence from the mens rea determination did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment.
