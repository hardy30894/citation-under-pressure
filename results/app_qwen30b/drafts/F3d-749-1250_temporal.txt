**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in admitting the testimony of FBI Special Agent Charles Jones as an expert on deception and truthfulness, thereby violating Stanley Hill’s constitutional right to a fair trial under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. The admission of Agent Jones’s opinion testimony—based on subjective, unscientific, and inherently prejudicial observations of demeanor and speech—constituted plain error under Federal Rule of Evidence 103(d), and the error was so fundamental as to undermine the integrity of the judicial process.

Under the well-established principles of *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969 (D.C. Cir. 1972) (en banc), cert. denied, 411 U.S. 935 (1973), and *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 980 (D.C. Cir. 1972), the admission of expert testimony must be grounded in scientific or technical knowledge that is both reliable and relevant. The Supreme Court in *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923), established the foundational standard for admitting expert testimony: the scientific method or principle must be “generally accepted in the particular field to which it belongs.” This standard remains binding and controlling in this Circuit, as reaffirmed in *United States v. Hines*, 177 F.2d 857 (10th Cir. 1949), cert. denied, 339 U.S. 947 (1950).

Agent Jones’s testimony failed to meet even the most basic threshold of reliability under *Frye*. He claimed to possess expertise in “identifying deception in statements and truths in statements,” yet offered no scientific basis for his conclusions. His assertions—that evasiveness is “common among the criminal element,” that a statement of willingness to die rather than face charges is “never seen with an innocent person,” and that invoking faith to validate truthfulness is a sign of deception—were not derived from any recognized scientific method or empirical study. These are not the kind of “generalized principles” that have achieved “general acceptance” in the field of psychology, linguistics, or criminal investigation. Indeed, the *Frye* court itself rejected the admissibility of lie detector tests, which are far more systematically developed than the subjective observations offered by Agent Jones. See *Frye*, 293 F. at 1014.

Moreover, the testimony was not only unreliable but also inherently prejudicial. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 980 (D.C. Cir. 1972), emphasized that “the danger of prejudice outweighing probative value” is a critical consideration in the admission of expert testimony. Agent Jones’s opinion that Stanley’s statements “did not make sense” and that his invocation of God was a sign of deception directly invaded the jury’s role as the sole judge of credibility. As the Court held in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d at 980, “the jury is the ultimate arbiter of credibility,” and expert testimony that usurps that function is improper. The jury was thus misled into believing that a federal agent had scientifically determined Stanley’s guilt based on demeanor and speech—factors that are notoriously unreliable and subject to misinterpretation.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Hines*, 177 F.2d 857 (10th Cir. 1949), is misplaced. In *Hines*, the court upheld the admission of testimony from a police officer who had observed the defendant’s conduct during a chase and concluded he was “trying to escape.” That case involved a lay witness’s observation of physical behavior in a real-time situation, not an expert’s opinion on the psychological meaning of speech patterns or religious references. The distinction is critical: *Hines* did not involve the admission of an expert opinion on deception, nor did it endorse the use of subjective, unscientific judgments about truthfulness. The testimony here was not merely a lay observation; it was presented as expert opinion, and thus subject to the stricter *Frye* standard.

The failure to object at trial does not waive the error because the admission of Agent Jones’s testimony constituted plain error under Rule 103(d). The Supreme Court in *United States v. Carver*, 328 U.S. 495 (1946), held that “a trial court may not permit the jury to be misled by evidence that is so prejudicial and so lacking in probative value that it undermines the fairness of the trial.” The testimony here was not only lacking in probative value—it was actively misleading. It suggested that Stanley’s emotional responses and religious expressions were evidence of guilt, a conclusion that is not only unsupported by science but also contrary to the constitutional presumption of innocence.

The error was not harmless. The jury was left with the impression that a federal agent had concluded Stanley was lying, despite the absence of any physical evidence linking him to the robbery. The government’s case rested heavily on circumstantial evidence, and the jury’s decision was thus vulnerable to the influence of improper expert opinion. As the Court stated in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d at 980, “when the jury is misled by expert testimony that is not grounded in accepted scientific principles, the result is a trial that is fundamentally unfair.”

For these reasons, the admission of Agent Jones’s testimony violated the principles of *Frye*, *United States v. Brawner*, and *United States v. Hines*, and constituted plain error that deprived Stanley Hill of a fair trial. The judgment must be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
