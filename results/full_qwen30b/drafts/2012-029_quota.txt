**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state-based legal malpractice claims that require the application of federal patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case—must be answered in the affirmative. The Supreme Court of Texas erred in concluding that federal jurisdiction is not triggered merely because the patent issue is not the “primary” issue in the malpractice suit. Under *Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09, 108 S. Ct. 2166, 100 L. Ed. 2d 811 (1988), federal jurisdiction arises under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) when a plaintiff’s state-law claim “necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” This standard is not contingent on the prominence of the patent issue in the overarching litigation but on whether the resolution of that issue is *necessary* to the malpractice claim. Here, the experimental use exception is not merely incidental—it is the *central factual and legal predicate* of Minton’s malpractice claim. Without a federal determination of whether the TEXCEN lease constituted experimental use, Minton cannot prove causation, the essential element of his claim.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal jurisdiction under § 1338(a) extends to state-law claims that “necessarily depend” on federal patent law. In *Christianson*, the Court held that “a case arises under federal law when the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” 486 U.S. at 809, 108 S. Ct. 2166. This principle applies with equal force to malpractice claims arising from patent litigation. In *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed. Cir. 2007), the Federal Circuit affirmed exclusive federal jurisdiction over a legal malpractice suit where the plaintiff’s claim turned on whether a patent was valid under the on-sale bar—a question of federal patent law. The court emphasized that “establishing patent infringement is a necessary element of a malpractice claim stemming from alleged mishandling of earlier patent litigation,” and that such claims “arise under” federal law. Id. at 1273. Minton’s claim is indistinguishable: his malpractice claim hinges entirely on whether the experimental use exception could have saved the patent from invalidation under § 102(b). Without a federal court’s resolution of that issue, Minton cannot prove that his attorneys’ failure to raise the exception caused his loss.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314, 125 S. Ct. 2363, 162 L. Ed. 2d 257 (2005), further confirms that federal jurisdiction is proper when a state-law claim “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” Id. Here, all four *Grable* factors are satisfied. First, resolving the experimental use exception is *necessary* to Minton’s malpractice claim: he cannot prove causation without showing that the patent would have survived the on-sale bar had the exception been raised. Second, the issue is *actually disputed*—the Federal Circuit has already ruled that the exception was not timely raised, and the validity of the defense remains contested. Third, the issue is *substantial*: the experimental use exception is a well-established doctrine in patent law, and its application determines whether a patent is valid. See *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Finally, federal jurisdiction does not disturb the balance of federal and state responsibilities. As the Court noted in *Grable*, “federal courts are well equipped to handle such claims,” and “the federal interest in uniformity of patent law is substantial.” 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S. Ct. 2363. Allowing state courts to resolve patent validity issues in malpractice suits would undermine that uniformity.

The dissent’s reliance on *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 384 (5th Cir. 2008), is misplaced. *Singh* did not address a malpractice claim arising from patent litigation and thus is inapposite. Moreover, the Fifth Circuit’s later decision in *USPPS, Inc. v. Katten Muchin Rosenman LLP*, 647 F.3d 273 (5th Cir. 2011), explicitly adopted the *Air Measurement* framework and rejected the notion that federalism concerns preclude federal jurisdiction. See id. at 282. The court held that “the federal interest in uniformity of patent law is paramount,” and that “federal courts are the appropriate forum to resolve patent-related malpractice claims.” Id. at 281. The same reasoning applies here.

Other courts have consistently upheld federal jurisdiction in similar circumstances. In *New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 702 N.W.2d 336, 341 (Neb. 2005), the Nebraska Supreme Court held that “when patent issues are merely implicated incidentally in a cause of action, federal courts do not have jurisdiction.” But here, the patent issue is not incidental—it is the *core* of the malpractice claim. In *E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 189 Cal. App. 4th 1140, 1152, 117 Cal. Rptr. 3d 516 (2010), the California Court of Appeal rejected federal jurisdiction, but only because the patent issue was not substantial.
