ARGUMENT

I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE, BY ITS TERMS, PROTECTS AGAINST BEING TWICE PUT IN JEOPARDY FOR THE “SAME OFFENCE.” SENTENCING PROCEEDINGS, PARTICULARLY THE DETERMINATION OF PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATIONS FOR SENTENCE ENHANCEMENT, DO NOT INVOLVE A SEPARATE “OFFENCE.” THE CLAUSE DOES NOT APPLY.

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. The plain text limits the prohibition to “offence”—a term that refers to the substantive criminal charge, not to the factual findings that inform the sentence. A prior conviction allegation is not a separate offense; it is a sentencing factor that authorizes an increased punishment for the crime of which the defendant has already been convicted. The Double Jeopardy Clause therefore does not bar retrial of such an allegation.

II. THE SUPREME COURT HAS CONSISTENTLY HELD THAT THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE DOES NOT CONSTRAIN SENTENCING DETERMINATIONS IN NONCAPITAL CASES.

In *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), the defendant was convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to life imprisonment. After the Supreme Court reversed the conviction, the defendant was retried, convicted again, and sentenced to death. This Court held that the death sentence did not violate double jeopardy. “The defendant was not again put in jeopardy for the same offense,” the Court explained, because the reversal “opened the way for a new trial of the entire case, including the question of the punishment to be inflicted.” *Id.* at 20. The sentencing determination was part of the original jeopardy. If the Clause does not bar a wholly new trial and a harsher sentence, it cannot bar a limited resentencing proceeding.

In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court emphasized that sentencing is a distinct phase of the criminal process, not a trial on guilt. The Court rejected the argument that the Due Process Clause requires full trial procedures at sentencing, noting that “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” *Id.* at 251. If due process does not require trial-like protections at sentencing, the Double Jeopardy Clause—which is even more specifically tied to the concept of “offence”—cannot require that a sentencing hearing be treated as a separate jeopardy.

In *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711 (1969), the Court considered whether the Double Jeopardy Clause bars imposition of a more severe sentence upon reconviction after a successful appeal. The Court held that it does not. “We hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar the imposition of a more severe sentence upon reconviction,” the Court stated, because the original jeopardy had not terminated. *Id.* at 719. If the Clause does not bar a harsher sentence after a full retrial, it certainly does not bar retrial of a sentencing factor alone.

III. THE COURT’S DECISION IN *SPECHT v. PATTERSON* CONFIRMS THAT DOUBLE JEOPARDY DOES NOT APPLY TO SENTENCING ENHANCEMENT PROCEEDINGS.

In *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605 (1967), the Court considered a Colorado statute that required a separate hearing to determine whether a defendant should be sentenced under the Sex Offenders Act. The Court held that due process required notice and an opportunity to be heard, but it did not extend double jeopardy protection to that hearing. The Court distinguished the proceeding from a trial on guilt, noting that the statute “does not make the commission of a specified crime the basis for sentencing.” *Id.* at 608. The absence of any double jeopardy analysis in *Specht* confirms that the Clause has no application to such sentencing determinations.

IV. THE CALIFORNIA COURT OF APPEAL ERRONEOUSLY RELIED ON CAPITAL SENTENCING CASES DECIDED AFTER 1970. NO PRE-1970 DECISION OF THIS COURT EXTENDS DOUBLE JEOPARDY TO A NONCAPITAL SENTENCING ENHANCEMENT PROCEEDING.

The California Court of Appeal applied *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), both of which are capital cases decided after 1970. Those cases are not controlling here. The Court has never held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings. In *Stroud*, *Williams*, and *Pearce*, the Court repeatedly declined to extend the Clause to sentencing. The fact that a state elects to provide trial-like procedures for the determination of prior convictions—as California has done by statute—does not transform that proceeding into a “jeopardy” for a separate offense. The state can grant procedural protections as a matter of state law without invoking the federal Double Jeopardy Clause.

V. EVEN IF SOME PROCEDURAL PROTECTIONS ARE REQUIRED AT SENTENCING, THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE IS NOT AMONG THEM.

The state may, as a matter of state law, provide for a jury trial and a reasonable-doubt standard for prior conviction allegations. But the Double Jeopardy Clause is a constitutional guarantee that applies only to “offence.” Because the sentencing enhancement is not a separate offense, the Clause does not bar retrial of the prior conviction allegation. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be affirmed.
