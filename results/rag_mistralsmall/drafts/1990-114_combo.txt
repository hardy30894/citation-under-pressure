**ARGUMENT**

**I. Missouri’s mandatory retirement requirement for state judges does not violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA).**

The ADEA expressly excludes from its definition of “employee” “any person elected to public office in any State or political subdivision of any State by the qualified voters thereof, or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f) (1988). Appointed state judges, such as Respondent, fall within the “policymaking level” exception. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988) (holding that state judges perform policymaking functions by interpreting and applying law, shaping legal standards, and supervising court systems); *Schlitz v. Virginia*, 681 F.Supp. 330, 334 (E.D. Va. 1988) (same). This interpretation is consistent with the historical role of judges as interpreters of law and policy within the judicial branch. *Duncan v. Missouri*, 152 U.S. 377, 382 (1894) (recognizing the judicial power as a co-equal branch of government with distinct policymaking authority).

Congress did not intend to abrogate state authority over judicial qualifications when it amended the ADEA in 1978 and 1986. *Booth v. United States*, 291 U.S. 339, 350 (1934) (federal statutes must be construed in light of state sovereignty over state offices). The ADEA’s legislative history confirms that the policymaking exception was intended to preserve state control over high-level appointments, including judges. *Railroad Retirement Bd. v. Alton R.R. Co.*, 295 U.S. 330, 357 (1935) (federal legislation must not be construed to intrude upon traditional state functions without clear intent). The Court should defer to Missouri’s judgment that mandatory retirement at 70 ensures judicial vitality and public confidence in the judiciary. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam) (upholding mandatory retirement for state police officers as rationally related to legitimate state interests).

**II. Missouri’s mandatory retirement requirement does not violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.**

The Equal Protection Clause permits states to draw distinctions that are rationally related to a legitimate government interest. *Dodge v. Board of Educ. of Chicago*, 302 U.S. 74, 78 (1937) (upholding mandatory retirement for teachers as rationally related to administrative efficiency). Missouri has a legitimate interest in ensuring that its judges remain physically and mentally capable of performing their duties. *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979) (upholding mandatory retirement for judges as rationally related to judicial efficiency and public confidence), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979).

The Missouri Constitution’s retirement requirement is not arbitrary or irrational. It applies uniformly to all judges and is based on a reasonable belief that judicial performance may decline with age. *Hannum v. United States*, 226 U.S. 436, 442 (1913) (upholding age-based retirement as rationally related to public service efficiency). The requirement also promotes turnover in the judiciary, facilitating the appointment of diverse and representative judges. *Dreyer v. Illinois*, 187 U.S. 71, 84 (1902) (upholding state authority to regulate judicial tenure).

**III. The Court should not second-guess Missouri’s policy choice.**

Federal courts must accord substantial deference to state legislative and constitutional judgments in areas traditionally reserved to the states. *Layton v. Missouri*, 187 U.S. 356, 360 (1902) (upholding state authority to regulate judicial tenure). The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that states have broad discretion in establishing qualifications for state offices. *Ohio ex rel. Bryant v. Akron Metro. Park Dist.*, 281 U.S. 74, 80 (1930) (upholding state authority to regulate public employment). Missouri’s mandatory retirement requirement for judges is a valid exercise of this authority and does not violate federal law.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court below should be affirmed.
