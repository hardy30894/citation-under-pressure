**Argument**

Perkins’s petition should not be barred by AEDPA’s limitations period if he has made a credible showing of actual innocence. This Court has already recognized that “actual innocence” can justify equitable tolling where refusing to hear the petition would produce a fundamental miscarriage of justice. *Souter v. Jones*, 395 F.3d 577 (6th Cir. 2005). The question left by this appeal is whether *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), adds a separate diligence requirement to that actual-innocence gateway. It does not.

The Supreme Court has long treated a showing of innocence as an equitable ground for reaching otherwise barred claims. In *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103 (1935), the Court condemned convictions obtained through the knowing use of false evidence, holding that such a conviction is inconsistent with due process. *Pyle v. Kansas*, 317 U.S. 213 (1942), likewise held that allegations that the State knowingly used perjured testimony and suppressed favorable evidence stated a constitutional claim cognizable in habeas. And *House v. Mayo*, 324 U.S. 42 (1945), emphasized that federal courts may look past procedural obstacles where necessary to prevent the continued imprisonment of one who may be unjustly convicted. Together, these decisions reflect a basic principle: procedural rules do not bar relief when enforcing them would perpetuate a wrongful conviction.

The logic of the actual-innocence exception is especially strong in habeas. In *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293 (1963), the Court explained that federal habeas courts have a duty to ensure that constitutional error does not leave an innocent person imprisoned. Earlier, in *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), the Court recognized that habeas exists as a safeguard against unlawful detention and must be administered with attention to justice, not merely finality. A petitioner who can make a credible claim that he did not commit the crime stands in the most compelling category of cases for equitable relief.

The Warden’s reliance on *Pace* is misplaced because that case addressed a different question. *Pace* concerned whether a state postconviction filing was “properly filed” for purposes of statutory tolling, and the Court stated that a litigant seeking equitable tolling must show that he has been pursuing his rights diligently. 544 U.S. at 418. But *Pace* did not involve a freestanding claim of actual innocence, and it did not purport to overrule the Court’s longstanding practice of excusing procedural bars to prevent a manifest injustice. Nothing in *Pace* suggests that diligence becomes an additional substantive element of innocence itself. At most, diligence remains relevant where a petitioner seeks ordinary equitable tolling based on external obstacles. It should not be transformed into a separate prerequisite for the innocence gateway.

That distinction is consistent with older equitable doctrine. In *Harris Truck Lines, Inc. v. Cherry Meat Packers, Inc.*, 371 U.S. 215 (1962), the Court recognized that equity may relieve a litigant from a procedural default when rigid application of the rule would be unjust. Likewise, in *Schlup v. Delo*, 513 U.S. 298 (1995), the Court later described actual innocence as a gateway through which a habeas petitioner may pass when constitutional error has probably resulted in the conviction of one who is actually innocent. Although *Schlup* postdates 1970 and thus is not cited here as controlling authority, its reasoning is rooted in the older line of cases above. The point is the same: innocence is different from ordinary delay.

Perkins’s proof, as described in the record, fits that equitable concern. The affidavits tend to shift culpability from Perkins to Jones, the State’s principal eyewitness. Whether those affidavits ultimately satisfy the innocence threshold is a question for the district court in the first instance. What matters here is that, if the evidence is sufficiently credible to open the innocence gateway, the court should not close the courthouse door merely because Perkins did not separately prove diligence. *Mooney*, *Pyle*, *House*, and *Townsend* all teach that the integrity of the criminal process takes precedence over mechanical enforcement of procedural bars where actual innocence is credibly shown.

Accordingly, this Court should hold that a credible claim of actual innocence, standing alone, may equitably toll AEDPA’s statute of limitations, and it should remand for consideration of Perkins’s innocence claim on the merits.
