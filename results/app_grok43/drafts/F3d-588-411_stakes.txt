**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in furnishing the jury a supplemental definition of “shielding” that was both untethered to the statutory text and broader than the conduct Congress proscribed. The evidence was likewise insufficient to prove that Ye acted with the specific intent to prevent detection by the government.

**A. Supplemental Jury Instruction**

A district court’s duty is to instruct the jury on the law; it may not supply definitions that enlarge the elements Congress enacted. See *United States v. Santos*, 553 U.S. 507, 511 (2008) (courts must “give effect to every clause and word of a statute”). When the jury requested definitions of “concealing” and “shielding,” the court was obliged to decline or, at minimum, to adhere to the ordinary meaning of those terms as used in 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). Instead, it adopted a non-statutory formulation drawn from other circuits that permitted conviction upon any act that “facilitated the aliens’ continued unlawful presence,” without requiring proof that the act was undertaken to avoid detection. That formulation finds no support in the text, which pairs “shielding from detection” with “concealing” and “harboring,” all of which presuppose affirmative steps to keep the aliens’ presence hidden from authorities. See *Almendarez-Torres v. United States*, 523 U.S. 224, 228 (1998) (statutory terms must be read in context).

Because Ye timely objected, the error is reviewed for abuse of discretion. *United States v. Johnson*, 529 U.S. 53, 58 (2000). An instruction that misstates an element of the offense is reversible unless the government can show the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 15 (1999). The government cannot make that showing here: the jury’s request for definitions demonstrates that it was focused on the precise scope of the term, and the instruction given allowed conviction on facts that amounted only to employment and housing, not concealment from detection.

**B. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

Even under the government’s view of the statute, the evidence failed to prove that Ye harbored or shielded the aliens “for the purpose of commercial advantage or private financial gain” by preventing their detection. 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(B)(i). A conviction may stand only if, “after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). The record shows that Ye hired the workers, paid them in cash, leased apartments, and submitted incomplete I-9 forms. These acts establish employment and financial gain, but they do not establish that Ye took any step calculated to keep the aliens’ presence unknown to immigration authorities. To the contrary, the evidence reveals repeated contacts with government agents in which Ye made no effort to hide the workers’ existence. Because no rational jury could find the requisite intent to shield from detection, the conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) must be reversed.
