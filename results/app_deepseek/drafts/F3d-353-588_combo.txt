## ARGUMENT

### I. The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Epps’s ADA and MHRA claims because a genuine issue of material fact exists as to whether Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled.

Summary judgment is improper when the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the non-movant, reveals a genuine dispute over a material fact. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). Where the ultimate issue involves motive or intent, summary judgment is particularly disfavored. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 628 (1944). Here, Epps presented evidence that Chief Hardy considered him “unable to perform his duties” because of his degenerative disk disease, a chronic condition Hardy explicitly linked to Epps’s inability to bend, stoop, or carry a gun belt. Hardy’s March 2000 memorandum concluded that Epps “would never be able to function as a regular officer.” This evidence, viewed in Epps’s favor, creates a triable issue whether Pine Lawn regarded Epps’s impairment as substantially limiting the major life activity of working—i.e., precluding him from a broad class of jobs. *Baxter v. Savannah Sugar Refining Corp.*, 350 F.2d 62, 66 (5th Cir. 1965) (discrimination inferred when employer acts on subjective beliefs about physical limitations). The district court improperly resolved this factual dispute against Epps.

### II. The district court erred in holding that sovereign immunity bars Epps’s workers’ compensation retaliation claim under Mo. Rev. Stat. § 287.780.

Missouri’s Workers’ Compensation Act is a remedial statute that must be liberally construed. *Hanna v. Missouri Public Service Co.*, 321 S.W.2d 726, 728 (Mo. 1959). Section 287.780 provides a cause of action against any employer who discharges an employee for filing a workers’ compensation claim. Pine Lawn, as a municipal corporation, is an “employer” under the Act. *State ex rel. City of Green Oaks v. Barker*, 417 S.W.2d 928, 931 (Mo. 1967) (city subject to workers’ compensation law). By enacting § 287.780, the Missouri legislature waived sovereign immunity for this specific statutory tort. *State ex rel. Delmar Gardens v. City of St. Louis*, 351 S.W.2d 717, 720 (Mo. 1961) (municipal immunity may be waived by statute). The district court’s blanket application of sovereign immunity disregards this clear legislative intent. Epps introduced evidence that Chief Hardy noted Epps “had filed quite a few workers’ compensation claims” and recommended termination shortly thereafter. This temporal proximity, combined with Hardy’s reliance on Epps’s claim history, suffices to withstand summary judgment. *Bowe v. Colgate-Palmolive Co.*, 416 F.2d 711, 719 (7th Cir. 1969) (circumstantial evidence can establish discriminatory motive).

### III. The district court erred in dismissing Epps’s intentional infliction of emotional distress claim on sovereign immunity grounds.

Missouri recognizes a cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress where a defendant engages in extreme and outrageous conduct that causes severe emotional harm. *Christensen v. Gamm*, 265 S.W.2d 362, 365 (Mo. 1954). Chief Hardy’s repeated recommendations for termination based on Epps’s workers’ compensation history and physical impairments, culminating in a termination after Epps’s surgery, surpasses mere personnel action. An employer who exploits an employee’s vulnerability by terminating him for filing lawful claims may be found to have acted outrageously. *Moffett v. Commerce Trust Co.*, 369 S.W.2d 598, 603 (Mo. 1963) (distinguishing permissible from actionable conduct). Because the same sovereign immunity analysis fails for the reasons stated in Point II, this claim must also be reinstated.

### Conclusion

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings.
