# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Section 1407(a) Unambiguously Commands Remand of Lexecon's Action Once Pretrial Proceedings Concluded

Statutory interpretation begins, and where the text is plain, ends, with the words Congress enacted. *See* Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) ("It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms."). Congress could not have chosen more unequivocal language than that of Section 1407(a): the Panel "shall" remand a transferred action to the transferor court "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a).

This Court has repeatedly held that "shall" is a word of command. *See* Anderson v. Yungkau, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947) (the word "shall" is ordinarily "the language of command"). Where a statute imposes a mandatory duty with a defined temporal trigger, courts may not disregard it as a matter of convenience. *See* Escoe v. Zerbst, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935) (a mandatory statutory command cannot be dispensed with simply because compliance may be inconvenient). Section 1407(a)'s remand command, keyed to "the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings," leaves no room for a transferee court to retain a transferred case through trial.

## II. Section 1404(a)'s General Transfer Authority Does Not Permit a Section 1407 Transferee Court to Override the Specific Remand Mandate

Milberg's position—that Section 1404(a)'s general authority to transfer "any civil action" for convenience, 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a), empowers a Section 1407 transferee court to assign a case to itself for trial—inverts the settled canon that a specific statutory provision controls over a general one addressing the same subject.

In Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp., 353 U.S. 222 (1957), this Court confronted an analogous question: whether the general venue provisions of Sections 1391(c) and 1404(b) could supplement the specific patent venue statute, Section 1400(b). The Court held that the specific provision "is the sole and exclusive provision controlling venue in patent infringement actions" and refused to let the general statute expand it. *Id.* at 228–29. The same logic governs here. Section 1407(a) speaks specifically and exclusively to the timing and mechanism by which a multidistrict-transferred action must return to the transferor court; Section 1404(a) is merely the ordinary, general venue-transfer statute. The general provision cannot be read to swallow the specific one's unambiguous command.

This Court's decision in Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55 (1949), confirms that Section 1404(a) operates as a convenience mechanism functioning *within*, not *against*, a specific statutory scheme. There, the Court permitted use of Section 1404(a) without disturbing the specific venue provisions of the Federal Employers' Liability Act because Section 1404(a) did not purport to override the substantive scheme Congress had already prescribed. *Id.* at 60–61. Here, by contrast, allowing a transferee court to invoke Section 1404(a) to transfer a case to itself does not operate alongside Section 1407(a)'s remand command—it nullifies it, converting a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent one for trial. Nothing in *Collett* licenses that result.

## III. Considerations of Convenience Cannot Justify Judicial Amendment of Section 1407(a)'s Plain Command

The court below effectively rewrote Section 1407(a) to include an unwritten exception permitting a transferee court to retain a case it finds convenient to try. But "[t]o supply omissions transcends the judicial function." Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 251 (1926). Had Congress intended transferee courts to retain multidistrict cases for trial, it would have said so, just as it specified with precision when remand is required. *See* United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, Inc., 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940) (where statutory language is plain, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms" absent an ambiguity yielding an absurd result). No absurdity exists here: Congress rationally chose to permit coordinated pretrial proceedings in a convenient forum while preserving the parties' settled expectations regarding venue for trial itself.

The practice the Ninth Circuit sanctioned—permitting transferee courts to retain multidistrict cases for trial through Section 1404(a)—rests on a judicially manufactured judgment that remand would be inconvenient, not on any textual authority found in Section 1407(a) itself. Convenience is not law. Where, as here, Congress has spoken with unmistakable clarity, the judiciary's role is to enforce that command, not to supplant it with its own view of efficient case management. *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions that it be returned to the Northern District of Illinois for trial.
