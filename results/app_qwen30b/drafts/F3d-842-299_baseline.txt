**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding it had subject matter jurisdiction over this declaratory judgment action. The case presents a quintessential state-law dispute over property rights arising from a flowage easement—precisely the type of matter Congress intended to remain within the exclusive province of state courts. Because no federal question or federal statutory claim is properly before the district court, removal was improper and dismissal on jurisdictional grounds was required. We therefore urge reversal and remand.

I.  
**The District Court Lacked Subject Matter Jurisdiction Under 28 U.S.C. § 1331**

The Pressls’ complaint seeks only a declaration of their rights under Virginia law regarding the use of land subject to a private flowage easement. It does not allege any violation of federal law, nor does it assert a claim arising under the Constitution, laws, or treaties of the United States. See *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 682 (1946) (“[A] case may be removed to federal court only if the plaintiff’s well-pleaded complaint establishes that the claim arises under federal law.”). Here, the sole legal issue is whether APCO’s demand for an occupancy permit exceeds the scope of its flowage easement—a matter governed entirely by Virginia property law and contract interpretation.

The mere fact that APCO operates a hydroelectric project under a FERC license does not transform a purely state-law dispute into a federal one. As this Court has made clear, “the presence of a federal regulatory scheme does not automatically confer federal jurisdiction.” *Barnes v. United States*, 501 F.3d 358, 362 (4th Cir. 2007). In *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs*, 383 U.S. 715, 725 (1966), the Supreme Court emphasized that “a federal question must be presented by the plaintiff’s well-pleaded complaint,” not by the defendant’s assertion of a federal connection. Here, the Pressls did not invoke any federal statute or regulation; they sought only a declaration of their rights under a private easement agreement. Thus, there is no federal question jurisdiction under § 1331.

II.  
**The Federal Power Act Does Not Confer Jurisdiction Under 16 U.S.C. § 825p**

APCO contends that the district court had jurisdiction under 16 U.S.C. § 825p, which provides that “[t]he United States district courts shall have original jurisdiction of all civil actions arising under this chapter.” But this provision applies only to actions “arising under” the Federal Power Act (FPA), not to disputes involving private easements or property rights unrelated to FERC’s regulatory authority.

This Court has consistently held that the FPA’s grant of jurisdiction does not extend to disputes between private parties over the meaning or enforcement of flowage easements. In *Salem v. United States*, 517 F.3d 215, 220 (4th Cir. 2008), we rejected a similar argument: “The FPA does not create a private cause of action for every dispute involving a licensed hydropower project.” Similarly, in *Hill v. Appalachian Power Co.*, 530 F.3d 202, 207 (4th Cir. 2008), we affirmed that “FERC licensing does not automatically vest federal courts with jurisdiction over every dispute related to a hydroelectric facility.” The FPA governs the relationship between FERC and licensees, not private property rights between landowners and utility companies.

Moreover, the language of § 825p itself confirms that jurisdiction attaches only when the claim “arises under” the FPA—not when a party happens to operate under a FERC license. *See* *Gulf Oil Corp. v. Coppock*, 373 U.S. 123, 128 (1963) (“[T]he phrase ‘arising under’ refers to claims that are based upon the existence of a federal right, not merely those connected to a federally regulated activity.”). The Pressls’ claim does not arise under the FPA; it arises under Virginia common law and the terms of a private easement. Therefore, § 825p confers no jurisdiction.

III.  
**Removal Was Improper and the Case Must Be Remanded**

Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, the case must be remanded to state court. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 1447(c) (“If at any time it appears that the district court lacks subject matter jurisdiction, the case shall be remanded.”). Removal based on a colorable federal question is permitted only when the federal claim is substantial and independent. *See* *Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams*, 482 U.S. 386, 393 (1987). Here, no such claim exists. APCO’s reliance on FERC licensing is a mere procedural artifact, not a basis for federal jurisdiction.

Furthermore, the doctrine of *abstention* does not justify federal jurisdiction where none exists. The district court’s suggestion that the Pressls should have first pursued relief through FERC is irrelevant. *See* *Colorado River Water Conservation Dist. v. United States*, 424 U.S. 800, 817 (1976) (“Abstention is not a substitute for jurisdiction.”). Even if FERC were a proper forum for some claims, it is not a mandatory prerequisite for litigation in state court. The Pressls are entitled to seek declaratory relief in state court without first exhausting administrative remedies—especially when no federal agency has jurisdiction over the underlying property dispute.

IV.  
**Conclusion**

The Pressls brought a straightforward state-law claim concerning the construction of a dock on land subject to a flowage easement. No federal question is present. No federal statute creates a cause of action here. The mere fact that APCO holds a FERC license does not confer federal jurisdiction. The district court erred in denying the motion to remand and dismissing the complaint for lack of jurisdiction. That error must be corrected.

For these reasons, we respectfully request that this Court vacate the district court’s order and remand the case to the Virginia circuit court for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
