**ARGUMENT**

**I. A DEFAULTED CLAIM OF INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF APPELLATE COUNSEL MAY SUPPLY "CAUSE" FOR THE DEFAULT OF ANOTHER CLAIM WITHOUT A SEPARATE SHOWING THAT THE INEFFECTIVE-ASSISTANCE CLAIM ITSELF SURVIVES DEFAULT**

**A. Federal Habeas Relief Turns on Whether the Petitioner Himself Deliberately Bypassed State Procedures**

This Court has long held that a state prisoner's failure to comply with state procedural rules bars federal habeas review only where the prisoner, personally, made a considered choice to forgo his state remedy. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court explained that the relevant standard "is a familiar one" borrowed from the law of waiver: an "intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Id.* at 439. Default is excused unless the prisoner "understandingly and knowingly forewent the privilege of seeking to vindicate his federal claims in the state courts," in what the Court described as "the deliberate by-passing of state procedures." *Id.* at 438-39. That inquiry looks to the petitioner's own choice, not to the technical adequacy of counsel's paperwork.

Carpenter made no such choice. He did not decide to forgo a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence; his appointed appellate counsel simply failed to raise it. Under *Fay*, a default produced by counsel's omission—rather than by the client's deliberate election—cannot be treated as a bypass attributable to the prisoner.

**B. Ineffective Assistance of Appellate Counsel Is Itself a Denial of the Very Representation That Fay Presupposes**

This Court has recognized that the right to counsel is not satisfied by the mere presence of an attorney but requires the genuine assistance the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments guarantee. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), held that "lawyers in criminal courts are necessities, not luxuries." *Id.* at 344. *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), extended that guarantee to a defendant's first appeal, holding that without counsel "there is lacking that equality demanded by the Fourteenth Amendment where the rich man ... enjoys the benefit of counsel's examination into the record, research of the law, and marshalling of arguments on his behalf, while the indigent ... is forced to shift for himself." *Id.* at 357-58. And in *Anders v. California*, 386 U.S. 738 (1967), the Court insisted that appointed appellate counsel must function as "an active advocate," *id.* at 744, precisely because an appeal defended by counsel in name only is no different, for purposes of fairness, than an appeal with no counsel at all.

Carpenter's appellate counsel's failure to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence supporting an aggravated-murder conviction is the paradigm of the passive, non-adversarial representation *Anders* condemns. Because that failure deprived Carpenter of the very "equality" *Douglas* demands, the resulting default of the sufficiency claim is not one Carpenter himself brought about. It is state action—or, more precisely, state inaction in providing effective counsel—that produced the default, not any deliberate choice by Carpenter.

**C. Treating the Ineffective-Assistance Claim as Itself Subject to Independent Default Would Convert a Constitutional Safeguard into a Second Procedural Trap**

This Court's decisions confirm that a procedural bar should not be permitted to compound itself against a petitioner who was denied adequate representation at the very moment his rights were being forfeited. In *Reece v. Georgia*, 350 U.S. 85 (1955), the Court refused to apply a state timeliness rule to bar a claim where the petitioner's opportunity to raise it had been foreclosed by the circumstances of his representation, holding that the ordinary procedural rule "cannot be deemed an adequate answer" when it operates in that manner. *Id.* at 90. Similarly, in *House v. Mayo*, 324 U.S. 42 (1945), the Court declined to give effect to a state procedural forfeiture where the prisoner lacked the assistance necessary to comply with it. *Id.* at 45-46.

These decisions reflect the settled principle, reiterated in *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), that federal habeas corpus exists to guarantee "that state prisoners have a forum in the federal courts for the vindication of their federal rights," *id.* at 500 (opinion of Frankfurter, J.), and in *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293 (1963), that a federal court sitting in habeas must independently ensure that constitutional claims receive a full and fair hearing. *Id.* at 312. To require Carpenter to have separately, and successfully, exhausted his ineffective-assistance claim before it may excuse the default of the sufficiency claim would erect exactly the kind of second procedural trap these cases forbid—penalizing Carpenter again for the same deficiency in representation that caused the original default. As *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443 (1965), makes clear, a state procedural default forecloses federal review only when it rests on a "legitimate state interest," *id.* at 447, and no such interest is served by demanding that a habeas petitioner exhaust a claim whose very subject is his own counsel's failure to protect his rights.

For these reasons, the Sixth Circuit correctly held that Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim could excuse the default of his sufficiency claim without separately surviving procedural default itself.
