**ARGUMENT**

**I. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities within the boundaries of federally administered conservation units.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not withdraw the Park Service’s authority to regulate activities occurring on non-federal lands physically situated within the boundaries of federally administered conservation system units.  The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) preserves federal regulatory authority over “public lands” within conservation system units, and the statute does not purport to limit federal authority over non-federal lands within those same boundaries.  *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (2012) (“Only those lands within the boundaries of [conservation system units] which are public lands (as defined in section 1001.02 of this title) shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit.”).  This construction is consistent with Congress’s plenary authority to regulate activities within the boundaries of federally administered conservation units.  *See Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 540 (1976) (“The power over the public land, including the power to dispose of it and to regulate its use, has been granted to Congress, and the power has been held to be without limitations.”); *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911) (“The right to make all laws necessary and proper for carrying into execution the powers granted to the federal government is expressly given by the Constitution, and the power to regulate the use of federal lands is a necessary and proper means of executing the powers granted to the federal government.”).

**II. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate navigable waters.**

The hovercraft regulation at issue here is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate navigable waters under the Commerce Clause.  *See United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 426 (1940) (“The power of the United States over its waters is not limited to navigation, but extends to the protection and control of the navigable waters of the United States in the interest of navigation, commerce, and public safety.”); *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 196 (1824) (“The power to regulate commerce comprehends the control for that purpose, and to the extent necessary, of all the navigable waters of the United States.”).  The Nation River, a tributary of the Yukon River, is a navigable water of the United States.  *See Alaska v. United States*, 545 U.S. 75, 84 (2005) (recognizing the Nation River as a navigable water of the United States).  Congress’s power to regulate activities on navigable waters includes the power to regulate the use of hovercraft on those waters.  *See United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co.*, 229 U.S. 53, 69 (1913) (“The control of Congress over the navigable waters of the United States is absolute, and extends to all matters affecting the use and enjoyment of such waters.”).

**III. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation does not violate the Property Clause of the Constitution.**

The Property Clause of the Constitution grants Congress the power to “make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States.”  U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2.  This power is plenary and extends to the regulation of activities occurring on non-federal lands within the boundaries of federally administered conservation units.  *See Light v. United States*, 220 U.S. 523, 537 (1911) (“The power of Congress over the public domain is not limited to the disposition of the lands and the fixing of the terms and conditions upon which they may be acquired. It also extends to the protection of the lands and the preservation of their resources.”); *United States v. San Francisco*, 310 U.S. 16, 29 (1940) (“The power of Congress to make all needful rules and regulations for the protection and control of the public lands is not limited to the disposition of the lands and the fixing of the terms and conditions upon which they may be acquired.”).

**IV. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation does not violate the Tenth Amendment.**

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states powers not delegated to the federal government, but it does not limit Congress’s power to regulate activities occurring on non-federal lands within the boundaries of federally administered conservation units.  *See United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1, 67 (1936) (“The Tenth Amendment provides nothing new and did not add to the Constitution. It merely expresses the truism that the federal government is one of delegated powers.”); *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941) (“The amendment states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered. There is nothing in the history of its adoption to suggest that it was more than declaratory of the relationship between the national and state governments as it had been established by the Constitution before the amendment or that its purpose was other than to allay fears that the new national government might seek to exercise powers not granted, and to guarantee to the people and the states the powers not granted to the federal government.”).

**V. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities affecting federal lands.**

Even if the hovercraft regulation is construed as regulating activities occurring on non-federal lands, it is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities affecting federal lands.  *See United States v. Alford*, 274 U.S. 264, 267 (1927) (“The power of Congress to regulate the use of federal lands extends to the protection of those lands and the preservation of their resources, even when the regulated activities occur on non-federal lands.”); *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. at 517 (“The power to regulate the use of federal lands is a necessary and proper means of executing the powers granted to the federal government, and it extends to the protection of those lands and the preservation of their resources.”).

**VI. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities affecting the federal interest in navigable waters.**

Even if the hovercraft regulation is construed as regulating activities occurring on non-federal lands, it is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities affecting the federal interest in navigable waters.  *See United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co.*, 229 U.S. at 69 (“The control of Congress over the navigable waters of the United States is absolute, and extends to all matters affecting the use and enjoyment of such waters, even when the regulated activities occur on non-federal lands.”); *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) at 196 (“The power to regulate commerce comprehends the control for that purpose, and to the extent necessary, of all the navigable waters of the United States and all activities affecting the use and enjoyment of such waters.”).

**VII. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities affecting the federal interest in wildlife conservation.**

Even if the hovercraft regulation is construed as regulating activities occurring on non-federal lands, it is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities affecting the federal interest in wildlife conservation.  *See Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 434 (1920) (“If it is a proper exercise of the national power, it is not rendered invalid by the fact that state laws are in conflict with it, or that state laws embody a different policy.”); *United States v. Shauver*, 214 F. 154, 160 (E.D. Ark. 1914) (“The power of Congress to regulate the taking of game on federal lands extends to the protection of wildlife resources, even when the regulated activities occur on non-federal lands.”).

**VIII. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities affecting the federal interest in public safety.**

Even if the hovercraft regulation is construed as regulating activities occurring on non-federal lands, it is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities affecting the federal interest in public safety.  *See United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. at 517 (“The power to regulate the use of federal lands is a necessary and proper means of executing the powers granted to the federal government, and it extends to the protection of those lands and the preservation of their resources, as well as to the protection of the public safety.”); *United States v. Alford*, 274 U.S. at 267 (“The power of Congress to regulate the use of federal lands extends to the protection of those lands and the preservation of their resources, as well as to the protection of the public safety, even when the regulated activities occur on non-federal lands.”).
