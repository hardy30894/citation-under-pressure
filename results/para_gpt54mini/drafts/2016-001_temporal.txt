**Argument**

The Patent Act’s design-patent damages rule does not authorize a plaintiff to capture an infringer’s profits beyond the value of the patented article itself. Congress provided that a patentee prevailing on a design patent is entitled to the infringer’s “total profit,” but that phrase must be read in light of the statute’s requirement that the patented design be applied to an “article of manufacture.” *Act of Feb. 4, 1887, ch. 105, 24 Stat. 387*; see *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439, 444 (1885) (rejecting recovery of profits not shown to arise from the patented design and explaining that damages must be tied to the value of the thing actually protected). Where the asserted design covers only a component, the relevant “article” is not the entire multicomponent product sold in commerce, but the component embodying the protected ornamentation.

This Court has long refused to award profits untethered to the invention actually patented. In *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10, 17-18 (1886), the Court held that a design patentee could not recover the defendant’s total profits from carpeting absent proof that those profits were attributable to the design itself. The Court reasoned that the patentee must show “what profits” were made “by reason of” the infringement, not merely that the defendant profited from sales of the overall product. *Id.* at 17. Likewise, in *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439, 445-46 (1885), the Court rejected a damages award resting on the defendant’s entire gains from the sale of carpet fabric, emphasizing that the design patent covered only a part of what made the article marketable and that profits must be apportioned to the protected feature.

Those decisions reflect a broader patent principle: a patentee may recover only for the invasion of the monopoly actually granted. *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884), held that when a patented feature is only part of a product, the patentee must present “evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant’s profits and the patentee’s damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features.” The Court explained that, unless the entire value of the product is attributable to the patented feature, the patentee “must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion.” *Id.* That rule was not limited to utility patents in function; it expressed the ordinary measure of patent compensation: recovery for the value actually contributed by what was patented, not the value of unpatented components.

The same limitation follows from *Westinghouse Electric & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Electric & Mfg. Co.*, 225 U.S. 604, 614-15 (1912), where the Court held that profits may be recovered only so far as they are shown to result from the patented invention. If the infringer shows that part of the profit is due to factors other than the patent, the patentee cannot take more than the portion properly attributable to the protected invention. *Id.* at 615. The Court later reaffirmed that “the burden is on the patentee to give evidence separating or apportioning the profits.” *Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co.*, 235 U.S. 641, 647-48 (1915). These cases leave no room for awarding profits generated by features the patent does not cover.

Apple will argue that the design-patent statute’s “total profit” language requires the entire profit on the accused smartphone. But this Court has never interpreted patent damages to permit recovery beyond the patented subject matter itself. *Birdsall v. Coolidge*, 93 U.S. 64, 70-71 (1876), recognized that design protection is for the “appearance” of the article, not its mechanical utility or unrelated features. And in *Smith v. Whitman Saddle Co.*, 148 U.S. 674, 679-80 (1893), the Court explained that a design patent protects the visual impression of the design, not the article in the abstract. A damages rule that sweeps in profits from an entire smartphone simply because it incorporates a patented front-face design would contradict that principle.

Nor does *Mowry v. Whitney*, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 620, 653-54 (1872), support Apple. There the Court emphasized that damages must reflect the value of the patented improvement, not the value of other unpatented elements. Likewise, *Crosby Steam-Gauge & Valve Co. v. Consolidated Safety-Valve Co.*, 141 U.S. 441, 451-52 (1891), refused to treat the entire commercial success of a multi-part device as recoverable when the patent covered only a component feature.

The correct rule, then, is simple: when a design patent covers only a component of a larger product, damages must be limited to the infringer’s profits attributable to that component. That rule respects the statutory focus on the patented “article of manufacture,” conforms to *Garretson*, *Westinghouse*, and *Dowagiac*, and prevents a windfall untethered to the protected design. The judgment below should be reversed.
