**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Massachusetts Buffer Zone Is a Constitutional Time-Place-Manner Regulation**

The Massachusetts statute is a content-neutral regulation of speech in a quintessential public forum. As this Court has long recognized, the government may impose reasonable restrictions on the time, place, and manner of protected expression, provided they are content-neutral, serve a significant governmental interest, and leave open ample alternative channels. *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 160 (1939) (municipalities may “regulate the conduct” in public places “so as to prevent interference with the primary use of the streets and sidewalks”). The Act here does precisely that: it prohibits standing within 35 feet of clinic entrances—a modest spatial restriction—while allowing all forms of speech, including conversations, signs, and prayers, from any point beyond that zone.

**A. The Act Is Content-Neutral**

The law applies to all persons regardless of viewpoint. It exempts only those entering or leaving the facility, employees, emergency responders, and persons passing through—exemptions that are wholly unrelated to the content of any message. This Court has upheld similar neutral regulations. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 555 (1965) (upholding parade permit law because it was “not based on the content of the speech”). The Act’s purpose is to ensure safe access to healthcare, not to suppress any particular opinion. *See Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966) (upholding trespass statute applied to protesters because the regulation was “not aimed at the suppression of speech”).

**B. The Act Serves Significant Governmental Interests**

Massachusetts has compelling interests in protecting patient safety, preventing obstruction, and ensuring unimpeded access to medical facilities. This Court has repeatedly upheld laws that protect the safety and convenience of persons using public spaces. *Cameron v. Johnson*, 390 U.S. 611, 618 (1968) (upholding anti-picketing statute that “merely regulates the use of the streets and sidewalks so as to prevent obstruction of the public ways”). The buffer zone is a modest means to prevent harassment and physical confrontation—harms that this Court has recognized as legitimate targets of regulation. *Feiner v. New York*, 340 U.S. 315, 320–21 (1951) (upholding conviction when speech “tended to incite the crowd to violence”). The Act advances these interests without banning any message.

**C. The Act Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels**

Petitioners remain free to communicate from just outside the 35-foot zone. They may speak, distribute literature, carry signs, pray, and engage in one-on-one counseling. The alternative channels are “ample.” *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 88 (1949) (upholding ban on sound trucks because speakers could “use other methods of communication”). Indeed, the record shows that petitioners have successfully persuaded women to forgo abortions even with the buffer in place. The law does not foreclose any means of expression; it merely moves the location by a few yards. *See Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 576 (1941) (upholding parade license requirement that did not “forbid[] the speaking of anything”).

**D. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored**

The 35-foot radius is the minimum necessary to ensure safe passage. It is far narrower than the 100-foot zone upheld in earlier analogous cases. The Act exempts persons merely passing through, and enforcement requires clear marking and signage. This tailoring respects the principle that regulations must not “substantially burden” speech. *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 406 (1953) (upholding permit requirement for religious meeting in public park when “there was no arbitrary suppression of any particular speech”). Here, the restriction is calibrated to the governmental interest without unnecessarily restricting expression.

**II. The Law Is Consistent with This Court’s Established First Amendment Doctrine**

The petitioners’ claim that the Act is unconstitutional cannot be reconciled with decades of precedent upholding neutral regulations in public forums. From *Schneider* to *Cox* to *Cameron*, this Court has recognized that “the rights of free speech and a free press” under the First and Fourteenth Amendments do not confer a right to obstruct public ways. *Patterson v. Colorado ex rel. Attorney General*, 205 U.S. 454, 462 (1907) (Harlan, J., dissenting) (noting that First Amendment freedoms are protected against state action through the Fourteenth Amendment). The Massachusetts Act falls squarely within the long line of cases permitting reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions. *See also Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515–16 (1939) (opining that streets and parks may be regulated “in the interest of all” to assure “the safety and convenience of the people”).
