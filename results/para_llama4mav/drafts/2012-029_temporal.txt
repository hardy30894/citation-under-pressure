The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Vernon Minton's state-law legal malpractice claim because it necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. The Supreme Court has established that federal jurisdiction extends to cases "in which a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, in that patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims." Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 809 (1988) (citing Franchise Tax Bd. of Cal. v. Constr. Laborers Vacation Trust for S. Cal., 463 U.S. 1, 27-28 (1983)); see also Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897) ("If it appears that some substantial and not merely colorable federal question is necessarily involved in the determination of the cause of action, the jurisdiction is sustained.").

In this case, Minton's legal malpractice claim against his former attorneys requires the application of federal patent law to determine whether the attorneys' alleged negligence in failing to argue the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar cost Minton his patent infringement case. The experimental use exception is a critical aspect of federal patent law, and its application is necessary to resolve Minton's malpractice claim. As the Supreme Court has noted, a suit "does not arise under the federal law 'unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction or effect of [federal law], upon the determination of which the result depends.'" Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Here, the resolution of Minton's malpractice claim depends on the application of federal patent law, specifically the experimental use exception.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has cautioned against opening the "arising under" door too wide, and has instead adopted a "selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 118 (1936). In this case, the federal patent issue is substantial and not merely incidental to Minton's malpractice claim. Therefore, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's claim, and the state court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction to hear the case.

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be affirmed.
