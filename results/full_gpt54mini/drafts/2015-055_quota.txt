**Argument**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. Under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), new substantive rules apply retroactively, while new procedural rules generally do not. A rule is substantive when it “alter[s] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). *Johnson* did exactly that. By invalidating the ACCA’s residual clause as void for vagueness, *Johnson* removed an entire category of offenses from the statute’s reach and narrowed the class of persons eligible for the ACCA’s 15-year mandatory minimum. That is substantive, not procedural. See *Welch v. United States*, 578 U.S. 120, 129-31 (2016).

This Court’s retroactivity precedents compel that result. In *Teague*, the Court explained that new substantive rules include decisions that “narrow the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms” and rules that place “particular conduct or persons covered by the statute beyond the State’s power to punish.” 489 U.S. at 307. *Johnson* fits both descriptions. It invalidated the residual clause because it “produced more unpredictability and arbitrariness than the Due Process Clause tolerates.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. Once the clause was struck down, a sentencing court could no longer rely on it to impose ACCA’s enhanced penalty. That is a change in the permissible reach of punishment, not merely the procedures used to impose it.

The government cannot avoid retroactivity by characterizing *Johnson* as a procedural decision about vagueness doctrine. This Court has repeatedly held that rules that “modif[y] the elements of an offense or alter the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes” are substantive. *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353. In *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620-21 (1998), the Court held that a decision narrowing the interpretation of a federal criminal statute applied retroactively because it changed the substantive reach of the statute. Likewise, in *Fiore v. White*, 531 U.S. 225, 228-29 (2001), the Court recognized that a conviction resting on conduct not criminal under state law cannot stand. *Johnson* similarly made clear that ACCA does not reach defendants whose sentence enhancements depended on the residual clause.

Nor does *Beard v. Banks*, 542 U.S. 406 (2004), help the government. *Beard* applied *Teague* to a rule regulating jury instructions and the allocation of decision-making authority, which was procedural. 542 U.S. at 412-13. *Johnson* is different because it “alter[s] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353. The residual clause’s invalidation means the ACCA enhancement cannot constitutionally be imposed where it was previously available; the sentence is unlawful because the statute, as applied, no longer reaches the defendant’s prior convictions. See *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190, 200-01 (2016) (substantive rules must be given retroactive effect because they carry a “significant risk that a defendant faces a punishment that the law cannot impose upon him”).

The retroactive effect of *Johnson* also follows from the Court’s treatment of void-for-vagueness decisions. In *Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville*, 405 U.S. 156, 162 (1972), the Court explained that vague laws offend due process because they leave people without fair notice and invite arbitrary enforcement. *Johnson* held that the ACCA residual clause suffered from the same constitutional defect. 576 U.S. at 597-602. When a criminal law is invalidated on that ground, the sentencing enhancement it supported cannot constitutionally remain in place. See *Welch*, 578 U.S. at 130-31.

Finally, finality concerns do not override retroactivity here. *Teague* itself recognized that substantive rules apply retroactively because the Constitution forbids the continued punishment of conduct or persons that the law cannot reach. 489 U.S. at 311. That principle is decisive. Welch’s ACCA sentence rests on a clause this Court has declared void. The judgment therefore cannot stand. The Court should reverse and hold that *Johnson* applies retroactively on collateral review.
