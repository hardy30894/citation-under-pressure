# ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status can never satisfy § 1255(a)’s requirement that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled.” Congress specifically provided that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment of status. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This Court’s longstanding precedent confirms that Congress may create “admission” by legal fiction—without requiring a physical border crossing following inspection—and that courts must give that fiction its intended effect.

**I. This Court has long recognized that Congress may deem an alien lawfully admitted through legal fiction rather than physical entry.**

The clearest illustration is § 249 of the INA, construed by this Court in *Mrvica v. Esperdy*, 376 U.S. 560 (1964). That provision allows “[a] record of lawful admission for permanent residence” to “be made in the case of any alien, as of the date of” a prior event, entirely divorced from any actual inspection at the border. *Id.* at 566. If Congress may authorize the Attorney General to manufacture a “record of lawful admission” retroactively, there is no textual or structural reason why Congress could not likewise deem a TPS recipient to have been “admitted” by operation of § 1254a(f)(4)’s command that such an alien be treated as maintaining “lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” Admission, in other words, is not exclusively a physical event; it is frequently a legal conclusion that Congress is free to assign.

That understanding is reinforced by *United States ex rel. Stapf v. Corsi*, 287 U.S. 129 (1932), where the statute at issue directed that an alien seaman unlawfully present be brought before a board of special inquiry “for examination as to his qualifications for admission to the United States.” *Id.* at 131. Admission there was not a self-executing consequence of the physical crossing that had already occurred; it was the product of an administrative determination of qualification—precisely the kind of determination Congress made categorically, in advance, for TPS recipients through § 1254a(f)(4).

**II. Congress’s express statutory command distinguishes this case from precedents denying immigration benefits absent explicit statutory authorization.**

In *Chang Chan v. Nagle*, 268 U.S. 346 (1925), this Court refused to treat marriage to a United States citizen as conferring admission because no statute so provided; the Court emphasized that the wives remained “ineligible to citizenship” and that “[n]otwithstanding their marriage to citizens of the United States they did not become citizens and remained incapable of naturalization.” *Id.* at 352–53. The absence of a statutory hook was fatal. Here, by contrast, Congress supplied precisely the missing statutory hook: § 1254a(f)(4) expressly directs that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” Where *Chang Chan* found no vehicle for converting an alien’s circumstance into lawful immigration status, § 1254a(f)(4) is that vehicle. The Third Circuit’s error was treating this case as governed by the *absence* of statutory authorization that doomed the petitioners in *Chang Chan*, when in fact Congress supplied the very authorization that was missing there.

**III. The parole cases relied upon by the court of appeals are readily distinguishable because they involved no comparable congressional grant of status.**

The Government below leaned on this Court’s parole decisions, but those cases prove petitioners’ point rather than refute it. In *Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185 (1958), and its companion *Rogers v. Quan*, 357 U.S. 193 (1958), this Court held that parole did not render excluded aliens “within the United States” because Congress had not attached that legal consequence to parole; parole remained an act of physical custody, not a status. *Leng May Ma*, 357 U.S. at 187 (parole “did not alter her status as an excluded alien”). Those aliens had received no affirmative statutory grant of lawful nonimmigrant status—unlike Sanchez, who was affirmatively granted TPS pursuant to § 1254a(f)(4)’s command that he be treated as “maintaining[] lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” The absence of any comparable statutory status-conferral in the parole context is precisely why those cases turned out as they did; it is also precisely why they cannot control here.

**IV. Reading § 1254a(f)(4) to satisfy § 1255(a) is consistent with this Court’s approach to discretionary relief provisions designed to ameliorate harsh consequences for aliens with equities in remaining.**

This Court has consistently read remedial immigration provisions to accomplish their evident humanitarian purpose. In *United States ex rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy*, 353 U.S. 72 (1957), the Court recognized that Congress vested the Attorney General with authority to grant relief from deportation where equities—including the interests of American citizen family members—warranted it. *Id.* at 73–74. Similarly, in *Ceballos v. Shaughnessy*, 352 U.S. 599 (1957), the Court examined the statutory prerequisites for suspension of deportation with an eye toward the relief Congress intended to make available to aliens who had established a foothold and equities in this country. TPS serves an analogous humanitarian function—protecting aliens from disaster-stricken homelands—and § 1254a(f)(4) reflects Congress’s judgment that such aliens should not be permanently locked out of the adjustment process by the manner of their original entry.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
