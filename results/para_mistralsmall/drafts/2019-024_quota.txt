**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ninth Circuit Correctly Held That “Actual Knowledge” Under ERISA § 1113(2) Requires Awareness That The Alleged Breach Occurred And That It Was Wrongful.

The plain text of ERISA § 1113(2) provides that an action “may not be commenced more than three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit correctly interpreted this provision to require that a plaintiff have actual knowledge both that the fiduciary acted and that the action was imprudent. *Sulyma v. Intel Corp. Investment Policy Comm.*, 927 F.3d 589, 595 (9th Cir. 2019). This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s guidance that ERISA’s limitations provisions are to be read in light of their remedial purpose. *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 575 U.S. 523, 533 (2015) (“[W]e have been especially ‘reluctan[t] to adopt an interpretation of a congressional enactment which renders some part of it a nullity.’”) (quoting *Ratzlaf v. United States*, 510 U.S. 135, 140 (1994)).

The Ninth Circuit’s construction also aligns with this Court’s precedent in analogous contexts. In *Merck & Co. v. Reynolds*, 559 U.S. 633 (2010), the Court interpreted the discovery rule in securities fraud cases to require “inquiry notice” before the limitations period begins to run. *Id.* at 648 (“[T]he statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff actually discovers the facts constituting the violation or shows that a reasonably diligent plaintiff would have done so.”). Similarly, in *Rotella v. Wood*, 528 U.S. 549 (2000), the Court held that the RICO statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff knows the “critical elements” of the alleged fraud. *Id.* at 556 (“[T]he statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff knows (or has reason to know) of the injury that is the basis of the civil action.”). These cases confirm that actual knowledge requires awareness of the wrongful act, not merely access to information that could have revealed it.

II. The Ninth Circuit Properly Rejected Intel’s Attempt To Equate “Actual Knowledge” With Constructive Notice.

Intel argues that Sulyma’s receipt of disclosures about the investment strategy should suffice to trigger the limitations period. This argument misreads the statute. As the Ninth Circuit recognized, Congress repealed the constructive knowledge provision in 1987, leaving only the actual knowledge requirement. *Sulyma*, 927 F.3d at 597 (citing Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330). The Court has repeatedly cautioned against reviving discarded provisions through judicial interpretation. *Lamie v. U.S. Trustee*, 540 U.S. 526, 538 (2004) (“[W]e will not read a statute to add language that is not there, especially when doing so would override the otherwise plain meaning of the statute.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision is also consistent with this Court’s admonition that limitations periods are to be strictly construed in favor of plaintiffs in ERISA cases. *Tibble*, 575 U.S. at 533 (“[W]e have been especially ‘reluctan[t] to adopt an interpretation of a congressional enactment which renders some part of it a nullity.’”) (quoting *Ratzlaf*, 510 U.S. at 140). If constructive knowledge were sufficient, the three-year limitations period would effectively be reduced to a one-year period, as plan administrators could rely on disclosures to trigger the limitations period regardless of whether the plaintiff actually read or understood them. *See id.* at 534 (rejecting an interpretation that would “shorten the time for bringing suit”).

III. The Ninth Circuit Correctly Held That There Was A Genuine Dispute Of Material Fact As To Whether Sulyma Had Actual Knowledge.

The Ninth Circuit properly deferred to the record evidence showing that Sulyma did not recall seeing the disclosures and was unaware that his retirement funds were invested in alternative assets. *Sulyma*, 927 F.3d at 600. This Court has repeatedly recognized that summary judgment is inappropriate where there is a genuine dispute of material fact. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 247-48 (1986) (“[T]he judge’s function is not ... to weigh the evidence and determine the truth of the matter but to determine whether there is a genuine issue for trial.”). The Ninth Circuit’s decision is consistent with this standard, as it recognized that Sulyma’s testimony created a factual dispute as to whether he had actual knowledge of the alleged breach.

IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Does Not Undermine ERISA’s Purpose Of Promoting Plan Efficiency.

Intel argues that the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation will lead to increased litigation and undermine ERISA’s goal of promoting plan efficiency. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1)(B) (requiring fiduciaries to act “in accordance with the documents and instruments governing the plan insofar as such documents and instruments are consistent with the provisions of this subchapter”). This argument is unpersuasive. As the Ninth Circuit recognized, the limitations period still provides a meaningful check on stale claims. *Sulyma*, 927 F.3d at 595. Moreover, this Court has rejected similar efficiency arguments in the past. *See Varity Corp. v. Howe*, 516 U.S. 489, 512 (1996) (“[W]e are not free to rewrite the statute to reflect a policy judgment that the balance it strikes is too ‘harsh.’”).

V. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Is Consistent With The Decisions Of Other Circuits.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation aligns with the approach taken by the Second, Fourth, and Sixth Circuits, which have all required actual knowledge of the alleged breach to trigger the limitations period. *See, e.g., In re Schering Plough Corp. ERISA Litig.*, 589 F.3d 58, 69 (2d Cir. 2009) (“[A]ctual knowledge requires more than access to the relevant information; it requires awareness of the alleged breach.”); *Brown v. Gen. Dynamics Corp.*, 42 F.3d 1407, 1410 (6th Cir. 1994) (“[A]ctual knowledge means more than the opportunity to acquire knowledge; it means the plaintiff must have known of the alleged breach.”); *Bussian v. RJR Nabisco, Inc.*, 223 F.3d 286, 295 (4th Cir. 2000) (“[A]ctual knowledge requires awareness of the alleged breach, not merely access to the information.”). This uniformity supports the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation.

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that “actual knowledge” under ERISA § 1113(2) requires awareness that the alleged breach occurred and that it was wrongful. This interpretation is consistent with the statute’s text, this Court’s precedent, and the decisions of other circuits. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
