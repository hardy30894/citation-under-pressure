**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Equal Protection Clause gives rise to a cause of action on behalf of a “class of one”—is one of profound constitutional significance. The Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit answered in the affirmative, holding that a plaintiff may state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause when the government’s action is motivated solely by “a spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” *Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 215 F.3d 671, 675 (7th Cir. 2000). This holding, while superficially appealing, is fundamentally at odds with the settled principles of equal protection jurisprudence and would eviscerate the requirement of a cognizable class or group, thereby transforming the Fourteenth Amendment into a vehicle for individualized grievances that are properly redressed through tort or contract law, not constitutional litigation.

The Equal Protection Clause does not create a right to be treated identically in every instance. *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985). Rather, it protects against arbitrary or irrational distinctions drawn by the government between individuals or groups. *See id.* at 440–41. The Clause is not a “general proscription against unfairness,” *Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620, 632 (1996), but a safeguard against classifications that lack a rational basis or that burden fundamental rights. *See id.* at 631–32. The doctrine of “class of one” equal protection claims—where a plaintiff alleges that the government treated him differently from others similarly situated without any rational justification—has been recognized only in the narrowest of circumstances, and even then, only when the plaintiff can demonstrate that the government’s action was not merely arbitrary, but “so bizarrely disparate as to suggest bad faith or spite.” *See Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000) (plurality opinion).

The plurality in *Olech* acknowledged that “a plaintiff must show that he was intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated and that there was no rational basis for the difference in treatment.” *Id.* at 564. But the Court of Appeals went further, holding that a plaintiff need not allege membership in a class or group—indeed, that the very absence of a class is what makes the claim cognizable. This is a misreading of *Olech* and a dangerous expansion of constitutional doctrine. The plurality in *Olech* did not hold that a “class of one” claim could be brought without any showing of similarly situated individuals. On the contrary, the plurality emphasized that the plaintiff must be “similarly situated” to others who were treated differently. *Id.* at 564. The Court did not purport to eliminate the requirement of comparability; it merely recognized that the “class” need not be formally defined or large in number. *See id.* at 565.

The Seventh Circuit’s interpretation, however, collapses this essential requirement. By holding that a plaintiff may allege an equal protection violation based solely on a “spiteful effort,” the court effectively allows any individual to bypass the normal channels of redress—contract, tort, or administrative law—by invoking the Constitution. This is not the role of the Equal Protection Clause. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, “the Constitution does not require that the government treat all persons identically, but only that it treat persons similarly situated in a similar manner.” *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440. To allow a claim based on mere animus or spite, without any showing of similarly situated individuals, would render the Clause a “generalized grievance” mechanism, which this Court has consistently rejected. *See, e.g., Warth v. Seldin*, 422 U.S. 490, 500 (1975) (“[A] plaintiff must have suffered an injury that is concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent.”).

Moreover, the Court of Appeals’ approach creates an unmanageable standard. What constitutes “spite”? How is one to prove a “spiteful effort” without access to internal government deliberations? The absence of a class or group renders the claim inherently speculative and subjective. The Equal Protection Clause is not a remedy for personal grievances. *See id.* at 501 (“[T]he Constitution does not confer a general right to have the government act in a manner that is fair or reasonable.”). To allow such claims would invite endless litigation based on subjective perceptions of unfairness, undermining the stability and predictability of government action.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision also conflicts with the principle that equal protection claims must be grounded in a classification. *See, e.g., Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440. A “class of one” claim is not a claim about the absence of a class, but about the existence of a classification that is irrational. *See Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564. The plaintiff must show that the government treated him differently from others who were similarly situated. Without such a comparison, there is no classification, and thus no equal protection violation. The Court of Appeals’ holding that a plaintiff need not allege membership in a class or group effectively eliminates the very foundation of the doctrine.

In sum, the Equal Protection Clause does not provide a cause of action for a “class of one” where the plaintiff fails to allege membership in a class or group. The Clause protects against irrational classifications, not personal animus. The Seventh Circuit’s decision, by allowing a claim based solely on spite and without any showing of similarly situated individuals, misreads *Olech*, undermines settled precedent, and opens the door to constitutional claims that belong in the realm of tort and contract law. The judgment should be reversed.

**For the reasons stated, the petition should be granted.**
