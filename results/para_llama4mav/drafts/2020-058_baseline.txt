ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a does not constitute an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. A straightforward reading of the relevant statutes, informed by the principles of statutory interpretation, reveals that TPS recipients are eligible for adjustment of status.

I. The Statutory Text Supports Petitioners' Eligibility for Adjustment of Status

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) defines the terms "admission" and "admitted" as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). However, the INA also provides that an alien granted TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The Ninth Circuit correctly held in Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017), that being considered in "lawful status as a nonimmigrant" is equivalent to being considered "as though [the alien] had been inspected and admitted" for purposes of § 1255.

The Third Circuit's contrary holding relies on a narrow reading of the term "admission" that ignores the broader statutory context. As the Supreme Court has noted, "the meaning of a statutory term is not to be determined by focusing on a single sentence or phrase in isolation, but rather by considering the statute as a whole." Brown v. Duchesne, 60 U.S. 183, 194 (1857). When considered in context, it is clear that a grant of TPS satisfies § 1255's admission requirement.

II. The Statutory Structure and Purpose Support Petitioners' Eligibility for Adjustment of Status

The Third Circuit's interpretation of §§ 1254a and 1255 is also inconsistent with the overall structure and purpose of the INA. Congress created the TPS program to provide relief to aliens who are unable to return to their home countries due to extraordinary conditions. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Allowing TPS recipients to adjust their status is consistent with this purpose, as it enables them to remain in the United States lawfully and build a stable life.

In contrast, the Third Circuit's holding would create an anomaly in the statutory scheme. TPS recipients who entered the United States lawfully would be eligible for adjustment of status, while those who entered unlawfully would be barred. See Saliba v. Att'y Gen., 828 F.3d 182, 186 (3d Cir. 2016) (nonimmigrant who lawfully entered the United States on a student visa applied for, and received, TPS). This disparate treatment is not supported by the statutory text or purpose.

III. The Third Circuit's Decision Conflicts with Precedent from Other Circuits

The Third Circuit's decision conflicts with the Sixth and Ninth Circuits' holdings in Flores v. USCIS, 718 F.3d 548 (6th Cir. 2013), and Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954 (9th Cir. 2017), respectively. In both cases, the courts held that a grant of TPS constitutes an "admission" for purposes of § 1255. The Third Circuit's contrary holding creates a circuit split that should be resolved in favor of the petitioners.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Third Circuit's decision and hold that a grant of TPS under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.
