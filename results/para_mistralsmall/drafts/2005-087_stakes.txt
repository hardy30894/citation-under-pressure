**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Confer a Right to Present Expert Mental-Health Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Independent of an Insanity Defense**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause does not require states to permit criminal defendants to introduce expert mental-health evidence to negate mens rea separate and apart from an insanity defense. *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 76 (1985) (holding that due process guarantees indigent defendants access to psychiatric assistance only “when the State presents psychiatric evidence of a certain kind”); *United States v. Lyons*, 731 F.2d 243, 246 (5th Cir. 1984) (“The Constitution does not mandate a particular allocation of the burden of proof on the issue of sanity, nor does it require that psychiatric evidence be admitted for any purpose other than an insanity defense.”).

This Court has long recognized that states retain broad authority to define the elements of crimes and the defenses thereto, so long as those definitions do not offend fundamental principles of justice. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201–02 (1977) (“[T]he Due Process Clause does not forbid the States to abolish insanity as a defense or to place the burden of proof of insanity on the defendant.”). Arizona’s rule restricting expert mental-health evidence to the insanity defense falls squarely within this tradition. *See Clark v. Arizona*, 204 Ariz. 404, 412 (2003) (en banc) (“The state may rationally limit the use of psychiatric testimony to the insanity defense, where the legislature has defined the contours of that defense.”), *aff’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds*, 548 U.S. 735 (2006).

**II. Arizona’s Rule Comports with the Fundamental Principle That the Prosecution Bears the Burden of Proving Every Element of a Crime Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert mental-health evidence does not violate due process because it does not shift the burden of proof on any element of the offense. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (“The Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.”). The State’s burden remains unchanged: it must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Clark acted with the requisite mens rea. *See id.* Arizona’s rule merely prevents Clark from using expert evidence to negate that mens rea indirectly, rather than through the statutorily defined insanity defense. *Clark*, 204 Ariz. at 413 (“The legislature could reasonably conclude that permitting psychiatric evidence to negate mens rea would undermine the efficacy of the insanity defense and confuse juries.”).

This Court has repeatedly upheld state procedural rules that restrict the introduction of evidence, so long as those rules do not prevent the defendant from presenting a full and fair defense. *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 42 (1996) (plurality opinion) (“States are free to adopt rules limiting the type of evidence that may be presented to the jury, even if the excluded evidence might have helped the defendant.”). Arizona’s rule is no different. It does not preclude Clark from presenting any evidence about his mental state; it merely channels that evidence into the insanity defense. *See Clark*, 204 Ariz. at 412.

**III. The Arizona Supreme Court’s Decision Did Not Create an Unconstitutional Distinction Between Insanity and Mens Rea**

Clark argues that Arizona’s rule creates an unconstitutional distinction between the insanity defense and the mens rea requirement. *See Brief for Petitioner at 20*. This argument misapprehends the nature of Arizona’s rule. The Arizona Supreme Court did not create a substantive due process right to use expert evidence to negate mens rea; it merely deferred to the legislature’s judgment in defining the scope of the insanity defense. *Clark*, 204 Ariz. at 411 (“The legislature has the authority to define the insanity defense and to limit the use of psychiatric evidence accordingly.”).

This Court has recognized that states may differentiate between insanity and mens rea without violating due process. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952) (“The State is free to adopt any definition of insanity it wishes, so long as it does not offend constitutional principles.”). Arizona’s rule does not offend those principles. It does not abolish the insanity defense or render it meaningless; it merely requires that evidence of mental illness be presented within the framework of that defense. *See Clark*, 204 Ariz. at 412.

**IV. The Rule Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment’s Prohibition on Cruel and Unusual Punishment**

Clark’s Eighth Amendment claim fares no better. *See Brief for Petitioner at 25*. The Eighth Amendment does not require states to permit the introduction of expert evidence to negate mens rea. *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 44 (“The Eighth Amendment does not constitutionalize the rules of evidence.”). Arizona’s rule is a procedural limitation, not a substantive one, and it does not implicate the Eighth Amendment’s core concerns. *See id.*

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed. Arizona’s rule restricting the use of expert mental-health evidence to the insanity defense does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause, the Sixth Amendment’s right to present a defense, or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. The decision below is consistent with this Court’s precedents and should be sustained.
