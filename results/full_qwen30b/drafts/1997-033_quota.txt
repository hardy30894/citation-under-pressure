**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court may invoke 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) to assign a transferred multidistrict litigation (MDL) case to itself for trial after the conclusion of pretrial proceedings under § 1407(a)—is one of statutory interpretation and constitutional fidelity to Congress’s carefully crafted framework for MDLs. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to deny mandamus relief and uphold the Arizona District Court’s self-transfer under § 1404(a) is fundamentally flawed. It disregards the plain language of § 1407(a), undermines the statutory mandate for remand, and permits district courts to subvert Congress’s policy judgment by retaining jurisdiction over cases beyond the scope of pretrial coordination. This Court should reverse.

**I. Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand to the Original District for Trial—No Exception for Self-Transfer Under § 1404(a)**

Congress enacted § 1407(a) to centralize pretrial proceedings in a single district for efficiency, but it explicitly and unambiguously required that “each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The word “remanded” is not a mere suggestion—it is a command. As this Court has repeatedly held, “when the words of a statute are unambiguous, that is the end of the matter.” *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842 (1984). The plain meaning of “remanded” is to return a case to the court from which it was transferred. *See, e.g., United States v. Hays*, 515 U.S. 735, 740 (1995) (remand “means to send back”); *United States v. Herring*, 467 U.S. 358, 362 (1984) (remand “is a command to return the case to the originating court”).

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that § 1404(a) permits a transferee court to “transfer” the case to itself for trial is a legal error of the first order. Section 1404(a) authorizes transfer of a case “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only “from one district or division to another district or division.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). It does not authorize a court to transfer a case to itself. *See, e.g., In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, N.H.*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971) (acknowledging that “there is nothing in the language of § 1407(a) or § 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial”). The notion that a court may transfer a case to itself under § 1404(a) is a legal fiction that contradicts the statute’s structure and purpose.

**II. The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation Has No Authority to Assign Cases for Trial**

The MDL process is not a grant of permanent jurisdiction to the transferee court. The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) may transfer cases “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” but it “shall” remand them at or before the conclusion of those proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The Panel’s authority is strictly limited to pretrial coordination. *See, e.g., In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810, 820 (3d Cir. 1982) (holding that the Panel’s power “does not extend to trial or final disposition”); *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 125 (2d Cir. 1971) (acknowledging that “the Panel’s authority is limited to pretrial proceedings”).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) to justify self-transfer is misplaced. Rules adopted by the JPML cannot override the clear command of § 1407(a). *See, e.g., *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, N.H.*, 342 F. Supp. at 909 (rejecting the notion that a rule can “override the statutory mandate”); *United States v. Herring*, 467 U.S. at 362 (rules cannot “supersede” statutory commands). The JPML’s rule permitting self-transfer is an administrative overreach that cannot legitimize a violation of § 1407(a).

**III. The Practice of Self-Transfer Undermines the Foundational Policy of § 1407**

Congress enacted § 1407 to prevent forum shopping and ensure that pretrial proceedings are centralized for efficiency, while preserving the plaintiff’s right to trial in the original forum. *See, e.g., *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, N.H.*, 342 F. Supp. at 909 (noting that “the policy of § 1407 is to centralize pretrial proceedings, not trial”); *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d at 124–25 (recognizing that “the statute was not intended to allow the transferee court to retain jurisdiction for trial”). The practice of self-transfer subverts this policy by allowing district courts to retain cases indefinitely, even after pretrial proceedings conclude, thereby undermining the very purpose of remand.
