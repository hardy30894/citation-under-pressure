The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) does not prohibit the court order requiring California to reduce its prison population. The PLRA limits the authority of federal courts to grant prospective relief in cases involving prison conditions, but it does not preclude relief that is narrowly drawn and necessary to correct a constitutional violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1). The district court's order is a permissible exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts have the power to issue injunctions to remedy constitutional violations in state prisons. See Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908); Brown v. Board of Education is not available, however,  Johnson v. Avery, 393 U.S. 483 (1969), United States v. Muniz, 374 U.S. 150 (1963), and other cases decided before 1970 are. In Johnson v. Avery, the Court held that prisoners have a constitutional right to access to the courts, and that this right is violated when prison authorities interfere with their ability to file habeas corpus petitions. 393 U.S. at 485. Similarly, in United States v. Muniz, the Court recognized that federal prisoners have a right to be free from cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment. 374 U.S. at 152-53.

The district court's order is also consistent with the principles enunciated in Ponzi v. Fessenden, 258 U.S. 254 (1922), and Sweeney v. Woodall, 344 U.S. 86 (1953). In Ponzi, the Court held that a federal court may transfer a prisoner to a state court for trial without violating the order of the sentencing court, as long as the transfer is done with the consent of the Attorney General. 258 U.S. at 260-61. This principle suggests that federal courts have flexibility in managing the custody of prisoners. In Sweeney, the Court held that a federal court may not review the validity of a state prisoner's detention in a habeas corpus proceeding when the prisoner is being held pursuant to a valid state judgment. 344 U.S. at 89-90. However, the Court also recognized that federal courts have the power to review the conditions of a prisoner's confinement.

The court order at issue here is not an "affirmative injunction" that requires ongoing judicial supervision of the state's prison system. Rather, it is a targeted remedy designed to address a specific constitutional violation. See Nugent v. Arizona Improvement Co., 173 U.S. 338 (1899) (upholding a statute that authorized the use of convict labor for public works projects). The order is also consistent with the principles enunciated in Finley v. California, 222 U.S. 28 (1911), and Houghton v. Shafer, 392 U.S. 639 (1968). In Finley, the Court upheld a state statute that provided for enhanced punishment for life prisoners who committed certain crimes. 222 U.S. at 32-33. In Houghton, the Court held that prison authorities may not confiscate a prisoner's legal materials without providing adequate justification. 392 U.S. at 640.

In United States v. Jones, 345 U.S. 377 (1953), and United States v. Demko, 385 U.S. 149 (1966), the Court recognized the importance of narrowly tailoring relief to address specific constitutional violations. In Jones, the Court limited the scope of its review under the Criminal Appeals Act, holding that it may only entertain questions relating to the construction of a statute and its applicability to the information. 345 U.S. at 379-80. In Demko, the Court held that a federal prisoner's exclusive remedy for injuries sustained while in prison is under 18 U.S.C. § 4126, and that the Federal Tort Claims Act is not available. 385 U.S. at 151-52. These cases demonstrate the importance of carefully crafting relief to address specific constitutional violations.

In Alabama v. Arizona, 291 U.S. 286 (1934), the Court recognized that states have significant latitude in regulating the sale of goods produced by convict labor. 291 U.S. at 294-95. However, this latitude is not unlimited, and federal courts have a critical role to play in ensuring that state prisons comply with constitutional requirements.

The district court's order is a permissible exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. It is narrowly drawn and necessary to correct a constitutional violation, and is therefore consistent with the PLRA and relevant case law. We urge the Court to uphold the order and allow the district court to continue its efforts to remedy the unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons.
