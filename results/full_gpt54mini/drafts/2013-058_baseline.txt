## Argument

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt an inherited IRA from a debtor’s estate. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and §522(d)(12) protect only “retirement funds” held in a tax-exempt account, 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12), and an inherited IRA is not a debtor’s retirement fund within the ordinary meaning of that term. The Supreme Court should reverse.

The starting point is the statutory text. Section 522 protects “retirement funds,” not merely any account that once contained retirement savings. 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C); 11 U.S.C. §522(d)(12). In *Clark v. Rameker*, 573 U.S. 122 (2014), the Court held that inherited IRAs do not fall within that phrase because they are not objectively set aside for the debtor’s retirement. The Court emphasized that inherited IRAs differ in three critical respects: the beneficiary may not add money to the account, the beneficiary must withdraw money regardless of retirement status, and the beneficiary may withdraw the entire balance at any time without a tax penalty. Id. at 124–25, 127–28. Those features show that inherited IRAs are “not funds objectively set aside for retirement,” but instead “are like an ordinary account.” Id. at 127–28.

That conclusion follows from the Bankruptcy Code’s function. Exemptions protect assets needed for a debtor’s post-employment support, not assets available for present consumption. See *Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320, 329–30 (2005) (explaining that retirement funds are “money set aside for the day an individual stops working”). In *Clark*, the Court distinguished *Rousey* because an inherited IRA “lacks many of the legal characteristics of traditional or Roth IRAs” and is not “set aside for use after the owner’s retirement.” 573 U.S. at 128–29. A debtor who inherits an IRA is not preserving a retirement nest egg; the debtor has received a spendable asset subject to mandatory distributions, and the account can be used for current consumption. Id. at 127–29.

The Seventh Circuit reached the same conclusion below. Judge Easterbrook explained that an inherited IRA is “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013). He reasoned that by the time of bankruptcy the funds “did not represent anyone’s retirement funds,” because they had been the original owner’s retirement funds but “when she died they became no one’s retirement funds.” Id. at 563. That analysis accords with the Supreme Court’s later decision in *Clark v. Rameker*, 573 U.S. at 127–29.

The contrary view, adopted by the Fifth Circuit in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), cannot be squared with the text or purpose of §522. The Fifth Circuit reasoned that because the money was once retirement funds in the original owner’s hands, it remains exempt in the beneficiary’s hands. Id. at 491–94. But the Bankruptcy Code does not say “funds that were once retirement funds”; it says “retirement funds.” 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). As *Clark* explained, Congress chose a present-tense, functional inquiry focused on what the debtor actually holds at the time of bankruptcy. 573 U.S. at 126–29. The inherited IRA fails that test.

Nor is there any canon requiring the exemption to be read expansively in the debtor’s favor. The Court has rejected the notion that “the usual rule of construction for exemptions” overrides the statute’s plain meaning. *Clark*, 573 U.S. at 134; see also *Rousey*, 544 U.S. at 330–31. Where Congress drew a line around retirement funds, courts may not enlarge it to cover assets that are not retirement funds under ordinary usage and function. *Clark*, 573 U.S. at 128–29.

Because an inherited IRA is not “retirement funds” within §522, it is property of the bankruptcy estate and available to creditors. 11 U.S.C. §541(a); *Clark v. Rameker*, 573 U.S. at 127–30. The judgment should be affirmed.
