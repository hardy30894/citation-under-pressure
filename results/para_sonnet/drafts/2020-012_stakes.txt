# ARGUMENT

**A Board decision denying a request to reopen a prior benefits determination is not a "final decision" subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f).**

## I. The Text and Structure of Section 355(f) Confine Judicial Review to Merits Determinations Reached After Exhaustion of Administrative Remedies.

Judicial review of Railroad Retirement Board decisions is available only as provided by the Railroad Unemployment Insurance Act, incorporated into the Railroad Retirement Act at 45 U.S.C. § 231g. Section 355(f) permits review of "any final decision of the Board." As the Fifth Circuit has explained, "[u]nder the plain language of § 355(f), the jurisdiction of the federal courts of appeals is limited to the review of Board decisions on the merits of a claim for benefits after administrative appeals have been exhausted." *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139, 140 (5th Cir. 2003).

A decision refusing to reopen a stale claim is categorically different from a decision on the merits of that claim. The merits decision—here, the August 2006 denial and the February 2007 denial of reconsideration—became final when Salinas failed to pursue further administrative appeal. A subsequent request to reopen does not reconstitute that final merits adjudication; it asks the agency, as a matter of grace, to revisit a controversy it has already conclusively resolved. Because Congress limited appellate jurisdiction to "decisions on the merits ... after administrative appeals have been exhausted," *id.*, a discretionary refusal to disturb a already-final decision falls outside the statute's grant of jurisdiction.

## II. *Califano v. Sanders* Forecloses Judicial Review of an Agency's Discretionary Refusal to Reopen a Final Benefits Decision, and That Reasoning Applies With Equal Force Here.

The Fifth Circuit, along with a majority of circuits construing analogous statutory language, has held that principles announced in *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), preclude review of an agency's refusal to reopen. In *Sanders*, the Supreme Court held that the Social Security Act's judicial-review provision does not authorize review of the Secretary's discretionary denial of a petition to reopen a prior benefits determination. The Court reasoned that reopening is a matter of administrative grace created entirely by regulation, not by statute, and that permitting judicial review of refusals to reopen would allow claimants to circumvent the statutory limitations period Congress attached to the underlying benefits decision.

That reasoning governs here. Just as the Social Security Act's reopening procedure is a creature of regulation rather than statutory command, the Board's reopening regulations, 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, are discretionary administrative mechanisms layered atop the statutory adjudicatory scheme. Salinas's 2006 claim became administratively final on February 9, 2007. Nearly seven years later, he sought to revive that claim through the reopening procedure—precisely the kind of end-run around finality and limitations periods that *Sanders* forbids. Allowing circuit courts to review the denial of reopening requests would permit claimants to relitigate stale claims indefinitely, undermining the finality that both the Social Security Act and the Railroad Retirement Act are designed to protect.

Recognizing this parallel, the Fifth Circuit "join[ed] several of our sister circuits in determining that we have no jurisdiction [under section 355(f)] to review the Board's decision not to reopen a prior claim for benefits." *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 140. It did so after "find[ing] the reasoning of the Fourth, Sixth, Seventh and Tenth circuits persuasive" in light of *Sanders*. *Id.* at 141; *see Harris v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 198 F.3d 139, 142 (4th Cir. 1999); *Abbruzzese v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 63 F.3d 972, 974 (10th Cir. 1995); *Gutierrez v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 918 F.2d 567, 570 (6th Cir. 1990); *Steebe v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 708 F.2d 250, 254–55 (7th Cir. 1983). Each of these courts recognized that the statutory text authorizing review of "final decisions" was not intended to sweep in ancillary, discretionary rulings on reopening requests made years after a claim's merits were conclusively resolved.

## III. The Minority Position Adopted in *Stovic* Should Not Be Extended to This Case.

Petitioner urges reliance on *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), in which the D.C. Circuit held that "the Railroad Retirement Act grants the [circuit] Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations." *Id.* at 502. That decision, however, breaks from the weight of authority construing materially identical review provisions in both the RRA and the analogous Social Security Act context, and it does not adequately grapple with the finality concerns that animated *Sanders*. The Second and Eighth Circuits' earlier departures from the majority rule, *see Szostak v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 370 F.2d 253, 254–55 (2d Cir. 1966); *Sones v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991), predate or fail to reconcile fully with *Sanders*'s teaching that reopening decisions are not the type of "final decision" subject to review.

Permitting review of reopening denials would effectively eliminate the limitations periods Congress and the Board have established for challenging benefits determinations, transforming every stale, unappealed denial into a perpetually revivable claim. That result cannot be squared with the statutory design reflected in § 355(f), nor with the finality principles the Supreme Court articulated in *Sanders*. The Fifth Circuit correctly adhered to its own controlling precedent in *Roberts* and correctly dismissed Salinas's petition for want of jurisdiction.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
