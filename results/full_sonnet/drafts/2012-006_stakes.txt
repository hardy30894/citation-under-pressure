# ARGUMENT

## I. Once a Defendant Produces Evidence of Withdrawal Before the Limitations Period, the Government Must Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That the Conspiracy—and the Defendant's Membership in It—Continued Into the Statutory Period.

The Due Process Clause "protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged." *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), holding that a State cannot relieve itself of the burden of proving every element of an offense simply by labeling a contested fact an "affirmative defense." *Id.* at 698-701. The touchstone is functional, not formal: where a purported defense negates a fact the prosecution must otherwise prove, *Winship* controls. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 210 (1977); *Martin v. Ohio*, 480 U.S. 228, 233-34 (1987).

Continued participation in the charged conspiracy during the limitations period is not a collateral matter—it is the offense. A conspiracy prosecution requires the government to prove that the defendant was a member of an unlawful agreement at some point within the five years preceding indictment. *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 114-15 (1970) (statutes of limitations are designed to spare defendants from having "to defend themselves against charges when the basic facts may have become obscured by the passage of time" and to encourage prompt investigation). Where, as here, a defendant introduces evidence that the conspiracy he once joined broke apart or that he left it before the limitations window opened, that evidence goes directly to whether the government has proved the very element the Constitution requires it to prove—that *this* defendant was a member of *this* conspiracy at the relevant time. To relieve the government of that burden merely because the defendant raised the issue is precisely the burden-shifting *Mullaney* forbids.

## II. Withdrawal Does Not Excuse Conduct That Would Otherwise Be Criminal; It Negates the Element of Continuing Membership, and Therefore the Burden Cannot Be Placed on the Defendant.

This Court has permitted burden-shifting only where the defense in question does not negate an element of the crime but instead offers a legally recognized excuse or justification for conduct the prosecution has already proved. In *Martin v. Ohio*, the Court sustained Ohio's placement of the burden of proving self-defense on the defendant precisely because self-defense "did not controvert any of the elements which the State is required to prove." 480 U.S. at 234. Similarly, in *Patterson*, extreme emotional disturbance did not negate the elements of murder that New York required the prosecution to establish. 432 U.S. at 206-07.

Withdrawal is fundamentally different. A conspiracy is "a partnership in criminal purposes," *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640, 644 (1946), and a defendant's membership in it must exist within the limitations period for the prosecution to lie at all. When a defendant withdraws, he is no longer a member of the conspiracy from that point forward; the partnership as to him has ended. Evidence of withdrawal thus does not excuse conduct that would otherwise satisfy the elements of the offense—it disproves that the element of continuing membership existed during the statutory period. Under *Mullaney* and *Winship*, the government, not the defendant, must bear the burden on that element.

## III. The Common-Law Understanding of Conspiracy and Persuasive Circuit Authority Confirm That the Government Bears This Burden.

This Court has long recognized that conspiracy is a continuing offense that persists "until abandoned or accomplished," and that a member's departure from the agreement terminates his liability for subsequent acts of his confederates. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912). Withdrawal requires "affirmative acts inconsistent with the object of the conspiracy and communicated in a manner reasonably calculated to reach co-conspirators." *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 464-65 (1978). Because withdrawal is defined in terms of severing the very relationship the government must prove existed within the limitations period, the Seventh Circuit correctly held that once a defendant comes forward with evidence of withdrawal predating the limitations period, "the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant did not withdraw" before that period began. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1233 (7th Cir. 1981). That approach honors *Winship* by keeping the ultimate burden of persuasion on the sovereign that seeks to deprive a citizen of his liberty, while still requiring the defendant to come forward with evidence sufficient to place withdrawal genuinely in issue—a production burden wholly consistent with due process. *See Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 210.

## IV. Any Other Rule Would Require Defendants to Prove a Negative and Would Undermine the Purposes the Statute of Limitations Serves.

Requiring a defendant to disprove, by a preponderance of the evidence, his continued membership in a conspiracy that the government itself charged as unified and ongoing inverts the presumption of innocence. It forces the defendant to establish an absence of participation in events that, by hypothesis, occurred in secret. This Court has never sanctioned such a scheme where the fact at issue is coextensive with an element of the crime. *Mullaney*, 421 U.S. at 698. The judgment below should be reversed.
