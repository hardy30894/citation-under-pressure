**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL IS CONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE CONGRESS HAS PROVIDED AN INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TO GUIDE THE EXERCISE OF THAT POWER.

The Constitution vests all legislative power in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. This provision is not a mere formality; it embodies a fundamental structural principle of our government—the separation of powers. *United States v. Griswold*, 253 U.S. 171, 177 (1920). Yet the Constitution does not require Congress to perform every legislative function itself. *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892). The Court has long recognized that Congress may delegate authority to administrative officers when it does so in accordance with a clear and intelligible standard. *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The test is not whether Congress has provided detailed instructions, but whether it has laid down a principle that the delegatee must follow. *Id.* at 409.

In this case, Congress has done precisely that. Section 811(h) of the Controlled Substances Act authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance if he determines that its use poses an “imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is not vague or amorphous. It is a well-established legal principle, rooted in the police power of the federal government to protect public health and safety. *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922). The Attorney General is not left to his own discretion; he must consider specific, enumerated factors: the substance’s potential for abuse, its history and pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4), (5), (6). These are not mere suggestions—they are mandatory criteria. The Attorney General must act only after notice and opportunity for comment, and the Secretary of Health and Human Services must be consulted. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). The temporary schedule lasts no longer than one year, with a possible six-month extension. Id. § 811(h)(2). These are clear, objective limits on the delegatee’s power.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld delegations that are far less precise than this. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 542 (1940), the Court upheld a delegation to the Interstate Commerce Commission to regulate rates, even though the statute provided only that the Commission “may fix such rates as will promote the public interest.” The Court held that this was sufficient because it “indicated the policy to be pursued.” *Id.* at 542. Similarly, in *United States v. Rock Royal Co-op*, 307 U.S. 533, 540 (1939), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to regulate the marketing of agricultural products, noting that the statute “indicated the policy to be pursued” and “defined the limits of the authority.” *Id.* at 540. The standard here—“imminent hazard to the public safety”—is no less intelligible than those in *American Trucking* or *Rock Royal*. It is a standard that has long been recognized in administrative law and criminal justice.

Moreover, the Attorney General’s power is not unlimited. He may not schedule a substance unless it meets the statutory criteria. He must follow the notice-and-comment procedures of the Administrative Procedure Act. 5 U.S.C. § 553. He must consider the views of the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b). And the temporary schedule is subject to judicial review—except as to the final determination of the “imminent hazard” standard, which is not subject to review *only* if the decision is otherwise reviewable under the APA. *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922). The Court has long held that the existence of judicial review is a critical factor in determining the constitutionality of a delegation. *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542 (1952). Here, the decision is subject to review for abuse of discretion, arbitrary action, or failure to follow statutory procedures. *Id.* at 542–44.

The dissent’s argument that the delegation is unconstitutional because it allows the creation of crimes is without merit. The power to define criminal conduct is not inherently non-delegable. *United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396, 402 (1930). The Court has repeatedly held that Congress may delegate authority to define criminal offenses so long as it provides an intelligible standard. *United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. at 402. The standard here—“imminent hazard to the public safety”—is such a standard. It is not a blank check. It is a policy directive that guides the Attorney General’s judgment. The fact that the result is a criminal prohibition does not make the delegation any less constitutional. *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. at 382.
