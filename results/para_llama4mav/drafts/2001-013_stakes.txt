ARGUMENT

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels, which are subject to minimal Coast Guard regulation. The Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). However, this provision only preempts OSHA jurisdiction when another federal agency has exercised its authority to regulate the specific working conditions at issue. See Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Ass'n, 505 U.S. 88, 102-03 (1992) (holding that section 4(b)(1) preemption requires a showing that another federal agency has exercised its authority to regulate the specific working conditions at issue).

The Coast Guard has not exercised its authority to regulate the working conditions at issue in this case. While the Coast Guard has some regulatory authority over uninspected vessels, its regulations are limited to specific areas such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation systems. 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq; 46 C.F.R. §§ 26.01 et seq. The Coast Guard's regulations do not comprehensively address the working conditions of seamen on uninspected vessels, and therefore do not preempt OSHA's jurisdiction under section 4(b)(1).

The Fifth Circuit's reliance on Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. While these cases held that OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation, they did not address the specific issue of whether the Coast Guard's regulation of uninspected vessels preempts OSHA's jurisdiction. Furthermore, the vessels at issue in Clary and Donovan were likely subject to more comprehensive Coast Guard regulation than the uninspected vessel at issue in this case.

In contrast, the Ninth Circuit has held that OSHA has jurisdiction to regulate the working conditions of employees on uninspected vessels. See Marshall v. Nichols, 914 F.2d 1331, 1334-35 (9th Cir. 1990) (holding that OSHA has jurisdiction to regulate the working conditions of employees on an uninspected vessel because the Coast Guard's regulations did not comprehensively address the working conditions at issue).

The Fifth Circuit's holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precludes OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) is overly broad and inconsistent with the language and purpose of the Act. OSHA has jurisdiction to issue citations to uninspected vessels, and the Court should reverse the Fifth Circuit's decision.

In conclusion, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Coast Guard's limited regulation of uninspected vessels does not preempt OSHA's jurisdiction under section 4(b)(1), and the Fifth Circuit's holding to the contrary was erroneous.
