# ARGUMENT

## I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE DOES NOT APPLY TO ALIENS WHOSE ILLEGAL REENTRY OCCURRED BEFORE THE STATUTE'S 1997 EFFECTIVE DATE

Section 241(a)(5) of the INA provides that when the Attorney General finds an alien "has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed," the prior order of removal "is reinstated from its original date." 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). This Court has long held that statutes are presumed to apply only prospectively absent clear congressional intent to the contrary. *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 265 (1994) (the "presumption against retroactive legislation is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence"). Nothing in the text of § 1231(a)(5) or its legislative history indicates that Congress intended it to reach aliens like Petitioner, whose illegal reentry occurred in 1982—fifteen years before the statute's 1997 effective date.

The statute's structure confirms this reading. Congress enacted § 1231(a)(5) as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 to deter *future* illegal reentries by making clear that such conduct would carry severe consequences. A statute aimed at deterrence logically applies to conduct occurring after its enactment, not to reentries that occurred decades earlier when no such consequence existed. *See INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 315 (2001) (courts should be "reluctant to give retroactive effect to statutory provisions...particularly when those consequences are so severe").

This Court has repeatedly instructed that the relevant "event" for retroactivity analysis is the primary conduct Congress sought to regulate. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321. Here, the relevant conduct is the illegal reentry itself, not the alien's continued presence or subsequent application for relief. Because Petitioner's reentry occurred in 1982, applying the reinstatement bar to him attaches new legal consequences to a completed act.

## II. APPLICATION OF § 1231(a)(5) TO PETITIONER'S PRE-ENACTMENT REENTRY IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE

Even if the statutory text were ambiguous, the *Landgraf* framework compels the conclusion that applying § 1231(a)(5) to Petitioner would have retroactive effect and is therefore impermissible absent clear congressional direction. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280.

**A. Petitioner Had a Vested Interest in Seeking Adjustment of Status**

This Court in *St. Cyr* held that a statute has retroactive effect when it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." 533 U.S. at 321 (quoting *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270). In *St. Cyr*, this Court found impermissible retroactivity where IIRIRA eliminated discretionary relief under former § 212(c) for aliens who had pleaded guilty before the statute's enactment, because those aliens had relied on the availability of such relief. *Id.* at 321-23. The Court emphasized that "[t]here can be little doubt that, as a general matter, alien defendants considering whether to enter into a plea agreement" would have taken the availability of relief "into account." *Id.* at 322.

The Tenth Circuit concluded that Petitioner had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" because he entered illegally. But this reasoning proves too much—it would eliminate retroactivity concerns for the very population Congress most clearly intended to protect through the ordinary operation of prospective lawmaking. Petitioner, like the petitioner in *St. Cyr*, structured his affairs—marrying a United States citizen and building a life in this country over nineteen years—based on the legal landscape as it existed. Before 1997, an alien in Petitioner's position could pursue adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255 notwithstanding a prior deportation order. The 1997 reinstatement statute retroactively extinguished that possibility.

**B. The New Legal Consequence Attaches to Petitioner's 1982 Reentry, Not His Continued Presence**

The government's position that § 1231(a)(5) applies because Petitioner remained in the United States after 1997 confuses the triggering conduct with mere continuation of status. This Court rejected similar reasoning in *Landgraf* itself, recognizing that "the largest category of cases in which we have applied the presumption against statutory retroactivity has involved new provisions affecting contractual or property rights, matters in which predictability and stability are of prime importance." 511 U.S. at 271.

Just as the *St. Cyr* petitioner's decision to plead guilty was the relevant "event" for retroactivity purposes—not his continued presence in removal proceedings—Petitioner's decision to reenter in 1982, and his subsequent decision to remain and build a family life, were the relevant events. The reinstatement statute attaches a new and severe legal consequence (permanent ineligibility for any relief, including adjustment of status based on marriage to a citizen) to that completed 1982 conduct.

**C. The Severity of the Consequence Confirms Retroactive Effect**

This Court has recognized that the harshness of a statute's practical operation bears on whether it operates retroactively. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 325 (noting that "the sheer magnitude of th[e] effect" supports finding retroactivity). Section 1231(a)(5) does not merely reinstate a removal order; it forecloses *all* forms of relief under the INA, including adjustment of status based on marriage to a United States citizen—relief that Congress has historically prioritized. This is a substantially more severe consequence than the loss of discretionary relief at issue in *St. Cyr*.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that § 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens whose illegal reentry preceded the statute's effective date, or, alternatively, that such application is impermissibly retroactive under *Landgraf* and *St. Cyr*.
