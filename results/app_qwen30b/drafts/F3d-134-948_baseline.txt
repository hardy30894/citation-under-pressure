**ARGUMENT**

I. THE TRIBAL COURT DENIED SELAM HIS RIGHT TO COMPULSORY PROCESS UNDER THE INDIAN CIVIL RIGHTS ACT, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), AND THIS DENIAL WAS NOT WAIVED.

The Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”) guarantees to every person accused of a crime in a tribal court the right to compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in their favor. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). This right is not merely procedural—it is a fundamental safeguard of due process, essential to a fair trial. As this Court has recognized, “the right to compulsory process is a cornerstone of the American system of justice.” *United States v. Valenzuela-Bernal*, 458 U.S. 858, 873 (1982). The right ensures that a defendant may present a defense by calling witnesses who can testify on their behalf, and it is not forfeited by failure to request subpoenas if the defendant has made a good-faith effort to secure witnesses and the court has failed to act on that effort.

Here, Selam made a clear and repeated effort to secure character witnesses. He provided his appointed counsel, Patricia Leno-Baker, with a list of five potential witnesses. He repeatedly offered to assist in contacting them, but was met with excuses and inaction. Leno-Baker ultimately located three of the five, but two of those witnesses informed her that their testimony would be damaging. Despite this, the tribal judge and counsel advised Selam of his right to compel the appearance of witnesses, and Selam did not refuse that right. Indeed, he had no reason to believe he could not invoke it.

Yet, when Selam did not request subpoenas, the tribal court did not inquire whether he desired to do so. It did not offer to assist him in securing witnesses. It did not explain the consequences of failing to request subpoenas. It simply allowed the trial to proceed without any defense witnesses—except Selam himself. This failure to act, in the face of a defendant’s clear effort to present a defense, constitutes a denial of compulsory process under ICRA.

The Supreme Court has held that “the right to compulsory process is not a mere formality; it is a vital component of the right to a fair trial.” *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967). In *Valenzuela-Bernal*, the Court emphasized that “the right to present witnesses is not a privilege that may be arbitrarily denied.” 458 U.S. at 873. The tribal court here failed to ensure that Selam’s right to compulsory process was meaningfully protected. It did not affirmatively assist him in securing witnesses, nor did it inquire whether he wished to exercise his right. The court’s silence in the face of a defendant’s efforts to present a defense is not a neutral procedural posture—it is a denial of constitutional rights.

The district court erred in concluding that Selam waived this claim by not raising it in the tribal court of appeals. The doctrine of exhaustion does not apply where a claim is not reasonably cognizable in the tribal appellate forum or where the tribal court failed to provide a meaningful opportunity to raise it. *See, e.g., United States v. Mendoza-Lopez*, 481 U.S. 828, 839 (1987) (exhaustion not required where “the remedy is inadequate or ineffective to protect the rights of the petitioner”). Here, the tribal court of appeals denied Selam a hearing altogether, dismissing his appeal without review. Under such circumstances, exhaustion is not required. *See* *United States v. Mendoza-Lopez*, 481 U.S. at 839; *Henderson v. United States*, 312 U.S. 304, 310 (1941) (where appellate forum is unavailable, exhaustion is excused).

Moreover, the right to compulsory process is a fundamental right under ICRA, and such rights are not subject to waiver by default. *See* *United States v. Herring*, 467 U.S. 358, 363 (1984) (constitutional rights may not be waived by failure to assert them where the defendant is not given a meaningful opportunity to do so). Selam was not given such an opportunity. The tribal court failed to ensure that he understood his right or that he could exercise it. The failure to act by the court, not the defendant, is the source of the error.

II. THE TRIBAL COURT’S FAILURE TO ALLOW THE PRESENTATION OF CHARACTER WITNESSES VIOLATED THE CONFRONTATION CLAUSE OF THE ICRA.

Selam’s claim under the Confrontation Clause of ICRA—25 U.S.C. § 1302(6)—is not waived. The clause provides that “no person shall be deprived of liberty without due process of law,” and includes the right “to be confronted with the witnesses against him.” This right is coextensive with the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause, and courts have consistently held that it applies to tribal courts. *See* *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28, 34 (1913); *United States v. Mendoza-Lopez*, 481 U.S. at 839.

Selam’s right to confront witnesses was not only violated by the absence of defense witnesses, but also by the admission of the five-year-old victim’s out-of-court statements through multiple hearsay witnesses. The prosecution introduced the child’s statements through Dr. Beamer, her parents, and Investigator Clements—none of whom were subject to cross-examination. The child herself did not testify. This is precisely the kind of testimonial hearsay that the Confrontation Clause was designed to prevent. *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 54 (2004) (“The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of confrontation is violated when testimonial statements of a witness who does not appear at trial are admitted against a defendant, absent a showing of unavailability and a prior opportunity for cross-examination.”).

The tribal court’s failure to require the child’s testimony or to ensure that the statements were subject to cross-examination constitutes a violation of Selam’s right to confrontation.
