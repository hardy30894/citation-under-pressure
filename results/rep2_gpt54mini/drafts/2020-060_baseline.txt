**Argument**

CERCLA’s text and this Court’s precedent confirm that Guam may proceed under section 107(a) because its 2004 Clean Water Act consent decree did not “resolve[] its liability to the United States . . . for some or all of a response action” within the meaning of section 113(f)(3)(B). 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Section 113(f)(3)(B) is triggered only by an administrative or judicially approved settlement that actually resolves CERCLA liability for a response action or response costs. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004). Guam’s decree did no such thing. It settled Clean Water Act claims, not CERCLA claims, and it contained no finding or admission that Guam had incurred CERCLA liability at the Ordot Dump.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that CERCLA authorizes two distinct remedial pathways: section 107(a) cost recovery and section 113(f) contribution. *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 135-36 (2007). Section 107(a) permits recovery of “necessary costs of response” incurred by a party that has itself undertaken cleanup, while section 113(f) applies when a party seeks to shift costs after being subject to a suit or settlement resolving liability. *Id.* at 139-41. Those provisions are complementary, but they are not interchangeable. *Id.* at 140-41. Where section 113(f) is unavailable, section 107(a) remains available to a party seeking to recover its own cleanup costs. *Id.* at 139.

The Government’s theory would improperly collapse the statutory distinction. The decree at issue required Guam to close the Ordot Dump and install a cover system to address ongoing Clean Water Act discharges. But a settlement concerning environmental injunctive relief does not automatically resolve CERCLA liability. In *Cooper Industries*, the Court held that a party may not seek contribution under section 113(f)(1) unless it has been subject to a civil action under section 106 or 107. 543 U.S. at 168-69. And in *Atlantic Research*, the Court explained that section 107(a) remains available for direct cleanup expenditures precisely because Congress did not intend section 113(f) to swallow all cleanup-cost claims. 551 U.S. at 139-41.

The text of section 113(f)(3)(B) is especially important. It permits contribution only where a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). This Court’s decision in *Territory of Guam v. United States*, 950 F.3d 104, 110-13 (D.C. Cir. 2020), erred by treating a Clean Water Act decree as enough, even though it did not resolve CERCLA liability. That reading conflicts with the statutory requirement that the settlement resolve liability “for” a CERCLA response action, not merely require unrelated compliance measures. *See id.* at 112-13.

Other courts of appeals have likewise emphasized that section 113(f)(3)(B) turns on whether the settlement resolves CERCLA liability for cleanup costs, not on generic regulatory obligations. *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1001-03 (6th Cir. 2015); *Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1122-24 (9th Cir. 2017); *Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries Inc.*, 937 F.3d 928, 931-33 (8th Cir. 2019). Those decisions correctly reject any rule that would transform every environmental consent decree into a contribution trigger. *Florida Power*, 810 F.3d at 1002-03; *Asarco*, 866 F.3d at 1123-24.

That matters because CERCLA’s remedial scheme was designed to ensure that “responsible parties foot the bill.” *Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998). The Government’s position would do the opposite here: it would allow the United States to evade its own alleged responsibility for the Ordot Dump merely because Guam later settled a different statutory claim with EPA. Nothing in CERCLA authorizes that result. Under *Atlantic Research*, Guam’s direct cleanup expenditures are the very kind of costs section 107(a) was designed to cover. 551 U.S. at 137-39.

The Court should therefore hold that Guam’s 2004 Clean Water Act decree did not resolve CERCLA liability under section 113(f)(3)(B), reverse the dismissal of Guam’s section 107(a) claim, and permit Guam to seek recovery of its response costs from the United States.
