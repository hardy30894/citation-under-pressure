# ARGUMENT

## I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, drawing all reasonable inferences in favor of the non-moving party. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) (holding that at summary judgment "the evidence of the non-movant is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in his favor"). Claim construction, including the construction underlying the district court's written-description analysis, is likewise reviewed de novo. *Cybor Corp. v. FAS Techs., Inc.*, 138 F.3d 1448, 1456 (Fed. Cir. 1998) (en banc); *Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc.*, 517 U.S. 370, 388–91 (1996) (holding claim construction "purely" a matter of law for the court). Because the district court resolved the priority and anticipation issues on summary judgment, PowerOasis need show only a genuine dispute of material fact—or a legal error in the court's framework—to warrant reversal.

## II. The Asserted Claims Are Entitled to the February 6, 1997 Filing Date of the Original Application

### A. Legal Framework

Section 120 allows a later application to claim the benefit of an earlier application's filing date only if the earlier application satisfies the written description requirement of 35 U.S.C. § 112 for the claims ultimately asserted. *In re Chu*, 66 F.3d 292, 297 (Fed. Cir. 1995) (explaining that a claim is entitled to an earlier priority date only if "the disclosure of the application relied upon ... reasonably conveys to the artisan that the inventor had possession" of the later-claimed subject matter). The test is not whether the earlier disclosure uses identical words, but whether it "reasonably conveys to those skilled in the art that the inventor had possession" of the claimed invention as of the earlier filing date. *Vas-Cath Inc. v. Mahurkar*, 935 F.2d 1555, 1563 (Fed. Cir. 1991). Critically, "the applicant does not have to describe exactly the subject matter claimed"; in haec verba support is not required. *Union Oil Co. of Cal. v. Atl. Richfield Co.*, 208 F.3d 989, 1015 (Fed. Cir. 2000). The written description inquiry is answered from the perspective of a person of ordinary skill, who reads the specification as a whole rather than parsing isolated sentences. *Lockwood v. Am. Airlines, Inc.*, 107 F.3d 1565, 1572 (Fed. Cir. 1997) (written description "must clearly allow persons of ordinary skill in the art to recognize" that the inventor invented what is claimed). And where new language in a later application merely elaborates upon a concept already inherent in, or necessarily implied by, the original disclosure, it does not constitute impermissible "new matter" precluding priority. *In re Wertheim*, 646 F.2d 527, 537 (C.C.P.A. 1981) (a later-filed application may still be entitled to an earlier priority date where the later disclosure "does not add new matter" but merely makes explicit what was implicit).

### B. The Original Application Already Discloses a Customer Interface Located on the Customer's Own Device

The district court's error was treating the 2000 CIP's elaboration on the customer-interface concept as the *creation* of new subject matter, rather than as a permissible clarification of what the Original Application already conveyed. The stated purpose of the invention from its inception was to enable "a customer to connect a laptop to a telecommunications channel"—not merely to a machine that operates independently of that laptop. A vending machine whose entire function is to grant a customer's *own device* access to a telecommunications channel necessarily contemplates that status information regarding that access—confirmation of connection, time remaining, or termination—passes through software and hardware resident on that same device. As this Court has recognized, the written description requirement does not demand that every claim limitation be recited verbatim; it is satisfied where the concept is "necessarily present" in the earlier disclosure, even if not "specifically discussed." *Union Oil*, 208 F.3d at 997. A skilled artisan reading the Original Application's teaching of laptop-based channel access would necessarily understand that the "customer interface" contemplated by the invention could reside on the very laptop being connected—because that is the only device through which the customer, engaged in a data session, would perceive the status of that session in real time.

The district court's contrary conclusion rested entirely on the fact that more elaborate language appeared for the first time in the 2000 CIP Application. But the mere fact that later-added language is more explicit does not transform an already-disclosed concept into new matter. *In re Wertheim*, 646 F.2d at 537. As in *Amgen Inc. v. Hoechst Marion Roussel, Inc.*, 314 F.3d 1313, 1330 (Fed. Cir. 2003), where this Court examined whether the "essence" of the later claims was already present in the earlier specification, the proper inquiry here required the district court to ask whether the *concept* of a customer-side interface was already conveyed—not merely whether identical phrasing appeared. Nor can the absence of a specific example defeat priority, since *In re Gosteli*, 872 F.2d 1008, 1012 (Fed. Cir. 1989), confirms that priority is assessed claim-by-claim against what the earlier disclosure conveys as a whole, not against isolated silence on a particular embodiment.

## III. Because the Asserted Claims Are Entitled to the 1997 Priority Date, the MobileStar Network Cannot Anticipate Under § 102(b)

Once the asserted claims are properly accorded the February 6, 1997 filing date, the undisputed pre-June-1999 MobileStar activity falls outside the § 102(b) critical date, and cannot constitute invalidating prior art as a matter of law. Summary judgment of anticipation therefore cannot stand, and the district court's contrary judgment should be reversed.
