# ARGUMENT

## THE CONFERRAL OF TEMPORARY PROTECTED STATUS CONSTITUTES AN "ADMISSION" UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1255

### A. The Plain Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Deems TPS Recipients Admitted

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. Congress chose the word "considered"—a legal fiction that instructs courts to treat TPS recipients *as if* they possess nonimmigrant status, notwithstanding the circumstances of their actual entry. This Court has recognized that such statutory fictions must be given their full, intended effect. *See Astoria Fed. Sav. & Loan Ass'n v. Solimino*, 501 U.S. 104, 108 (1991) (courts must presume Congress "says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says").

Critically, nonimmigrant status cannot exist in the abstract; it is a status conferred only upon those who have been admitted. As the Ninth Circuit correctly recognized in the decision below's companion case, "[b]y the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted." *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017) (internal quotation marks omitted). The Sixth Circuit reached the identical conclusion, holding that "an alien who has TPS has, by definition, been 'admitted' to the United States." *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548, 554 (6th Cir. 2013). These sister circuits properly gave effect to the statutory fiction Congress created.

### B. Adjustment of Status Under § 1255 Does Not Require a Predicate Physical Entry

Petitioner does not dispute that "admission" is ordinarily tied to physical entry after inspection. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). But § 1254a(f)(4) operates precisely to supply that admission by legal fiction where none occurred in fact. This Court has long approved of Congress's authority to create such fictions to serve remedial statutory purposes. *See Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284 (1922).

The government's contrary position—and the Third Circuit's holding below—rests on an artificial and unsupported distinction between "admission" and "lawful status" that finds no footing in the statutory text. The Board of Immigration Appeals has itself recognized that "[a]n adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission." *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). If an adjustment of status—an entirely paper transaction occurring within the United States—can constitute an admission, there is no principled reason why a grant of TPS, which likewise confers nonimmigrant status without regard to physical entry, cannot do the same.

### C. The Third Circuit's Reliance on a Rigid Admission/Status Dichotomy Cannot Be Reconciled With § 1254a's Remedial Purpose

The decision below invoked *Hanif v. Attorney General*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012), for the proposition that "admission" and "status" are categorically distinct. But *Hanif* addressed a different statutory context entirely—one lacking any analogue to § 1254a(f)(4)'s explicit deeming provision. The Third Circuit's invocation of the canon that "where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally," *I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987), cuts against, not for, its holding: Congress *did* include language deeming TPS recipients to hold nonimmigrant status, and nonimmigrant status is unattainable without admission.

This Court's interpretive methodology requires reading statutory provisions "as a symmetrical and coherent regulatory scheme," fitting each provision into the overall statutory scheme. *Food & Drug Admin. v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp.*, 529 U.S. 120, 133 (2000). Congress enacted TPS as a humanitarian measure to protect nationals of countries suffering armed conflict or natural disaster. Reading § 1254a(f)(4) to withhold the benefits of admission from TPS recipients—even though it explicitly deems them to hold nonimmigrant status—undermines the remedial purpose Congress sought to achieve. Where a statute is "designed to remedy" a particular harm, courts must give it "a fair and reasonable construction to accomplish the purposes for which it was enacted." *Tcherepnin v. Knight*, 389 U.S. 332, 336 (1967).

### D. Superfluity Concerns Cut in Petitioner's Favor

The Third Circuit worried that Petitioner's reading would render § 1255(h) and (i)'s explicit admission exceptions for special immigrants and visa-eligible aliens superfluous. But this concern is misplaced. Those provisions except aliens from the admission requirement *entirely*; § 1254a(f)(4) instead *satisfies* that requirement by deeming an admission to have occurred through the grant of nonimmigrant status. There is no redundancy where one statute waives a requirement and another fulfills it. This Court has cautioned against strained readings adopted merely to avoid a perceived redundancy where the more natural reading is otherwise available. *See Ysleta del Sur Pueblo v. Texas*, 596 U.S. 685, 698 (2022) (surplusage canon is "not absolute" and must yield to clearer textual commands).

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that a grant of Temporary Protected Status constitutes an "admission" under 8 U.S.C. § 1255, and reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit.
