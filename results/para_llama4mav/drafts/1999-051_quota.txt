Argument

The Sixth Circuit's decision that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim is supported by the Supreme Court's precedent. In Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722, 753 (1991), the Court held that a habeas petitioner must demonstrate cause and prejudice to overcome a procedural default. The Court further explained in Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986), that cause can be established by showing that the default was the result of "some objective factor external to the defense" that impeded counsel's efforts to comply with the State's procedural rule.

Ineffective assistance of counsel can constitute cause to excuse a procedural default, as established in Martinez v. Ryan, 566 U.S. 1, 9 (2012). Although the ineffective-assistance claim itself was procedurally defaulted in this case, the Supreme Court has held that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim can serve as cause to excuse the default of another claim if the ineffective-assistance claim is "substantial" and the default was the result of ineffective assistance of post-conviction counsel, as seen in Martinez, 566 U.S. at 14.

The Sixth Circuit's decision is consistent with the principles established in Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), which set forth the standard for evaluating ineffective assistance of counsel claims. To establish ineffective assistance, a petitioner must demonstrate that counsel's performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced the defense, as stated in Strickland, 466 U.S. at 687. The Court of Appeals applied this standard in evaluating Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim and found that his appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has recognized that the right to effective assistance of counsel is essential to the fairness of a trial, as noted in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963). The failure to provide effective assistance can result in prejudice to the defendant, as seen in United States v. Cronic, 466 U.S. 648, 658 (1984).

The Court of Appeals' finding of prejudice from counsel's failure to raise the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim on direct appeal is supported by the Supreme Court's decision in Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979), which established the standard for evaluating the sufficiency of the evidence. The Court held that a conviction must be supported by sufficient evidence, and that a habeas petitioner is entitled to relief if the evidence is insufficient to support a conviction, as stated in Jackson, 443 U.S. at 324.

Additionally, the Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of ensuring that a defendant's conviction is supported by sufficient evidence, as seen in In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). The failure to raise a meritorious sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim on direct appeal can result in prejudice to the defendant, as noted in Smith v. Robbins, 528 U.S. 259, 285 (2000).

The respondent acknowledges that the Sixth Circuit's decision may be subject to challenge, but argues that it is supported by the Supreme Court's precedent. The decision is consistent with the principles established in Coleman, Murray, Martinez, and Strickland, among other cases. Therefore, the respondent respectfully submits that the Sixth Circuit's decision should be affirmed.

In conclusion, the Sixth Circuit's decision that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim is supported by the Supreme Court's precedent, including Coleman v. Thompson, Murray v. Carrier, Martinez v. Ryan, and Strickland v. Washington. The decision is also consistent with other relevant case law, such as Jackson v. Virginia, In re Winship, and Smith v. Robbins.
