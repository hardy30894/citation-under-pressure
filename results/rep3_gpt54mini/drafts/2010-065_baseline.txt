**Argument**

Bond lacks standing to press a Tenth Amendment objection to 18 U.S.C. § 229. The Supreme Court has long held that a private party may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal action unless the States themselves are the proper complainants. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939), the Court rejected utility companies’ attempt to contest federal conduct as an invasion of state authority, explaining that “the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [A]mendment.” That rule is not an aside; it is the Court’s holding. See *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (explaining that *Tennessee Electric*’s standing discussion was “an alternative ground, rather than obiter dictum”); *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95 (1998) (jurisdictional issues must be resolved before the merits).

The Court’s later Tenth Amendment decisions confirm that the Amendment protects the constitutional balance between the National Government and the States, not a free-standing individual defense to federal prosecution. In *Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898, 935 (1997), the Court described the Tenth Amendment as a structural limitation on federal power, but it did so in the context of state officials resisting federal commandeering. Likewise, in *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 155-57 (1992), the Court entertained a Tenth Amendment claim brought by a State, not a criminal defendant. Bond, by contrast, is a private litigant seeking to assert what is at bottom a state sovereignty objection to a federal criminal statute. That is precisely the situation *Tennessee Electric* forecloses. See *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004); *Brooklyn Legal Services Corp. v. Legal Services Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005); *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Services Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009).

Bond’s contrary argument depends on circuit authority that has permitted private parties to raise Tenth Amendment claims, but that view is not controlling and is inconsistent with Supreme Court precedent. See *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693, 703-04 (7th Cir. 1999); *Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. United States Department of Energy*, 666 F.2d 1359, 1368 n.16 (11th Cir. 1982). The better-reasoned decisions reject standing because the States, not private defendants, are the constitutional guardians of federalism. See *Parker*, 362 F.3d at 1284-85; *Hacker*, 565 F.3d at 525-27. As the First Circuit explained, “the State represents the interests of its citizens in general,” and if the State declines to object, private citizens are not thereby vested with Article III standing to litigate the structural claim in federal court. *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35.

Even if Bond could identify some generalized federalism concern, her own prosecution supplies no basis for standing. The Tenth Amendment limits federal authority in the abstract; it does not confer upon an individual defendant a personal immunity from a federal criminal statute that Congress validly enacted under the Treaty Power and Necessary and Proper Clause. See *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432-33 (1920); *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004). Bond’s complaint is therefore not that she suffered a personal constitutional injury distinct from the State’s interests, but that the federal government invaded a domain she believes should have been left to state prosecutors. That is a structural claim she may not assert on her own. *Tennessee Electric*, 306 U.S. at 143-44.

Nor does Bond’s guilty plea alter the jurisdictional analysis. Standing is antecedent to merits review and cannot be created by a defendant’s choice to plead guilty while reserving constitutional objections. See *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 94-95. Because Bond is a private criminal defendant, not a State or state officer, she lacks standing to challenge § 229 under the Tenth Amendment. The Court should therefore affirm the judgment below on this threshold ground.
