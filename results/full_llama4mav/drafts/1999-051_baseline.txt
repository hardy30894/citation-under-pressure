The Sixth Circuit's decision in this case is consistent with established Supreme Court precedent, and its holding that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim should be affirmed.

In Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), the Supreme Court held that a state prisoner who fails to comply with a state procedural rule may not obtain federal habeas relief absent a showing of cause and prejudice. Id. at 750. The Court defined "cause" as "a legitimate excuse for the default," and noted that "the existence of cause for a procedural default must ordinarily turn on whether the prisoner can show that some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel's efforts to comply with the State's procedural rule." Id. at 753 (citing Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986)).

In Murray v. Carrier, the Court held that ineffective assistance of counsel can constitute cause to excuse a procedural default. 477 U.S. at 488. The Court reasoned that "if the procedural default is the result of ineffective assistance of counsel, the Sixth Amendment itself requires that responsibility for the default be imputed to the State." Id. at 488.

The question presented here is whether a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can, in turn, serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. The Sixth Circuit correctly held that it can. As the Court noted in Martinez v. Ryan, 566 U.S. 1 (2012), "a procedural default will not bar a federal habeas court from hearing a substantial claim of ineffective assistance at trial if, in the initial-review collateral proceeding, there was no counsel or counsel in that proceeding was ineffective." Id. at 17. While Martinez addressed a specific context (initial-review collateral proceedings), its reasoning is applicable here.

The Court's decision in Edwards v. Carpenter, 529 U.S. 446 (2000), is not to the contrary. In Edwards, the Court held that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim, unless the ineffective-assistance claim was itself properly exhausted. Id. at 453. However, the Court did not address the situation where the ineffective-assistance claim is itself a constitutional claim that can serve as cause.

Here, Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim is a constitutional claim that can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The Sixth Circuit correctly held that the District Court's determination that Carpenter's appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective was not procedurally defaulted, and that this claim could serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of the sufficiency claim.

The Sixth Circuit's decision is consistent with the Supreme Court's precedent, and its holding that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim should be affirmed.
