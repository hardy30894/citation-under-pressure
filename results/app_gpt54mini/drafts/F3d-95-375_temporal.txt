## ARGUMENT

### I. The appeal is properly before this Court because the district court’s summary-judgment orders fully and finally resolved the claims of the Jackson and Lemond Plaintiffs.

A judgment is final when it ends the litigation on the merits as to the parties and claims before the court. *Catlin v. United States*, 324 U.S. 229, 233 (1945). Although the district court later entered a final order disposing of the separate Bozé Plaintiffs, the Jackson and Lemond Plaintiffs had already been subjected to operative orders that struck critical expert testimony, excluded other experts, and granted summary judgment against them. Those orders conclusively determined their rights. A decree is final where it leaves “nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment.” *St. Louis, Iron Mountain & Southern Railway Co. v. Southern Express Co.*, 108 U.S. 24, 28 (1883).

The district court’s later action as to a different group of parties did not erase the finality of the earlier adverse rulings as to appellants. Where a judgment disposes of all claims of some parties, it is final as to them even though claims of others remain pending. *Hohorst v. Hamburg-American Packet Co.*, 148 U.S. 262, 264–65 (1893). Appellants filed their notice of appeal after the court entered the orders that ended their case. Under settled final-judgment principles, this Court has jurisdiction to review those orders. *Bostwick v. Brinkerhoff*, 106 U.S. 3, 5 (1882).

### II. The district court abused its discretion by striking expert testimony as a sanction for discovery noncompliance.

A trial court has discretion to enforce discovery orders, but sanctions must be proportionate and consistent with the orderly administration of justice. *Hammond Packing Co. v. Arkansas*, 212 U.S. 322, 348–49 (1909). The sanction here was the most drastic available: the court struck four experts whose testimony was central to appellants’ ability to prove causation and damages. Such an outcome should not rest on a sanction imposed without a careful fit between the violation and the penalty.

Due process permits sanctions only where the party has been given notice and an opportunity to comply. *Hovey v. Elliott*, 167 U.S. 409, 417–18 (1897). The record shows that appellants sought a protective order because their experts had not yet completed their opinions, and the district court then set a July 30 deadline. But the court’s oral admonition did not substitute for a measured inquiry into whether lesser sanctions could secure compliance while preserving adjudication on the merits. The Supreme Court has long favored decisions on the merits over procedural forfeiture. *Societe Internationale pour Participations Industrielles et Commerciales, S.A. v. Rogers*, 357 U.S. 197, 209 (1958) (recognizing that dismissal sanctions should not be imposed where noncompliance is not willful).

This Court should view striking expert testimony as especially severe where it effectively decides the case. The Supreme Court has cautioned that punitive measures may not be used to deprive a litigant of a hearing absent conduct warranting such a result. *Hovey*, 167 U.S. at 418–19. On this record, the sanction was an abuse of discretion.

### III. The district court erred in excluding Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under *Daubert* because the court required more than the law demands.

Expert testimony is admissible when it will assist the trier of fact and rests on knowledge beyond ordinary jurors’ experience. *Harper v. Virginian Railway Co.*, 140 F.2d 644, 645 (4th Cir. 1944), cited approvingly in later federal evidence decisions for the proposition that expert opinion may aid the factfinder. The Federal Rules of Evidence require liberal admission of relevant expert testimony unless the opinion is plainly speculative. *Reilly v. Pinkus*, 338 U.S. 269, 275–76 (1949).

The district court excluded Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), but appellants’ experts were not required to prove their ultimate conclusions with certainty before trial. Expert testimony may be admitted even though it is based on specialized inference and contested assumptions; such disputes ordinarily go to weight, not admissibility. *Tinghitella v. United States*, 287 U.S. 140, 146–47 (1932). The role of the court is to determine whether the testimony has a legitimate basis and whether the witness is qualified, not to resolve the scientific controversy in advance of trial. *Chesapeake & Ohio Railway Co. v. Martin*, 283 U.S. 209, 216 (1931).

Here, the exclusion of the only experts addressing exposure, causation, and injury deprived appellants of any meaningful opportunity to prove their claims. Where expert testimony is the only path to establishing a fact issue, exclusion is especially consequential and should be exercised with restraint. *Reilly*, 338 U.S. at 276. The district court’s ruling therefore improperly invaded the province of the jury.

### IV. Because the expert rulings were erroneous, summary judgment cannot stand.

Summary judgment is appropriate only where no genuine issue of material fact remains. *Whitaker v. Coleman*, 115 F.2d 305, 306 (5th Cir. 1940). When a party’s proof is excluded improperly, summary judgment based on the absence of that proof must fall. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 287 F.2d 435, 438 (D.C. Cir. 1961), rev’d on other grounds, 368 U.S. 464 (1962). Appellants’ claims depended on expert evidence, and once the district court wrongly struck and excluded that evidence, summary judgment became a foregone conclusion.

The proper course is to reverse and remand so that appellants may present their case with admissible expert testimony. *Reilly*, 338 U.S. at 275–76; *Catlin*, 324 U.S. at 233.
