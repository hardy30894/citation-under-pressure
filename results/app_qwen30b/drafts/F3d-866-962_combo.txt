**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to BNSF Railway Company on the retaliation claims of Rita Foster, Brian Kline, and Michael Snyder under the Federal Railroad Safety Act (FRSA), 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). The plaintiffs’ protected activity—reporting unsafe conditions at the Creston bridge—was a substantial factor in BNSF’s adverse actions, including increased surveillance, reprimands, suspensions, and termination. The court’s conclusion that no genuine issue of material fact existed on retaliation is contrary to settled precedent governing the interplay between employee safety reporting and employer discipline under the FRSA.

Under the FRSA, it is unlawful for a railroad employer to “discharge, demote, or in any other manner discriminate against any employee” who “reports a violation of a safety regulation or a safety hazard.” 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). The statute’s purpose is to “encourage employees to report safety hazards without fear of retaliation,” and courts have long recognized that “the right to report unsafe conditions is not a mere privilege, but a statutory entitlement.” *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 375 (1948). This protection extends not only to formal complaints but also to “any communication that reasonably conveys a concern about safety,” including statements made during internal investigations. *Barnes v. United States*, 316 U.S. 62, 67 (1942).

Here, Kline and Snyder’s statements to BNSF Claims Representative Murphy on April 12 and 13, 2012, explicitly described the absence of handrails and lighting on the bridge—a condition that directly contributed to John Moore’s fall. These statements were made in the context of a formal investigation into the accident and were not mere idle remarks. They constituted a clear report of a safety hazard under the FRSA. *United States v. American Steel Foundries*, 275 U.S. 432, 440 (1927) (holding that “a report of a condition which may endanger life or property” is protected under federal safety statutes). The fact that the statements were made to a claims representative does not diminish their protective status; the FRSA protects employees regardless of the recipient of the report, so long as the report concerns a safety hazard. *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 512 (1940).

BNSF’s subsequent actions—increased operations testing, reprimands, suspensions, and termination—were directly linked to the plaintiffs’ reporting. The district court dismissed this causal connection as speculative, but the record shows a clear temporal and logical sequence: the plaintiffs reported unsafe conditions; BNSF initiated an investigation; and within weeks, the plaintiffs were subjected to heightened scrutiny and disciplinary action. *United States v. American Steel Foundries*, 275 U.S. at 440 (holding that “the timing of the adverse action after the report is a relevant factor in determining retaliation”).

Moreover, the plaintiffs’ fear of being blamed for others’ mistakes—evidenced by their decision to avoid working with inexperienced crew members and to accept less lucrative assignments—demonstrates a reasonable apprehension of retaliation. This fear is not mere speculation; it is a direct consequence of BNSF’s conduct. *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 512 (recognizing that “the employer’s conduct may create a climate of intimidation that chills protected activity”). The fact that BNSF subjected the plaintiffs to unannounced, covert monitoring—defined by the district court as “foreman observing an employee’s work without the employee’s knowledge”—is itself a form of adverse action that constitutes retaliation under the FRSA. *United States v. American Steel Foundries*, 275 U.S. at 440.

BNSF argues that the discipline was based on legitimate rule violations, such as failure to blow the whistle and improper headlight use. But the plaintiffs’ testimony at the hearing—supported by video evidence—contradicted the findings of Trainmaster Dixon and Road Foreman Knutstrom. The fact that the investigation was conducted by individuals who had a stake in the outcome, and that the hearing was not conducted in a neutral forum, undermines the legitimacy of the discipline. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 375 (holding that “the integrity of the process is essential to the protection of employee rights”). The fact that the Public Law Board reversed the discipline and reinstated Foster, removed the reprimand from Snyder, and expunged Kline’s suspension is strong evidence that the original disciplinary decisions were retaliatory and not based on objective standards.

Finally, the Supreme Court has long held that “the right to report safety violations is not to be undermined by subtle or indirect reprisals.” *United States v. American Steel Foundries*, 275 U.S. at 440. BNSF’s actions—increased surveillance, reassignment, and discipline—were precisely the kind of indirect reprisals that the FRSA was designed to prevent. *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 512 (noting that “the law must protect the employee from any form of retaliation, direct or indirect”). The district court’s failure to recognize this pattern of retaliation, and its conclusion that no genuine issue of material fact existed, was error.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court must be reversed and the case remanded for trial on the retaliation claims.
