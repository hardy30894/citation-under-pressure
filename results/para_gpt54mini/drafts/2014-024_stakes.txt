The conviction cannot stand because § 875(c) is not a strict-liability offense, and the First Amendment independently requires proof that the defendant meant his words as a threat or knew they would be understood that way. This Court has repeatedly read criminal statutes to require “awareness of some wrongdoing” when Congress is silent on mens rea. *Liparota v. United States*, 471 U.S. 419, 425 (1985); *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250–63 (1952). The Court has likewise refused to interpret statutes to impose severe criminal punishment absent a culpable mental state. *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605–19 (1994); *X-Citement Video, Inc. v. United States*, 513 U.S. 64, 71–73 (1994).

Section 875(c) contains no mens rea term, but it criminalizes only the transmission of “any communication containing any threat.” 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). That text does not say that an objectively frightening statement suffices regardless of the speaker’s purpose. Where Congress omits mens rea, courts ordinarily “read into the statute only that mens rea which is necessary to separate wrongful conduct from otherwise innocent conduct.” *Carter v. United States*, 530 U.S. 255, 269 (2000) (internal quotation marks omitted). Here, the Ninth Circuit has recognized that a true-threat prosecution must be limited by a culpable mental state. *United States v. Bagdasarian*, 652 F.3d 1113, 1117–18 (9th Cir. 2011).

The Third Circuit’s objective standard cannot be squared with *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003). There, the Court held that “true threats” are “those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” *Id.* at 359. That formulation focuses on the speaker’s intent, not merely on whether a reasonable listener might react with fear. The Court also explained that the prohibition on true threats protects individuals “from the fear of violence” and from “the disruption that fear engenders.” *Id.* at 360. But protecting listeners from fear does not justify punishing speech when the speaker lacked any intent to threaten. The First Amendment permits punishment of threats because such speech “is not protected,” *id.* at 359, not because juries may convict on an objective negligence standard.

That distinction is central. An objective test sweeps in speech that is crude, exaggerated, hyperbolic, or artistic, even when the speaker does not actually threaten. This Court has repeatedly recognized that context matters in distinguishing protected speech from criminal threats. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam); *NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886, 927–29 (1982). Elonis’s Facebook posts were plainly offensive and disturbing, but the Constitution does not allow a felony conviction unless the government proves the requisite subjective culpability.

The government may rely on the proposition that speech may be punished when a reasonable person would perceive a threat. But *Watts* rejected a literal reading of threatening language where the statement was political hyperbole, 394 U.S. at 708, and *Black* used language of intentional communication of violence, 538 U.S. at 359. The lower court’s rule collapses the constitutional inquiry into the perspective of the listener alone. That is incompatible with the Court’s repeated insistence that criminal liability ordinarily turns on the defendant’s guilty mind. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250–51; *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 607.

The error was not harmless. The jury was instructed that it could convict if Elonis “intentionally ma[de] a statement” that a reasonable person would view as threatening. That instruction allowed conviction even if Elonis intended his posts as venting, parody, or rap-style expression rather than as threats. Because the Third Circuit affirmed on an objective standard, the conviction rests on an unconstitutional and statutorily overbroad theory.

The judgment should be reversed.
