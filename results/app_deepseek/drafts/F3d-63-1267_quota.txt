## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Denying Judgment as a Matter of Law Because Stelwagon Failed to Prove Actual Antitrust Damages.

A Robinson-Patman plaintiff must prove not only a prima facie violation but also “actual injury” causally linked to the discrimination. *J. Truett Payne Co. v. Chrysler Motors Corp.*, 451 U.S. 557, 562 (1981). Even assuming a violation, Stelwagon presented no evidence that Tarmac’s allegedly discriminatory prices caused it to lose sales or profits. Stelwagon’s damage expert merely calculated a “lost profits” figure based on the assumption that it would have made every sale Tarmac made to competitors—an assumption unsupported by market realities. This is precisely the type of speculative proof the Supreme Court rejected in *Payne*, holding that a plaintiff must show “that it actually lost sales or profits as a result of the discrimination.” *Id.* at 565.

Moreover, Stelwagon failed to establish antitrust injury—the type of injury the antitrust laws were designed to prevent. *Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc.*, 429 U.S. 477, 489 (1977). Stelwagon’s claimed injury—lost profits from competition—is not the kind of injury that flows from price discrimination; rather, it is the ordinary consequence of lawful competition. *See Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, 594 (1986) (antitrust laws protect competition, not competitors).

Damages must be proven with “reasonable certainty,” not left to speculation. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946); *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931). Stelwagon’s expert offered no evidence of actual lost customers, lost market share, or any specific transactions it would have won but for the discrimination. This Court has consistently required such proof. *See American Bearing Co. v. Litton Indus., Inc.*, 729 F.2d 943, 953 (3d Cir. 1984) (damages must be “based on concrete evidence of actual loss”). The district court itself recognized the damages were “grossly excessive” and unsupported, yet it denied JMOL. *Stelwagon Mfg. Co. v. Tarmac Roofing Sys., Inc.*, 862 F. Supp. 1361, 1369 (E.D. Pa. 1994). That was error.

### II. The Breach of Contract Claim Fails as a Matter of Law.

The alleged oral exclusive distributorship agreement is unenforceable under Pennsylvania’s statute of frauds because it could not be performed within one year. *See* 33 Pa. Stat. Ann. § 1 (West 1995); *Albert v. Schenley Distillers, Inc.*, 385 A.2d 462, 464 (Pa. Super. 1978) (oral exclusive dealing agreement for indefinite duration unenforceable). Moreover, Stelwagon provided no consideration beyond its preexisting duty to promote Tarmac’s products. *See Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 73 (1981)*. Even if enforceable, Stelwagon failed to prove damages with reasonable certainty—its expert’s lost-profits model was identical to the antitrust damages model and equally speculative. *See Spang & Co. v. USX Corp.*, 599 A.2d 978, 983 (Pa. Super. 1991) (contract damages must be proven with reasonable certainty).

### III. The District Court’s Remittitur Did Not Cure the Fundamental Defect.

A remittitur cannot substitute for a complete failure of proof. *See Rose v. Bartle*, 871 F.2d 331, 340 (3d Cir. 1989) (JMOL appropriate when evidence insufficient). The district court’s reduction of damages from $2,272,000 to $450,383.60 was arbitrary and still unsupported by any record evidence. *See Dimick v. Schiedt*, 293 U.S. 474, 486 (1935) (remittitur must be based on evidence, not judicial speculation). Because Stelwagon failed to prove any actual damages, the district court should have granted judgment for Tarmac.

### Conclusion

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law and direct entry of judgment in favor of Tarmac on all claims.
