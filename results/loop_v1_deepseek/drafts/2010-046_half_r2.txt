## ARGUMENT

The Prison Litigation Reform Act does not bar the three-judge court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy ongoing Eighth Amendment violations. The order satisfies each of the PLRA’s requirements: it is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the constitutional violations, and is the least intrusive means of doing so. The three-judge court expressly found that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the unconstitutional conditions and that no other relief would remedy the violation. Those findings are amply supported by the record and are entitled to deference.

Federal courts have long possessed the equitable power to remedy constitutional violations by ordering appropriate relief tailored to the wrong. The conditions in California’s prisons—pervasive inadequate medical care, untreated mental illness, and preventable deaths—violate the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishments. *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1879) (holding that punishments involving “torture or a lingering death” are “cruel and unusual”). The systemic failures in California’s prisons are precisely the kind of barbarous treatment the Eighth Amendment forbids.

This Court has repeatedly affirmed the breadth of federal equitable authority to remedy constitutional wrongs. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954), the Court declared that in fashioning equitable relief, courts will be guided by “equitable principles” and that traditional equity requires consideration of “the great variety of local conditions.” *Id.* at 495-496. The Court further explained that courts may require “a prompt and reasonable start toward full compliance” and may order additional steps to achieve a constitutional system. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955). A court may, in its remedial discretion, require the necessary steps to bring a State into constitutional compliance, even when those steps involve the release of prisoners.

Similarly, in *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 16-17 (1958), this Court reaffirmed that state officers are bound by the Constitution and that federal courts have the authority to enforce constitutional requirements against state actors. *Id.* at 19. The principle is clear: a federal court’s equitable power may reach state policies and practices that violate the Constitution.

The PLRA does not displace these fundamental equitable principles. The PLRA’s requirements—that relief be “narrowly drawn,” “extend no further than necessary,” and be the “least intrusive means”—echo traditional equitable standards. They do not erect a per se bar to population caps when the court finds, as it did here, that nothing short of a significant reduction in the inmate population will cure the constitutional violation.

Moreover, the three-judge court complied with the PLRA’s procedural requirements. The order was issued by a three-judge panel convened under 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(B). The court made the requisite findings that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the constitutional violations and that “no other relief” would suffice. The court also found that the order is the “least intrusive means” of correcting the violation. These findings are supported by voluminous evidence, including the State’s own admissions that for years it failed to provide constitutionally adequate medical care and that overcrowding made compliance impossible.

The State’s suggestion that the PLRA forbids any order requiring the release of prisoners is incorrect. The PLRA permits a prisoner release order if the three-judge court finds by clear and convincing evidence that crowding is the “primary cause” of the constitutional violation and that “no other relief” will remedy it. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii). The court made both findings here. The State’s argument would effectively read these provisions out of the statute, rendering the PLRA a bar to any remedy regardless of the severity of the constitutional violation.

This Court has long recognized that federal courts may enforce the Constitution even against recalcitrant States. In *Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339, 346-347 (1880), the Court held that “the Constitution of the United States, in its own force and by its own operation, binds all persons, whether States or individuals, to the observance of its provisions.” *Id.* at 347. The Court further explained that “the judicial power of the United States can be exerted to compel the performance of that duty” when a State defaults. *Id.* at 346. The three-judge court acted within that tradition.

Finally, this Court has long recognized that federal courts may hold persons in contempt for defiance of orders necessary to effectuate constitutional remedies. In *Ex parte Robinson*, 86 U.S. 505, 510-511 (1874), the Court affirmed that the power to punish for contempt is “inherent in all courts” and that “its preservation is essential to the administration of justice” and necessary to enable courts “to enforce their orders, judgments, and decrees.” *Id.* at 511. The State consented to the original remedial orders and to the appointment of the Receiver; it cannot now claim immunity from the consequences of its own failure to comply.

The PLRA does not insulate California from its constitutional obligations. The three-judge court’s order is a lawful exercise of equitable authority, fully consistent with the statute and with this Court’s precedents. The judgment should be affirmed.
