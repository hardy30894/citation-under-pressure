# ARGUMENT

## I. THE ACT IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION THAT SATISFIES INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY

This Court has long recognized that the government may impose reasonable time-place-manner restrictions on speech occurring in public fora, provided such restrictions "are justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech, . . . are narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and . . . leave open ample alternative channels for communication." *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). The Massachusetts Act satisfies each requirement.

**A. The Act Is Content-Neutral.**

The Act does not single out any topic, viewpoint, or message. It restricts entry into a defined thirty-five-foot zone around reproductive health care facilities "regardless of viewpoint." *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 719 (2000). A pro-life counselor and a pro-choice escort are equally forbidden from standing within the buffer absent an exemption. This Court has already sustained a strikingly similar statute against a content-neutrality challenge in *Hill*, holding that a Colorado law restricting "protest," "education," and "counseling" within eight feet of persons near a health care facility was content-neutral because it applied to "all demonstrators," not particular messages. *Id.* at 723–25. The Massachusetts Act is, if anything, more plainly neutral, since it employs no speech-defined triggers at all—merely a fixed geographic buffer applicable to "any person" absent enumerated, content-independent exemptions. Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½(b), (c).

Petitioners argue the exemptions for clinic employees and patients render the statute content- or viewpoint-based. But this Court rejected the analogous argument in *Hill*, explaining that a statute is not content-based merely because enforcement requires examining "what is actually said" to determine whether a person falls within an exemption. 530 U.S. at 721. The exemptions here—for patients, employees, and passersby—turn on status and location, not message, precisely the sort of "facially neutral" line this Court has approved. *See also Members of City Council of L.A. v. Taxpayers for Vincent*, 466 U.S. 789, 804 (1984) (ordinance neutral where "there is not even a hint of bias or censorship").

**B. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored.**

A time-place-manner regulation need not be the least restrictive means available; it need only be narrowly tailored to serve the government's significant interest, meaning it does not "burden substantially more speech than is necessary." *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799. Massachusetts's interests—ensuring "public safety," "unobstructed access" to healthcare facilities, and protection of "the right to obtain medical services in an unobstructed manner"—are precisely the interests this Court deemed sufficient in *Hill*. 530 U.S. at 715. Indeed, this Court in *Hill* upheld a buffer even *larger* in the aggregate—a hundred-foot regulated zone with an eight-foot no-approach rule—reasoning that "the State's interests . . . justify a modest restriction." *Id.* at 729–30.

The First Circuit correctly found that thirty-five feet is a "measured response" calibrated to Massachusetts's documented history of clinic obstruction, harassment, and violence, following two prior iterations of buffer-zone legislation that this same court had upheld. *McGuire v. Reilly*, 260 F.3d 36 (1st Cir. 2001); *McGuire v. Reilly*, 386 F.3d 45 (1st Cir. 2004). The legislature is entitled to rely on this record. *See Burson v. Freeman*, 504 U.S. 191, 211 (1992) (plurality) (upholding a 100-foot campaign-free zone around polling places based on legislative judgment about the "practical problems of preventing intimidation and election fraud").

**C. Ample Alternative Channels Remain.**

The trial record—unrebutted—demonstrates that petitioners retain robust means of communication: they may speak at conversational volume just outside the buffer, use amplification, display signs, distribute literature, wear expressive clothing, and pray individually or in groups. Plaintiff McCullen herself testified to persuading approximately eighty women against abortion *after* the buffer zones took effect. Such continued effectiveness confirms that alternative channels are not merely theoretical but genuinely adequate, satisfying *Ward*'s requirement. 491 U.S. at 791; *see also Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474, 484 (1988) (upholding a total ban on targeted residential picketure where other means of communicating remained open).

## II. *HILL v. COLORADO* WAS CORRECTLY DECIDED AND CONTROLS THIS CASE

Petitioners' invitation to overrule *Hill* should be declined. *Stare decisis* "promotes the evenhanded, predictable, and consistent development of legal principles," *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827 (1991), and petitioners identify no "special justification," *id.*, for departing from precedent barely a decade old and twice reaffirmed by the First Circuit's careful application in the *McGuire* line of cases and in *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir. 2009).

Petitioners' reliance on intervening decisions such as *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), and *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), is misplaced. Those cases addressed wholly different contexts—campaign speech, funeral picketing, and pharmaceutical marketing data—and articulated conventional, unremarkable First Amendment principles rather than casting doubt on the specific analytic framework *Hill* applied to buffer zones around healthcare facilities. As the First Circuit observed, petitioners cannot use "conventional First Amendment principles recited . . . in the context of factual scenarios far different than the scenario at issue here" as a "wedge to subvert" *Hill*'s specific holding.

*Hill* remains sound because it correctly recognized the "unique" and "narrow" problem of protecting patients' "right to be let alone" in the vulnerable moments before entering a medical facility, 530 U.S. at 716–17, while preserving speakers' ability to engage in "personal, sympathetic" communication, *id.* at 729. That balance—reasonable, content-neutral, narrowly tailored—remains the correct constitutional approach today.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
