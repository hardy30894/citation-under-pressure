**ARGUMENT**

**I. DEBTS “FOR MONEY . . . OBTAINED BY . . . ACTUAL FRAUD” UNDER § 523(a)(2)(A) INCLUDE ONLY THE VALUE OF THE PROPERTY FRAUDULENTLY OBTAINED, NOT PUNITIVE, TREBLE, OR OTHER NON-COMPENSATORY AWARDS.**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt . . . for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by . . . actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The plain language of this provision limits nondischargeability to the “extent” of the value actually “obtained by” the fraud. Compensatory damages for losses directly caused by fraud fit within this scope. Punitive, treble, or other non-compensatory awards do not, because they are not “obtained by” the debtor from the victim. Instead, they are imposed by law as a sanction, entirely independent of any property the debtor received.

The Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit correctly recognized this distinction in *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir. 1991), holding that punitive damages awarded in a fraud action are dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). As the *Levy* court explained, “the plain language of the statute indicates that the only debt excepted from discharge is one for money, property, services, or credit ‘to the extent obtained by’ fraud.” *Id.* at 198. The “obtained by” language naturally limits the exception to “the value of the money, property, services, or credit that the debtor fraudulently obtained.” *Id.* at 198. Punitive damages, by contrast, “are not damages ‘for’ money obtained by fraud, but rather are damages imposed to punish the debtor and to deter future misconduct.” *Id.* at 198. Because punitive damages are not “obtained by” the debtor from the creditor, they fall outside the statutory exception.

The Third Circuit’s contrary holding in this case—that treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are nondischargeable—cannot be reconciled with the statutory text. The treble award here was not money, property, services, or credit that Cohen “obtained” from his tenants. He obtained $31,382.50 in excessive rent. The treble damages of $94,147.50 were a statutory punishment imposed by the court. The compensatory component of that award is nondischargeable; the punitive trebling is not. *See* *Cohen v. de la Cruz*, 523 U.S. 213, 220 (1998) (majority opinion) (rejecting this argument). 

**II. THE “FRESH START” POLICY FAVORS A NARROW CONSTRUCTION OF DISCHARGE EXCEPTIONS, INCLUDING § 523(a)(2)(A).**

The Bankruptcy Code’s “fresh start” policy is central to its remedial scheme. As this Court has repeatedly held, exceptions to discharge “should be strictly construed against the creditor and liberally construed in favor of the debtor.” *Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915); *see also* *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991) (acknowledging that the Code provides a “fresh start” for the “honest but unfortunate debtor”). This principle requires that any ambiguity in § 523(a)(2)(A) be resolved in favor of discharge.

The phrase “to the extent obtained by” is ambiguous. It could refer either to the entire debt arising from the fraudulent transaction (including punitive, treble, and attorney’s fees awards) or solely to the value of the property fraudulently obtained. The traditional rule of strict construction resolves this ambiguity in favor of the narrower reading. *See* *Brown v. Felsen*, 442 U.S. 127, 135 (1979) (noting that exceptions to discharge are narrowly construed). A broad reading would enable creditors to convert dischargeable punitive awards into nondischargeable debt by linking them to fraud, undermining the Code’s rehabilitative purpose.

The majority in this case, however, adopted an expansive interpretation, holding that any debt that “results from” fraud—including treble damages—is nondischargeable. *Cohen v. de la Cruz*, 523 U.S. at 220. This approach unduly widens the exception. It penalizes the debtor not merely for what he took, but for the legal consequences of his conduct. That result conflicts with the Code’s design to give honest debtors a meaningful opportunity to reorder their financial affairs. *See* *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 680 (11th Cir. 1993) (adopting the opposite view). 

**III. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN HOLDING THAT TREBLE DAMAGES UNDER THE NEW JERSEY CONSUMER FRAUD ACT ARE NONDISCHARGEABLE BECAUSE THEY ARE “FORESEEABLE.”**

The Third Circuit reasoned that the treble damages award was nondischargeable because the New Jersey statute made the penalty “entirely foreseeable by the debtor at the time he made the false representations.” *Cohen v. de la Cruz*, 523 U.S. at 221 (quoting court of appeals’ rationale). This rationale mistakes foreseeability for the statutory requirement. Section 523(a)(2)(A) does not ask whether the debtor could have anticipated being punished; it asks whether the debt is “for money . . . obtained by” fraud. A foreseeable penalty is still a penalty, not a debt “for” property obtained. The statute’s focus is on the debtor’s gain, not the creditor’s remedy. 

Foreseeability may be relevant to whether the debtor intended to deceive, but it cannot transform a punitive award into a compensatory one. *See* *Kawaauhau v. Geiger*, 523 U.S. 57, 62 (1998) (rejecting a foreseeability test for dischargeability under § 523(a)(6)). The same logic applies here. The treble damages were not “obtained by” fraud; they were imposed by law. They are therefore dischargeable.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
