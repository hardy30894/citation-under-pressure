**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Claim for Nominal Damages Does Not Prevent Mootness When the Underlying Constitutional Challenge Is Otherwise Moot.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that Appellants’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief became moot when Georgia Gwinnett College (“GGC”) revised its policies and Uzuegbunam graduated. *See Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc). The sole remaining claim—for nominal damages—cannot revive an otherwise moot constitutional challenge.

This Court has long recognized that nominal damages serve a symbolic function, but they do not create a live case or controversy where none otherwise exists. *See Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256 (holding that “a prayer for nominal damages will not, standing alone, save an otherwise moot case”). The Eleventh Circuit applied this principle directly, concluding that Appellants’ “claims for nominal damages could not save their otherwise moot constitutional challenges.” *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*, No. 18-12676, slip op. at 3 (11th Cir. July 1, 2019). That holding is consistent with the weight of federal authority. *See, e.g., Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 109 (1998) (holding that a plaintiff must demonstrate a “personal injury fairly traceable to the defendant’s allegedly unlawful conduct” to maintain standing, and that nominal damages alone cannot satisfy Article III’s case-or-controversy requirement).

Appellants argue that their claim for nominal damages distinguishes this case from *Flanigan’s*, but the Eleventh Circuit correctly rejected that argument. The court noted that Appellants’ complaint sought only nominal damages, not compensatory damages, and that the factual allegations did not describe any actual injury. *Uzuegbunam*, slip op. at 10-11. As the court explained, “compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit [must] be based on actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the ‘abstract value’ of the constitutional rights that may have been violated.” *Id.* at 10 (quoting *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000)). Because Appellants alleged no such injury, their claim for nominal damages was insufficient to maintain a live controversy.

**II. Appellants’ Request for Compensatory Damages Was Not Properly Pled.**

Appellants contend that their complaint included a request for compensatory damages, but the Eleventh Circuit correctly found otherwise. The complaint’s prayer for relief explicitly requested only “nominal damages,” and the factual allegations described no actual injury. *Uzuegbunam*, slip op. at 10-11. The court properly rejected Appellants’ “after-the-fact contentions” that they sought compensatory damages, noting that the complaint mentioned “injury” only twice and did not specify any concrete harm. *Id.* at 10.

This Court has consistently held that a plaintiff must plead actual injury to recover compensatory damages under § 1983. *See Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 264 (1978) (holding that compensatory damages require proof of “actual injury”). Appellants’ failure to allege any such injury—whether monetary loss, physical pain, emotional distress, or reputational harm—doomed their claim. *Uzuegbunam*, slip op. at 10-11.

**III. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Denying Leave to Amend.**

Appellants argue that the district court should have permitted them to amend their complaint to add a request for compensatory damages. But the Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the district court did not abuse its discretion. *Uzuegbunam*, slip op. at 18-20. Appellants sought leave to amend only in a response to a motion to dismiss, not through a properly filed motion. *Id.* at 18. This Court has made clear that “[w]here a request for leave to file an amended complaint simply is imbedded within an opposition memorandum, the issue has not been raised properly.” *Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (quoting *Rosenberg v. Gould*, 554 F.3d 962, 967 (11th Cir. 2009)). Moreover, Appellants failed to specify the substance of their proposed amendment, as required by *Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC*, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018). The district court therefore acted within its discretion.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
