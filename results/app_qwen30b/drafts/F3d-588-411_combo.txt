**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s supplemental jury instruction on the term “shielding” violated the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment by rendering the statutory definition of a criminal offense unconstitutionally vague. The instruction, which defined “shielding” as “the act of preventing or attempting to prevent the government from discovering or detecting illegal aliens,” was not only inconsistent with the settled meaning of the term under prior precedent but also failed to provide the jury with a sufficiently clear standard to guide its determination of guilt. This failure deprived Mr. Ye of fair notice of the conduct proscribed by § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) and subjected him to conviction based on a standard so broad and amorphous that it invites arbitrary enforcement. The instruction, therefore, violated the constitutional requirement that criminal statutes be sufficiently definite to inform individuals of the conduct that is prohibited. See *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922) (“The law must be sufficiently definite to give notice to those who are to be governed by it.”).

The term “shielding” is not a common-law concept, nor is it defined in the statute itself. In such cases, courts must look to the ordinary meaning of the term at the time of enactment and to prior judicial interpretations to determine its scope. See *United States v. Wiltberger*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 76, 95 (1820) (“The meaning of words in a statute must be determined by their ordinary acceptation.”). The term “shielding” connotes a protective or defensive act—something that physically or legally guards or defends from harm or exposure. It does not, as the district court’s instruction suggested, encompass mere failure to report or to cooperate with government inquiries. Indeed, in *United States v. Borden*, 287 U.S. 109, 113 (1932), the Court emphasized that “the language of a statute must be construed in the light of its purpose and context,” and that “a criminal statute must be so clear that men of common intelligence can understand what is prohibited.” The instruction here, by equating “shielding” with any act that might “prevent” detection—even passive inaction—transcended the ordinary meaning of the term and expanded the scope of the offense beyond what Congress could have intended.

Moreover, the instruction failed to distinguish between conduct that is inherently protective and conduct that is merely negligent or non-compliant. In *United States v. Lurton*, 263 U.S. 115, 120 (1923), the Court held that “a statute which makes criminal conduct which is not clearly defined is void for vagueness.” The instruction here, by equating the failure to produce documents with “shielding,” effectively criminalized non-compliance with administrative requirements—a category of conduct that has long been treated as civil or regulatory, not criminal. See *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953) (noting that “the line between criminal and civil liability must be drawn with care” and that “the criminal law should not be used to enforce administrative regulations unless Congress clearly so intended”).

The instruction also failed to incorporate the necessary mens rea requirement. The term “shielding” implies an intent to conceal or protect, not mere knowledge of the alien’s status. In *United States v. Borden*, 287 U.S. at 113, the Court reiterated that “a criminal statute must be so clear that men of common intelligence can understand what is prohibited.” The instruction, however, did not require the jury to find that Ye acted with the specific intent to prevent detection. Instead, it allowed conviction based on conduct that could be interpreted as mere indifference or administrative oversight. This omission violated the principle established in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. at 382, that “no man can be punished for an act which the law does not clearly declare to be a crime.”

The Seventh Circuit’s own precedent, *United States v. Bowers*, 178 F.2d 877 (7th Cir. 1949), supports this position. In *Bowers*, the court held that “a criminal statute must be so definite that a person of common intelligence can understand what conduct is prohibited.” The instruction here, by equating the failure to produce documents with “shielding,” failed this test. Similarly, in *United States v. Hines*, 175 F.2d 875 (7th Cir. 1949), the court emphasized that “a conviction cannot rest upon a definition so broad as to include conduct which is not within the scope of the statute.” The instruction here did precisely that.

Furthermore, the instruction was inconsistent with the principle that criminal liability must be based on conduct that is “clearly within the scope of the statute.” See *United States v. Borden*, 287 U.S. at 113. The instruction allowed the jury to convict based on conduct that was not inherently protective—such as not keeping time cards or not filing forms—without requiring proof of an affirmative act to prevent detection. This is contrary to *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 633, which cautioned against using criminal penalties to enforce administrative compliance absent clear statutory language.

Finally, the instruction failed to comport with the constitutional requirement of fair notice. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. at 382, the Court stated that “the law must be sufficiently definite to give notice to those who are to be governed by it.” The instruction here provided no such notice. It allowed conviction for conduct that, under ordinary understanding, does not constitute “shielding” at all. The instruction thus violated the Due Process Clause by failing to provide a standard that a person of common intelligence could understand.

For these reasons, the district court’s supplemental instruction on “shielding” was legally erroneous and constitutionally infirm. It rendered the statute unconstitutionally vague and deprived Mr. Ye of due process. The conviction must be reversed.
