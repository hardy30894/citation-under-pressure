**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—is one of first impression in this Court. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Perry v. MSPB*, 857 F.3d 308 (D.C. Cir. 2017), rests on a strained and legally unsound distinction between “procedural” and “jurisdictional” dismissals that the Supreme Court has never endorsed. The Court’s decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596, 184 L. Ed. 2d 433 (2012), compels a contrary conclusion: when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds—specifically, because the employee’s action was deemed voluntary—judicial review must lie in federal district court, not the Federal Circuit. The D.C. Circuit’s refusal to follow *Kloeckner*’s logic and its reliance on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), as a binding precedent is error. This Court should reverse.

### I. *Kloeckner* Establishes That Mixed Cases Dismissed on Procedural Grounds Are Reviewable in District Court—And Its Logic Applies Equally to Jurisdictional Dismissals

The cornerstone of the D.C. Circuit’s reasoning is its claim that *Kloeckner* “did not involve the precise issue raised by both this case and *Powell*,” and thus does not “eviscerate” its precedent. *Perry*, 857 F.3d at 314. This is a misreading of *Kloeckner*. The Court in *Kloeckner* did not limit its holding to “procedural” grounds; it held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case—defined as one in which an employee “(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” federal law—judicial review lies in district court, regardless of the *reason* for dismissal, so long as the case was properly a mixed case at the outset. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The Court emphasized that “the question is not whether the Board reached the merits, but whether the case is a mixed case.” *Kloeckner*, 133 S. Ct. at 604.

The Court’s reasoning in *Kloeckner* is rooted in statutory interpretation. The Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA) provides that “mixed cases” shall be filed in district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). The statute does not condition review on the Board’s *reason* for dismissal; it conditions it on the *nature* of the claim. As the Court explained, “the statutory language is clear: if the case is a mixed case, review is in district court.” *Kloeckner*, 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Board’s dismissal on *any* ground—procedural or jurisdictional—does not alter the fact that the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB” and alleged discrimination. Id. at 604. The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* on the basis of “procedural” versus “jurisdictional” grounds is not only unsupported by the text of the statute but contradicts the Court’s own analysis.

### II. The D.C. Circuit’s “Jurisdictional vs. Procedural” Distinction Is Not a Valid Legal Category Under the CSRA

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* to uphold a jurisdictional dismissal in the Federal Circuit is inconsistent with *Kloeckner* and the broader statutory framework. *Powell* held that when the MSPB dismisses a case for lack of jurisdiction—because the employee voluntarily accepted the action—review lies in the Federal Circuit. 158 F.3d at 599–600. But *Kloeckner* made clear that the *source* of the dismissal is irrelevant so long as the case qualifies as a mixed case. The Court rejected the idea that “the Board’s jurisdictional determination” should control the forum for review. *Kloeckner*, 133 S. Ct. at 604. Instead, the Court focused on whether the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” Id. at 604.

This statutory focus is consistent with *Garcia v. Department of Homeland Security*, 437 F.3d 1322 (Fed. Cir. 2006), where the Federal Circuit held that the Board lacks jurisdiction over voluntary retirements. Id. at 1328. But *Garcia* does not support the D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that review must go to the Federal Circuit. Rather, it confirms that the Board’s jurisdiction turns on whether the action was voluntary. When the Board finds it was, the case is not appealable. But when the employee alleges that the action was *not* voluntary—because of discrimination or coercion—the case becomes a mixed case under § 7702(a)(1), and review lies in district court.

### III. The D.C. Circuit’s Precedent in *Powell* Is Overruled by *Kloeckner* and Is Inconsistent with the Statutory Scheme

The D.C. Circuit claims it is “bound” by *Powell*. But *Kloeckner* directly contradicts *Powell*’s result. In *Powell*, the court held that a jurisdictional dismissal meant review in the Federal Circuit. *Kloeckner* holds that a mixed case dismissed on *any* ground—procedural or jurisdictional—must be reviewed in district court.
