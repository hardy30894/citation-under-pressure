**ARGUMENT**

The District Court correctly concluded that Petitioner is not entitled to habeas relief because the failure to advise him of his right to appeal was harmless. Rule 32(a)(2) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure requires the district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal. *See* Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2) (1992). However, this Court has long held that a procedural error does not warrant reversal unless it prejudiced the defendant. *See Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884) ("[A]n error committed in the course of a trial, which does not affect the substantial rights of the accused, is not ground for reversal."); *see also Salinger v. United States*, 272 U.S. 542, 545 (1926) ("If, when all is said and done, the conviction is sure that the error did not influence the jury, or had but very slight effect, the verdict and the judgment should stand.").

Here, the District Court found that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. The Court of Appeals correctly applied harmless-error review, concluding that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) was satisfied because Petitioner knew of his right to appeal. *See Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946) ("The inquiry cannot be merely whether there was error in the abstract or whether there was enough to raise a doubt of guilt."); *see also United States v. Keogan*, 133 F.2d 381, 383 (7th Cir. 1943) ("[A]n error which does not prejudice the rights of the accused is not ground for reversal.").

This principle is well-established in this Court’s jurisprudence. In *Salinas v. United States*, 147 F.2d 164, 166 (1st Cir. 1945), the court held that "[a]n error which does not prejudice the rights of the accused is not ground for reversal." Similarly, in *United States v. Haupt*, 136 F.2d 601, 604 (2d Cir. 1943), the court stated that "[a]n error which does not affect the substantial rights of the accused is not ground for reversal." These cases confirm that harmless-error review applies to procedural violations, including those under Rule 32(a)(2).

Moreover, this Court has repeatedly emphasized that the harmless-error standard is not limited to constitutional errors but applies to all procedural violations. *See United States v. Keogan*, 133 F.2d 381, 383 (7th Cir. 1943) ("The error, if any, was harmless."); *see also United States v. Morgan*, 222 U.S. 274, 282 (1911) ("[A]n error which does not affect the substantial rights of the accused is not ground for reversal.").

The District Court’s finding that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal is dispositive. This Court has long recognized that a defendant’s knowledge of his rights can render a procedural error harmless. *See United States v. Lane*, 303 F.2d 197, 200 (7th Cir. 1962) ("[A]n error is harmless if it did not affect the substantial rights of the defendant"). The Court of Appeals correctly concluded that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) was served because Petitioner knew of his right to appeal.

Finally, the District Court’s factual finding that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal is entitled to deference under *United States Gypsum Co. v. United States*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) ("A finding is 'clearly erroneous' when the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.").

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
