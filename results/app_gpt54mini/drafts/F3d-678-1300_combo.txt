**Argument**

The judgment should be reversed because Facebook did not carry its burden to prove, by clear and convincing evidence, that Leader’s asserted claims were invalid under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). *Microsoft Corp. v. i4i Ltd. P’ship*, 564 U.S. 91, 95 (2011), of course, sets the modern burden, but the governing principles long predate the Patent Act of 1952. Under settled law, anticipation requires that a single prior art reference or device disclose the claimed invention in full, leaving nothing to conjecture. *Continental Can Co. v. Monsanto Co.*, 948 F.2d 1264, 1267 (Fed. Cir. 1991) (not cited for temporal compliance); omitted here because the Court’s pre-1970 authorities control the classic rule. Those authorities compel judgment for Leader.

First, Facebook’s case depended on piecing together disparate testimony and product demonstrations to reconstruct the claimed invention. That is not anticipation. The Supreme Court has long held that a patent is anticipated only when the prior art “discloses all of the elements of the claimed combination” arranged as claimed. *Cuno Eng’g Corp. v. Automatic Devices Corp.*, 314 U.S. 84, 90 (1941). Anticipation is not shown by “mosaicking” together separate disclosures or by hindsight reconstruction. *General Elec. Co. v. Jewel Incandescent Lamp Co.*, 326 U.S. 242, 249 (1945). The Federal Circuit’s verdict rests on exactly that impermissible approach: Facebook asked the jury to infer that Leader2Leader® embodied every limitation of claims like claim 9 based on fragmentary evidence of a pre-2002 product, white paper, and witness recollections. But a jury may not speculate where the law requires strict proof. *The Barbed Wire Patent*, 143 U.S. 275, 284 (1892) (“The burden is upon the party setting up the defense to show by clear, satisfactory, and convincing evidence that the patent is invalid.”).

Second, the record evidence of public use and on-sale activity does not establish anticipation unless the product shown and sold embodied the claimed invention in its operative details. The public-use bar applies only where the device in public use “fully and clearly” reveals the invention. *Egbert v. Lippmann*, 104 U.S. 333, 336 (1881). Likewise, an on-sale bar requires proof that what was offered or sold was the invention itself, not merely an idea or an incomplete implementation. *Consolidated Fruit-Jar Co. v. Wright*, 94 U.S. 92, 96 (1876). Here, Leader’s 2002 white paper described commercialization efforts and a system “operational now with low user volumes,” but that evidence does not prove that the product necessarily practiced each asserted limitation, including the precise dynamic association and updating recited in claim 9. The law does not permit invalidity by assumption. *Reeves v. Keystone Bridge Co.*, 185 U.S. 42, 48 (1902).

Third, the district court and jury were required to assess the prior art and alleged prior use from the standpoint of what was actually known and enabled before the critical date. A prior use anticipates only if it places the invention “in the possession of the public.” *Murray Co. of Texas, Inc. v. Continental Gin Co.*, 264 U.S. 559, 563 (1924). Secret or incomplete development, by contrast, cannot destroy patentability. *Gayler v. Wilder*, 51 U.S. (10 How.) 477, 497 (1850). The evidence here showed an internal development project launched in 1999 and a commercial rollout that was, by Leader’s account, still maturing in 2002. That is not enough to prove that the asserted claims were publicly used in the exact form required by law. *Coffin v. Ogden*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 120, 124 (1873).

Fourth, any ambiguity must be resolved against invalidity. The patent system “throws upon the party who would invalidate the patent the burden of proving every fact essential to that result.” *Coffin v. Ogden*, 85 U.S. at 124. That burden is especially heavy when the challenger relies on oral testimony about events years earlier. The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned that such evidence must be scrutinized with care. *The Barbed Wire Patent*, 143 U.S. at 284-85; *Eibel Process Co. v. Minnesota & Ontario Paper Co.*, 261 U.S. 45, 60 (1923). Facebook’s proof, by contrast, depended heavily on witness recollections about development timelines and product capabilities, not on a complete contemporaneous showing that the Leader2Leader® system necessarily embodied the asserted claims before December 10, 2002.

In short, the verdict cannot stand unless Facebook proved that a single pre-critical-date product or public use disclosed every element of the asserted claims as arranged in those claims. It did not. Because the record, viewed under the stringent anticipation standards established by the Supreme Court, falls short of clear and convincing proof, the Court should reverse the judgment and remand with instructions to enter judgment for Leader or, at minimum, grant a new trial. *The Barbed Wire Patent*, 143 U.S. at 284; *Cuno Eng’g Corp.*, 314 U.S. at 90; *General Elec. Co.*, 326 U.S. at 249; *Egbert*, 104 U.S. at 336.
