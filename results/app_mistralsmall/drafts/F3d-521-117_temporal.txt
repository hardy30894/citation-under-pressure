**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE PAT-FRISK OF SOARES WAS UNREASONABLE UNDER THE FOURTH AMENDMENT.**

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals from unreasonable searches and seizures. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 9 (1968). A pat-frisk is a search under the Fourth Amendment, and it is unreasonable unless the officer has reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. *Id.* at 27. The government bears the burden of proving that the pat-frisk was reasonable. *Beck v. Ohio*, 379 U.S. 89, 91 (1964).

Here, the government has failed to meet its burden. The officers stopped the car for a traffic violation—driving without headlights—and then expanded the scope of the stop into a criminal investigation based on nothing more than the passengers’ furtive movements. *See Henry v. United States*, 361 U.S. 98, 104 (1959) ("The mere fact that the officers were suspicious of [the defendant] did not justify a search of his parcel."). The government cites no authority for the proposition that mere nervousness or agitation, without more, justifies a pat-frisk. *See Rios v. United States*, 364 U.S. 253, 262 (1960) (holding that reasonable suspicion must be based on "specific and articulable facts" and not on "inchoate and unparticularized suspicion or hunch").

The government also relies on the fact that the officers were in a "high-crime area" and that Soares was "verbally abusive." *See Draper v. United States*, 358 U.S. 307, 313 (1959) (noting that "the reputation of the neighborhood" may be considered in assessing probable cause). However, the Supreme Court has made clear that a high-crime area alone does not justify a pat-frisk. *See Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 63 (1968) ("[T]he fact that [the officer] knew that [the defendant] was a narcotics addict and that he had been observed in a neighborhood where narcotics traffic was common did not, without more, constitute probable cause."). Similarly, verbal abuse, without more, does not give rise to a reasonable suspicion that a suspect is armed and dangerous. *See Brown v. Texas*, 443 U.S. 47, 52 (1979) (noting that "the fact that [the defendant] was in a neighborhood frequented by drug users" and "his unprovoked flight upon seeing police officers" did not justify a stop).

The government’s reliance on *Adams v. Williams*, 407 U.S. 143 (1972), is misplaced, as that case was decided after the relevant time period for this appeal. Moreover, even in *Adams*, the Supreme Court cautioned that a pat-frisk must be justified by specific and articulable facts, not mere hunches. *Id.* at 147.

Here, the officers had no reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous. The pat-frisk was therefore unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment, and the district court erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress.

II. **THE FRISK WAS NOT CONDUCTED PURSUANT TO A LAWFUL INVESTIGATORY STOP.**

Even if the initial stop was lawful, the officers exceeded the permissible scope of a traffic stop by expanding it into a criminal investigation without reasonable suspicion. *See United States v. Sharpe*, 470 U.S. 675, 682 (1985) (noting that "the investigative methods employed should be the least intrusive means reasonably available to verify or dispel the officer’s suspicion in a short period of time"). Here, the officers had no reasonable suspicion to believe that criminal activity was afoot beyond the traffic violation. *See Henry*, 361 U.S. at 104 ("[T]he officers were not entitled to expand the scope of the inquiry beyond the scope of the justification for the initial stop.").

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Brignoni-Ponce*, 422 U.S. 873 (1975), is again misplaced, as that case was decided after the relevant time period. Moreover, *Brignoni-Ponce* involved a border patrol stop, not a routine traffic stop, and the Supreme Court has made clear that the rules governing border stops do not apply to routine traffic stops. *See United States v. Ortiz*, 422 U.S. 891, 896 (1975).

Here, the officers had no reasonable suspicion to expand the scope of the stop, and the pat-frisk was therefore unlawful.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress. The pat-frisk was unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment, and the evidence obtained as a result of the frisk should have been suppressed. The judgment of the district court should be reversed.
