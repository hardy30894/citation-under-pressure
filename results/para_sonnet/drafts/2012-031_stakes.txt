# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court's Finding That Damages Could Be Measured on a Class-Wide Basis Through Common Evidence Was Not Clearly Erroneous.

A district court's decision to certify a class is reviewed only for abuse of discretion, which exists "if the district court's decision rests upon a clearly erroneous finding of fact, an errant conclusion of law or an improper application of law to fact." *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 312 (3d Cir. 2008). The "clearly erroneous" standard is demanding: a reviewing court may not disturb a factual finding merely because it "is convinced that it would have decided the case differently." *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985). Rather, "[i]f the district court's account of the evidence is plausible in light of the record viewed in its entirety, the court of appeals may not reverse it even though convinced that had it been sitting as the trier of fact, it would have weighed the evidence differently." *Id.* at 573–74. Indeed, "[w]here there are two permissible views of the evidence, the factfinder's choice between them cannot be clearly erroneous." *Id.* at 574. This deference traces to *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), which held that a finding of fact may be overturned only when the reviewing court "is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *Id.* at 395.

That standard was not met here. The District Court did not accept Respondents' damages theory on the pleadings alone. It conducted a four-day evidentiary hearing, heard live testimony from fact and expert witnesses, and considered thirty-two expert reports before concluding, on an eighty-one-page record, that "the class has met its burden to demonstrate that the element of antitrust impact is capable of proof at trial through evidence that is common to the class rather than individual to its members, and that there is a common methodology available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis." *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). This is precisely the sort of "rigorous analysis" this Court has demanded before certification—an inquiry that may "delve beyond the pleadings" and even overlap with the merits when necessary to resolve a Rule 23 requirement. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 316–17. Having undertaken that analysis, the court's ultimate factual determination "is completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support displaying some hue of credibility" only if it bears "no rational relationship to the supportive evidentiary data." *Krasnov v. Dinan*, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972). Petitioners cannot make that showing; they simply urge this Court to reweigh a record the district court considered for four days and eighty-one pages—exactly the exercise *Anderson* forbids.

## II. Rule 23(b)(3) Requires Only That Common Questions Predominate, Not That Damages Be Proven with Certainty at the Certification Stage.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires "that the questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). The predominance inquiry "tests whether proposed classes are sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation." *Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997). It does not require Respondents to prove, at the certification stage, the precise amount of damages each class member will ultimately recover. The question is whether a common methodology exists that can be applied class-wide—a question the District Court answered affirmatively after finding that Dr. McClave's regression models constituted "common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis." *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 191.

This Court's decision in *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 131 S.Ct. 2541 (2011), confirms that a "rigorous analysis" of Rule 23 "will entail some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff's underlying claim," but does not transform certification into a trial on the ultimate merits; *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin* still forecloses "a merits inquiry for any other pretrial purpose." *Wal-Mart*, 131 S.Ct. at 2551–52 & n.6. The District Court respected that line. It resolved the *predominance* question—whether antitrust impact and damages methodology were susceptible to common proof—without purporting to adjudicate whether Comcast actually violated the Sherman Act or the ultimate quantum of damages owed to any individual subscriber. That is the proper scope of a certification inquiry, and Petitioners' insistence on more searching proof of damages calculation before a single class member has tried the case conflates the predominance inquiry with a merits trial.

## III. The Evidentiary Record Amply Supports Certification.

The class here alleged a single, common theory of antitrust impact—that Comcast's "clustering" conduct deterred entry by overbuilders throughout the Philadelphia Designated Market Area, thereby maintaining supracompetitive prices for all subscribers. *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 154; App. 00032. Because the alleged anticompetitive conduct and its market-wide effect were common to the class, the resulting overcharge was likewise susceptible to common, formulaic measurement. The District Court's careful, four-day evidentiary examination of that methodology, and its resulting factual findings, are entitled to the deference *Anderson* and *Hydrogen Peroxide* require. This Court should affirm.
