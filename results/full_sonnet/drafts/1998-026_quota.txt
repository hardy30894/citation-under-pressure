**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Violation of Rule 32(a)(2) Is Not Automatically Cognizable on Collateral Review Absent a Showing of Prejudice.**

Relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 is not available for every departure from the formal requirements of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure. This Court has long held that collateral relief is reserved for defects that are jurisdictional or constitutional, or that constitute "a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice." *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). In *Hill*, the Court refused to vacate a sentence for the district court's failure to comply with a rule requiring the defendant be given an opportunity to speak before sentencing, explaining that "the failure to follow the formal requirements of [the Rule] is neither a jurisdictional nor constitutional error." *Id.* at 429.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780 (1979), where the Court held that a technical violation of Rule 11—there, the failure to advise a defendant of a mandatory special parole term—did not entitle the defendant to relief under § 2255 because he "does not argue that he was actually unaware of the special parole term . . . or that, if he had been properly advised . . . he would not have pleaded guilty." *Id.* at 784. The Court emphasized that collateral relief should not be granted for "formal" violations that cause no actual harm. *Id.* at 783–84. Likewise, in *Davis v. United States*, 417 U.S. 333 (1974), this Court confirmed that § 2255 relief is available only where an error results in a complete miscarriage of justice, not for mere noncompliance with procedural formalities. *Id.* at 346.

Rule 32(a)(2)'s advisement requirement is precisely the sort of formal, non-constitutional rule addressed in *Hill* and *Timmreck*. Nothing in the Rule's text purports to create a substantive right independent of the underlying right to appeal itself; it is a mechanism designed to ensure that defendants are aware of a right they already possess. Where, as here, the defendant already possessed that awareness, the failure to repeat it in open court cannot be elevated into an error warranting collateral relief.

**II. Because the Purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) Was Fulfilled, Any Violation Was Harmless.**

Even where a procedural error has occurred, this Court has consistently applied harmless-error principles to determine whether relief is warranted, asking whether the error affected the outcome or infringed upon the interest the rule was designed to protect. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764–65 (1946) (error is harmless unless it had "substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the . . . verdict"). This standard governs collateral proceedings with even greater force than on direct appeal. In *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619 (1993), the Court held that habeas petitioners must show that an error resulted in "actual prejudice," reasoning that "society's interest in the finality of criminal judgments" outweighs relief for errors that did not affect the proceeding's outcome. *Id.* at 637.

The sole purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)'s advisement requirement is to ensure that a defendant, "if he so desires," is informed of his right to appeal and the applicable time limits. Where the sentencing court finds as fact—after an evidentiary hearing—that the defendant already knew of that right, the rule's purpose has been fully vindicated notwithstanding the court's failure to repeat the advisement aloud. Peguero's own testimony and the record confirmed that he understood his appellate rights; he simply chose, for whatever reason, not to exercise them. Under *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967), even constitutional errors are subject to harmless-error analysis where it can be said the error was "harmless beyond a reasonable doubt." A fortiori, a non-constitutional, non-jurisdictional procedural omission that had no effect whatsoever on the defendant's actual knowledge or decision-making cannot be a ground for disturbing a final judgment.

**III. Requiring Automatic Vacatur Regardless of Prejudice Would Contravene This Court's Insistence on Actual Prejudice in Collateral Proceedings.**

This Court's collateral-review jurisprudence consistently requires a petitioner to demonstrate actual prejudice flowing from an asserted error, not merely a technical departure from procedure. In *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152 (1982), the Court held that a § 2255 petitioner raising a procedurally defaulted claim must show "actual prejudice," not merely a "possibility of prejudice." *Id.* at 170. Similarly, in *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614 (1998), the Court reiterated that collateral relief is available only when the petitioner establishes actual prejudice or a fundamental miscarriage of justice, not simply a procedural irregularity. *Id.* at 622.

Petitioner's proposed rule—automatic vacatur whenever a district court's Rule 32(a)(2) advisement is imperfect, irrespective of the defendant's actual knowledge—would sever the prejudice inquiry from the remedy entirely. Such an approach cannot be reconciled with *Frady*, *Bousley*, *Timmreck*, or *Hill*, all of which condition collateral relief on some showing that the error mattered. Petitioner has never disputed the district court's factual finding, made after a full evidentiary hearing, that he knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. Because the very interest Rule 32(a)(2) protects—awareness of the right to appeal—was fully served, no miscarriage of justice occurred, and no basis for § 2255 relief exists.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
