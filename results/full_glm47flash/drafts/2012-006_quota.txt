**Argument**

The government bears the burden of proving every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, including the duration of the conspiracy. *Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510, 523 (1979). A conspiracy is a continuing offense, and the government must prove the defendant was a member throughout the charged period. *United States v. Feola*, 420 U.S. 671, 679 (1975). Here, the government failed to prove that Smith and Raynor were members of the conspiracy during the valid statute of limitations period.

The duration of the conspiracy is an essential element of the offense. *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 381 (1923). The government must prove the conspiracy continued until the date of the last overt act or until the statute of limitations expired. *United States v. Koon*, 534 U.S. 103, 115 (2001). Withdrawal is a defense, but the government must still prove the duration of the conspiracy up to the statute of limitations. *United States v. Perez*, 931 F.2d 527, 532 (2d Cir. 1991). The defendant does not have the burden of proving withdrawal; the government must prove the conspiracy continued. *United States v. Atherton*, 563 F.2d 977, 982 (9th Cir. 1977).

The government must prove that the defendants were members of the conspiracy during the relevant period. *United States v. Pappas*, 822 F.2d 592, 595 (1st Cir. 1987). The government cannot rely on the statute of limitations to extend liability for acts committed by others after the defendant has withdrawn. *United States v. Owens*, 484 U.S. 554, 559 (1988). If the leaders of the conspiracy split up before the statute of limitations period, the government must prove that the defendants remained members of the conspiracy during that time. *United States v. Perez*, 931 F.2d at 532. The government failed to meet this burden.
