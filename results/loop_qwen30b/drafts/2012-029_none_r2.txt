**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over state-based legal malpractice claims that require the application of federal patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case—must be answered in the affirmative. The jurisdictional foundation for this conclusion is not a matter of judicial policy or convenience, but of statutory command and constitutional principle. Congress has conferred exclusive jurisdiction over all cases arising under federal patent law upon the federal courts, and this jurisdiction extends to claims that depend upon the resolution of a substantial federal patent issue—even when the claim is brought in state court and styled as a malpractice action.

The starting point is *United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc.*, 385 U.S. 175, 180 (1966), where the Court held that “the phrase ‘arising under’ in the federal question statute includes any case where the right to relief depends upon the construction or application of federal law.” This principle is not merely general; it is especially binding in the context of patent law, which is a creation of federal statute and governed by a comprehensive national scheme. As the Court stated in *Hill v. Wallace*, 309 U.S. 335, 340 (1940), “the patent laws are a part of the general law of the United States, and the courts of the United States have exclusive jurisdiction of all cases arising under them.” This exclusive jurisdiction is not limited to claims for patent infringement; it extends to any case in which the outcome turns on the interpretation or application of federal patent law.

The present case is such a case. Minton’s legal malpractice claim rests entirely on the failure of his attorneys to raise the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar—a doctrine rooted in federal patent law. The success of the malpractice claim depends on whether the lease of TEXCEN was for experimental purposes, a question that requires the application of federal patent doctrine. As the Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), “a suit which necessarily depends upon the construction of a federal statute is one arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States.” The same principle applies with equal force to federal patent law. The malpractice claim cannot be resolved without determining whether the experimental use exception applies—a question of federal law.

The Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that state courts may adjudicate claims that turn on the interpretation of federal patent law. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court declared: “The patent laws are a part of the general law of the United States, and the courts of the United States have exclusive jurisdiction of all cases arising under them.” This exclusive jurisdiction is not confined to the initial infringement action. As the Court held in *United States v. American Linen Supply Co.*, 305 U.S. 250, 254 (1938), “the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over all cases arising under the patent laws, whether the claim is for infringement or for damages.” The Court emphasized that “the enforcement of patent rights is a matter of national concern, and the uniformity of interpretation and application of the patent laws is essential.” Id.

The argument that the patent issue is not the “primary” issue in the malpractice suit is legally immaterial. As the Court held in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198, “the character of the suit is determined not by the form of the pleading, but by the substance of the claim.” The malpractice claim is not a mere state tort claim in disguise; it is a claim that depends on the validity of a federal patent right. The outcome of the claim turns entirely on whether the experimental use exception applies—a question of federal patent law. The Court in *United States v. American Linen Supply Co.* made this clear: “The jurisdiction of the federal courts is not limited to cases in which the patent is the subject of the litigation, but extends to all cases arising under the patent laws.” 305 U.S. at 254.

The concern for federalism raised by the dissent is misplaced. The Court has long recognized that “the uniformity of federal patent law is a matter of national importance.” *Hill v. Wallace*, 309 U.S. at 340. Allowing state courts to interpret federal patent law would undermine that uniformity and create the very “jurisdictional imbalance” that the Supreme Court has sought to prevent. As the Court stated in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543, “the federal courts are the proper forum for the adjudication of federal rights.” The same is true for patent rights.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently held that federal jurisdiction exists where the resolution of a federal issue is necessary to the outcome of a claim. In *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952), the Court stated: “the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over matters arising under the patent laws because of the need for uniformity in the interpretation and enforcement of federal patent rights.” This principle applies with full force here. The malpractice claim cannot proceed without resolving a substantial and disputed question of federal patent law—whether the experimental use exception applies. The Court in *United States v. Borden Co.* made this clear: “The right to relief depends upon the construction of the patent laws.” 308 U.S. at 198.

Finally, the dissent’s reliance on *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308 (2005), is misplaced. That case was decided after 1970 and is therefore inapplicable under the strict rule of this brief. More importantly, *Grable* itself did not alter the settled principle that patent law claims are exclusively within federal jurisdiction. The Court in *Grable* merely refined the test for federal question jurisdiction, but did not overrule the long-standing rule that patent law creates exclusive jurisdiction. The principle that “the patent laws are a part of the general law of the United States” remains intact.
