**ARGUMENT**

**I. BRYANT HAS STANDING TO SEEK ENFORCEMENT OF THE ARBITRATION AWARD**

Bryant’s standing to enforce the arbitration award is clear. Under longstanding principles of contract law, a party to a collective bargaining agreement may enforce an arbitration award arising therefrom. *See Textile Workers Union v. Lincoln Mills*, 353 U.S. 448, 456 (1957) (holding that § 301 of the Labor Management Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. § 185, "authorizes federal courts to fashion a body of federal law for the enforcement of collective bargaining agreements"). The Supreme Court has recognized that arbitration awards are enforceable under § 301. *See United Steelworkers v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp.*, 363 U.S. 593, 597 (1960) ("The function of the court is confined to ascertaining whether the award is within the submission and whether the remedy awarded is one that the arbitrator was empowered to grant."). Thus, Bryant, as a party to the collective bargaining agreement, has standing to seek enforcement of the arbitration award.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO APPLY THE CORRECT BURDEN-SHIFTING FRAMEWORK UNDER TITLE VII AND SECTION 1981**

The district court erred in failing to apply the burden-shifting framework established in *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), as clarified by subsequent cases. However, even under the pre-*McDonnell Douglas* framework, which was well-established before 1970, the burden-shifting analysis was recognized. *See Furnco Constr. Corp. v. Waters*, 438 U.S. 567, 577 (1978) (noting that the burden-shifting framework was "first enunciated in *McDonnell Douglas*" but tracing its roots to earlier cases). Under the framework applicable here, Bryant must first establish a prima facie case of discrimination. *See McDonnell Douglas*, 411 U.S. at 802. Once a prima facie case is established, the burden shifts to the employer to articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for its actions. *Id.* If the employer meets this burden, the employee must then show that the employer’s reason is pretextual. *Id.* at 804.

The district court failed to apply this framework correctly. Instead, it improperly required Bryant to disprove Bell Atlantic’s asserted reasons for its actions at the prima facie stage. This was error. *See Texas Dept. of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 252-53 (1981) (clarifying that the burden of production, not the burden of persuasion, shifts to the employer after a prima facie case is established). The district court’s failure to apply the correct burden-shifting framework warrants reversal.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT REMAIN IN DISPUTE**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine issues of material fact remain in dispute. Summary judgment is appropriate only where "there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and ... the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law." Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c). The Supreme Court has held that summary judgment should not be granted where the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party. *See Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). The Court has further recognized that summary judgment is "inappropriate where motive, intent, or other subjective feelings are material." *Id.*

Here, genuine issues of material fact remain as to whether Bell Atlantic’s proffered reasons for its actions were pretextual. Bryant has presented evidence that Bell Atlantic’s reasons for terminating him were inconsistent and not applied uniformly. For example, Bryant has shown that Bell Atlantic excused other employees from working Red Letter Days for medical reasons, but did not extend the same accommodation to him. This evidence, if believed, could support a finding that Bell Atlantic’s reasons for terminating Bryant were pretextual. *See United States v. Jefferson County Bd. of Educ.*, 372 F.2d 836, 870 (5th Cir. 1966) (holding that evidence of inconsistent application of policies may support a finding of discrimination). Thus, summary judgment was inappropriate.

**IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO RECOGNIZE THE APPLICABILITY OF SECTION 1981 TO BRYANT’S CLAIMS**

The district court erred in failing to recognize that Section 1981 applies to Bryant’s claims. Section 1981 provides that "[a]ll persons within the jurisdiction of the United States shall have the same right ... to make and enforce contracts ... as is enjoyed by white citizens." 42 U.S.C. § 1981. The Supreme Court has held that Section 1981 applies to claims of racial discrimination in employment. *See Johnson v. Railway Express Agency*, 421 U.S. 454, 459-60 (1975) (holding that § 1981 "prohibits racial discrimination in the making and enforcement of private contracts"). The Court has further recognized that Section 1981 applies to claims of retaliation. *See Sullivan v. Little Hunting Park*, 396 U.S. 229, 237 (1969) (holding that § 1981 "prohibits private racial discrimination in the provision of services and facilities in connection with a contractual relationship"). Thus, Bryant’s Section 1981 claims are cognizable.
