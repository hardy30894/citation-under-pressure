# ARGUMENT

## I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT'S TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION SATISFIES THE INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TEST AND DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER.

Article I vests "[a]ll legislative powers" in Congress, U.S. Const. art. I, § 1, but this Court has long recognized that Congress may enlist the assistance of the Executive Branch so long as it "lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This flexible standard reflects a "practical understanding that in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives." *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989). Since *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), the Court has consistently upheld delegations that provide even general statutory guidance, and outside the singular circumstances of 1935, the Court has never invalidated a congressional delegation under the intelligible-principle test. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373.

Section 811(h) easily meets this standard. Congress specified the precise objective the Attorney General must pursue—avoidance of "an imminent hazard to the public safety," 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1)—and enumerated the specific factors he must consider: the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health. *Id.* § 811(h)(3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)). Congress further cabined the delegation by requiring advance notice in the Federal Register, consideration of the Secretary of Health and Human Services' comments, and a strict durational limit of one year, extendable by only six months. *Id.* § 811(h)(2), (4). These substantive and procedural constraints are markedly more confining than delegations this Court has already sustained. In *Mistretta*, the Court upheld the Sentencing Commission's authority to promulgate binding Guidelines carrying criminal consequences because Congress specified goals, a final product, and limiting factors—precisely the template Congress followed here. 488 U.S. at 374-79.

The temporary scheduling provision likewise resembles delegations upheld in analogous emergency contexts. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 423-24 (1944), the Court sustained the Price Administrator's power to fix commodity prices "generally fair and equitable" and to effectuate the purposes of the Emergency Price Control Act, recognizing that "the essentials of the legislative function are the determination of the legislative policy and its formulation as a rule of conduct." The rapidity of change in the market for designer drugs—the precise problem Congress sought to address in 1984—parallels the wartime exigency in *Yakus*, where the need for prompt executive action justified a broad delegation. Similarly, in *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court upheld a delegation to bank regulators against nondelegation attack because the regulated field possessed "well-known and generally acceptable standards," just as the CSA's scheduling factors track scientific criteria already employed in the Act's permanent scheduling provisions. And in *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952), the availability of habeas review reinforced the delegation's validity—here, defendants may still challenge on due process grounds whether a particular scheduling determination was arbitrary, even though § 811(h)(6) forecloses direct APA review of the order itself.

Petitioners' reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In both cases, Congress supplied no criteria whatsoever to guide the President's discretion; the NIRA "declared no policy," established "no standard," and left the President's judgment "unconfined and vagrant." *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415, 430. Section 811(h) is nothing like that boundless grant. It supplies both a goal (avoiding "imminent hazard") and a closed list of factors to be weighed—precisely the "intelligible principle" *Panama Refining* found lacking.

## II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL LAWFULLY SUBDELEGATED TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA.

Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Act. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This express statutory authorization forecloses any subdelegation objection. This Court has long recognized that an executive officer may redelegate authority to subordinates where Congress has so provided, or even where the redelegation is consistent with the statutory scheme's structure and purpose. *See United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 84-85 (1932) (upholding subdelegation of authority to promulgate regulations carrying criminal penalties where Congress had authorized the delegation). The Attorney General's 1973 and 1987 regulations, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), assigning CSA functions to the DEA Administrator, are squarely within the authority Congress granted in § 871(a). Every court of appeals to consider the question has reached the same conclusion. *See United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1428 (10th Cir. 1987).

The subdelegation is also consistent with this Court's recognition in *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 785-86 (1948), that Congress may permit the exercise of delegated authority through executive subordinates without transgressing separation-of-powers principles, so long as accountability remains with the delegate officer. Because the Attorney General retains ultimate statutory responsibility and the DEA acts pursuant to his validly promulgated regulation, the subdelegation here is fully authorized.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
