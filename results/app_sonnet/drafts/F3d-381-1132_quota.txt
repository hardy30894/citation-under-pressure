**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Court of Federal Claims Erred in Treating the Scope of the Easement as Irrelevant to the Takings Analysis**

The court below held that "[t]he scope of the easement is not relevant in this court because it cannot give rise to a physical taking." That ruling cannot survive this Court's en banc decision in *Preseault v. United States*, 100 F.3d 1525 (Fed. Cir. 1996). There, this Court squarely held that when the government converts a right-of-way to a use beyond the scope of the grant, it imposes a new burden on the servient estate that "the owner never conveyed," requiring compensation. *Id.* at 1550. Scope was not incidental to that analysis — it was the *sine qua non* of it, because a physical occupation exceeding the terms of the original conveyance intrudes on property rights the landowner retained. *See also Preseault v. Interstate Commerce Comm'n*, 494 U.S. 1, 8 (1990) (recognizing that whether a conversion of use "results in a taking" depends on the scope of the underlying property interest).

The 1953 conveyance authorized BPA to install "signal lines... necessary in connection" with its transmission lines — nothing more. That limiting language defines the outer boundary of the occupation to which Tuthill's predecessor consented. Cable capacity installed and maintained for a for-profit telecommunications lease has nothing to do with operating the power line, and thus falls outside the grant. The lower court's refusal to even examine that boundary was legal error.

**II. BPA's Retention and Commercial Leasing of Excess Fiber Capacity Effected a Physical Occupation Subject to Per Se Analysis**

A permanent physical occupation authorized by the government is a taking "without regard to the public interests that it may serve." *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 426 (1982). *Loretto* teaches that it is the *fact* of occupation, not its size, that carries constitutional weight. *Id.* at 427. BPA's fiber-optic cables occupy Tuthill's land continuously, in capacity the agency concedes it does not need for its own operations and instead leases for private profit. That the excess fibers share a jacket with the fibers actually used for grid control does not diminish the intrusion; the property interest at stake — the right to exclude — has been given away to a stranger without Tuthill's consent or compensation.

This Court has already held that a continuous government-authorized physical presence, even one installed for non-proprietary purposes, is a taking. In *Hendler v. United States*, 952 F.2d 1364 (Fed. Cir. 1991), this Court found a taking where EPA placed groundwater monitoring wells on private land, explaining that "the character of the invasion, not the amount of damage resulting from it, so long as the damage is substantial," determines whether a taking occurred. *Id.* at 1377 (quoting *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 266 (1946)). In *Causby* itself, the Supreme Court held that repeated low-altitude overflights, though leaving no structure on the land, effected "a taking of an air easement" because the invasion was direct and continuous. 328 U.S. at 265–66. The same is true here: BPA's dedication of cable capacity to a commercial telecommunications carrier is a direct, ongoing physical intrusion never authorized by the 1953 easement.

The right destroyed by that intrusion is foundational. As the Supreme Court explained in *Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164 (1979), the right to exclude others is "one of the most essential sticks in the bundle of rights that are commonly characterized as property." *Id.* at 176. Tuthill relinquished its right to exclude BPA only to the extent necessary for power-line signal lines — not for an unrelated commercial telecommunications venture. *Cf. Nollan v. California Coastal Comm'n*, 483 U.S. 825, 831–32 (1987) (a required physical invasion of property is treated as a taking "in its most obvious form," regardless of the public benefit asserted). And because the occupation here is the product of an affirmative governmental grant to a third party rather than a mere regulatory restriction, it falls within the class of physical-occupation cases that "give[s] a formula for recovery," rather than the balancing tests applicable to non-invasive regulation. *Yee v. City of Escondido*, 503 U.S. 519, 528 (1992).

**III. The Undisputed Facts Entitle Tuthill to Judgment as a Matter of Law**

The government does not dispute that (1) BPA installed cable capacity far exceeding its own operational needs; (2) BPA leases that excess capacity to an unrelated telecommunications company; and (3) the easement's terms are limited to signal lines "necessary in connection" with power transmission. On these undisputed facts, the leased fibers are not "necessary" to BPA's transmission function within the meaning of the grant, and their placement and continued maintenance for a third party's commercial benefit constitute a physical occupation outside the scope of the 1953 easement. Under *Loretto*, *Kaiser Aetna*, *Causby*, *Hendler*, and *Preseault*, that occupation is a compensable taking as a matter of law, not merely a trespass claim relegated to another forum.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Federal Claims and remand with instructions to enter summary judgment for Tuthill on the issue of liability, with just compensation to be determined on remand.
