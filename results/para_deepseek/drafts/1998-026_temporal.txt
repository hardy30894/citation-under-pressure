ARGUMENT

I. The District Court’s Failure to Advise Respondent of His Right to Appeal Is Subject to Harmless-Error Review, and Any Error Was Harmless Because Respondent Knew of His Right and Chose Not to Appeal.

The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure are not constitutional commands; they are procedural guidelines designed to ensure fairness. See *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (Rule 32’s requirement that a defendant be asked whether he has anything to say before sentencing “is not a constitutional mandate” but “a rule of procedure”). Accordingly, a violation of Rule 32’s advice-of-appeal provision does not automatically entitle a defendant to collateral relief. To obtain habeas relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255, a petitioner must demonstrate that the error “resulted in a complete miscarriage of justice or in a proceeding inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure.” *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428.

Where, as here, the defendant *already knew* of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing, the purpose of Rule 32’s advice requirement is fully satisfied. The rule exists to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights; it does not impose a rigid duty to repeat information the defendant already possesses. See *United States v. Robinson*, 361 U.S. 220, 228-29 (1960) (holding that a defendant’s knowing failure to appeal waives the right even if the court failed to advise him). In *Robinson*, the Court explained that “the failure of the trial court to advise the defendant of his right to appeal does not entitle him to a new trial where he made no effort to appeal and does not show that he was unaware of his right.” *Id.* at 229.

This Court has long recognized that harmless procedural errors do not require reversal. *See Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967) (distinguishing between constitutional errors that require automatic reversal and “trial errors” that are subject to harmless-error analysis). The failure to give a Rule 32 advisory is a non-constitutional, procedural error. *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428. Such errors are harmless if they do not affect the defendant’s substantial rights. *See Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) (holding that errors affecting substantial rights require reversal, but “if, when all that happened is considered, we cannot say ‘with fair assurance’ that the judgment was substantially swayed by the error,” the error is harmless).

Here, the District Court expressly found that Respondent “knew of his right to appeal when the sentencing hearing occurred.” That finding is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous. Because Respondent knowingly elected not to appeal, the omission of the advisory caused him no prejudice. The purpose of Rule 32 was served; the error was harmless.

II. Habeas Relief Is Not Available for a Mere Procedural Defect That Does Not Prejudice the Defendant.

This Court has consistently held that collateral relief under § 2255 is not a remedy for every procedural irregularity. *See United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 784 (1979) (citing *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428) (a violation of Rule 11’s plea-acceptance procedure does not automatically warrant § 2255 relief). In *Hill*, the Court denied habeas relief where the trial court violated Rule 32(a) by failing to ask the defendant personally whether he wished to speak before sentencing. The Court held that such a “departure from the rule” did not “constitute an error of the character or magnitude cognizable under § 2255.” *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428.

The same analysis applies here. Rule 32’s advice-of-appeal requirement is analogous to the allocution requirement in *Hill*. Both are “precise procedural steps” that “do not involve the denial of a constitutional right.” *Id.* at 427. A defendant who already knows of his right to appeal suffers no “miscarriage of justice” from the court’s failure to recite the advisory. *Id.* at 428.

Moreover, Respondent did not merely fail to object; he affirmatively chose not to appeal. This Court has long held that a defendant’s knowing and voluntary decision to forgo an appeal constitutes a waiver of the right. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver is “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”). Having made that choice, Respondent cannot now claim that the District Court’s oversight entitles him to a second opportunity to appeal.

III. The Court of Appeals Correctly Applied Harmless-Error Review.

The Third Circuit held that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmless-error analysis and that the error was harmless because Respondent knew of his right. This approach is consistent with this Court’s precedents. *See, e.g., Chapman*, 386 U.S. at 22 (harmless-error analysis applies to “trial errors”); *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 764-65 (same). The Third Circuit’s reasoning—that “the rule’s purpose had been served since Peguero was aware of his right to appeal”—echoes *Robinson*, 361 U.S. at 229. No more is required.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
