**Argument**

A state prisoner may not use a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to establish cause for the procedural default of another federal claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself is properly exhausted and not itself defaulted, or the petitioner can separately establish cause and prejudice for that default. **Edwards v. Carpenter, 529 U.S. 446, 451–53 (2000).** This rule follows directly from the Court’s settled doctrine that “cause” to excuse default must be an external factor, and that a claim used as cause must itself satisfy ordinary procedural rules. See **Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986)**; **Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 84–87 (1977)**; **Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991)**.

Here, Edwards’s sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim is defaulted because he did not raise it in the state courts in a timely and proper manner. Under **Coleman**, federal habeas review is barred unless Edwards shows cause and prejudice. **501 U.S. at 750.** He attempts to supply cause through an ineffective-assistance claim against appellate counsel. But that ineffective-assistance claim was itself presented to the Ohio courts only through an untimely application to reopen the appeal, which the state courts dismissed as untimely. A claim rejected by the state courts on a state procedural ground is itself defaulted for federal habeas purposes. See **Coleman, 501 U.S. at 729–30, 750**.

The court of appeals erred by treating the ineffective-assistance claim as automatically available as cause, regardless of its own default. That approach conflicts with **Murray v. Carrier**, which explained that attorney error can constitute cause only when it amounts to constitutionally ineffective assistance and is itself properly preserved. **477 U.S. at 488–89.** The Court there emphasized that a procedurally defaulted claim “cannot serve as cause” unless the petitioner first satisfies the ordinary cause-and-prejudice requirements for that very default. **Edwards, 529 U.S. at 453** (citing **Murray, 477 U.S. at 488–89**). In other words, “ineffective assistance adequate to establish cause for the procedural default of some other constitutional claim is itself an independent constitutional claim,” and as such it must be exhausted and not defaulted. **Murray, 477 U.S. at 489; Edwards, 529 U.S. at 451–52.**

That principle is also compelled by **Coleman**. There, the Court held that attorney error in state collateral proceedings could not constitute cause because the attorney acts as the prisoner’s agent, and “the attorney’s ignorance or inadvertence” is attributed to the client. **501 U.S. at 752–54.** If counsel’s failure in a prior proceeding is attributable to the petitioner, then a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot be used to bypass the state procedural bar on a different claim. To hold otherwise would allow a petitioner to evade state default rules simply by alleging, after the fact, that counsel was ineffective in failing to present the very claim he defaulted.

The Sixth Circuit’s rule also undermines the comity and finality principles that animate procedural default doctrine. Federal habeas review is not designed to relitigate claims omitted from state court in violation of state rules. **Wainwright, 433 U.S. at 87–91; Coleman, 501 U.S. at 731–32.** Requiring the ineffective-assistance claim itself to be properly preserved ensures that state courts have the first opportunity to correct any constitutional error. See **Rose v. Lundy, 455 U.S. 509, 518–19 (1982)**; **O’Sullivan v. Boerckel, 526 U.S. 838, 844–45 (1999).**

Edwards may argue that his ineffective-assistance claim should be considered as cause because it was the “underlying” constitutional defect. But **Edwards v. Carpenter** forecloses that position. The Court held that “a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim only if the petitioner can satisfy the cause-and-prejudice standard with respect to the ineffective-assistance claim itself.” **529 U.S. at 450–51.** That is the controlling rule here. Edwards made no showing that some external cause excused the untimeliness of his reopening application, nor any separate showing of prejudice on that default. Without that, the ineffective-assistance claim cannot do the work he assigns it.

Nor can Edwards avoid default by invoking the district court’s merits determination. Federal courts may not reach the merits of a defaulted claim simply because the underlying issue seems substantial. See **Coleman, 501 U.S. at 750**. The procedural bar is independent of the merits. And the state court’s timeliness ruling is itself an adequate and independent state ground barring habeas review. See **Baldwin v. Reese, 541 U.S. 27, 29 (2004)**; **Walker v. Martin, 562 U.S. 307, 315–21 (2011)**.

Because Edwards’s ineffective-assistance claim was itself procedurally defaulted, it cannot excuse the default of his sufficiency claim absent an independent showing of cause and prejudice for the ineffective-assistance default. He made none. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
