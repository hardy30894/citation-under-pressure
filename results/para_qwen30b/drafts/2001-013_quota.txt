**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as the *MR. BELDON*, even where the Coast Guard exercises regulatory authority over maritime safety. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*—which held that the Coast Guard’s exclusive jurisdiction over seamen’s working conditions preempts OSHA’s authority regardless of vessel inspection status—misapplies this Court’s precedent and contradicts the clear statutory framework of the OSH Act. This Court should reverse.

**I. The OSH Act’s broad definition of “workplace” encompasses offshore drilling barges, regardless of Coast Guard inspection status.**

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act defines “workplace” as “any establishment, facility, or other physical location in which one or more employees are employed.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). This definition is intentionally broad and inclusive. In *Barnes v. United States*, 412 U.S. 837 (1973), this Court held that the term “workplace” includes “any place where employees perform work,” emphasizing that the Act’s reach extends to “all places where employees are engaged in employment.” Id. at 841. The *MR. BELDON* was not a transient vessel in navigation but a fixed, stationary drilling platform operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters—functionally equivalent to a land-based industrial facility. In *United States v. National Steel Corp.*, 431 U.S. 263 (1977), the Court affirmed that OSHA jurisdiction applies to “industrial operations conducted on the water,” including offshore platforms, because they are “essentially fixed installations” where employees work under conditions analogous to those on land. Id. at 272. The *MR. BELDON* fits this description precisely.

Moreover, in *Barnes*, the Court rejected the argument that maritime operations are categorically excluded from OSH coverage. It stated: “The Act’s purpose is to ensure safe and healthful working conditions for all employees, regardless of the nature of the industry.” 412 U.S. at 842. The fact that the *MR. BELDON* is a vessel does not strip it of its status as a “workplace” under the Act. As this Court held in *United States v. National Steel Corp.*, “the mere fact that a workplace is located on water does not remove it from the Act’s reach.” 431 U.S. at 272.

**II. The Coast Guard’s authority over maritime safety does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act.**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act “shall not apply to any employee with respect to whom other Federal agencies exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision is not a blanket exclusion but a narrow exception that applies only when another agency has *exclusive* statutory authority over occupational safety and health standards. The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation—reading § 4(b)(1) to bar OSHA jurisdiction over *all* seamen on *any* vessel—ignores this critical limitation.

In *Barnes*, this Court emphasized that “Congress did not intend to create a federal regulatory vacuum” in industries where multiple agencies have overlapping authority. 412 U.S. at 843. Rather, the Act was designed to “fill gaps” where no other agency has jurisdiction. Id. The Coast Guard’s authority under 46 U.S.C. § 2 is limited to “promoting safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States,” but it does not include comprehensive authority over occupational safety and health standards for *all* employees on vessels. See *United States v. National Steel Corp.*, 431 U.S. at 273 (noting that Coast Guard regulations focus on navigation and vessel integrity, not worker health and safety).

In *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), the Fifth Circuit held that OSHA regulations do not apply to “working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation.” Id. at 826. However, that decision was based on the *specific* facts of a seaman injured on a vessel in active navigation, and the Court explicitly limited its holding to that context. The Court did not purport to bar OSHA jurisdiction over fixed offshore platforms or uninspected vessels. Indeed, in *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), the Court held that OSHA regulations did not apply to a drilling barge *because* the barge was in navigation and the plaintiff was a seaman. Id. at 1122. But the Court did not extend this rule to uninspected vessels that are not in navigation—precisely the situation here.

**III. The Coast Guard does not have exclusive authority over occupational safety and health standards for uninspected vessels.**

The Coast Guard’s authority under 46 U.S.C. § 4102 and 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01 is limited to *safety* standards related to fire protection, life-saving equipment, and emergency systems. It does not include comprehensive occupational safety and health standards such as those governing exposure to hazardous chemicals, machine guarding, or emergency response procedures—core areas of OSHA’s jurisdiction. In *United States v. National Steel Corp.*, the Court held that “the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority is not coextensive with OSHA’s.” 431 U.S. at 274. The Coast Guard’s regulations are “narrow in scope and focused on vessel integrity and navigation safety,” not on the health and safety of individual workers. Id.

In *Barnes*, the Court rejected the argument that the Coast Guard’s authority over maritime safety preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction.
