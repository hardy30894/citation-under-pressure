**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCSLA’S CHOICE-OF-LAW PROVISION DOES NOT PERMIT STATE WAGE AND HOUR LAWS TO GOVERN ACTIVITIES ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF UNLESS FEDERAL LAW LEAVES A “SIGNIFICANT VOID OR GAP.”

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) provides that “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted, the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State … are declared to be the law of the United States” for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (1964). This Court has repeatedly held that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law under § 1333(a)(2)(A) only where federal law fails to address the subject matter at issue. In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Supreme Court explained that the OCSLA incorporates state law “to fill the interstitial voids” in federal law. *Id.* at 359. The Court emphasized that where federal law provides a comprehensive remedy, state law does not apply. *Id.* at 366–67.

Consistent with *Rodrigue*, the Fifth Circuit has long required a “significant void or gap” in federal law before state law may be applied on the OCS. *Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). In *Cont’l Oil*, the court held that state law could not supplement federal maritime law where the federal scheme was “complete and comprehensive.” *Id.* at 1036. The court reasoned that the OCSLA’s incorporation of state law is “limited to situations where federal law is silent or inadequate.” *Id.* at 1035.

This Court has likewise recognized that state law is not applied on the OCS where federal law is comprehensive. In *United States v. Louisiana*, 339 U.S. 699 (1950), the Supreme Court held that state law does not govern submerged lands under federal jurisdiction unless federal law expressly defers to state law. *Id.* at 705. The Court explained that federal law occupies the field where it is “complete and dominant.” *Id.* at 706.

Similarly, in *United States v. Texas*, 339 U.S. 707 (1950), the Supreme Court reiterated that state law is displaced where federal law provides a “complete and self-contained” regulatory scheme. *Id.* at 718. The Court cautioned that state law may not be applied to supplement federal law where the federal scheme is “exclusive and pervasive.” *Id.* at 719.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), further supports this principle. In *Sharpnack*, the Court held that state criminal laws could not be applied on federal enclaves where federal law provided a “complete and detailed” statutory scheme. *Id.* at 293. The Court explained that state law is not adopted as federal law unless federal law is “silent or inadequate.” *Id.* at 294.

These authorities establish that state law is not applied on the OCS unless federal law leaves a “significant void or gap.” *Cont’l Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036. The Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”) is a comprehensive federal statute that regulates wages, hours, and working conditions. 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq. (1964). It provides a detailed framework for minimum wages, overtime, and recordkeeping, and it expressly allows for more protective state laws only where they are not inconsistent with the FLSA. *Id.* § 218. Because the FLSA is a complete and self-contained scheme, state wage and hour laws do not apply on the OCS.

II. CALIFORNIA’S WAGE AND HOUR LAWS ARE INCONSISTENT WITH THE FLSA AND THEREFORE DO NOT GOVERN ACTIVITIES ON THE OCS.

Even if state law were applicable, California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA and thus do not apply on the OCS. The OCSLA provides that state law is adopted as federal law only to the extent it is “not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (1964). In *Rodrigue*, the Supreme Court held that state law is inconsistent with federal law where it conflicts with federal policy or undermines federal objectives. 395 U.S. at 366–67.

The FLSA’s savings clause expressly permits state laws that are “more protective” of workers, but only where they are not inconsistent with the FLSA. 29 U.S.C. § 218. California’s meal period requirement conflicts with the FLSA’s framework, which does not mandate meal periods but instead regulates overtime and minimum wages. *See* 29 U.S.C. §§ 206, 207. Because California’s meal period requirement imposes obligations beyond those in the FLSA, it is inconsistent with federal law and cannot be applied on the OCS.

This Court’s decision in *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19 (1947), supports this conclusion. In *California*, the Supreme Court held that state laws are inconsistent with federal law where they impose obligations that conflict with federal policy. *Id.* at 26–27. The Court explained that state law must yield where it “stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.” *Id.* at 27.

Similarly, in *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52 (1941), the Supreme Court held that state laws are preempted where they conflict with federal immigration law. *Id.* at 67. The Court explained that state law must give way where it “tends to frustrate the operation of the federal program.” *Id.* at 66.

These authorities establish that California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA and cannot be applied on the OCS. Because the FLSA is a comprehensive federal scheme and California’s laws conflict with it, the district court correctly dismissed Newton’s state law claims.
