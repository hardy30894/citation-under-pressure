The Fifth Amendment provides that private property shall not be taken for public use without just compensation. Chicago, Burlington & Quincy R.R. Co. v. Chicago, 166 U.S. 226, 241 (1897). A physical invasion by the United States constitutes a taking. United States v. Causby, 328 U.S. 256, 261 (1946) (“if the United States erected an elevated railway over respondents’ land, it would be taking property”).

The 1953 grant conveyed only “a perpetual easement and right to enter and erect, operate, maintain, repair, rebuild, and patrol one or more electrical power transmission lines and appurtenant signal lines, poles, towers, wires, cables, and appliances necessary in connection therewith.” The Bonneville Power Administration installed fiber-optic cables whose capacity far exceeds any present necessity for signal lines appurtenant to power transmission and then leased the surplus strands to third parties. That additional, permanent physical presence on Tuthill’s land is an occupation outside the terms of the easement.

An easement limited to a stated purpose does not authorize a different or greater use. Western Union Tel. Co. v. Pennsylvania R.R. Co., 195 U.S. 540, 570 (1904). When the government places structures or wires that exceed the granted right, the excess constitutes a new and compensable servitude. St. Louis v. Western Union Tel. Co., 148 U.S. 92, 98-99 (1893). The same principle applies to federal easements acquired for limited public purposes. United States v. Cress, 243 U.S. 316, 328 (1917).

Because the fiber-optic installation and third-party leases impose a physical occupation not authorized by the 1953 instrument, the United States has effected a per se taking. The Court of Federal Claims therefore erred in granting summary judgment on the ground that no property interest had been taken.
