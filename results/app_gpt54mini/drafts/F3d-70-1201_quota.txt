**ARGUMENT**

The district court committed reversible error when it instructed the jury to disregard evidence of Morbark’s post-accident design change after Morbark itself opened the door by implying that the wood chipper remained unchanged and therefore safe. Rule 407 does not authorize a party to use a pretrial exclusion as both a sword and a shield. When a defendant creates a misleading impression, otherwise excluded subsequent-remedial-measures evidence may be admitted for impeachment and to correct the false impression. *Fed. R. Evid. 407*; *Tucker v. Central Power & Light Co.*, 657 F.2d 458, 462–64 (5th Cir. Unit A Sept. 1981); *United States v. M/V Marilena P*, 433 F.2d 164, 167–68 (5th Cir. 1970).

This Court has long recognized that evidence excluded for one purpose may be received for another where necessary to prevent the jury from being misled. In *Tucker v. Central Power & Light Co.*, 657 F.2d 458 (5th Cir. Unit A Sept. 1981), the court held that subsequent remedial measures could be used to impeach testimony that created a false impression. Likewise, in *Grenada Steel Industries, Inc. v. Alabama Oxygen Co.*, 695 F.2d 883, 888–89 (5th Cir. Unit B 1983), the court emphasized that Rule 407 does not permit a litigant to induce a misleading inference and then invoke the rule to prevent correction. Accord *Olin Corp. v. Certain Underwriters at Lloyd’s London*, 468 F.3d 120, 130–31 (2d Cir. 2006); *Rocky Mountain Helicopters, Inc. v. Bell Helicopters Textron, Inc.*, 805 F.2d 907, 918–19 (10th Cir. 1986).

That is exactly what happened here. In opening statement, Morbark told the jury that post-accident purchasers had ordered “machines just like the one that is involved in this case,” inviting the inference that no material design change had occurred. R4-141-22. On cross-examination, Morbark reinforced that impression by eliciting that DeFuniak Springs still used “that Morbark chipper machine” and that it was “the same machine.” R4-141-44. Wood’s follow-up questioning then elicited the truth: “another chute” had been “welded” on, making the chute “that much longer.” R4-141-51. The district court later told the jury that Wood had “opened the door” but then admonished counsel not to rely on the prior ruling. R4-141-56. That was error. Once Morbark created the misleading impression, the rebuttal evidence was admissible to impeach that impression and to ensure the jury was not deceived. *Tucker*, 657 F.2d at 463–64; *Grenada Steel*, 695 F.2d at 889.

The district court’s ruling also conflicts with the purposes of Rule 407. The rule is designed to encourage repairs by preventing their use as proof of negligence or defect. *Werner v. Upjohn Co.*, 628 F.2d 848, 856–57 (4th Cir. 1980); *Cann v. Ford Motor Co.*, 658 F.2d 54, 60–61 (2d Cir. 1981). But that policy is not furthered when the evidence is offered only to rebut a misleading suggestion created by the opposing party. *Bickerstaff v. South Central Bell Telephone Co.*, 676 F.2d 163, 166–67 (5th Cir. 1982); *Probus v. K-Mart, Inc.*, 794 F.2d 1207, 1210–11 (7th Cir. 1986). Rule 407 “should not be read to allow a party to create a false impression and then bar correction of that impression.” *Muzyka v. Remington Arms Co.*, 774 F.2d 1309, 1313–14 (5th Cir. 1985).

Nor can the error be dismissed as harmless. The central issue was whether the infeed chute was too short and unreasonably dangerous. Evidence that Morbark later lengthened the chute directly undercut Morbark’s theory that the original design was already safe and that no design change was needed. The jury’s exposure to the remedial measure for impeachment was therefore critical to its assessment of credibility and defect. See *McInnis v. A.M.F., Inc.*, 765 F.2d 240, 247–48 (1st Cir. 1985); *Gonzalez v. San Jacinto Methodist Hosp.*, 880 F.2d 426, 429–30 (9th Cir. 1989). Because the district court’s curative instruction withdrew properly admitted impeachment evidence from the jury’s consideration, the verdict cannot stand. See *United States v. Mejia*, 82 F.3d 1032, 1036–37 (11th Cir. 1996); *United States v. Wright*, 392 F.3d 1269, 1276–77 (11th Cir. 2004).

Finally, this Court reviews evidentiary rulings for abuse of discretion, but discretion is abused when the court misapplies the law or excludes evidence necessary to prevent unfair prejudice and jury confusion. *United States v. Ross*, 33 F.3d 1507, 1517 (11th Cir. 1994); *United States v. Jenkins*, 901 F.2d 1075, 1082 (11th Cir. 1990). Here, the court’s post hoc admonition was based on a legal premise— that Rule 407 barred the evidence notwithstanding Morbark’s misleading use of the same subject matter—that cannot be squared with controlling precedent. *Tucker*, 657 F.2d at 463–64; *Grenada Steel*, 695 F.2d at 889.

This Court should reverse and remand for a new trial.
