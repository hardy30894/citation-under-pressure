**Argument**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only debts “for money, property, services, or . . . credit, to the extent obtained by” fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). That language limits nondischargeability to the property actually obtained by the fraud and does not sweep in every collateral consequence a state may attach to the underlying wrong. The court of appeals’ contrary approach reads the phrase “to the extent obtained by” out of the statute.

The Bankruptcy Act and the discharge provisions have always been construed in light of the Bankruptcy Code’s central policy of a fresh start for the honest debtor. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). At the same time, exceptions to discharge are to be confined to their terms, because discharge is the rule and exception the exception. *Wetmore v. Markoe*, 196 U.S. 68, 77 (1904). This Court has repeatedly recognized that bankruptcy legislation is meant to relieve the debtor from preexisting obligations, not to impose new liabilities beyond the statutory text. *Stellwagen v. Clum*, 245 U.S. 605, 617 (1918); *Johnson v. Norris*, 190 U.S. 103, 106 (1903).

Under that settled approach, Cohen’s obligation to refund the excess rents is plainly nondischargeable because it represents money obtained through fraud. But the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act’s treble-damages, fees, and costs award is different. Those amounts were not “obtained by” Cohen from the tenants. They are statutory penalties imposed after the fact. Bankruptcy law has long distinguished between a debt for the thing obtained and an added liability imposed as punishment. In *Crawford v. Burke*, 195 U.S. 176, 189 (1904), the Court explained that the discharge turns on the nature of the debt as fixed by the statute. Likewise, *Dunbar v. Dunbar*, 190 U.S. 340, 352 (1903), treated the discharge inquiry as controlled by the statutory description of the debt, not by policy-based enlargement.

That distinction is especially important where the damages at issue are punitive. A punitive award does not restore property to the creditor; it punishes the debtor. The Court has long treated punitive obligations differently from compensatory ones in bankruptcy-related contexts. See *McIntyre v. Kavanaugh*, 242 U.S. 138, 141-42 (1916) (holding that a discharge does not protect a debtor from liabilities arising from wilful misconduct); *Fidelity & Deposit Co. v. Arenz*, 290 U.S. 66, 68-69 (1933) (looking to the nature of the obligation created by the wrongful act). But those cases do not authorize expanding the nondischargeability exception beyond the debt actually obtained by fraud. Congress knew how to write broader language when it wanted to. It did not do so here.

The phrase “to the extent obtained by” is limiting language. It ties nondischargeability to the extent of the money or property acquired by fraud, not to every later-created debt that happens to be associated with the fraud. The debt for treble damages here arose only because state law chose to penalize the conduct further. That award was not “obtained” by Cohen in any ordinary sense. It was imposed on him. The better reading is therefore that only the amount fraudulently obtained is nondischargeable, while the punitive increment is dischargeable.

The court below relied on the view that all components of a fraud judgment share the same fate. But that conflicts with the Code’s text and this Court’s insistence that exceptions to discharge remain tied to their statutory elements. *Local Loan Co.*, 292 U.S. at 244; *Wetmore*, 196 U.S. at 77. It also creates an unnecessary federal displacement of state remedial choices. State law may define the fraud remedy, but federal bankruptcy law determines discharge. Once that distinction is kept in view, the answer is clear: the debt to refund the overcharge is excepted from discharge; the treble-damages overlay is not.

For those reasons, the judgment should be reversed insofar as it treats the treble-damages, attorney’s-fee, and cost components as nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A).
