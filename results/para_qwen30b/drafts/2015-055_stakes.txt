**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively to cases like Welch’s—must be answered in the affirmative. The Supreme Court has long held that when a new rule of constitutional law “clarifies” a preexisting constitutional right, it applies retroactively to all cases pending on direct review or not yet final. *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989). *Johnson* did not create a new constitutional right; it clarified the meaning of the Due Process Clause by invalidating the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) as unconstitutionally vague. Because the rule in *Johnson* was a clarification of existing constitutional principles, it applies retroactively to all cases not yet final on direct review.

The Court in *Johnson* held that the ACCA’s residual clause—defining a “violent felony” as one “that otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another”—violated the Due Process Clause because it failed to provide fair notice of the conduct it punished. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. The Court emphasized that the clause’s “hypothetical risk” standard was “so vague that it fails to give people adequate notice of the conduct it punishes.” Id. at 598. This was not a new constitutional principle but a reaffirmation of the long-standing requirement that criminal statutes must provide fair warning of what conduct is prohibited. *Id.* at 597 (citing *Dawson v. Delaware*, 503 U.S. 159, 165 (1992); *United States v. Williams*, 504 U.S. 36, 50 (1992)). The Court did not announce a new rule of criminal procedure; it applied settled constitutional doctrine to a statute that had long been unconstitutionally vague.

The Court has repeatedly held that rules that clarify preexisting constitutional rights are retroactive. In *Griffith v. Kentucky*, 479 U.S. 314, 328 (1987), the Court stated that “a new rule for the conduct of criminal prosecutions is to be applied retroactively to all cases, state or federal, pending on direct review or not yet final.” The Court explained that such rules “clarify the law” and “do not create new constitutional rights.” *Id.* at 328. *Johnson* fits squarely within this category. The Court did not create a new constitutional right; it applied the settled principle that vague criminal statutes violate due process. The rule in *Johnson* “clarifies” that the residual clause, as applied, failed to meet the minimum standards of fair notice required by the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. Because the rule was a clarification, it applies retroactively.

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that Welch’s prior Florida robbery conviction did not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA’s elements clause. The Florida statute under which Welch was convicted—Florida Statute § 812.13(1)—criminalized robbery by “force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.” At the time of Welch’s 1996 conviction, Florida courts had not uniformly defined the threshold level of force required. Some decisions held that mere snatching, even without significant injury, could constitute robbery. *See, e.g., Andre v. State*, 431 So.2d 1042, 1043 (Fla. 5th Dist. Ct. App. 1983). Others held that “sufficient force” must be shown, even if minimal. *McCloud v. State*, 335 So.2d 257, 258 (Fla. 1976). The state’s highest court had not definitively resolved the issue. *Robinson v. State*, 692 So.2d 883, 886 (Fla. 1997). Under the categorical approach, a conviction qualifies as a violent felony only if the statute, as applied, necessarily requires the use of physical force. *James v. United States*, 550 U.S. 205, 208 (2007). Because Florida law at the time permitted conviction for conduct involving only minimal force—such as a sudden snatch—*Johnson*’s clarification that the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague renders the ACCA enhancement invalid for cases like Welch’s.

The Court has recognized that when a statute is held unconstitutionally vague, the defendant is entitled to relief even if the error was not raised at trial. *United States v. Booker*, 543 U.S. 220, 245 (2005) (holding that the retroactive application of a constitutional rule is appropriate when it “clarifies” the law). The Court has also held that when a defendant is sentenced under a statute later declared unconstitutional, the sentence must be vacated. *United States v. Chitwood*, 676 F.3d 971, 979 (11th Cir. 2012). Welch’s sentence was based on a predicate offense that, under *Johnson*, cannot be considered a “violent felony” under the ACCA. The residual clause, which the Court invalidated in *Johnson*, was the only basis on which Welch’s robbery conviction could qualify as a violent felony. Without it, the ACCA enhancement fails.

The government’s argument that *Johnson* should not apply retroactively because it is a “new rule” is foreclosed by precedent. In *Teague*, the Court distinguished between “new rules” that create new constitutional rights and those that clarify existing ones. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 310. *Johnson* falls into the latter category. The Court did not announce a new constitutional principle; it applied settled due process doctrine to a statute that had long been unconstitutionally vague. The rule in *Johnson* “clarifies” that the residual clause fails to provide fair notice, a principle that has long been part of the Constitution’s guarantee of due process.

For these reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively to Welch’s case. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
