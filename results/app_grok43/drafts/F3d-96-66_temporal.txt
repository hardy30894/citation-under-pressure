The district court erred in granting summary judgment, as the record discloses triable issues whether the vessel breached its duty to maintain the snatch blocks in the upright position required for safe cargo operations. Under settled maritime principles, a shipowner must exercise reasonable care to furnish longshoremen a safe place to work and to correct known hazards in the vessel's equipment. See Gutierrez v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 373 U.S. 206, 214 (1963) (recognizing liability where the owner "failed to exercise reasonable care" in conditions affecting cargo handling); Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn, 346 U.S. 406, 409 (1953) (affirming that the owner "owes to the stevedore's employees the duty of exercising reasonable care").

The crew's responsibility for positioning the fixed snatch blocks, combined with the block's improper downward placement on cargo, created a hazard that the vessel had both the means and the obligation to eliminate before longshoremen resumed work. See Alaska S.S. Co. v. Petterson, 347 U.S. 396, 397 (1954) (imposing liability for unseaworthy appliances that render the workplace unsafe). Serbin's decision to reposition the block himself does not, as a matter of law, negate the vessel's antecedent breach; maritime law has long rejected the notion that a workman assumes the risk of dangers created by the owner's negligence when he acts to facilitate ongoing operations. See Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith, 305 U.S. 424, 428 (1939) (holding that assumption of risk is unavailable where the hazard stems from the owner's failure to provide safe equipment).

Genuine factual disputes therefore remain concerning the vessel's negligence and proximate causation of the fall. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
