**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction in any cases involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between federal patent policy and the proper allocation of judicial authority between state and federal systems. The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over patent-related claims when the resolution of a substantial federal issue is necessary to the outcome of a state-law cause of action. *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09, 108 S. Ct. 2166, 100 L. Ed. 2d 811 (1988). Here, the state-law legal malpractice claim *necessarily depends* on the resolution of a substantial federal patent issue—the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar—making federal jurisdiction not only appropriate but exclusive.

The central premise of the Texas Supreme Court’s opinion is that the federal patent issue in this case is “necessary, disputed, and substantial” within the context of the malpractice suit. *Gunn v. Minton*, 54 Tex. Sup. Ct. J. 538, 542 (2011). This conclusion is sound and consistent with the Supreme Court’s framework in *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314, 125 S. Ct. 2363, 162 L. Ed. 2d 257 (2005), which establishes that federal jurisdiction exists where (1) resolving a federal issue is necessary to resolution of the state-law claim; (2) the federal issue is actually disputed; (3) the federal issue is substantial; and (4) federal jurisdiction will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. The Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit has repeatedly applied this test in the context of patent malpractice claims, and its precedents are binding on this Court in interpreting § 1338(a) of the Judicial Code.

In *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed. Cir. 2007), the Federal Circuit held that a legal malpractice claim arising from patent litigation “arises under” federal patent law when “establishing patent infringement is a necessary element of the malpractice claim.” The court emphasized that the patent issue must be “substantial and contested,” and that federal resolution was “intended by Congress.” Id. at 1273. The same principle applies here: Minton’s malpractice claim hinges entirely on whether the experimental use exception would have allowed the '643 patent to survive the on-sale bar. Without a favorable determination on that federal issue, Minton cannot prove causation—i.e., that but for his attorneys’ failure to raise the exception, he would have prevailed in the underlying patent suit. *See* *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004) (“To prevail on a legal malpractice claim, a plaintiff must show that ... [his attorney’s negligence] proximately caused the plaintiff’s injuries.”). Thus, the federal patent issue is not merely incidental—it is the *very foundation* of the malpractice claim.

The Federal Circuit has consistently applied this principle in similar cases. In *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281, 1285 (Fed. Cir. 2007), the court affirmed that “when a state-law malpractice claim depends on the resolution of a substantial federal patent issue, federal jurisdiction is proper.” The court further noted that “the uniformity of patent law” is a compelling federal interest that justifies exclusive federal jurisdiction. Id. at 1286. This interest is not diminished simply because the malpractice claim is brought in state court. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “Congress has provided federal courts jurisdiction over civil actions generally ‘arising under’ federal law and also over actions specifically ‘arising under’ any federal law relating to patents.” *28 U.S.C. § 1338(a)*. The language of § 1338(a) is not limited to claims where patent law creates the cause of action; it extends to any case where the plaintiff’s right to relief *necessarily depends* on federal patent law. *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808–09.

The Texas Supreme Court’s dissenting opinion mischaracterizes the Federal Circuit’s approach as “misguided” and “unresponsive” to federalism concerns. But the dissent fails to engage with the *Grable* factors. The federal issue here is not merely “implicated”—it is *disputed*, *substantial*, and *necessary*. The experimental use exception was not a speculative defense; it was a recognized doctrine in patent law, *see* *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005), and was raised in the federal appellate court. The fact that the Federal Circuit ultimately rejected it does not diminish its substantiality. Indeed, the very fact that the issue was litigated and contested in federal court underscores its importance.

App. 2010), is misplaced. Those cases involved malpractice claims where patent issues were “merely implicated incidentally,” *New Tek*, 702 N.W.2d at 342, or where the claim did not depend on patent law for its resolution.
