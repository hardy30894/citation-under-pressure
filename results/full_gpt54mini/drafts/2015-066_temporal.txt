Section 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment because it does not punish Bryant for his prior tribal convictions; it punishes his present domestic assault, and prior convictions may be used to establish recidivist liability. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a recidivist statute “is a stiffened penalty for the latest crime, which is considered to be an aggravated offense because a repetitive one,” Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948). Likewise, habitual-offender laws do not “create[] a separate offense” based on prior convictions; they enhance the consequences of the new crime. Moore v. Missouri, 159 U.S. 673, 677 (1895); McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311, 312-13 (1901).

That principle defeats Bryant’s Sixth Amendment claim. The right to counsel attaches to the proceeding in which liberty is actually at stake, but a later federal prosecution may permissibly rely on the historical fact of a prior conviction. See Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 68-69 (1932); Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109, 114-15 (1967) (holding that a conviction obtained without counsel could not be used “to support guilt or enhance punishment” where the prior conviction itself was constitutionally infirm). Here, however, the tribal convictions were not constitutionally infirm in the relevant forum. Tribal courts are not bound by the federal Constitution’s Sixth Amendment. Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376, 384-85 (1896) (holding that the Constitution does not operate as a restraint upon tribal self-government in the same manner as upon the federal government).

Bryant’s argument improperly treats a valid tribal conviction as though it were void merely because it would have required counsel in a state or federal court. That is not the rule. Courts have long distinguished between the validity of a prior conviction and the later use of that conviction to prove an element or enhance punishment. In Moore, the Court upheld use of prior convictions under a habitual-offender statute even though the prior convictions were not themselves retried. 159 U.S. at 677-78. In McDonald, the Court similarly sustained a recidivist prosecution based on earlier convictions, emphasizing that the statute “simply imposes a severer punishment for the second offense than would otherwise be imposed.” 180 U.S. at 313. Section 117(a) operates in precisely that way.

Nor does the Sixth Amendment bar Congress from defining the predicate offenses for a federal recidivist statute by reference to prior tribal convictions. Congress may classify offenses and determine what facts constitute recidivism. See Williams v. State of North Carolina, 317 U.S. 287, 291-92 (1942) (recognizing the effect of prior judgments in later proceedings); Reynolds v. United States, 98 U.S. 145, 167-68 (1878) (explaining that constitutional analysis turns on the legal character of the proceeding at issue). Section 117(a) uses prior convictions only as a legislative trigger for enhanced punishment of a new federal domestic assault. Nothing in the Sixth Amendment forbids Congress from relying on convictions valid when entered under the law governing the tribunal that entered them.

Bryant also cannot transform this case into one about reliability. The Supreme Court has never held that every uncounseled conviction is so unreliable that it cannot be recognized in later proceedings. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly allowed collateral use of prior judgments where the prior tribunal had jurisdiction and the judgment was valid on its face. See Ex parte Watkins, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193, 202-03 (1830); Custis v. United States, 137 U.S. 297, 300-01 (1890) (limiting collateral attack on prior convictions in later proceedings). A contrary rule would create an anomaly: a conviction valid under the governing law of the forum would become unusable merely because Congress later chose to punish repeat offenders more severely.

Finally, Bryant’s position would conflict with the federal government’s settled power to define offenses by reference to prior status or conduct. In Ex parte Wilson, 114 U.S. 417, 423-24 (1885), the Court recognized that the legal consequences of a conviction may differ depending on the later statutory context. Section 117(a) reflects that same legislative judgment. It does not invalidate tribal convictions; it simply gives them the recidivist effect Congress chose for a new federal crime.

Because Section 117(a) punishes Bryant’s latest domestic assault, and because the predicate tribal convictions were valid in the court that entered them, the Sixth Amendment is not violated. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
