ARGUMENT

**I. The Tenth Amendment Protects the Reserved Powers of the States, Not a Personal Right That a Private Criminal Defendant May Invoke.**

The Tenth Amendment provides that powers not delegated to the United States "are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." U.S. Const. amend. X. This Court has already answered whether a private party, rather than a State, may invoke that reservation to defeat a federal enactment. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies argued that the operations of a federal instrumentality intruded on matters reserved to the States. This Court rejected the claim on the threshold ground that the private litigants, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." *Id.* at 144. The logic of that holding controls here. Bond, like the utilities in *Tennessee Electric Power Co.*, is not a State and does not purport to act on any State's behalf. She therefore may not commandeer the Tenth Amendment to vindicate an interest that belongs, if it belongs to anyone, to the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania.

That allocation of criminal authority between the national government and the States is itself a structural, sovereign-to-sovereign matter, not an individual entitlement. As this Court explained in *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), "[u]nder our federal system[,] the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of [its] delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States." *Id.* at 109. The question whether Congress has exceeded its delegated powers in creating a particular offense is a question about the boundary between two sovereigns; it is not a personal liberty interest of the sort that this Court has historically permitted individual defendants to raise on their own behalf.

**II. Individual Defendants Already Possess, and Bond Exercised, the Personal Constitutional Protections Available to Vindicate Liberty Interests in Criminal Prosecutions; the Tenth Amendment Adds Nothing to That Personal Arsenal.**

This Court's precedents demonstrate that a defendant's personal liberty in a criminal prosecution is protected through guarantees that run directly to the individual—not through the Tenth Amendment's structural allocation of sovereign power. In *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581 (1900), the Court explained that the grand-jury guarantee of the Fifth Amendment operates as a direct personal protection: "In a Federal court no person can be held to answer for a capital or otherwise infamous crime unless by indictment by a grand jury, with the exceptions stated in the Fifth Amendment." *Id.* at 605. Likewise, in *Giaccio v. Pennsylvania*, 382 U.S. 399 (1965), this Court vindicated an individual defendant's personal due-process interest by striking down, as unconstitutionally vague, a Pennsylvania statute permitting juries to assess costs against acquitted defendants. These decisions confirm that when the Constitution intends to arm an individual with a personal shield against prosecution, it does so through provisions—the Due Process Clause, the grand-jury guarantee, equal protection—that speak directly to persons. *Cf. Lang v. New Jersey*, 209 U.S. 467 (1908) (evaluating a defendant's equal-protection challenge to a statutory classification of criminal defendants on its own terms, without resort to structural federalism doctrine). Bond herself invoked exactly this personal arsenal below, arguing that § 229 was unconstitutionally vague as applied to her conduct. She needs no additional, freestanding Tenth Amendment cause of action to protect her personal liberty; the vehicle already exists, and she used it.

**III. Because Standing Is a Threshold, Jurisdictional Inquiry, the Absence of a Cognizable Interest Under the Tenth Amendment Forecloses Reaching the Merits of Bond's Claim.**

This Court has long treated the existence of a party's right to raise a particular constitutional claim as a threshold inquiry logically antecedent to the merits. In *Serra v. Mortiga*, 204 U.S. 470 (1907), the Court observed that whether an indictment states an offense "is a question which has to be met at almost every stage of criminal proceedings," and that a court's jurisdiction to decide such questions is distinct from whether it decides them correctly. *Id.* at 475–76. And in *Jenkins v. McKeithen*, 395 U.S. 411 (1969), this Court noted with evident significance that the lower court "did not explicitly rule on the issue of whether appellant lacked standing to challenge the Act," underscoring that the standing question must be squarely addressed before a federal enactment's validity is adjudicated. *Id.* at 414. Consistent with that practice, the proper course here is to resolve the standing question first—and, applying *Tennessee Electric Power Co.*, to hold that Bond, a private individual unaccompanied by the Commonwealth, lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to 18 U.S.C. § 229.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
