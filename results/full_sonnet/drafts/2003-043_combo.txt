**ARGUMENT**

**I. An assessment against a partnership does not extend the limitations period for collecting the debt from an individual partner.**

Section 6502(a) extends the time to collect an assessed tax to ten years, but only "where the assessment of any tax imposed by this title has been made within the period of limitation properly applicable thereto." 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). That extension runs to the taxpayer assessed, not to some other legally distinct person who might ultimately share liability for the underlying debt. This reading follows the structure Congress chose: an assessment must "provide identification of the taxpayer," 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1, and "taxpayer" means "any person subject to any internal revenue tax." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14). A partnership and its partners are different "persons" under the Code, 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), each with its own taxpayer identification number and its own three-year assessment clock. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

This Court's precedents confirm that an assessment binds only the person it names. In *E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co. v. Davis*, 264 U.S. 456 (1924), the Court explained that an assessment functions as the administrative equivalent of a judgment, permitting the government to proceed against the property of the person assessed—precisely because assessment fixes liability against a particular, identified debtor. An assessment naming only a partnership fixes no such judgment-like liability against a partner never named.

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this principle below. In *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), the court affirmed a district court that had squarely rejected the government's present theory, explaining that although a partner may be substantively liable for partnership taxes under state law, the government's own theory conceded that "the only reason for naming such individuals … is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action." *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). Absent an individual assessment, the government's only recourse is "court action"—precisely what it failed to pursue here within three years.

*United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), reinforces the point. The panel there affirmed a holding that a tax lien could not attach to a partner's individual property where the assessment named only the partnership and unidentified partners, because § 6203 requires identification of "the taxpayer" against whom the assessment runs. *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 675–76 (S.D. Cal. 1958). The same logic controls the limitations question here: because no assessment was ever made against the Gallettis or the Briguglios individually, no ten-year extension arose as to them, and the ordinary three-year period of § 6501(a) expired long before the IRS filed its proofs of claim.

**II. This Court has repeatedly required strict, not implied, compliance with the procedural prerequisites for extending the government's collection period.**

The rule that limitations extensions in tax collection be strictly, not liberally, construed is well established. In *Bowers v. New York & Albany Lighterage Co.*, 273 U.S. 346 (1927), the Court held that an extension of the assessment period could arise only through the specific procedural mechanism Congress prescribed and would not be implied from surrounding circumstances. The same insistence on formal compliance governs here: Congress tied the ten-year period to "the assessment of the tax" against "the taxpayer," 26 U.S.C. §§ 6502(a), 6203, and nothing in the statute allows an assessment against one taxpayer to be read as an assessment against another.

*United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. 489 (1930), is instructive by analogy. There the Court held that a suit against transferees of a dissolved corporation was untimely because the limitations period applicable to the transferees' own liability controlled, not the taxpayer's, and that statutory exceptions extending the government's time to collect were not to be enlarged by implication beyond their terms. The same principle forecloses the government's effort here to borrow the Partnership's extended period and apply it to partners never assessed.

Likewise, in *Botany Worsted Mills v. United States*, 278 U.S. 282 (1929), and *Florsheim Bros. Drygoods Co. v. United States*, 280 U.S. 453 (1930), the Court declined to construe ambiguous statutory language governing tax limitations periods in the government's favor, insisting on adherence to the specific mechanism Congress provided for extending the collection window. And in *Rothensies v. Electric Storage Battery Co.*, 329 U.S. 296 (1946), the Court warned against equitable doctrines that would "make a shambles of the statute of limitations in tax matters," *id.* at 302—a warning equally applicable to the government's request that an assessment against a partnership be treated, without more, as an assessment against each partner.

Finally, *Bull v. United States*, 295 U.S. 247 (1935), confirms that assessment is the mechanism by which the government converts a claimed liability into an enforceable debt against a specific taxpayer; until that mechanism is invoked against a particular person, no extension of the collection period arises as to him.

**III. State law confirms that a claim against the partnership is not a claim against the partners.**

Even apart from the federal assessment requirement, California law required the IRS to obtain a judgment against each partner before collecting from that partner's individual assets. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c) ("A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner."). The IRS obtained no such judgment, and the three-year window to do so has long since closed. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

Because the IRS never assessed Debtors individually and never obtained a judgment against them within the applicable limitations period, its claims were properly disallowed. The judgment below should be affirmed.
