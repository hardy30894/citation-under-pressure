# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF § 1692a(6) DEFINES "DEBT COLLECTOR" BY REFERENCE TO WHOSE DEBT IS BEING COLLECTED, NOT WHEN THE DEBT WAS ACQUIRED

The FDCPA's operative definition of "debt collector" turns on a straightforward distinction: whether a person collects debts "owed or due another" or collects debts owed to itself. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). As the Fourth Circuit correctly held below, "the material distinction between a debt collector and a creditor . . . is whether a person's regular collection activity is only for itself (a creditor) or whether it regularly collects for others (a debt collector)—not, as the plaintiffs urge, whether the debt was in default when the person acquired it." *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA, Inc.*, 817 F.3d 131, 136-37 (4th Cir. 2016). This Court's precedent confirms that reading. In *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995), the Court observed that "[t]he Act's definition of the term 'debt collector' includes a person 'who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] . . . another.'" Nothing in that definition asks when the debt fell into default; it asks only for whom the collector is acting.

Petitioners' theory—that a purchaser of defaulted debt is automatically a "debt collector" because the debt was in default at acquisition—finds no textual support. Petitioners rest heavily on the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which removes from the debt-collector definition "any person collecting or attempting to collect any debt . . . owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another to the extent such activity . . . concerns a debt which was not in default at the time it was obtained by such person." But that provision is an *exclusion*—it operates only after a person has already satisfied one of the affirmative definitions in the body of § 1692a(6). As the Eleventh Circuit explained, "where a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a 'debt collector' as a matter of course. [Instead] . . . he must satisfy the Act's substantive requirements." *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1314 (11th Cir. 2015). Petitioners invert this structure, treating an exclusion designed to protect loan servicers of non-defaulted debt—see S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977)—as an affirmative source of liability for creditors who purchase defaulted debt for their own account.

Petitioners' reliance on the "creditor" definition in § 1692a(4) fares no better. That provision excludes from "creditor" status "any person to the extent that he receives an assignment or transfer of a debt in default *solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another*." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) (emphasis added). Santander did not acquire petitioners' loans to facilitate collection for CitiFinancial; it purchased the receivables outright, for its own account, as part of a $3.55 billion bundle. The narrow exclusion for agents and servicers simply does not apply to an entity that has purchased the debt itself.

## II. RESPONDENT COLLECTED DEBTS OWED TO ITSELF, NOT DEBTS "OWED OR DUE ANOTHER"

Once CitiFinancial Auto sold the defaulted loans to Santander, the debts became Santander's own; Santander was no longer collecting "debts owed or due another." § 1692a(6). Petitioners argue that the phrase "owed or due another" should be read to encompass debts that were originally owed to a different entity, even after assignment. That reading cannot be squared with the statute's purpose or its temporal logic. As the Ninth Circuit held, "[t]he statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs'] interpretation that 'owed or due another' means 'originally owed or due another.'" *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013). The relevant inquiry is not whether a debt was *originally* owed to someone else, but whether it is owed to another *at the time of the collection activity*. *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1318 ("[O]ur inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether Capital One regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to Capital One; our inquiry is whether Capital One regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection.").

This construction follows basic principles of statutory interpretation. The phrase "asserted to be owed or due another" modifies the same temporal frame as "owed or due another"; both describe the status of the debt when the collector acts. See *Paroline v. United States*, 572 U.S. 434, 447 (2014) ("When several words are followed by a clause which is applicable as much to the first and other words as to the last, the natural construction of the language demands that the clause be read as applicable to all.").

## III. THE STRUCTURE AND PURPOSE OF THE FDCPA CONFIRM THAT CREDITORS COLLECTING THEIR OWN DEBTS ARE NOT REGULATED

Congress designed the FDCPA "to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors," 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e)—not to regulate creditors collecting on their own accounts. This distinction pervades the statute's structure, which "generally does not regulate creditors, when they collect debt on their own account." *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 133. Petitioners' rule would create an unworkable regime in which a consumer finance company like Santander—which lends money, services loans, and sometimes collects for others—would become subject to the FDCPA for *all* of its activities merely because it also purchases defaulted receivables. Congress did not intend such a sweeping result; it aimed only at conduct undertaken while acting as an agent-collector for another's debt.

Because Santander was collecting on debts it had purchased and owned outright, it was acting as a creditor, not a debt collector, when it engaged in the challenged conduct. The judgment below should be affirmed.
