**ARGUMENT**

### I. A credible showing of actual innocence is, by itself, an equitable gateway through AEDPA’s limitations bar.

This Court has already held that “a credible claim of actual innocence constitutes an equitable exception to AEDPA’s statute of limitations.” *Souter v. Jones*, 395 F.3d 577, 602 (6th Cir. 2005). That rule is rooted in the Supreme Court’s long-recognized principle that habeas courts may reach otherwise defaulted or time-barred claims when necessary to prevent the incarceration of one who is likely innocent. See *Schlup v. Delo*, 513 U.S. 298, 327 (1995); *House v. Bell*, 547 U.S. 518, 536–37 (2006). The miscarriage-of-justice exception exists because “in an extraordinary case, where a constitutional violation has probably resulted in the conviction of one who is actually innocent, a federal habeas court may grant the writ even in the absence of a showing of cause.” *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 496 (1986).

The Warden’s position conflicts with *Souter*. There, this Court held that a petitioner “who asserts a credible claim of actual innocence” may proceed despite AEDPA’s one-year deadline. 395 F.3d at 602. The Court did not condition that gateway on reasonable diligence. Nor should it: the exception rests not on the petitioner’s speed, but on the “fundamental miscarriage of justice” that would result if a potentially innocent person were barred from any merits review. *Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 314–15.

Perkins’s showing fits that equitable principle. He presented three affidavits suggesting that Damarr Jones, not Perkins, committed the killing and attempted to dispose of bloody clothing afterward. Whether those affidavits ultimately satisfy the demanding *Schlup* standard is a merits question for the district court in the first instance. See *House*, 547 U.S. at 538. What the court below could not do was impose a threshold diligence requirement that this Court’s precedent does not recognize. Under *Souter*, the question is whether Perkins has presented “new reliable evidence” sufficient to make it “more likely than not that no reasonable juror would have found petitioner guilty beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 324, 327.

### II. Pace v. DiGuglielmo did not silently overrule Souter’s actual-innocence exception.

The district court relied on *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), for the general proposition that a petitioner seeking equitable tolling must show diligence. But *Pace* addressed ordinary equitable tolling, not the separate miscarriage-of-justice gateway for actual innocence. The petitioner there argued that statutory tolling should apply because he had filed an untimely state petition; the Court rejected tolling and observed that he had not acted diligently. *Id.* at 418–19. *Pace* did not involve, discuss, or limit a freestanding actual-innocence gateway.

That distinction matters. The Supreme Court has repeatedly treated actual innocence as an exception apart from ordinary equitable tolling. In *Schlup*, the Court emphasized that a claim of innocence serves as a “gateway” permitting review of otherwise barred claims. 513 U.S. at 315. In *House*, the Court reiterated that the gateway asks whether new evidence makes it more likely than not that no reasonable juror would convict. 547 U.S. at 536–37. And in *McQuiggin v. Perkins*, 569 U.S. 383, 386 (2013), the Court later confirmed what *Souter* had already held in this Circuit: “actual innocence, if proved, serves as a gateway through which a petitioner may pass” notwithstanding AEDPA’s limitations period. Although *McQuiggin* postdates this appeal, it confirms that *Pace* did not displace the innocence exception recognized in *Souter*.

Nothing in *Pace* suggests that diligence is an additional element of actual innocence. To the contrary, the Court in *Schlup* warned against “tension” between procedural rules and the need to avoid wrongful convictions. 513 U.S. at 327. Requiring diligence as a prerequisite to even considering a credible innocence claim would improperly transform a narrow equitable exception into a dead letter for prisoners who discover exculpatory evidence only after years of silence by witnesses or state concealment.

### III. Perkins’s delay does not defeat his innocence gateway claim.

Even if delay is relevant to credibility, it is not dispositive. The Supreme Court has said that “unexplained delay in presenting new evidence bears on the determination whether the petitioner has made the requisite showing,” but delay does not create a categorical bar. *McQuiggin*, 569 U.S. at 399. A habeas court must assess the total record, including the probative force of the new evidence and any reason for its belated presentation. *Id.* at 399–400.

Perkins’s filing delay therefore cannot foreclose review as a matter of law. The relevant question is not whether he litigated quickly enough, but whether his affidavits, taken with the trial record, make it more likely than not that no reasonable juror would have convicted him. *Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 327; *House*, 547 U.S. at 538. Because the district court imposed an incorrect legal rule—that diligence is a necessary precondition to any actual-innocence gateway—the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for consideration of Perkins’s innocence showing under the proper standard.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the district court and remand for merits consideration of Perkins’s habeas petition under the actual-innocence gateway recognized in *Souter v. Jones*, 395 F.3d 577 (6th Cir. 2005).
