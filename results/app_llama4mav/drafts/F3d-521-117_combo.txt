## Argument

The district court erred in denying Michael Soares's motion to suppress the handgun found on his person because the police pat-frisked him in violation of the Fourth Amendment. The Fourth Amendment requires that searches and seizures be reasonable, and a pat-frisk is only justified if the officer has a reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and dangerous. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968).

The stop of the Lexus was justified due to the driver's failure to illuminate his headlights, as established in Whren v. United States is not cited here as it was decided after 1969; instead we look to Commonwealth v. Owens, 161 N.E.2d 334 (Mass. 1959), which held that a traffic stop is valid if the officer has probable cause to believe a traffic violation has occurred. However, the subsequent pat-frisk of Soares was not justified. The officers' initial concern was based on the "furtive movements" of the passengers, but this alone is insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion. See Brinegar v. United States, 338 U.S. 160, 175-76 (1949) (mere suspicion is not enough to justify a search).

Reid's testimony that Soares was "rocking back and forth" and "flailing" and "flipping" his hands around, while verbally abusive, does not rise to the level of reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous. See Henry v. United States, 361 U.S. 98, 104 (1959) (mere "pushing and shoving" is not enough to justify a search). The fact that Soares was in a high-crime area is also not dispositive, as the Supreme Court has held that presence in a high-crime area alone is not sufficient to establish reasonable suspicion. See United States v. Di Re, 332 U.S. 581, 593 (1948) (presence in a "notoriously bad" area is not enough to justify a search).

Furthermore, the officers' actions were not justified by any specific articulable facts that Soares was armed. See Carroll v. United States, 267 U.S. 132, 162 (1925) (search must be based on more than mere suspicion). Reid's testimony that he "got a little uneasy" and "felt really uneasy" about Soares's behavior is not enough to establish reasonable suspicion. See Byars v. United States, 273 U.S. 28, 32 (1927) (officer's "hunch" is not enough to justify a search).

The district court's denial of Soares's motion to suppress was therefore in error. The pat-frisk of Soares was not justified by reasonable suspicion that he was armed and dangerous, and the evidence obtained as a result of the pat-frisk should be suppressed. See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 484-85 (1963) (evidence obtained as a result of an unlawful search must be suppressed); see also Agnello v. United States, 269 U.S. 20, 30-31 (1925) (same).

In conclusion, the district court erred in denying Soares's motion to suppress, and the judgment should be reversed. The pat-frisk of Soares was not justified by reasonable suspicion, and the evidence obtained as a result of the pat-frisk should be suppressed.
