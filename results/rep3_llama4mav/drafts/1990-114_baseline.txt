The Missouri Constitution's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges at age seventy does not violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) or the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause.

I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Missouri's Appointed State Court Judges

The ADEA exempts from its coverage "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri's appointed state court judges fall within this exemption because they exercise significant policymaking authority. As the First Circuit has recognized, judging involves lawmaking to fill gaps in authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents. EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). Missouri's judges, particularly those on the Supreme Court, play a crucial role in shaping the state's law and policy through their decisions, which establish common law for the entire state. Gustafson v. Benda, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc).

Moreover, Missouri's judges exercise considerable supervisory power, with the Chief Justice and other senior judges responsible for administering the court system and resolving disputes within the judicial hierarchy. Mo. Const. art. V, § 4; In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc). This level of responsibility and influence demonstrates that Missouri's appointed judges are indeed "on the policymaking level."

The legislative history of the ADEA's exemptions supports this interpretation. Although the conference report accompanying the 1972 amendments to the ADEA states that the exemptions should be construed narrowly, it also indicates that the conferees intended to exempt "persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments." Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180. The state judiciary can be considered one of the more powerful "agencies" of state government, and Missouri's appointed judges are policymakers within the meaning of the ADEA.

II. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Requirement Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause

The Equal Protection Clause requires that a state's actions be rationally related to a legitimate government interest. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 312-314 (1976) (per curiam). Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for judges is rationally related to several legitimate interests, including ensuring that judges remain mentally and physically capable of performing their duties, reducing the risk of death or disability on the bench, and promoting a more representative judiciary. Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979); Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988).

The state's interest in maintaining a competent and effective judiciary is a legitimate one, and the mandatory retirement requirement is a rational means of achieving this goal. The fact that some judges may remain capable beyond age seventy does not render the requirement irrational, as the state is entitled to draw a bright line to avoid the difficulties and unpleasantness of selectively removing aged judges. Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980).

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
