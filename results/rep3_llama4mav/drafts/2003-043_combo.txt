The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing the partners or obtaining a judgment against them is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess a tax against a "taxpayer" within three years of the filing of the taxpayer's return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, a partnership and its individual partners are separate "taxpayers" under the Code. United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961); see also Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960) (acknowledging that an individual partner is a separate taxpayer).

The government's assessment against the partnership does not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners. As the Ninth Circuit held in Coson, an assessment against a partnership does not automatically create a lien against the property of individual partners. 286 F.2d at 462-63. The court in Coson relied on the fact that the assessment did not identify the individual partners, highlighting the importance of identifying the taxpayer in an assessment. Id.; see also 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1 (requiring that an assessment "provide identification of the taxpayer").

The government's reliance on Young v. Riddell is misplaced. In Young, the district court held that a partner was not entitled to a refund of taxes paid because the partner was liable for the partnership's tax debt under state law. 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D.Cal.1959). However, the court also acknowledged that the government would have had to resort to court action or individually assess the taxes against the partner to collect the tax. Id. at 76,050. The Ninth Circuit's affirmance of the district court's decision in Young did not reject this interpretation. 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960).

Other courts have also recognized the importance of assessing or obtaining a judgment against individual partners to collect a partnership's tax debt. In United States v. Kaufman, 267 F. Supp. 351 (S.D.N.Y. 1967), the court held that the government could not collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without first obtaining a judgment against them. Similarly, in United States v. Wright, the Seventh Circuit noted that the government had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners, extending the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from both. 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995) (citing United States v. Wright, 868 F.Supp. 1070, 1071 & n. 1 (S.D.Ind.1994)).

Under California law, a creditor must obtain a judgment against a partner to collect a partnership debt from the partner's assets. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c); see also 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp.1969) (stating that a partner must be individually named and served in an action, and judgment entered against that partner, to reach the partner's personal assets). The government has not obtained a judgment against the individual partners, and the statute of limitations for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

The government's argument is also inconsistent with the principles established in other cases. For example, in Commissioner v. Stern, 357 U.S. 39 (1958), the Supreme Court held that the government could not collect a decedent's tax liability from a beneficiary of the decedent's life insurance policy without first establishing the beneficiary's liability under state law. Similarly, in United States v. Bess, 357 U.S. 51 (1958), the Court held that the government could not collect a taxpayer's liability from the taxpayer's life insurance policy without first establishing the taxpayer's liability.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the government must assess or obtain a judgment against individual partners to collect a partnership's tax debt from those partners. The government's failure to do so within the applicable statute of limitations bars its claim against the respondents.
