**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (“OSH Act”) establishes a broad federal mandate to assure safe and healthful working conditions for all employees. 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). Section 4(a) applies the Act to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). Section 4(b)(1) creates a narrow exception, excluding only “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The plain language of § 4(b)(1) requires that the other agency actually *exercise* its statutory authority. The Coast Guard has not exercised comprehensive authority over the occupational safety and health of employees on uninspected vessels like Mallard’s drilling barge. Therefore, OSHA retains jurisdiction.

The Court of Appeals erroneously relied on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), to hold that the Coast Guard’s general authority over vessels preempts OSHA. Those cases, however, involved vessels subject to extensive Coast Guard regulation. *Clary* addressed a drilling barge that was “in navigation” and subject to the Coast Guard’s comprehensive inspection regime. 609 F.2d at 1121. *Donovan* involved a tanker that was an “inspected vessel” with a full Coast Guard inspection program. 720 F.2d at 826. Here, the MR. BELDON was an uninspected vessel, subject only to minimal Coast Guard rules for specific equipment—fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. The Coast Guard has not issued regulations addressing the drilling operations, blowout prevention, or general workplace safety that caused the explosion. Where the Coast Guard has not exercised its authority, § 4(b)(1) does not preempt OSHA.

The Fifth Circuit’s own en banc decision in *Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. Usery*, 539 F.2d 386 (5th Cir. 1976), establishes that § 4(b)(1) preemption requires the other agency to “exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards.” *Id.* at 389. The court held that the Federal Railroad Administration’s general authority did not preempt OSHA unless the FRA had actually prescribed standards for the specific working condition. *Id.* at 389-90. *Accord Florida East Coast Railway v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Comm’n*, 605 F.2d 542 (5th Cir. 1979) (same). The Ninth Circuit similarly requires actual exercise of authority. *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. OSHA*, 725 F.2d 1237, 1243 (9th Cir. 1984) (“The plain language of § 4(b)(1) requires that the other agency actually exercise its statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards.”). The Coast Guard’s limited equipment regulations for uninspected vessels do not constitute an exercise of authority over the occupational safety hazards at issue here.

The Second Circuit directly addressed uninspected vessels in *Donovan v. Red Star Marine Services, Inc.*, 739 F.2d 774 (2d Cir. 1984). There, the court held that OSHA could apply to an uninspected vessel because the Coast Guard had not “exercised its statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards” for the particular working conditions. *Id.* at 777. The court distinguished *Clary* and *Donovan v. Texaco* as cases involving vessels subject to “comprehensive Coast Guard regulation.” *Id.* at 776. *Red Star* is directly on point: the vessel there, like Mallard’s barge, was an uninspected workboat, and the Coast Guard had not regulated the specific safety hazards. *Id.* at 777-78. The Fourth Circuit likewise held that a floating drydock is a “workplace” under § 4(a) and that OSHA jurisdiction is not preempted where the Coast Guard’s authority is not exercised. *Reich v. Mattix*, 22 F.3d 105, 108-09 (4th Cir. 1994).

The Third Circuit in *United States v. Marrero*, 677 F.2d 289 (3d Cir. 1982), recognized that the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited to specific enumerated equipment and does not extend to general occupational safety. *Id.* at 291-92. The Coast Guard’s own regulations for uninspected vessels confirm this narrow scope. 46 C.F.R. Parts 25, 26. The Coast Guard has not, for example, regulated well control procedures, blowout preventers, or the training of drilling crews. Those are the very conditions that led to the citation here.

The Court of Appeals also erred in holding that the employees were “seamen” whose working conditions are exclusively within Coast Guard jurisdiction. Even if the employees are seamen, the Coast Guard’s preemptive authority is limited to the conditions it actually regulates. The OSH Act’s definition of “employee” is broad, and the Act covers all workers in a “workplace.” The drilling barge is a workplace under § 4(a). *See Reich v. Mattix*, 22 F.3d at 108-09. The Act’s purpose is to protect workers, and the Coast Guard’s minimal regulation of uninspected vessels does not displace that protection.

Finally, the Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary* and *Donovan* creates a per se rule that the Coast Guard’s general authority over vessels preempts OSHA entirely. That rule contradicts the statutory text and the weight of authority from other circuits. *See, e.g., Red Star*, 739 F.2d at 777; *Phelps Dodge*, 725 F.2d at 1243. The Coast Guard itself has not asserted exclusive jurisdiction over uninspected vessels. The OSH Act was designed to fill gaps in workplace safety regulation, not to yield to phantom authority.

For these reasons, OSHA has jurisdiction to issue citations to uninspected vessels like the MR. BELDON. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
