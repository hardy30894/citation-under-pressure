The Supreme Court has established that federal jurisdiction extends to cases where a substantial question of federal law is involved. In Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), the Court held that a case "arises under" federal law when it involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of a federal law. The Court stated that a suit "does not so arise unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." Id. at 569. Here, Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, specifically the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar.

In Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), the Court cautioned against an overly broad interpretation of "arising under" jurisdiction. The Court noted that "not every question of federal law emerging in a suit is proof that a federal law is the basis of the suit." Id. at 115. Instead, the Court adopted a nuanced approach, stating that "the federal nature of the right to be established is decisive — not the source of the authority to establish it." Id. at 112. Applying this principle, it is clear that Minton's claim involves a substantial question of federal patent law that is necessary to the resolution of his state-based malpractice claim.

Peters, 33 U.S. 591 (1834), the Court noted that the federal patent laws are intended to provide a uniform system of patent regulation. Id. at 658. Allowing state courts to adjudicate patent issues in the context of malpractice claims could undermine this uniformity.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that federal jurisdiction is exclusive in cases involving patent law. In Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255 (1897), the Court held that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving the validity and infringement of patents. Id. at 259. This principle is consistent with the idea that federal law is supreme in matters involving patent law.

In addition, the Court has stated that when a case involves a federal question, it is not necessary for the federal issue to be the primary issue in the case for federal jurisdiction to apply. In Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. 738 (1824), the Court held that federal jurisdiction is proper when a case involves a federal question, even if it is not the sole issue in the case. Id. at 822.

Other cases also support the proposition that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over patent law cases. See, e.g., Wilson v. Sandford, 51 U.S. 99 (1850); Kendall v. Winsor, 62 U.S. 322 (1858). These cases demonstrate that the federal courts have long been recognized as the proper forum for resolving disputes involving patent law.
