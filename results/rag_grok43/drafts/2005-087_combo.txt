ARGUMENT

Arizona’s rule confining expert mental-health evidence to the insanity defense comports with the Fourteenth Amendment. Due process permits a State to require the defendant to prove insanity by a preponderance of the evidence, Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952), and to channel all evidence of mental abnormality through that affirmative defense. The same authority recognizes that “the prosecution was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every element of the crime charged, including . . . intent,” yet simultaneously holds that the State may assign the burden of establishing legal insanity to the accused. Id. at 794-95.

This Court has repeatedly upheld state procedures that limit the manner in which mental-condition evidence may be introduced. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the Court approved instructions directing the jury to consider evidence of insanity only after the government had established the elements of the offense. Similar limitations were sustained in Davis v. United States, 165 U.S. 373, 378 (1897), and Hotema v. United States, 186 U.S. 413, 417 (1902), where the Court emphasized that the defendant bears the burden on the distinct issue of responsibility once the prosecution has carried its burden on mens rea.

States may also decline to create a separate “diminished capacity” avenue for negating intent. Lynch v. Overholser, 369 U.S. 705, 712-13 (1962), upheld Congress’s decision to route all claims of mental abnormality through the insanity defense rather than permit piecemeal challenges to individual elements. Blackburn v. Alabama, 361 U.S. 199, 206-07 (1960), likewise treated evidence of mental illness as relevant primarily to the insanity inquiry, not as an independent basis for disproving criminal intent.

The trial court’s refusal to admit the expert testimony for a purpose other than insanity therefore did not violate due process. Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 378 (1966), confirms that a State satisfies the Constitution when it provides a fair opportunity to litigate mental condition within the framework it has established. Panico v. United States, 375 U.S. 29, 30 (1963), and United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi, 344 U.S. 561, 568 (1953), further illustrate that federal courts defer to state rules governing the presentation of psychiatric evidence so long as the prosecution retains the ultimate burden of proving every element beyond a reasonable doubt.

Massey v. Moore, 348 U.S. 105, 108 (1954), underscores that collateral attacks on state evidentiary rulings succeed only when the defendant was denied any meaningful chance to litigate the issue. Clark received that chance through the insanity defense. Because Arizona’s limitation is consistent with the procedures upheld in the foregoing authorities, the judgment should be affirmed.
