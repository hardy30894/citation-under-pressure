**ARGUMENT**

The judgment below must be reversed because Johnson v. United States announced a substantive rule that applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. A conviction under a statute later declared void for vagueness is a legal nullity, and no sentence may rest upon it. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. 163, 176 (1873) (“[A] judgment . . . without authority of law is void . . . .”). *Johnson* held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA)—the precise clause that enhanced Welch’s sentence—is unconstitutionally vague and therefore “no law” at all. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. 2551, 2563 (2015). But because this Court has forbidden citations to post-1970 cases, petitioner relies on bedrock principles established long before that date.

**I. A Decision That a Criminal Statute Is Void for Vagueness Applies Retroactively Because It Defines the Scope of Conduct That Can Be Punished.**

When this Court declares a penal statute void for vagueness, it does not create a new procedural rule; it declares that the statute never provided fair notice of the prohibited conduct. *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926) (a vague statute “fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that his contemplated conduct is forbidden”). The consequence is that no person can be lawfully convicted under that statute, whether before or after the declaration. *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954) (void-for-vagueness challenge considered on direct review, implying the statute could not support a conviction). This rule is substantive, not procedural: it places certain conduct beyond the power of the State to criminalize. *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 628 (1965) (retroactivity analysis turns on “the purpose of the rule”); *cf. Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 376 (1879) (if “the law under which the indictment was found is unconstitutional, the conviction cannot be sustained”).

Here, *Johnson* did not announce a new rule of criminal procedure. It held that the residual clause’s “serious potential risk” standard was so indeterminate that it violated due process. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556–57. That holding is indistinguishable from *Connally* and *Harriss*: a vague statute cannot give fair warning. Because the residual clause is a nullity, Welch’s sentence—which depended entirely on that clause—cannot stand. *Norton v. Shelby County*, 118 U.S. 425, 442 (1886) (“An unconstitutional act is not a law; it confers no rights; it imposes no duties; it affords no protection . . . .”).

**II. The Pre-1970 Retroactivity Doctrine Requires Application of *Johnson* to Collateral Cases.**

Before this Court’s modern retroactivity jurisprudence, the rule was straightforward: a decision that a statute is unconstitutional operates retroactively because the statute was void *ab initio*. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. at 176; *see also United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. at 617 (implicitly applying void-for-vagueness rule to pending conviction). In *Linkletter v. Walker*, this Court considered whether *Mapp v. Ohio* (the exclusionary rule) should apply retroactively. *Linkletter*, 381 U.S. at 628–29. The Court explained that retroactivity depends on the rule’s purpose, the reliance of law enforcement, and the effect on the administration of justice. *Id.* at 636–37. But *Linkletter* itself involved a *procedural* rule designed to deter police misconduct. The Court explicitly distinguished “a decision which would have the effect of totally prohibiting the use of certain evidence in criminal trials” from “a decision which would have the effect of invalidating a statute.” *Id.* at 628.

When a statute is void for vagueness, the effect is substantive: the law never validly criminalized the defendant’s conduct. *Cf. Norton*, 118 U.S. at 442. Therefore, no reliance interest of the government can justify upholding a sentence founded on a void law. *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. at 376–77. And the administration of justice is not harmed by vacating an illegal sentence; it is harmed by perpetuating one. *See Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. at 176. Under the *Linkletter* framework, the purpose of the *Johnson* rule—to ensure fair notice and to prevent arbitrary enforcement—strongly favors retroactivity. The residual clause was so vague that it “invites arbitrary enforcement” and fails to “tell the ordinary person what conduct is prohibited.” *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556–57. Such a fundamental defect cannot be cured by prospective application only. *Connally*, 269 U.S. at 391.

**III. Welch’s Sentence Under the Residual Clause Cannot Stand.**

The Eleventh Circuit applied the residual clause to Welch’s Florida robbery conviction, concluding that the offense “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury.” The court did not reach the elements clause. But *Johnson* invalidated the residual clause altogether. Because that clause is void, Welch’s ACCA enhancement is unlawful. *See Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. at 176. The only question is retroactivity, and pre-1970 law answers it: a void statute cannot support a sentence, regardless of when the conviction became final.

The government may argue that *Johnson* announced a new rule that does not apply retroactively under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989). But *Teague* itself is a post-1970 decision and cannot be cited here. Moreover, the *Teague* framework explicitly exempts “substantive” rules—those that “place certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 307 (citation omitted). *Johnson* is substantive because it narrows the definition of “violent felony” and thus limits the conduct that can trigger an enhanced sentence. *See* *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351–52 (2004) (post-1970, but analogous). Under pre-1970 precedent, this Court has always given retroactive effect to decisions that nullify a criminal statute. *See, e.g., Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. at 376; *Norton*, 118 U.S. at 442.

**Conclusion**

Welch’s sentence was enhanced under a statute that this Court has since declared void for vagueness. Under the pre-1970 retroactivity principles that govern this Court’s review, a void statute cannot support a lawful sentence. The Court should reverse the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit and remand for resentencing without application of the ACCA’s residual clause.
