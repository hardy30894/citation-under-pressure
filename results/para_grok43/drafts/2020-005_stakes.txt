**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits the exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Ford because the plaintiffs’ claims “arise out of or relate to” Ford’s contacts with Montana. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017) (quoting *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117, 127 (2014)). Specific jurisdiction exists when a defendant “purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State” and the litigation results from alleged injuries that “arise out of or relate to those activities.” *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472, 475 (1985) (quoting *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958)).

Ford has continuously and deliberately exploited the Montana market for decades by selling thousands of vehicles to Montana residents, operating dealerships in the State, advertising extensively there, and maintaining a network of authorized service centers that sell and install Ford parts. These contacts are not “random, fortuitous, or attenuated.” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475. They reflect Ford’s purposeful direction of its products into Montana with the expectation that they will be purchased and used by Montana consumers.

The claims at issue relate directly to those contacts. The plaintiffs allege that a Ford Explorer was defectively designed and that Ford failed to warn of known risks. Ford’s Montana activities—selling Explorers and replacement parts, advertising their safety, and servicing the vehicles—create the very relationship that makes jurisdiction fair. As this Court has explained, the “arise out of or relate to” requirement is satisfied when the defendant’s forum conduct “gave rise to the episode-in-suit.” *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 923 (2011). Ford’s systematic promotion and servicing of the same model line in Montana supplies that connection.

Ford’s contrary rule—that jurisdiction requires the precise vehicle to have been sold or serviced in Montana—finds no support in precedent. The Court has never imposed a strict causation requirement. *See World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297–98 (1980) (jurisdiction proper where manufacturer “serves or seeks to serve” the forum market). Requiring but-for causation would allow a corporation that floods a State with its products to evade jurisdiction simply by routing the particular item through another State—an outcome inconsistent with the “fair play and substantial justice” standard. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945).

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford’s extensive, ongoing contacts with the State relate to the claims asserted. That holding respects both the limits of due process and the reality of modern interstate commerce.
