The district court abused its discretion by certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that antitrust damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement and quantification. Class actions require that common questions predominate, a demand rooted in the equitable origins of representative suits. Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32, 41 (1940) (class relief available only when the named parties “fairly represent” absent members); Supreme Tribe of Ben Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 366 (1921) (same); Smith v. Swormstedt, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 302 (1853) (representative decree binds only when interests are “joint” and proof is uniform).

In antitrust actions, damages must be established by evidence that is neither speculative nor individualized. Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (plaintiff must prove “the fact and amount of damages” with “reasonable certainty”); Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931) (damages “not to be determined by mere speculation or guess”); Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co., 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927) (proof must rest on “facts from which the jury can make a reasonable estimate”).

Where the proposed class spans thousands of subscribers across multiple counties, common proof of injury and damages is indispensable. Thomsen v. Cayser, 243 U.S. 66, 88 (1917) (antitrust plaintiffs must show injury “common to all”); Continental Wall Paper Co. v. Louis Voight & Sons Co., 212 U.S. 227, 238 (1909) (recovery requires evidence of “the extent of the injury” applicable to the claimant); Standard Oil Co. v. United States, 221 U.S. 1, 75 (1911) (antitrust remedies turn on concrete proof of harm). The same requirement appears in United States v. Trenton Potteries Co., 273 U.S. 392, 401 (1927) (liability and remedy rest on demonstrated effects, not conjecture).

Petitioners’ expert models rest on assumptions that vary by subscriber location, contract terms, and viewing preferences—precisely the individualized inquiries that preclude predominance. Because the district court accepted these models without requiring proof that damages can be measured uniformly for every class member, its certification order rests on an erroneous legal standard and must be reversed.
