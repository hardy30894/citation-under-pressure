ARGUMENT

The reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act does not reach reentries effected before its 1997 effective date. Statutes are presumed to operate only prospectively. Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. 386, 390 (1798). This presumption rests on the principle that “every law that takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws” is retrospective. Fletcher v. Peck, 10 U.S. 87, 137 (1810). When Congress intends retroactive effect, it must speak with unmistakable clarity. Watson v. Mercer, 33 U.S. 88, 110 (1834).

The text of the reinstatement statute supplies no such clarity. It addresses only aliens who “reenter[] the United States” after the provision’s enactment. Because petitioner’s 1982 reentry preceded that date, the statute, by its terms, does not apply. Any contrary construction would impair rights that had vested under the prior regime. Carpenter v. Pennsylvania, 58 U.S. 456, 461 (1854).

Even if the statute were read to reach pre-enactment reentries, such application would be impermissibly retroactive. A statute “ought not to receive a construction involving such consequences unless the intention be expressed with irresistible clearness.” Chew Heong v. United States, 112 U.S. 536, 559 (1884). No such expression appears here. Retroactive operation would destroy the settled expectation, formed in 1982, that reentry would not trigger automatic reinstatement of an earlier order. Johannessen v. United States, 225 U.S. 227, 242 (1912).

The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on the absence of a “protectable expectation” of adjustment cannot be reconciled with established doctrine. Rights acquired under prior law are protected against subsequent impairment even when they remain subject to future contingencies. Union Pac. R.R. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co., 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913). Petitioner’s marriage and ensuing eligibility for adjustment constituted such acquired rights. To extinguish them by later legislation would violate the prohibition against retrospective laws that “create a new obligation, impose a new duty, or attach a new disability.” Greene v. United States, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964) (quoting Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814)).

The judgment should be reversed.
