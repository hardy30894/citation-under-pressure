**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is governed by the clear text, context, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Third Circuit erred in holding that a TPS grant satisfies § 1255(a)’s requirement that an alien must be “inspected and admitted or paroled” to be eligible for adjustment of status. That interpretation collapses two distinct statutory concepts—“admission” and “status”—into one, contrary to the INA’s express language and long-standing precedent.

**I. The Text of the INA Defines “Admission” as a Physical, Procedural Event, Not a Status Grant**

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely a suggestion—it is a threshold requirement for adjustment of status under § 1255(a). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “admission” is a factual, procedural event tied to physical entry, not a status conferred by administrative action. In *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012), this Court held that “the date of gaining a new status is not the same as the date of the physical event of entering the country.” Similarly, in *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013), we observed that “the words ‘entry’ and ‘into’ plainly indicate that ‘admission’ involves physical entrance into the country.” These principles are not confined to our circuit. The Fifth Circuit has recognized that “admission is an occurrence, defined in wholly factual and procedural terms,” while “status” describes the type of permission to be present. *Gomez v. Lynch*, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016).

The grant of TPS does not satisfy this definition. Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” But “lawful status” is not synonymous with “admission.” As the Supreme Court has made clear, “status” and “admission” are “fundamentally distinct concepts.” *Gomez*, 831 F.3d at 658. The Court has held that a grant of asylum, which confers lawful status, “is not an ‘admission.’” *In re H-G-G-*, 27 I. & N. Dec. 617, 635 (AAO 2019). Likewise, a grant of benefits under the Family Unity Program confers “status” but does not constitute “admission.” *Id.* at 635 (citing *In re Fajardo Espinoza*, 26 I. & N. Dec. 603, 605 (BIA 2015)). The same logic applies to TPS: the status conferred by § 1254a(f)(4) does not equate to an “admission” under § 1101(a)(13)(A).

**II. The Context and Structure of the INA Confirm That TPS Does Not Constitute Admission**

Congress did not intend for TPS to serve as a substitute for admission. The INA distinguishes between “admission” and “status” throughout its text. Section 1255(a) requires that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled” to adjust status. Section 1255(k)(1), however, provides an exception for certain aliens who are “present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission.” The use of “lawful admission” in § 1255(k)—a term that appears nowhere in § 1254a(f)(4)—demonstrates Congress’s intent to preserve the distinction between admission and status. If a grant of TPS were an admission, § 1255(k) would be superfluous, as all TPS recipients would already satisfy the “lawful admission” requirement.

Moreover, Congress created a special, supermajority requirement for adjusting the status of TPS recipients under § 1254a(h)(2). This provision exists precisely because Congress recognized that TPS is a temporary, non-immigrant status that does not automatically lead to permanent residence. To interpret § 1254a(f)(4) as satisfying § 1255(a)’s admission requirement would render § 1254a(h)(2) meaningless, as TPS recipients could adjust status without the need for special legislation. As the Supreme Court has instructed, “a statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.” *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004).

**III. The Purpose of TPS Supports the Government’s Interpretation**

The purpose of the TPS program is to provide temporary relief from removal for individuals already present in the United States due to extraordinary conditions in their home countries. *8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1)*. It is not a mechanism for lawful entry. The Supreme Court has recognized that “the TPS program was designed to shield aliens already in the country from removal when a natural disaster or similar occurrence has rendered removal unsa.” *Santos v. Mayorkas*, 958 F.3d 188, 197 (3d Cir. 2020) (en banc). To allow TPS recipients who entered illegally to adjust status would undermine this purpose by creating a de facto path to permanent residence for those who entered without inspection. The Court has long held that “a program that provides ‘limited, temporary’ relief should not be read to facilitate permanent residence for aliens who entered the country illegally.” *Id.* at 198.
