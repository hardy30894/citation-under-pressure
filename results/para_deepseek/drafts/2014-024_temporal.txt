## ARGUMENT

The district court instructed the jury that a “true threat” exists when a defendant “intentionally makes a statement in a context or manner that a reasonable person would foresee” as threatening. That instruction omitted the essential element of criminal speech: the defendant’s subjective intent to communicate a threat. Because 18 U.S.C. §875(c) criminalizes the transmission of “any threat,” and because the First Amendment protects all speech except a “true threat,” the government must prove that the speaker intended his words to be taken as a threat. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.

### I. The Common-Law Meaning of “Threat” Requires Proof of Subjective Intent

It is a “fundamental principle” of American criminal law that “an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention.” *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Congress is presumed to know and adopt the common-law meaning of the terms it borrows. *Id.* at 263. The word “threat” at common law denotes a communicated intention to inflict harm. The criminal offense of threatening therefore contains a double-intent requirement: the speaker must intend to communicate the words, and he must intend them as an expression of his own purpose to injure. The instruction below required only the former. It asked the jury to decide what a reasonable person would foresee, not what Elonis actually intended. That is negligence liability, not criminal liability. *Morissette* rejected the notion that a person may be convicted on “the mere doing of an act” without an inquiry into his state of mind. *Id.* at 250–51. The same rule governs here.

### II. The First Amendment Requires Subjective Intent Before Speech May Be Punished

In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707 (1969), this Court held that a “true threat” must be distinguished from constitutionally protected speech. The Court defined a true threat as a “serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” *Id.* at 708. The word “intent” is the key: a statement is a true threat only when the speaker seriously intends to communicate an actual purpose to inflict harm. *Watts* itself reversed a conviction because the statement was political hyperbole, not a threat. *Id.* at 708. An objective test collapses that distinction. The same words—even the same words in the same context—may be a joke to one speaker and a threat to another. Only the speaker’s subjective intent can separate them.

This Court has long guarded speech by requiring proof of specific intent before words can be punished. In *Herndon v. Lowry*, 301 U.S. 242, 258 (1937), the Court reversed a conviction for subversive advocacy because the statute did not require a “specific intent” to incite insurrection. The Court emphasized that the mere “dissemination of ideas” cannot be criminalized absent an intent to bring about the forbidden result. *Id.* at 261. The same reasoning applies to threats. If a speaker lacks the subjective intent to threaten, his words are no more a “true threat” than abstract advocacy is a call to insurrection. The objective instruction permitted the jury to convict Elonis for words a reasonable person might fear, without ever asking whether Elonis intended them as a threat.

The objective standard also chills protected speech. A person who makes a dark joke, vents frustration, or writes rap lyrics can be convicted if a reasonable listener is frightened. That prospect inevitably discourages the robust expression that the First Amendment protects. In *Winters v. New York*, 333 U.S. 507, 515 (1948), this Court held that a statute must give fair notice of the conduct it prohibits. A speaker cannot know whether his words violate the statute if liability depends on the subjective reactions of others rather than on his own intent. The subjective standard, by contrast, gives clear notice: a person is liable only if he intentionally communicates a threat.

### III. The Court of Appeals’ Fear-Based Rationale Does Not Justify an Objective Standard

The court of appeals defended the objective standard by invoking the statute’s purpose: protecting individuals from the fear of violence. But that purpose is fully served by punishing those who intentionally place others in fear. A speaker who subjectively intends to threaten is the only speaker who has deliberately chosen to exploit the victim’s fear. The government has no legitimate interest in punishing a speaker who did not intend his words to be threatening, because such a speaker has not directed a message of harm at anyone. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708. The objective test is also underinclusive: it can punish the careless joke, but it may miss the speaker who manages to conceal his true intent. The subjective test, in contrast, tracks moral culpability and the First Amendment alike.

Because the district court’s instruction removed subjective intent from the jury’s consideration, Elonis was denied his right to have every element of the offense found beyond a reasonable doubt. The conviction cannot stand.
