**ARGUMENT**

The School District violated the First Amendment when it denied Lamb’s Chapel access to school facilities because the proposed films were “church related.” Once a public entity opens its property for community use, it may not exclude speech based on its religious viewpoint absent a constitutionally sufficient justification. *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 44-46 (1983); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985).

The District’s own policy opened school facilities after hours for community expressive activity. That created at least a limited public forum. In such a forum, the government may not discriminate against speech because of its content where the exclusion is not necessary to preserve the forum’s limited purpose. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45-46. The Court has repeatedly held that when the State permits use of its property for expressive activity, it may not single out religious speech for disfavored treatment. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267-70 (1981). In *Widmar*, the Court rejected denial of access to a university forum because religious meetings were as much protected expression as any other group’s use. Id. at 269-70. The same principle controls here.

This case is also governed by *Board of Education of the Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 247 (1990). There, the Court held that a school that opened a limited forum for noncurriculum-related student clubs could not discriminate against a Christian club because of the religious content of its speech. Id. at 247-53. The District’s refusal to permit Lamb’s Chapel to show films about family values and child rearing from a Christian perspective is the same kind of content discrimination. A rule that permits discussion of family life but prohibits the same discussion “from a Christian perspective” is viewpoint discrimination in its purest form. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 276; *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 250-53.

The District cannot avoid the First Amendment by relabeling viewpoint discrimination as a prohibition on “religious purposes.” The Court has long distinguished permissible neutral regulation from suppression of speech because of the speaker’s religious identity or perspective. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940); *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 112-13 (1943). In *Cantwell*, the Court held that the State may not suppress religious expression simply because it is religious. 310 U.S. at 303-04. In *Murdock*, it rejected a tax scheme that burdened the dissemination of religious views. 319 U.S. at 112-13. So too here: the District excluded Lamb’s Chapel not because of scheduling or physical limitations, but because the films were religiously oriented. That is content-based exclusion. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45.

The District also cannot justify its discrimination by invoking the Establishment Clause. The Court has repeatedly held that equal access does not amount to establishment. *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313-15 (1952); *Cochran v. Louisiana State Board of Education*, 281 U.S. 370, 375 (1930). In *Zorach*, the Court upheld a released-time program and explained that the Constitution does not require hostility to religion. 343 U.S. at 314. In *Cochran*, the Court recognized that public programs touching schools are not invalid simply because they have indirect effects on religion. 281 U.S. at 375. And in *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 209-12 (1948), the Court invalidated in-school religious instruction because the State had actively used public-school machinery to provide it during school hours. That is a very different case from after-hours access to a forum open to community groups. Likewise, *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 424-25 (1962), involved official school prayer, not neutral access to school property.

Nor is there any basis to treat the District’s exclusion as a permissible nonpublic-forum restriction. A restriction in a nonpublic forum must be reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46. A blanket ban on all religious uses is not viewpoint neutral when the District otherwise permits presentations on the same subject matter. As the Court explained in *Widmar*, once the government opens a forum, it may not deny access because of disagreement with the speaker’s religious perspective. 454 U.S. at 269-70. And *Mergens* confirms that equal access to a school forum for religious speech does not establish religion. 496 U.S. at 248-53.

The judgment below should be reversed. The First Amendment forbids the District from opening its school facilities to community speakers while excluding Lamb’s Chapel solely because its films presented family and child-rearing themes from a Christian viewpoint. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 44-46; *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 267-70; *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 247-53; *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-04; *Murdock*, 319 U.S. at 112-13.
