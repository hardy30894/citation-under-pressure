## ARGUMENT

### I. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment

This Court’s decision in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), does not invalidate Florida’s capital sentencing scheme. In *Ring*, the Court held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find any aggravating factor that renders a defendant eligible for the death penalty, because such factors function as “the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense.” *Id.* at 609. But *Ring* expressly distinguished Florida’s scheme from Arizona’s. Under Arizona’s statute, the trial judge alone found aggravating factors after a jury conviction, whereas Florida’s scheme has always required a jury’s “advisory verdict” before the judge imposes sentence. *Id.* at 608 n.6 (citing *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 648 (1990), *overruled on other grounds by Ring*). As this Court recognized, Florida “gives the jury an advisory role in sentencing.” *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640-41 (1989) (per curiam). The Sixth Amendment is satisfied because the jury finds the defendant guilty of first-degree murder—an offense that itself carries a possible death sentence—and then renders an advisory recommendation. The judge’s independent weighing of aggravators and mitigators does not usurp the jury’s factfinding function because the jury has already determined the essential elements of the crime.

Moreover, this Court has consistently upheld capital sentencing schemes that assign sentencing authority to judges. In *Walton*, the Court rejected a Sixth Amendment challenge to Arizona’s scheme, holding that “the Sixth Amendment does not require that the specific findings authorizing the imposition of the sentence of death be made by the jury.” 497 U.S. at 648. Although *Ring* overruled *Walton* on that precise point, it did so only because Arizona’s scheme gave the judge sole authority to find aggravators. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609. Florida’s scheme is materially different: the jury participates by recommending a sentence, and the judge must give the recommendation “great weight.” *See State v. Steele*, 921 So. 2d 538, 547 (Fla. 2005). This hybrid structure respects the jury’s role while preserving the judge’s traditional sentencing discretion.

The Sixth Amendment does not demand jury unanimity on aggravating factors or on the ultimate sentence. Nothing in *Ring* or *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), requires a unanimous jury verdict on sentencing findings. In *Apprendi*, the Court stated that “the jury’s traditional function of finding the facts essential to punishment” must be “beyond a reasonable doubt,” but it did not impose any unanimity requirement. *Id.* at 490. The Court has long recognized that “jury unanimity” is a “common-law requirement” but has never held it to be a constitutional imperative in capital sentencing. *Johnson v. Louisiana*, 406 U.S. 356, 370 (1972) (Powell, J., concurring). Florida’s majority jury recommendation satisfies due process and the Sixth Amendment.

### II. Florida’s Scheme Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment

The Eighth Amendment requires that capital sentencing be “narrowed” to a “small class of the most serious offenders.” *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 190 (1976) (plurality opinion). Florida accomplishes this narrowing through its statutory aggravating factors, which are found by the judge after the jury’s advisory verdict. This process affords the “heightened reliability” that the Eighth Amendment demands. *See Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). The jury’s recommendation, even if not unanimous, provides a meaningful check on the judge’s discretion. Florida is not an “outlier” in any constitutional sense; states may adopt different procedures so long as they are not “fundamentally unfair.” *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 496 (1986).

Hurst’s argument that the Eighth Amendment requires jury unanimity is unsupported. This Court has never held that the Eighth Amendment mandates a unanimous jury in capital sentencing. *See Harris v. Alabama*, 513 U.S. 504, 512 (1995) (upholding Alabama’s scheme where judge could override jury’s life recommendation). Indeed, “the Constitution permits the death penalty to be imposed under a scheme that allows the sentencing authority to weigh aggravating and mitigating factors.” *Walton*, 497 U.S. at 653. Florida’s scheme is within that mainstream.

### III. The Jury Need Not Determine Mental Retardation

This Court in *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002), left to the States “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction” on executing the mentally retarded. Florida has done so by requiring the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence before the trial court. *See* § 921.137(4), Fla. Stat. This procedure comports with the Eighth Amendment. Mental retardation is a mitigating factor, not an element of the offense, and thus need not be found by a jury. *See Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004) (distinguishing “elements” from “sentencing factors”). The jury may consider mental retardation as mitigation, but the ultimate determination is for the judge. Hurst received a full evidentiary hearing and the trial court found he was not mentally retarded. That finding is entitled to deference. There is no Sixth or Eighth Amendment violation.

### CONCLUSION

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme satisfies both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
