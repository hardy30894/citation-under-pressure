**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause does not confer a cause of action whenever a single person claims to have been treated unfairly by government. It protects against intentional, arbitrary discrimination, and this Court has repeatedly required a showing that the challenged government action discriminates against a person because of membership in an identifiable class. See *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976); *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 274 (1979). Respondent alleges no membership in any class or group. Her claim therefore fails as a matter of equal protection doctrine.

This Court has never recognized a free-standing “class of one” equal protection claim untethered to class-based discrimination. To the contrary, the Court’s equal protection cases emphasize that the Clause is directed at governmental classifications that burden some persons while benefiting others on the basis of group characteristics. *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265-66 (1977). The Clause is violated when the government intentionally treats a person differently because of race, alienage, or another protected classification, or when a facially neutral law is applied in a discriminatory manner against a protected group. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373-74 (1886); *Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448, 456 (1962). But the Court has never extended the Clause to every dispute in which one person believes the government acted harshly toward him.

The Seventh Circuit’s rule below collapses equal protection into a generalized prohibition on bad government motives. That approach cannot be squared with *United States v. Armstrong*, 517 U.S. 456 (1996), which held that an equal protection claimant must provide evidence that similarly situated persons of a different class were not treated similarly. Id. at 465. The requirement of differential treatment is not optional; it is the core of an equal protection claim. See *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. at 274; *McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279, 292 (1987). Respondent alleges only that the Village required her to grant a larger easement than it required from others. Even if true, that allegation sounds in arbitrary administration, not class-based discrimination. Equal protection doctrine does not constitutionalize every mistaken, uneven, or even spiteful municipal decision.

Nor does *Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441 (1923), support respondent. There, the Court addressed intentional undervaluation of other taxpayers in a taxation scheme, where equalization among comparably situated taxpayers is itself the constitutional rule. Id. at 445-46. Tax cases are distinctive because taxation necessarily depends on comparison, and the Court has long cautioned against generalizing from that context. See *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Commission of Webster County*, 488 U.S. 336, 345-46 (1989). Respondent’s claim concerns a discretionary municipal condition attached to a request for water service, not a systemic classification in a tax regime. Nothing in the tax cases supports a general “class of one” theory.

The proper constitutional remedy for truly arbitrary administration lies elsewhere, not in equal protection. Where government acts without a rational basis, due process and state law may provide relief, but equal protection requires more than a showing of animus or unfairness. *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*, 553 U.S. 591, 602-03 (2008), later confirmed that a class-of-one theory is incompatible with discretionary governmental decisions because “[t]here are some forms of state action” in which differential treatment “is the very nature of the activity.” Id. at 603. Although *Engquist* post-dates this case, it reflects the logic already embedded in this Court’s precedents: equal protection is not a general anti-arbitrariness provision. See *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993) (government action is presumed valid and upheld if there is any reasonably conceivable state of facts that could provide a rational basis).

Respondent’s “spiteful effort to get him” formulation also cannot survive this Court’s rational-basis cases. Even if a plaintiff alleges ill will, a classification does not violate equal protection if it is rationally related to a legitimate government purpose. *Heller v. Doe*, 509 U.S. 312, 320 (1993). The Constitution does not invalidate government action merely because a particular official may have acted with hostility; there must still be a denial of equal treatment under law. *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. at 279.

This Court should therefore reverse. The Equal Protection Clause does not create a cause of action for a lone plaintiff who alleges neither class membership nor discrimination based on class. Recognizing such a claim would transform the Clause into a federal tort of arbitrary treatment, contrary to *Washington v. Davis*, *Arlington Heights*, and *Armstrong*. The judgment below should be reversed.
