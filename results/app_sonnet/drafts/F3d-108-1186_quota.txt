# ARGUMENT

## I. Standard of Review

Whether a jury verdict is ambiguous, and whether a trial court abused its discretion in refusing to resolve that ambiguity before discharge, are questions reviewed with the guiding principle that "the jury's verdict must be given effect if there is any way to reconcile" the jury's separate findings. Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, Inc. v. Ellerman Lines, Ltd., 369 U.S. 355, 364 (1962).

## II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Refusing to Question the Jury Before Accepting an Ambiguous Verdict

A trial court confronting an ambiguous verdict must resolve that ambiguity *before* discharging the jury, not reconstruct it afterward from the bench. Judge Learned Hand long ago explained that when a verdict is not clear, "the trial judge should . . . send the jury back for further deliberations" while it remains available to state its actual intent. Skidmore v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co., 167 F.2d 54, 60 (2d Cir. 1948). The Second Circuit later reaffirmed that a court's "duty is to attempt to harmonize the answers" the jury has given "if it is possible under a fair reading of them," but where harmonization requires guessing at the jury's intent, resubmission—not judicial reconstruction—is the proper course. Bevevino v. Saydjari, 574 F.2d 676, 683 (2d Cir. 1978).

The verdict here was susceptible to two irreconcilable readings. Under Unit's reading, the jury netted the repair cost against Unit's damages ($157,262.36 minus $86,494.30 equals $70,768.02), intending Unit to recover $70,768.02 outright. Under the district court's reading, the jury separately awarded Unit $70,768.02 and Enron an additional, un-netted set-off of $86,494.30, leaving Unit with nothing. Both readings fit the numbers; neither is compelled by them. Where jury findings are subject to competing readings, the Supreme Court has instructed courts to attempt reconciliation, and where reconciliation is uncertain, to resolve doubt through the jury itself while it remains impaneled. Gallick v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co., 372 U.S. 108, 119 (1963) (verdict sustained only if there is "any reasonable basis" for it). Unit specifically requested that the court question the jury before discharge. That request cost nothing and would have eliminated all doubt. The court's refusal, followed by its adoption of one plausible reading over an equally plausible one that favored Unit, was an abuse of discretion.

## III. The District Court's Interpretation Improperly Substituted Its Own Damages Calculation for the Jury's

Beyond the duty to clarify, the court's chosen interpretation displaced the jury's factfinding role. It is settled that a court may not assess damages in a manner that effectively overrides the jury's own computation once the jury has spoken. Cone v. West Virginia Pulp & Paper Co., 330 U.S. 212, 216 (1947). The instructions told the jury that if Unit only substantially performed, it should award "the amount of the unpaid invoices, less the cost to Enron of correcting any defects." The jury's Form 1 figure, $70,768.02, is precisely the unpaid-invoice total minus the repair cost, $86,494.30—the identical figure the jury wrote on Form 3. That congruence is not coincidence; it is strong evidence the jury had already performed the required subtraction and did not intend Enron's Form 3 figure to be subtracted a second time. This Court has recognized that doubtful verdicts should be resolved, where possible, in the manner that gives effect to the jury's evident intent rather than one that nullifies its award. Malandris v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc., 703 F.2d 1152, 1167 (10th Cir. 1981) (en banc). The district court's construction did the opposite, treating careful, invoice-specific arithmetic as coincidence and imposing a second subtraction the jury was never instructed to make—converting a plaintiff's verdict into a defense judgment.

## IV. The Defective Verdict Forms, Which Produced the Ambiguity, Warrant Relief Notwithstanding Rule 51

Unit recognizes that Fed. R. Civ. P. 51 required a specific objection before the jury retired, and that a general complaint that the forms were "difficult to understand" does not preserve the precise double-counting defect now at issue. See Neu v. Grant, 548 F.2d 281, 287 (10th Cir. 1977). But this Court reviews instructions "as a whole" for whether they misled the jury, and a defect so fundamental that it prevents a coherent verdict implicates a party's substantial rights regardless of the specificity of trial objections. Anixter v. Home-Stake Production Co., 77 F.3d 1215, 1222 (10th Cir. 1996). The absence of a single sentence instructing the jury not to award the same repair cost twice—once as a deduction on Form 1 and again as a set-off on Form 3—produced exactly the confusion that occurred. That defect, compounded by the court's refusal to clarify it while the jury remained available, deprived Unit of the benefit of a verdict it had, in substance, already won.

## Conclusion

This Court should reverse and remand with instructions to enter judgment for Unit in the amount of $70,768.02, or, at minimum, to grant Unit a new trial on damages.
