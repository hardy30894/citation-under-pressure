The question presented is whether an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act. The plain language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) requires only that the employer engage in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." This statutory text is dispositive, as it clearly sets forth the standard for awarding punitive damages.

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which introduced punitive damages to Title VII, indicates that Congress drew upon existing law, including 42 U.S.C. § 1981. Under § 1981, courts have long recognized that punitive damages serve to deter and punish egregious conduct. As the Supreme Court has observed, punitive damages are awarded "for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant," Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893). The Court has also noted that punitive damages are available when a defendant acts with a "bad motive, or so recklessly as to imply a disregard of social obligations," Milwaukee & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489, 493 (1875). This principle is further supported by cases such as Philadelphia, W. & B.R. Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202 (1858), and The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. 546 (1818), which establish that malice and reckless indifference are sufficient to warrant punitive damages.

The common law, as reflected in the Restatement of Torts, provides that punitive damages are available when a defendant's conduct is "outrageous, because of ... evil motive or ... reckless indifference to the rights of others," Restatement of Torts § 908(2) (1939). This standard is consistent with the language of § 1981a(b)(1) and supports the conclusion that an employer's state of mind, rather than the egregiousness of its conduct, is the relevant factor in determining whether punitive damages are available.

In the present case, the District Court erred in dismissing Kolstad's claim for punitive damages. The jury's finding of intentional discrimination, combined with evidence of the ADA's reckless indifference to Kolstad's federally protected rights, is sufficient to support an award of punitive damages. The ADA's actions, including preselecting Spangler for the promotion and ignoring Kolstad's efforts to discuss the position, demonstrate a level of malice or reckless indifference that warrants consideration of punitive damages. As the Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit has recognized, an employer's preselection of a job candidate, in violation of its own procedures, is "undeniably relevant to the "question of discriminatory intent," Krodel v. Young, 748 F.2d 701, 709 (D.C.Cir.1984). The evidence presented at trial, including the ADA's actions and motivations, was sufficient to support a finding of punitive damages under the standard established by Congress in § 1981a(b)(1).

In conclusion, the plain language of § 1981a(b)(1), combined with the legislative history and relevant case law, including Rood v. Wallace, 109 F. 924 (8th Cir. 1901), and Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), supports the conclusion that an employer's conduct does not need to be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings on Kolstad's punitive damages claim.
