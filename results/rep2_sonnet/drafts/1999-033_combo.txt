# ARGUMENT

The Equal Protection Clause commands that no State shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. From its earliest applications, this Court has understood that guarantee as a shield against *class*-based discrimination—the arbitrary sorting of persons into favored and disfavored groups—not as a general federal cause of action for every citizen who believes a single discretionary decision by a local official was mistaken, unfair, or even spiteful. The Seventh Circuit's recognition of a "class of one" equal protection claim severs the doctrine from its textual and historical mooring and would transform every zoning dispute, permit denial, or easement negotiation into a constitutional case. This Court should reverse.

## I. Equal Protection Doctrine Has Always Been Anchored in Classification, Not Individualized Grievance.

This Court's foundational equal protection cases speak uniformly in the language of *classes* and *groups similarly circumstanced*. In *F.S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia*, 253 U.S. 412 (1920), the Court explained that a classification "must be reasonable, not arbitrary, and must rest upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation, so that all persons similarly circumstanced shall be treated alike." *Id.* at 415. The inquiry the Court describes presupposes a *comparator class*—a group of similarly situated persons treated differently because of their membership in some cognizable category. Likewise, in *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61 (1911), the Court held that the Equal Protection Clause "does not take from the State the power to classify in the adoption of police laws," provided the classification rests on "some real and substantial distinction" bearing "a reasonable relation to the object sought to be attained." *Id.* at 78-79. Both decisions treat the constitutional question as one of *classification*—an inescapably relational concept that has no application where, as here, the plaintiff alleges no class membership at all.

Even where this Court has found administrative, as opposed to legislative, discrimination, it has framed the violation as discrimination *between persons in similar circumstances*. In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), the Court condemned the discretionary denial of laundry permits because the ordinance was "applied and administered by public authority with an evil eye and an unequal hand, so as practically to make unjust and illegal discriminations between persons in similar circumstances." *Id.* at 373-74. The violation in *Yick Wo* arose from a demonstrated pattern—over two hundred Chinese applicants denied while non-Chinese applicants were uniformly approved—establishing a *class* singled out for disfavor. Respondent alleges nothing comparable: no pattern, no class, no group identity linking her to any other similarly treated person.

## II. This Court's Precedents Require Intentional Discrimination Against an Identifiable Class of Similarly Situated Persons.

*Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), is directly controlling. There, the Court held that "the unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination." *Id.* at 8. That formulation—"unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike"—necessarily contemplates a *group* of persons entitled to like treatment. A lone individual, by definition, has no class of comparators "entitled to be treated alike" with her; she has only her own, isolated grievance.

*Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Township of Wakefield*, 247 U.S. 350 (1918), reinforces this class-based requirement. The Court there demanded proof of "an intentional violation of the essential principle of practical uniformity" before administrative unevenness could rise to a constitutional violation, rejecting the notion that "mere errors of judgment" by officials suffice. *Id.* at 352-53. Respondent's allegation that Willowbrook demanded an eighteen-foot larger easement from her than from other landowners is, at most, an assertion of an isolated administrative decision—precisely the kind of individualized dispute *Sunday Lake* excludes from constitutional cognizance absent proof of a systemic, class-wide pattern.

## III. Recognizing a Class-of-One Theory Would Federalize Ordinary Municipal Administration.

This Court has repeatedly cautioned against converting the Equal Protection Clause into a general vehicle for federal review of routine governmental line-drawing. In *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), the Court observed that "it is no requirement of equal protection that all evils of the same genus be eradicated or none at all," *id.* at 110, underscoring the wide latitude government must retain in incremental, individualized decisionmaking. And in *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483 (1955), the Court held that a regulatory scheme "need not be in every respect logically consistent with its aims to be constitutional," because "it is enough that there is an evil at hand for correction, and that it might be thought that the particular legislative measure was a rational way to correct it." *Id.* at 487-88. Municipal easement requirements, water-connection conditions, and similar administrative judgments are the everyday stuff of local governance—matters this Court in *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27 (1885), recognized are properly left to local authorities so long as they do not amount to "class legislation, discriminating against some and favoring others in the same class under like circumstances." *Id.* at 32.

## CONCLUSION

Because Olech alleged no membership in any class or group, she failed to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
