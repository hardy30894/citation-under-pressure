**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Clemency Proceeding Does Not Implicate a Protected Life or Liberty Interest Under the Due Process Clause.**

This Court has long recognized that executive clemency is a matter of grace, not of right. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. 333, 380 (1866) (the pardon power is “exclusively” in the executive and “is not subject to legislative control”). A prisoner has no constitutional entitlement to clemency. *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. 150, 160 (1833) (a pardon is an “act of grace” that “the prisoner may not claim”). The Ohio Constitution vests the Governor with “almost unfettered discretion” to grant or deny clemency. That discretion cannot be circumscribed by judicial due process requirements. *Ex parte Grossman*, 267 U.S. 87, 120–21 (1925) (the pardon power is “plenary” and “not subject to judicial control”).

Because clemency is not a matter of right, Woodard has no protected life or liberty interest in the clemency proceeding itself. *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927) (clemency decisions are “acts of grace” not “judicial proceedings”). This Court has consistently held that the Due Process Clause does not apply to discretionary executive actions that are not rooted in any entitlement created by state law. *See, e.g., Cafeteria Workers v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961) (due process protections attach only when a “right” or “status” is at stake). Woodard’s conviction and death sentence have been finally adjudicated. The clemency process is a separate, executive function that is not an “integral part” of the adjudicatory system. *McKane v. Durston*, 153 U.S. 684, 687 (1894) (no constitutional right to appeal; a fortiori, no right to clemency).

The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Woodard has no protected interest in the clemency procedure itself. That conclusion should be affirmed.

**II. The Voluntary Option to Participate in a Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment prohibits only *compelled* self-incrimination. *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951) (the privilege “protects against any disclosures that the witness reasonably believes could be used in a criminal prosecution”). It does not forbid a prisoner from voluntarily speaking. *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892) (the privilege is “against being compelled to testify”). Woodard is not compelled to request an interview, nor is he compelled to answer any question if he does. The Ohio regulation expressly provides that the interview is “optional” and that only the prisoner and the parole board representatives are present. No sanction or penalty attaches to a decision to decline the interview or to remain silent during it.

The risk of future self-incrimination is speculative. Woodard’s convictions are final, and any hypothetical future proceeding is too remote to create a present compulsion. *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 32 (1953) (the Fifth Amendment does not protect against “imaginary” or “speculative” incrimination). The mere possibility that a prisoner might volunteer incriminating information does not render the procedure unconstitutional. *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 8 (1964) (the privilege must be “real” and not “abstract”). Woodard remains free to assert his privilege at any time during the interview. *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609, 614 (1965) (no comment on silence is permitted, but the privilege itself must be claimed). The regulation does not require a waiver of the Fifth Amendment right.

The lower court’s remand for further factfinding on the Fifth Amendment claim was error. No constitutional violation exists on the face of the regulation. The case should be dismissed.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
