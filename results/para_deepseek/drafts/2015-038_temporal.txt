## ARGUMENT

The National Park Service (“NPS”) lawfully enforced its hovercraft prohibition against Mr. Sturgeon on the Nation River. The plain text of § 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (“ANILCA”) does not preclude such regulation.

### I. The Plain Language of § 103(c) Does Not Divest NPS of Authority Over Non-Federal Lands

Section 103(c) provides that only regulations “applicable solely to public lands” within conservation system units are displaced. 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The hovercraft ban is not so limited; it governs conduct throughout the entire unit. Where statutory language is unambiguous, “the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms.” *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The word “solely” means “exclusively.” *United States v. Missouri Pac. R.R.*, 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929). A regulation that applies to all lands within a park—both federal and state—cannot be one “applicable solely to public lands.” Congress thus preserved NPS’s general regulatory authority over non-federal land within Alaska park units.

### II. The Federal Government Possesses Plenary Authority Over Navigable Waters and Federal Lands

Even if the Nation River’s submerged lands are owned by Alaska, the United States retains paramount regulatory power under the Commerce Clause. Navigable waters are “the public property of the United States, and as such are subject to the sovereign authority of the United States as far as may be necessary for the regulation of commerce.” *Gilman v. Philadelphia*, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 713, 724 (1866). The federal government’s navigational servitude “is a federal right of regulation that is paramount over any state interest.” *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 122 (1967); *accord United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co.*, 229 U.S. 53, 61-62 (1913). The Nation River, as a tributary of the Yukon River, is navigable in fact. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871). The hovercraft ban—a safety and environmental regulation on that waterway—falls squarely within Congress’s power “to regulate commerce … among the several States.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3; *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 196 (1824).

Moreover, the Property Clause empowers Congress to “make all needful rules and regulations respecting the territory or other property belonging to the United States.” U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2. This power extends to protecting federal enclaves and their resources, even when those resources cross onto non-federal land. *Camfield v. United States*, 167 U.S. 518, 524-25 (1897) (upholding federal regulation of private lands within a federal enclave to protect public domain). A national preserve is federal territory; the NPS’s authority to manage it necessarily includes the power to regulate activities on state-owned inholdings that threaten park values. *United States v. Alford*, 274 U.S. 264, 267 (1927) (affirming federal hunting regulation on private land within a national park because “the land is within the park and … the protection of the game in the park requires that the regulation apply to it”).

### III. Sturgeon’s As-Applied Challenge Fails Because the Regulation Is Reasonable and Necessary

The hovercraft ban is a reasonable exercise of NPS’s delegated authority to “preserve and protect the national parks and their resources.” *United States v. Brown*, 431 F.2d 1046, 1049 (9th Cir. 1970).* Hovercrafts cause erosion, disturb wildlife, and create noise pollution incompatible with preserve purposes. The ban applies uniformly within the unit; it does not single out state lands. Sturgeon’s preferred use—hunting from a hovercraft on a navigable river—is not a constitutionally protected right. The Privileges or Immunities Clause guarantees only “the right to use navigable waters as a highway for commerce,” *The Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36, 79 (1872), not the right to employ any particular vessel. Sturgeon remains free to hunt on the Nation River using conventional watercraft; he merely cannot use a hovercraft. The regulation does not deprive him of any cognizable interest.

### IV. Congress Did Not Intend § 103(c) to Strip NPS of General Regulatory Authority

ANILCA was enacted to create significant new park units in Alaska while accommodating traditional uses. But Congress carefully balanced that goal by limiting § 103(c)’s displacement to “regulations applicable solely to public lands.” Had Congress intended to strip NPS of all authority over non-federal lands, it could have said so directly. “Where Congress has used a term of art, it is presumed to have intended its technical meaning.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). The term “solely” means “exclusively,” not “primarily” or “in part.” Reading § 103(c) as a wholesale bar would render the word “solely” superfluous, contrary to the canon that “every word of a statute is to be given effect.” *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955).

Furthermore, ANILCA § 1319 preserves all “existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies,” including NPS’s organic authority. 16 U.S.C. § 3207. The NPS Organic Act of 1916 grants broad regulatory power “to regulate the use of the national parks.” *Alford*, 274 U.S. at 267. Nothing in § 103(c) implicitly repeals that authority with respect to non-federal lands within Alaska park units. Repeals by implication are disfavored. *United States v. United Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. 258, 282 (1947).

### Conclusion

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that NPS’s hovercraft ban is not a regulation “applicable solely to public lands” and therefore falls outside § 103(c)’s limitation. The judgment should be affirmed.
