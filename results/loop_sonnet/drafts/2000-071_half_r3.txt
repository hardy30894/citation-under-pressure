# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Policy Reasonably Confines Its Limited Forum to Secular Community Uses, and Excluding Religious Worship Is a Permissible Definition of That Forum's Boundaries, Not Viewpoint Discrimination.

A school building is not a street or park that has "immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public and, time out of mind, . . . used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. Committee for Indus. Org.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Milford's building is a facility of limited and specific dedication—to the education of children during the day and, after hours, to social, civic, and recreational activities benefiting the community, as the Policy itself provides. Because the building is not a traditional public forum, the school retains broad authority to define the boundaries of the access it grants. As this Court has explained, "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). A government need not open its property to every use citizens might wish to make of it merely because it has permitted some limited access for other purposes. *Id.* This Court has likewise sustained municipal authority to condition and confine the use of public facilities according to their intended purposes, so long as the restriction does not single out particular ideas for suppression. *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 398 (1953). Milford's Community Use Policy operates on the same principle: it excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which faith or sect might wish to conduct it.

That line—between teaching morals and character generally, which Milford permits, and religious worship, instruction, and proselytizing, which it does not—is a reasonable, content-based limitation on the forum's subject matter, not an act of viewpoint suppression. This Court has long recognized that reasonable, evenhanded regulation of the use of public property is consistent with the First Amendment so long as it is not employed as a means of suppressing particular ideas or applied with an unequal hand. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 575–76 (1941). Milford's Policy excludes an entire category of activity without regard to which religious tradition seeks to engage in it. The record establishes that the Club's proposed meeting opens and closes in prayer and, in the lesson plan submitted by the Fourniers themselves, includes a segment expressly titled "Challenge and Invitation" that invites children to accept Christ as their personal savior and culminates in a recited sinner's prayer. That is not the teaching of morals from a religious perspective; it is religious instruction and worship of the very kind the Policy reserves against. Excluding it from a limited forum dedicated to civic and recreational activity is a permissible definition of the forum's boundaries, not discrimination against a viewpoint on a subject otherwise open.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Involving School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the Policy's application to the Club implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that permitting the Club's activities in its facility would create Establishment Clause difficulties was reasonable and sufficient justification. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that public schools occupy a uniquely sensitive position in Establishment Clause doctrine because they involve compulsory, impressionable, school-age children. "[I]t is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government," *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962), and the state may not sponsor devotional religious exercises within the school setting, *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 223 (1963). The concern is heightened, not diminished, when the activity takes place in the school building immediately after the close of the school day, using the same classrooms, in continuing proximity to the school's own authority, and directed at the same young children who attend that school. This Court has already held that a public school may not lend its facilities and machinery to the conduct of religious instruction of this kind, even where participation is nominally voluntary, because the school's cooperation in providing the students, the space, and the time impermissibly aids the spread of religious faith. *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 209–10 (1948).

To be sure, this Court has recognized that the Religion Clauses do not require the state to be actively hostile to religion, and that "[t]he First Amendment . . . does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952). But *Zorach* permitted released-time programs precisely because they occurred *off* school property, avoiding any appearance that the school itself sponsored religious exercise. *Id.* at 308–09. Milford's building, by contrast, is the very site of instruction, and the Establishment Clause commands that "the First Amendment mandates governmental neutrality between religion and religion, and between religion and nonreligion." *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 104 (1968). Given the Club's own description of its activities as prayer, scripture memorization, and religious conversion directed at grade-school children on school premises, Milford could reasonably conclude that hosting the Club would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *Engel*, *Schempp*, and *McCollum* forbid. The exclusion was therefore justified on this independent ground as well.
