## Argument

### I. The District Court Erred in Allowing American Steamship's Expert to Testify as to the Cause of the Line's Parting

The district court abused its discretion when it permitted American Steamship's expert to offer an opinion on the cause of the line's parting. The expert's testimony was not based on specialized knowledge, but rather on a factual determination that the jury was equally capable of making. As the Supreme Court held in Salem v. United States Lines Co., 370 U.S. 31, 35 (1962), expert testimony is only admissible when it will "assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue." Here, the expert's opinion on the cause of the line's parting was not expert testimony, but rather a factual conclusion that the jury could have reached on its own. See The Tomar, 67 F.2d 940, 941 (3d Cir. 1933) (holding that expert testimony is not admissible when it is based on facts that are within the knowledge and experience of the average juror).

### II. The District Court Erred in Denying Cook's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law on the Issue of Unseaworthiness

The district court should have granted Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. A vessel is considered unseaworthy if it is not reasonably fit for its intended purpose. See Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc., 362 U.S. 539, 550 (1960). Here, the line supporting the bos'n's chair parted, causing Cook's injury. The evidence established that the line was old and had been used extensively, and that it had been left rigged in the summer months. This evidence demonstrates that the vessel was not reasonably fit for its intended purpose, and therefore, it was unseaworthy as a matter of law. See Petterson v. Alaska S.S. Co., 205 F.2d 478, 479 (9th Cir. 1953) (holding that a vessel is unseaworthy when it is not reasonably fit for its intended purpose).

### III. The District Court Did Not Err in Admitting Evidence of Cook's Alcohol-Related Ailments

The district court did not abuse its discretion when it allowed American Steamship to introduce evidence of Cook's alcohol-related ailments. The evidence was relevant to Cook's damages claim, as it related to his overall health and well-being. See Gladden v. Stockard S.S. Corp., 112 F.2d 170, 172 (3d Cir. 1940) (holding that evidence of a plaintiff's pre-existing medical conditions is admissible to mitigate damages). The admission of this evidence was within the district court's discretion, and it did not unfairly prejudice Cook.

### Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment entered below should be vacated, and the case remanded for a new trial. The district court erred in admitting American Steamship's expert testimony and in denying Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. However, it did not err in admitting evidence of Cook's alcohol-related ailments.
