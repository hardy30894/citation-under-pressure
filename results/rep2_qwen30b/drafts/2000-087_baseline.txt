**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional principle: the government may not imprison an individual without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. In *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S. Ct. 2491, 150 L. Ed. 2d 653 (2001), the Court held that *indefinite* detention under § 1231(a)(6) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) raises “serious constitutional concerns” and that the statute must be construed to contain an “implicit ‘reasonable time’ limitation.” Id. at 2495. The Court further clarified that “once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” Id. at 2503. The Fifth Circuit’s initial decision, which upheld indefinite detention so long as the government made “good faith efforts” to deport, misread this constitutional boundary. The Court must now affirm the district court’s judgment that Zadvydas’ continued detention violates the Constitution because removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable.

The government’s argument that § 1231(a)(6) permits indefinite detention rests on a flawed reading of statutory text and a disregard for the constitutional limits on executive power. The statute provides that an alien may be detained beyond the 90-day removal period if he is “a risk to the community or unlikely to comply with the order of removal.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). But this provision does not authorize detention for its own sake. As the Court emphasized, the statute’s “basic purpose” is “assuring the alien’s presence at the moment of removal.” *Zadvydas*, 121 S. Ct. at 2504. When removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the purpose of detention vanishes. To hold otherwise would transform the statute into a mechanism for indefinite preventive detention—a form of punishment without trial, which the Constitution forbids.

The Fifth Circuit’s standard—that detention is lawful so long as the government makes “good faith efforts” to deport—fails to account for the constitutional requirement of reasonableness. That standard demands nothing more than the government’s assertion of effort, regardless of the likelihood of success. But the Supreme Court rejected such a permissive standard. It made clear that “the alien’s release may and should be conditioned on any of the various forms of supervised release that are appropriate in the circumstances,” and that “continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute” once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. Id. at 2504. The Court did not require the alien to prove *impossibility* of removal, but rather that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. The burden then shifts to the government to demonstrate that removal remains reasonably likely. Id. at 2505.

Here, the record shows that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. Zadvydas has been in custody for over three years beyond the removal period. Germany and Lithuania have both refused to accept him. His application for Lithuanian citizenship remains pending, but the government has not shown that it is likely to succeed. The INS has not identified any country willing to accept him, nor has it demonstrated that any such country will do so in the foreseeable future. The Court has recognized that “the period of prior post-removal confinement grows, what counts as the ‘reasonably foreseeable future’ conversely would have to shrink.” Id. at 2505. After more than three years of detention, the “reasonably foreseeable future” has effectively expired. The government has not rebutted Zadvydas’ showing that removal is not reasonably likely.

The government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 73 S. Ct. 625, 97 L. Ed. 956 (1953), is misplaced. *Mezei* involved an alien denied entry at the border, whose detention was justified by national security concerns and the government’s discretion to exclude. Id. at 212. The Court in *Zadvydas* explicitly distinguished *Mezei*, noting that it “dealt with aliens who had been stopped at the border, and had not effected entry into the United States.” 121 S. Ct. at 2500. Zadvydas is not an excludable alien; he is a long-term resident who has been ordered removed. His detention is not for exclusion, but for removal. The constitutional standards governing such detention are different. As the Court stated, “we nowhere deny the right of Congress to remove aliens, to subject them to supervision with conditions when released from detention, or to incarcerate them where appropriate for violation of those conditions.” Id. at 2501. But the right to supervise does not include the right to imprison indefinitely.

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that Zadvydas’ detention is lawful because deportation “has not been clearly established” as impossible is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s holding. The Court did not require the alien to prove impossibility, but only that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. The government’s burden is not to show that deportation is *possible*, but that it is *reasonably likely* in the foreseeable future. After three years of failed efforts, the government has not met that burden.

The district court correctly applied the constitutional standard. It found that Zadvydas “will never be deported because there is no place to send him,” a finding that necessarily includes a determination that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. The Fifth Circuit’s initial reversal of that finding was error. The Supreme Court’s remand made clear that the government’s burden is not merely to show effort, but to show that removal remains reasonably likely. The government has not done so.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court must be affirmed. The Constitution does not permit indefinite detention of a removable alien when removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. The post-removal-period statute authorizes detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. Once that period ends, the government may not detain the alien without a valid, time-bound purpose. Zadvydas’ continued detention exceeds that limit. The judgment of the district court is correct, and must be affirmed.
