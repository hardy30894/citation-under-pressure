**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt Heidi Heffron-Clark’s inherited IRA because the statutory text protects only “retirement funds,” and an inherited IRA is not retirement savings for the debtor’s own retirement. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). As this Court has explained, an inherited IRA is “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 562 (7th Cir. 2013). That is dispositive.

The Code exempts only funds that remain “retirement funds” in the debtor’s hands. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The plain language is forward-looking and functional: the exemption covers money set aside for retirement, not money that merely once had that character. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 562–64. When Ruth Heffron died, her IRA ceased to be her retirement fund and became Heidi’s inherited IRA, which by federal tax law cannot accept new contributions, cannot be rolled over, and must be distributed on an accelerated schedule. Id. at 562–63; 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(11)(A); 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B). Because the account must be used long before Heidi’s own retirement, it is not “retirement funds” within the ordinary meaning of that term. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563–65.

This Court’s reasoning in *Clark* is consistent with the Supreme Court’s interpretation of exemption statutes as tied to statutory text and structure, not free-floating policy preferences. See *Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320, 326–33 (2005) (looking to the ordinary meaning and statutory function of “retirement funds” in construing IRA exemption); *Patterson v. Shumate*, 504 U.S. 753, 758–60 (1992) (construing bankruptcy exemption in light of the Code’s text and surrounding statutory provisions); *Dewsnup v. Timm*, 502 U.S. 410, 415–20 (1992) (declining to rewrite bankruptcy text in the name of perceived policy); *Lamie v. United States Trustee*, 540 U.S. 526, 534–42 (2004) (where text is clear, courts must apply it as written); *Marrama v. Citizens Bank of Massachusetts*, 549 U.S. 365, 375–76 (2007) (bankruptcy courts must respect the Code’s structure and limits). The same principle forecloses the Clarks’ attempt to extend an exemption designed for retirement savings to assets available for current consumption. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564–66.

The contrary approach adopted below and by the Fifth Circuit in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and the Bankruptcy Appellate Panel in *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (B.A.P. 8th Cir. 2010), gives undue weight to the fact that the money was once retirement savings. But the Bankruptcy Code asks whether the debtor’s present interest is in “retirement funds,” not whether the funds had that character in a predecessor’s hands. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564–66. That distinction matters, just as it does in other exemption contexts. A homestead exemption depends on the debtor’s present use, not the prior owner’s. Id. at 566–67. And this Court has long recognized that bankruptcy exemptions are determined by the debtor’s circumstances at filing, not by a predecessor’s. See *In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985).

Nor does the statutory tax treatment rescue the Clarks. The fact that an inherited IRA continues to enjoy tax deferral until distribution does not make it “retirement funds.” *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 565–66. The Code requires both tax-qualified status and retirement character; tax deferral alone is insufficient. Id. Congress knows how to exempt inherited property when it wishes, but it did not do so here. See *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 566–67; *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) (courts may not enlarge statutory text simply because a broader rule might better serve an objective). The Clarks’ contrary reading would let funds that are presently available for use and that must be withdrawn long before retirement be shielded from creditors, contrary to the statute’s careful balance. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563–68.

This Court should therefore reverse the judgment below and hold that an inherited IRA is not exempt under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) or (d)(12).
