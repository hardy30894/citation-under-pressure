**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL.**

The Controlled Substances Act (“CSA”) does not violate the non-delegation doctrine because Congress provided an intelligible principle to guide the Attorney General’s exercise of authority. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The CSA directs the Attorney General to consider specific factors—including a substance’s potential for abuse, its pharmacological effects, and the risk to public health—before temporarily scheduling a drug. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3); *see also id.* § 811(c). This framework supplies sufficient guidance to withstand constitutional scrutiny. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373 (upholding delegation where Congress set forth “considerations relevant to the promulgation of the Guidelines”).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress provides even minimal standards. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 424-26 (1944) (upholding delegation to set price ceilings where statute required prices to be “fair and equitable”); *Fed. Communications Comm’n v. Fox Television Stations, Inc.*, 556 U.S. 502, 514 (2009) (delegation valid where statute required agency to act “as public interest, convenience, or necessity requires”). The CSA’s temporary scheduling provision is no different. *See Touby v. United States*, 909 F.2d 1419, 1422 (3d Cir. 1990) (upholding delegation under § 811(h)).

Petitioners’ reliance on *Panama Ref. Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved delegations with no statutory standards whatsoever. *Panama Ref. Co.*, 293 U.S. at 415 (finding no “criterion to govern the President’s course”); *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 542-43 (delegation lacked “definite standards”). The CSA, by contrast, provides explicit criteria. *See* 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3).

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL LAWFULLY DELEGATED TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA.**

The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator is authorized under 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), which permits the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions” under the CSA. *See Touby*, 909 F.2d at 1423 (upholding subdelegation under § 871(a)). Courts have long recognized that executive officials may subdelegate authority where the original delegation is valid. *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911) (upholding subdelegation of rulemaking authority to forest rangers); *United States v. Gibert*, 253 F.3d 343, 348 (4th Cir. 2001) (same).

Petitioners’ argument that the subdelegation violates the non-delegation doctrine fails because the Attorney General’s authority is itself constitutional. *See Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372. The CSA’s intelligible principle governs the DEA’s actions just as it governs the Attorney General’s. *See Touby*, 909 F.2d at 1423.

**III. JUDICIAL REVIEW IS NOT BARRED BY 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6).**

Section 811(h)(6) precludes review of the *substance* of a temporary scheduling order but does not foreclose constitutional challenges. *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977) (presuming judicial review of constitutional claims unless Congress clearly strips jurisdiction). Every circuit to consider the issue has held that § 811(h)(6) does not bar facial challenges to the delegation. *United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1428 (10th Cir. 1987); *United States v. Hovey*, 674 F. Supp. 161, 164 (D. Del. 1987).

**CONCLUSION**

The CSA’s temporary scheduling provision does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power, and the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA is lawful. The judgment below should be affirmed.
