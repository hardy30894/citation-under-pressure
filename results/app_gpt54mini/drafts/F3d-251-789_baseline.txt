**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in denying the petition for return under the Hague Convention and the International Child Abduction Remedies Act (“ICARA”), 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601–11610. The Convention requires return unless the respondent proves, by a preponderance of the evidence, a defense such as consent. 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(1)(A); see, e.g., *Mozes v. Mozes*, 239 F.3d 1067, 1070 (9th Cir. 2001). Consent under the Convention is a narrow inquiry focused on the petitioner’s objective manifestations at the time of removal, not on a later acquiescence or post-removal conduct. *Baxter v. Baxter*, 423 F.3d 363, 371-72 (3d Cir. 2005); *Nunez-Escudero v. Tice-Menley*, 58 F.3d 374, 376-77 (8th Cir. 1995).

Here, the record does not support a finding that Evelyn Gonzalez-Caballero consented to a permanent removal and retention of Danelsy in Arizona. At most, the evidence shows that Gonzalez-Caballero permitted Danelsy to travel to the United States while she pursued immigration arrangements and expected to join her daughter. Consent to a temporary visit is not consent to indefinite retention. *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371-72; *Diorinou v. Mezitis*, 237 F.3d 133, 145-46 (2d Cir. 2001). The district court therefore applied an overly broad view of consent.

First, the facts surrounding the parties’ pre-departure discussions negate any finding of consent to abandon custody. According to Gonzalez-Caballero, she and Ramon Mena discussed a plan for the family to travel together to the United States, or at minimum for Mena to help her immigrate so that she could later reunite with Danelsy. Mena’s own conduct confirms that understanding: he bought a one-way ticket for Danelsy, but only after a conversation in which Gonzalez-Caballero allegedly stated that she could no longer adequately care for the child and needed help. That evidence, even if credited, describes an arrangement for assistance and temporary relocation, not a surrender of custody. Consent must be “clearly and unequivocally” shown; ambiguous family arrangements do not suffice. *Antunez-Fernandes v. Connors-Fernandes*, 259 F. Supp. 2d 800, 813 (N.D. Iowa 2003); *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371.

Second, Gonzalez-Caballero’s conduct at the hotel and airport is inconsistent with a finding that she intended Mena to retain Danelsy permanently. She traveled with the child to Panama City, provided the child’s documents, and accompanied Mena to obtain exit paperwork. But those acts are equally consistent with facilitating a temporary departure while she arranged immigration. The Hague Convention distinguishes between consent to travel and consent to retention; the latter cannot be inferred merely because a parent cooperated in the logistics of departure. *In re B. Del C.S.B.*, 559 F.3d 999, 1014-15 (9th Cir. 2009); *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371-72.

Third, Gonzalez-Caballero’s immediate efforts to recover Danelsy demonstrate the opposite of consent. Within days, she consulted Panamanian child-custody authorities, the police, the United States Judge Advocate General liaison, the Institute for Children, radio stations, and the Panamanian government. Such prompt and sustained efforts to secure return are powerful evidence that she did not agree to a permanent retention. See *Friedrich v. Friedrich*, 78 F.3d 1060, 1068-69 (6th Cir. 1996) (distinguishing true consent from later efforts to regain custody); *Diorinou*, 237 F.3d at 145-46. The district court’s contrary finding overlooks these contemporaneous acts and instead relies on an inference of consent from a family dispute and a disputed promise of immigration assistance.

Fourth, Mena’s own statements establish that the retention exceeded any permission Gonzalez-Caballero may have given. He told her he would help her immigrate through her sister in Georgia, and later reiterated that she needed to arrange immigration that way. That is not an assertion of an unconditional right to keep the child; it is evidence that the child’s presence in the United States was linked to the mother’s continued ability to immigrate and reunite. Where a father promises assistance and conditions retention on further steps by the mother, consent to permanent retention is not established. *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371-72; *Diorinou*, 237 F.3d at 145-46.

Finally, the district court’s finding should be set aside because it rests on a legal error in defining consent too expansively. A court may not infer consent from uncertainty, from mixed testimony, or from a parent’s hope that cooperation will preserve family unity. The Convention’s return remedy would be substantially undermined if a temporary travel arrangement could be transformed into permanent retention based on equivocal evidence. *Mozes*, 239 F.3d at 1070-71; *Friedrich*, 78 F.3d at 1068.

Because Mena failed to prove consent to Danelsy’s removal and retention in the United States, the Hague Convention required the district court to order return. The judgment should be reversed.
