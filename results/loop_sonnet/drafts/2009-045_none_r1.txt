# ARGUMENT

## I. Equity Has Long Tied Attorney Compensation to the Quality of Representation and the Results Achieved

Petitioners assume that a "reasonable" fee is nothing more than hours multiplied by a market rate. That assumption cannot be reconciled with nearly a century of this Court's equitable fee jurisprudence, which fee-shifting statutes like § 1988 were built to serve.

In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1881), this Court approved compensating an attorney out of a common fund because his "services and expenditures" had produced a benefit for the fund's beneficiaries—tying compensation to the value created, not merely to time spent. *Id.* at 532. *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), reaffirmed that an attorney's entitlement turns on the benefit his efforts conferred on those who share it. *Id.* at 126. This Court applied the same logic in *Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co. of New York*, 259 U.S. 101 (1922), scrutinizing a fee award against the results actually obtained for the fund's beneficiaries, *id.* at 105, and again in *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), which grounded fee awards in the "historic power of equity to permit ... reimbursement" calibrated to the value the attorney's work created. *Id.* at 164. *Woods v. City National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago*, 312 U.S. 262 (1941), likewise measured a reorganization fee against both the benefit conferred and the caliber of the work performed. *Id.* at 268.

These decisions establish a settled principle: from the earliest exercises of federal equity jurisdiction over fee awards, courts have treated the *quality* of representation and the *result* obtained as legitimate—and at times decisive—components of a reasonable fee. Nothing in the text or history of § 1988 suggests that Congress meant to strip courts of that traditional equitable authority when it authorized fee awards to prevailing civil rights plaintiffs.

## II. Congress Enacted Fee-Shifting Statutes to Secure Counsel Capable of Producing Exceptional Results, Not Merely to Reimburse Hours

Congress did not legislate on a blank slate. This Court's decision in *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400 (1968), construing the materially identical fee provision in Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, confirms that fee-shifting statutes serve a purpose broader than mere reimbursement. The Court explained that "if successful plaintiffs were routinely forced to bear their own attorneys' fees, few aggrieved parties would be in a position to advance the public interest by invoking the injunctive powers of the federal courts." *Id.* at 402. That reasoning presupposes fee awards adequate to secure the kind of skilled, sustained advocacy that actually *vindicates* the statutory right—not merely advocacy that satisfies a minimum hourly threshold.

*Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714 (1967), reinforces the point. There, this Court declined to fashion a fee-shifting remedy absent statutory authorization, recognizing that Congress deliberately chooses when and how to displace the American Rule "to effectuate policies" it deems important. *Id.* at 719. Where Congress has made that choice specifically to enable enforcement of civil rights, as it did in § 1988, courts must give that choice practical effect. A fee regime that caps every award at a mechanical hourly product—regardless of how extraordinary the result—would frustrate rather than serve the statutory purpose *Piggie Park* and *Fleischmann* identify.

## III. The District Court's Firsthand Assessment of Counsel's Performance Warrants Deference

No court is better positioned than the district court to judge whether counsel's performance and the result obtained were exceptional. This Court has long recognized that the tribunal that observes proceedings firsthand deserves deference precisely because a reviewing court cannot recreate that vantage point from a cold record. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), this Court held that a trial court's factual findings should be disturbed only when the reviewing court, examining the entire record, "is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *Id.* at 395. That standard—rooted in the recognition that trial-level observation cannot be duplicated on appeal—applies with full force to a district judge's assessment, informed by years presiding over difficult litigation, of whether counsel's advocacy and the result achieved were extraordinary.

## IV. This Is the Rare Case in Which Enhancement Was Warranted

The district court did not rest on generalized praise. It made detailed, record-based findings concerning the scope of Georgia's foster-care crisis, the extraordinary relief secured for the plaintiff class, and counsel's singular skill and commitment over years of litigation. Those findings are precisely the kind of evidence that equity has always considered relevant to a reasonable fee. Consistent with the equitable principles reflected in *Greenough*, *Pettus*, *Newton*, *Sprague*, and *Woods*, the statutory purpose confirmed in *Piggie Park* and *Fleischmann*, and the deference commanded by *Gypsum*, the enhancement here should be affirmed.
