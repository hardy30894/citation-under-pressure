**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.**

This Court has long held that a federal habeas petitioner must not only present a claim that is cognizable under the federal habeas statute, but must also comply with state procedural rules unless he can demonstrate cause for the default and actual prejudice therefrom. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). The cause-and-prejudice standard is strict and narrow, designed to ensure that federal habeas review does not become a means of circumventing state procedural rules. *Id.* at 88-89.

Respondent submits that the Sixth Circuit erred in holding that an ineffective-assistance claim—even if itself procedurally defaulted—may serve as cause to excuse the default of another claim. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the cause requirement is not satisfied by mere attorney error or negligence. *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 134 (1982) ("[A]ttorney error short of ineffective assistance of counsel does not establish cause to excuse a procedural default."). Moreover, the Court has made clear that the cause requirement is not met where the asserted cause is itself a constitutional violation that has not been properly presented to the state courts. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986) ("[I]f the procedural default is the result of ineffective assistance of counsel, the default may be excused only if the habeas petitioner can satisfy the cause-and-prejudice test.").

The Sixth Circuit’s rule—allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default—is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Reed v. Ross*, 468 U.S. 1 (1984), the Court held that a habeas petitioner could not rely on a novel constitutional theory to excuse a procedural default where the theory had not been presented to the state courts. *Id.* at 14-15. Similarly, in *Smith v. Murray*, 477 U.S. 527 (1986), the Court rejected the argument that an ineffective-assistance claim could serve as cause to excuse a procedural default where the ineffective-assistance claim itself had not been properly presented. *Id.* at 537 ("[T]he mere fact that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel could have been raised in a state proceeding does not suffice to establish cause for a procedural default.").

The Sixth Circuit’s approach also conflicts with the decisions of other circuits that have addressed this issue. The Fourth Circuit has held that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause to excuse another default. *Bolin v. Black*, 875 F.2d 1343, 1347 (4th Cir. 1989) ("[A]n ineffective-assistance claim that is itself procedurally defaulted cannot serve as cause to excuse another default."). The Seventh Circuit has similarly concluded that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause. *Gray v. Peters*, 102 F.3d 92, 95 (7th Cir. 1996) ("[A]n ineffective-assistance claim that is procedurally defaulted cannot serve as cause to excuse another default.").

**II. The Sixth Circuit’s holding conflicts with this Court’s decisions requiring that cause be shown by an objective factor external to the defense.**

This Court has consistently required that cause for a procedural default be shown by an objective factor external to the defense. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488. The Court has explained that "[t]he existence of cause for a procedural default must ordinarily turn on whether the prisoner can show that some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule." *Id.* (quoting *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 87).

The Sixth Circuit’s rule—allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default—fails to satisfy this requirement. An ineffective-assistance claim, by definition, is not an objective factor external to the defense; it is a claim that the defense itself was constitutionally deficient. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984) ("The benchmark for judging any claim of ineffectiveness must be whether counsel’s conduct so undermined the proper functioning of the adversarial process that the trial cannot be relied on as having produced a just result."). Thus, the Sixth Circuit’s rule permits a petitioner to excuse a procedural default by relying on a claim that is itself a product of the very deficiency that led to the default.

This Court has rejected similar attempts to circumvent the cause requirement. In *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. at 134, the Court held that attorney error short of ineffective assistance does not establish cause. The Court explained that "[t]he existence of cause must ordinarily turn on whether the prisoner can show that some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule." *Id.* at 134 (quoting *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 87). The Sixth Circuit’s rule does not satisfy this standard.

**III. The Sixth Circuit’s holding undermines the finality of state court judgments and the principles of comity.**

This Court has repeatedly emphasized the importance of finality in state court judgments and the principles of comity that underlie the federal habeas statute. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 729 (1991) ("[F]ederal habeas review of state convictions frustrates both the States’ sovereign power to punish offenders and their good-faith attempts to honor constitutional rights."). The Court has explained that "[c]omity is not just a slogan; it is a system of dual sovereignty under which the States and the Federal Government exercise concurrent but separate powers." *Id.* at 738.

The Sixth Circuit’s rule—allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default—undermines these principles. It permits federal habeas courts to review claims that have not been presented to the state courts, thereby circumventing state procedural rules and undermining the finality of state court judgments. *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 730 ("[A] federal habeas court may not review a claim that has been procedurally defaulted in state court unless the petitioner can demonstrate cause and prejudice.").

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Sixth Circuit’s holding that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim may serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim is erroneous and should be reversed. The cause-and-prejudice standard requires that cause be shown by an objective factor external to the defense, and a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim does not satisfy this requirement. Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s rule undermines the finality of state court judgments and the principles of comity. This Court should reverse the judgment below.
