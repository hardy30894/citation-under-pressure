ARGUMENT

Missouri's constitutional mandate that state judges retire at age seventy neither conflicts with the Age Discrimination in Employment Act nor denies equal protection. The statute's definition of "employee" excludes "an appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Judges selected under the Missouri Plan exercise precisely such authority. Their decisions establish binding rules of decision that fill interstices in statutes and precedents, and they administer the judicial system itself. See Booth v. United States, 291 U.S. 339, 350 (1934) (judicial office continues during good behavior and compensation may not be diminished); Duncan v. Missouri, 152 U.S. 377, 382 (1894) (state may reorganize its courts and confer jurisdiction on divisions without violating due process).

Congress did not manifest a clear intention to displace this traditional state authority over its judiciary. See Railroad Retirement Board v. Alton Railroad, 295 U.S. 330, 374 (1935) (federal power must be exercised in subjection to the Constitution and may not invade reserved state functions). The exemption for policymaking appointees therefore applies, and the ADEA claim fails.

The equal-protection challenge likewise lacks merit. Age classifications in public employment are sustained when they bear a rational relation to a legitimate state interest. Dodge v. Board of Education, 302 U.S. 74, 80-81 (1937) (legislature may alter retirement annuities without impairing contract rights when the change is reasonable). Missouri's rule eliminates the risk that judicial faculties will decline with age, reduces delays from incapacity, and permits orderly succession. See Hannum v. United States, 226 U.S. 436, 440 (1913) (retirement statutes may distinguish among officers on the basis of service and capacity). The provision also facilitates broader representation on the bench. Joseph S. Finch & Co. v. McKittrick, 305 U.S. 395, 400 (1939) (state may enact measures to protect legitimate local interests).

Such classifications are not rendered irrational merely because reasonable minds differ. Ohio ex rel. Bryant v. Akron Metropolitan Park District, 281 U.S. 74, 81 (1930) (state procedural requirements do not deny equal protection when they apply uniformly). Nor does the mandatory retirement invade any fundamental right to public employment. See Layton v. Missouri, 187 U.S. 356, 360 (1902) (state may limit appellate jurisdiction without offending due process). The Due Process Clause affords no greater protection. Dreyer v. Illinois, 187 U.S. 71, 84 (1902) (separation of powers within a state is a matter of state concern).

Finally, the Missouri Constitution's own provisions governing judicial tenure are entitled to respect. Gorman v. Washington University, 316 U.S. 98, 101 (1942) (state constitutional limitations on taxation upheld against contract-clause attack). Because the retirement requirement rests on a rational basis, it satisfies the Fourteenth Amendment.
