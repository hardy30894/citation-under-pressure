**Argument**

Section 1255 requires that an applicant for adjustment of status have been “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). Congress separately provided that a person granted Temporary Protected Status “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The question is whether that lawful-status fiction also supplies the distinct statutory requirement of “admission.” It does not. But Petitioner’s case turns on the best reading of the statutes as enacted, and that reading is not the one adopted below.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that the INA draws a sharp line between “admission” and “status.” In *Hanif v. Attorney General*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), the Court explained that “the date of gaining a new status is not the same as the date of the physical event of entering the country.” Id. at 485. Likewise, *Taveras v. Attorney General*, 731 F.3d 281 (3d Cir. 2013), held that “admission” turns on “physical entrance into the country,” a concept distinct from adjustment proceedings that occur within the United States. Id. at 290. Those precedents foreclose the government’s attempt to collapse TPS-granted lawful status into admission.

The statutory definition confirms that result. Congress defined “admission” and “admitted” to mean “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This Court has already interpreted “admission” in light of that definition. *Hanif*, 694 F.3d at 485. Nothing in § 1254a says that the grant of TPS is itself a lawful entry, inspection, or authorization. Indeed, TPS is available only to aliens already present in the United States. *De Leon-Ochoa v. Attorney General*, 622 F.3d 341, 353–54 (3d Cir. 2010), recognized that TPS is not “a program of entry for an alien.” Id. at 353–54. That conclusion is dispositive here.

Petitioner’s contrary reading also conflicts with how courts distinguish lawful status from admission in other immigration contexts. The Fifth Circuit has explained that “Admission and status are fundamentally distinct concepts.” *Gomez v. Lynch*, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016). And the Eleventh Circuit held in *Serrano v. Attorney General*, 655 F.3d 1260 (11th Cir. 2011) (per curiam), that TPS “does not change § 1255(a)’s threshold requirement” of being “initially inspected and admitted or paroled.” Id. at 1265. That is the proper reading of § 1254a(f)(4): it protects status, not entry.

Petitioner relies heavily on *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954 (9th Cir. 2017), but *Ramirez* conflated lawful status with admission by reasoning that an individual obtaining TPS goes through inspection and is deemed admitted. Id. at 960. That approach cannot be squared with the text Congress chose. Nor can it be reconciled with the Board’s recognition that an “adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission” only because Congress has expressly made it so in that context. *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (B.I.A. 2011). Congress did not make that same choice for TPS.

The structure of the INA points the same way. Where Congress wanted to relieve applicants from the ordinary admission requirement, it said so expressly. See 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i); *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (Congress’s inclusion of particular language in one section and omission in another is presumed intentional). Reading TPS as an admission would also make § 1255’s separate status provisions largely redundant, contrary to *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004), which instructs that a statute should be construed so that “effect is given to all its provisions.” Id.

Finally, Petitioner’s position would distort the limited purpose of TPS. TPS is a temporary humanitarian shield, not a pathway that rewrites the threshold eligibility rules for permanent residency. *De Leon-Ochoa*, 622 F.3d at 353–54; *Gomez*, 831 F.3d at 658. Because Petitioners entered without inspection or admission, they do not satisfy § 1255(a). The judgment below should be reversed.
