**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to meet this constitutional obligation in the instant case. The record reveals substantial evidence of Richard Allan Moran’s mental instability at the time he discharged his counsel and pleaded guilty—evidence that should have triggered a good faith doubt about his competency and necessitated a hearing under the “reasoned choice” standard. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the waiver of fundamental constitutional rights demands more than mere rational understanding; it requires the defendant to possess the capacity to make a deliberate, informed, and intelligent decision among available legal alternatives. When a defendant’s mental state raises such doubt, due process mandates judicial inquiry, not deference to a flawed post-conviction determination.

The foundational principle that a defendant must possess the capacity for “reasoned choice” to validly waive constitutional rights was established in *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. 1 (1966). There, the Court emphasized that the waiver of a constitutional right—here, the right to cross-examine witnesses—must be “knowing and intelligent,” and that the trial court must ensure that the defendant “understands the nature of the proceedings and the consequences of his actions.” Id. at 5. The Court rejected the notion that a mere acknowledgment of a signature on a waiver form sufficed to establish a valid waiver, particularly where the record showed no meaningful inquiry into the defendant’s comprehension. The same principle applies with even greater force when a defendant seeks to waive the right to counsel, a right that is “so fundamental and so personal that no lawyer can properly be appointed to represent the defendant in the waiver of that right.” *Palmer v. Ashe*, 342 U.S. 134, 136 (1951). The Court in *Palmer* held that the Due Process Clause requires the state to ensure that a defendant is not deprived of counsel in circumstances where the absence of legal representation would render a trial fundamentally unfair. This principle is not limited to the initial appointment of counsel but extends to the moment of waiver.

The Court has further clarified that the standard for competency to waive counsel is higher than the standard for competency to stand trial. In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), the Court held that a defendant is competent to stand trial if he has “a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings against him, and is capable of assisting counsel.” But the Court has consistently recognized that the waiver of counsel or a guilty plea demands more. As the Ninth Circuit correctly observed, “competency to waive constitutional rights requires a higher level of mental functioning than that required to stand trial.” *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 269 (9th Cir. 1992). This higher standard—“reasoned choice”—was articulated in *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1973), where the court stated that a defendant must be able to “understand the nature of the alternatives and make a deliberate choice among them.” This standard is not merely theoretical; it is constitutionally mandated.

The record in this case presents compelling evidence that Moran lacked the capacity for reasoned choice. He had recently attempted suicide, was hospitalized, and was taking four different medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—all of which affect cognitive function, mood, and decision-making. The trial court, however, made no inquiry into the effects of these drugs, despite Moran’s own admission that he was on medication. This failure to investigate is fatal to the validity of the waiver. As the Court held in *Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173, 177 (1946), “the right to be represented by counsel is one which the defendant may waive or claim, as he shall determine.” But the Court also made clear that the waiver must be “voluntary and intelligent,” and that the court must ensure that the defendant is not “deprived of the opportunity to make a rational choice.” Id. The court’s failure to probe the impact of Moran’s medication and mental state rendered the waiver anything but voluntary.

Moreover, the state court’s post-conviction determination that Moran was competent to waive counsel was based on psychiatric evaluations conducted months earlier, which were designed solely to assess competency to stand trial—not to evaluate the higher “reasoned choice” standard. As the Ninth Circuit correctly noted, “the conclusions of the psychiatrists were the sole support for the state court’s postconviction ruling that Moran was competent to waive counsel.” *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 269. But the standard for competency to stand trial is not the same as the standard for competency to waive counsel. In *United States Ex Rel. Smith v. Baldi*, 344 U.S. 561, 564 (1953), the Court held that a defendant’s mental state at the time of the plea must be assessed with “utmost solicitude,” and that “the burden is on the state to show that the defendant was competent to plead guilty.” The state failed to meet this burden here, relying on evaluations that were irrelevant to the issue at hand.

The Court has also recognized that a defendant’s desire to prevent the introduction of mitigating evidence may be a sign of mental instability, not rational strategy. In *Rees v. Peyton*, 384 U.S. 312, 313 (1966), the Court noted that a defendant’s request to withdraw a habeas petition after expressing a desire to proceed with legal action raised “doubt on his mental competency.” Similarly, Moran’s insistence on preventing any mitigating evidence from being presented—despite the risk of a death sentence—suggests a lack of rational decision-making, not a reasoned choice. The trial court’s acceptance of such a plea without inquiry into its basis violated due process.
