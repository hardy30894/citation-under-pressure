**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—lies at the heart of the First Amendment’s protection against criminalizing speech absent a clear intent to threaten. The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective “true threat” standard, which permits conviction based solely on whether a reasonable person would perceive the statement as a threat, violates the constitutional guarantee of due process and contravenes the Supreme Court’s definitive holding in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003). This Court should reverse because the government must prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the defendant *subjectively intended* his statements to be taken as threats.

The First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive, alarming, or disturbing. *Cohen v. California*, 403 U.S. 15, 20 (1971). But the Supreme Court has long recognized an exception for “true threats,” which are not protected by the First Amendment because they “fall outside the scope of constitutionally protected speech.” *United States v. Alvarez*, 562 U.S. 596, 604 (2011) (quoting *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359). The critical question, however, is not whether a reasonable person would interpret the statement as a threat, but whether the speaker *intended* it to be understood as one. As the Court made clear in *Black*, the true threat exception “does not extend to mere expressions of anger or frustration” and “does not reach speech that is merely offensive or provocative.” 538 U.S. at 359. To be criminalized, a threat must be “a serious expression of intent to inflict injury.” Id. at 360.

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—requiring only that a “reasonable speaker” would foresee the statement as threatening—effectively criminalizes speech based on the audience’s perception, not the speaker’s intent. This standard is constitutionally deficient. In *Black*, the Court rejected a purely objective test, holding that “a true threat is a statement in which the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of intent to harm.” 538 U.S. at 359. The Court emphasized that “the speaker’s intent is central” to the true threat analysis. Id. The Court further explained that “a threat is not a threat unless the speaker means it to be one.” Id. This language is not mere dicta—it is the core of the Court’s holding. The Third Circuit’s instruction, which instructed the jury that a statement is a true threat if a “reasonable speaker” would foresee it as threatening, directly contradicts *Black* by substituting the speaker’s intent with a hypothetical observer’s reaction.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced. *Kosma* predated *Black* and did not address the subjective intent requirement. Moreover, *Kosma* did not purport to define the constitutional standard for true threats; it applied a pre-*Black* understanding of the doctrine. The Supreme Court has repeatedly made clear that lower courts must conform their interpretations to the constitutional standards set forth in its precedents. *See, e.g.,* *United States v. Booker*, 543 U.S. 220, 233 (2005) (“[W]e have long held that the Constitution requires that the government prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt.”). The Third Circuit’s refusal to apply *Black*’s subjective intent standard renders its interpretation of § 875(c) constitutionally infirm.

The government’s argument that a subjective intent standard would “fail to protect individuals from the fear of violence” is a mischaracterization of the First Amendment’s purpose. The Constitution does not require the government to criminalize speech based on its emotional impact on listeners. *See* *Cohen*, 403 U.S. at 20 (“The First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive or disturbing.”). The true threat exception is not a tool for managing public anxiety; it is a narrow exception for speech that poses a genuine, credible danger of violence. *See* *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. The government may still prosecute threats under § 875(c) when the speaker *intends* to threaten—when the words are not mere hyperbole, artistic expression, or venting—but a serious, subjective intent to harm. The subjective intent standard does not immunize dangerous speech; it ensures that only speech with a culpable mental state is punished.

The Third Circuit’s instruction also violates due process. The Due Process Clause requires that criminal statutes define the elements of a crime with sufficient clarity so that individuals can understand what conduct is prohibited. *See* *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). An objective standard that criminalizes speech based on how a reasonable person might interpret it—regardless of the speaker’s actual intent—creates a “dangerous uncertainty” about what speech is punishable. *See* *United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. 285, 292 (2008). This uncertainty violates the principle that “no man shall be held criminally responsible for conduct which he could not reasonably understand to be proscribed.” *See* *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994).

The judgment of the Third Circuit must be reversed. The Constitution requires that a conviction under § 875(c) be based on proof that the defendant *subjectively intended* his statements to be threats. The Third Circuit’s contrary instruction, which permits conviction based on an objective perception standard, is inconsistent with *Virginia v. Black* and violates the Due Process Clause. The judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s precedent.
