**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL”**

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act defines “employee” to exclude “any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri’s appointed judges fall squarely within this exclusion.

This Court has long recognized that judging inherently involves policymaking. In *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938), the Court acknowledged that federal courts “make” law in the absence of controlling precedent. State judges exercise the same function. As Justice Holmes observed, “the prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law.” *Oliver Wendell Holmes, The Path of the Law*, 10 Harv. L. Rev. 457, 461 (1897). When judges resolve unsettled questions, they necessarily create policy.

The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, has established common law for the entire state, rejecting established tort doctrines and adopting new rules. *See, e.g., Gustafson v. Benda*, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc). Such decisions involve the same discretion and judgment exercised by policymaking appointees in the executive and legislative branches.

Moreover, the structure of the ADEA supports this conclusion. Congress exempted “any person elected to public office” from the definition of “employee.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). If appointed judges were covered, then states with appointed judiciaries would face federal age discrimination liability, while states with elected judiciaries would not. This disparate treatment would undermine the federalism principles that this Court has consistently protected. *See United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971) (requiring clear statement before federal law intrudes on traditional state functions).

The legislative history confirms that the “policymaking level” exception was intended to be construed narrowly, but it does not exclude judges. The Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers states that the exemption covers “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments.” 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180. The judiciary is a coequal branch of state government, and its members are among the highest-level appointees in any state.

**II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause requires only that a classification bear a rational relationship to a legitimate state interest. *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78-79 (1911). Missouri’s mandatory retirement age of 70 for judges satisfies this deferential standard.

This Court has upheld mandatory retirement ages for public employees. In *Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam), the Court held that mandatory retirement at age 50 for state police officers was rational because physical ability declines with age. Similarly, in *Vance v. Bradley*, 440 U.S. 93, 108 (1979), the Court upheld a mandatory retirement age of 60 for Foreign Service officers, noting that Congress could rationally conclude that older employees were less able to meet the demands of overseas service.

The same reasoning applies to judges. The work of judging requires mental acuity, stamina, and the ability to process complex legal issues. As this Court recognized in *Murgia*, “the state may properly rely on generalities about age” when drawing lines. 427 U.S. at 314. Missouri could rationally conclude that age 70 is an appropriate point at which to require retirement, given the unique demands of judicial office.

Furthermore, mandatory retirement serves legitimate state interests beyond individual competence. It “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged judges,” *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980), and “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges,” *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979). These are rational bases for the classification.

Finally, the classification does not target a suspect class or burden a fundamental right. This Court has held that “the right of governmental employment per se is not fundamental.” *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 313. Age is not a suspect classification. *Id.* at 314. Therefore, rational basis review applies, and Missouri’s provision easily satisfies it.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
