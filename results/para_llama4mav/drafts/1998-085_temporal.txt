The Court of Appeals erred in holding that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The Civil Rights Act of 1991 allows punitive damages when a defendant engages in discriminatory practices "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). 

The statutory text does not require egregious or outrageous conduct independent of the employer's state of mind. In Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), this Court held that exemplary damages are awarded "as punishment for the defendant's wrongful act" and are "given to the plaintiff to vindicate his right." Id. at 107. The Court further stated that "the act, or the omission complained of, must be accompanied by some evil intent, or must be the result of such reckless disregard for the rights of others as to be equivalent to intentional violation of them." Id. 

Similarly, in Philadelphia, W. & B.R. Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202 (1858), the Court held that exemplary damages are recoverable when a defendant's conduct is "wanton, or oppressive, or ... accompanied by such circumstances of fraud or malice." Id. at 214. The Court emphasized that the focus is on the defendant's state of mind, not the severity of the conduct itself. 

In accordance with these precedents, the Court should hold that an employer's conduct does not need to be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. Rather, the employer must have acted with "malice or with reckless indifference" to the plaintiff's federally protected rights. The Court of Appeals' contrary holding was erroneous, and the judgment should be reversed.
