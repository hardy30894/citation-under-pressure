The district courts possess original jurisdiction over claims alleging that federal employment actions were infected by discrimination prohibited by federal statute. See Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678, 684 (1946) (recognizing that suits arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States belong in district court). That jurisdiction is not defeated when an agency interposes a threshold objection to its own authority. See Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm. v. McGrath, 341 U.S. 123, 140 (1951) (holding that the existence of a federal right supplies the predicate for judicial inquiry into agency action).

The same principle governs review of administrative determinations that rest on asserted lack of jurisdiction. See Larson v. Domestic & Foreign Commerce Corp., 337 U.S. 682, 689 (1949) (affirming that district courts may examine whether an officer has exceeded statutory bounds). A dismissal on voluntariness grounds is no different in kind; it remains a determination that the employee has been affected by an action claimed to rest on forbidden discrimination. See Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) (stating that official action predicated on impermissible classification violates equal protection).

Congress has long provided that federal officers may be compelled to answer in district court when their conduct trenches upon statutory rights. See Kendall v. United States ex rel. Stokes, 37 U.S. (12 Pet.) 524, 623 (1838) (issuing mandamus to enforce a statutory duty). That authority extends to claims that an agency settlement was procured through coercion amounting to discrimination. See Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 155 (1908) (permitting suit against officers acting under color of invalid authority).

Nor does the existence of an alternative appellate forum divest the district courts of their original jurisdiction. See Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738, 819 (1824) (holding that concurrent jurisdiction does not oust the courts of the United States). The Federal Circuit’s specialized role in reviewing certain personnel actions does not extend to cases in which the employee has invoked federal antidiscrimination statutes. See United States v. Lee, 106 U.S. 196, 220 (1882) (recognizing that suits to vindicate personal rights against governmental overreach belong in courts of general jurisdiction).

Finally, the presumption that retirements are voluntary cannot be applied to foreclose district-court review when the employee alleges that the presumption itself masks discrimination. See Shelley v. Kraemer, 334 U.S. 1, 22 (1948) (refusing to permit state action to give effect to private agreements that violate federal rights). The district court therefore remains the proper forum.
