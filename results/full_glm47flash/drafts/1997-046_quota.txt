**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Petitioner possesses a protected life or liberty interest in the clemency process.**

The Due Process Clause protects an inmate’s interest in the clemency process because it is an integral part of the state’s final determination of guilt and punishment. In *Griffin v. Illinois*, the Supreme Court held that the right to appeal is “an integral part of the fair and just administration of the criminal law,” and a state must provide meaningful appellate review. 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956). This logic extends to executive clemency, the final check on the state’s power to take life. In *Evitts v. Lucey*, the Court found a right to counsel on the first appeal as of right, emphasizing that a state’s creation of an appellate procedure “must meet the demands of the Due Process Clause.” 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985). While *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat* found no protected interest in clemency, that case involved a lack of a statutory right to a hearing, whereas here the state has created a specific procedure that directly impacts the inmate’s liberty. The dissent’s reliance on *Dumschat* to deny a protected interest ignores the reality that the state’s creation of a procedure affecting liberty triggers due process protections. Furthermore, the “inherent liberty interest” standard established in *Sandin v. Conner* suggests that the state cannot create procedures that are “at odds with the basic human needs that accompany the sentence of imprisonment.” 515 U.S. 472, 483 (1995). Unlike *Meachum v. Fano*, where the Court found no liberty interest in prison transfer, clemency represents a substantial change in conditions that implicates the core of liberty.

**II. The option to voluntarily participate in a clemency interview violates the Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination.**

The regulation creates an unconstitutional condition by effectively compelling a waiver of the Fifth Amendment privilege to access the clemency process. The state cannot force a waiver of a constitutional right to achieve a legitimate state goal. As the Court stated in *Palko v. Connecticut*, “The State may not, by its own act, deprive an individual of a right guaranteed by the Constitution.” 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). Here, the regulation presents a “Hobson’s choice” where the inmate must choose between risking self-incrimination or forgoing a potential avenue for relief. The dissent argues that the risk is “premature” under *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 324 (1976), but *Baxter* dealt with prison disciplinary hearings where the state’s power to use statements was speculative. In contrast, the state has the clear power to use statements against the inmate in future criminal proceedings, creating a present and substantial risk. The state’s interest in a clemency review does not outweigh the inmate’s fundamental right to remain silent. This is distinct from *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, where the Court recognized a liberty interest in parole, as parole is a conditional release, whereas clemency is a complete remission of the sentence. 442 U.S. 1, 15 (1979).
