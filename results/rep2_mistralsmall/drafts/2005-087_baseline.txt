**ARGUMENT**

I. ARIZONA’S LIMITATION ON THE USE OF EXPERT EVIDENCE OF MENTAL STATE DOES NOT VIOLATE DUE PROCESS

The Fourteenth Amendment’s guarantee of due process does not confer a freestanding right to present expert testimony about a defendant’s mental state to negate mens rea, independent of the insanity defense. The Supreme Court has long recognized that states retain broad authority to define crimes and defenses, including the contours of insanity and the admissibility of mental health evidence. *See Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952) (“The States enjoy broad discretion in framing their criminal laws and in prescribing the manner in which those laws shall be enforced.”); *see also Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 752 (2006) (upholding Arizona’s restriction on expert testimony to the insanity defense).

A defendant’s due process rights are satisfied when the state’s evidentiary rules do not “offend some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” *Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437, 445 (1992) (quoting *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 202 (1977)). Arizona’s rule does not offend any such principle. The Court has repeatedly deferred to state legislative judgments in structuring insanity defenses and related evidentiary rules. *See Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992) (“The states have considerable latitude in shaping their criminal laws.”); *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798. Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert mental health evidence to the insanity defense is a rational means of preventing jury confusion and ensuring that the insanity defense remains a discrete, statutorily defined exception to criminal responsibility. *See Clark*, 548 U.S. at 752–53.

This Court has never recognized a constitutional right to use mental health evidence to negate mens rea outside the insanity framework. To the contrary, in *United States v. Lyons*, 731 F.2d 243, 246 (5th Cir. 1984), this Court held that a defendant’s due process rights were not violated by a district court’s refusal to allow psychiatric testimony to negate intent, where the defendant was not asserting an insanity defense. The Court reasoned that the defendant’s proposed use of the evidence was “not a constitutionally protected right” but rather a strategic choice subject to reasonable evidentiary rules. *Id.* Arizona’s rule is no different. It does not bar all evidence of mental illness; it merely channels such evidence to the statutorily defined insanity defense. *See Clark*, 548 U.S. at 752.

II. ARIZONA’S RULE IS CONSISTENT WITH DUE PROCESS BECAUSE IT DOES NOT PREVENT THE DEFENDANT FROM PRESENTING EVIDENCE RELEVANT TO HIS DEFENSE

Due process does not require that a defendant be permitted to present all relevant evidence in his favor. *See Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 43 (1996) (plurality opinion) (“States are free to adopt rules of evidence and criminal procedure that reflect their own notions of fairness and policy.”). Arizona’s rule does not prevent Clark from presenting evidence of his mental illness; it merely limits the purpose for which that evidence may be used. *See Clark*, 548 U.S. at 752. The rule ensures that the jury does not improperly conflate evidence of mental illness with a lack of criminal intent, thereby avoiding confusion and preserving the integrity of the insanity defense. *Id.* at 753.

Moreover, the rule does not deprive Clark of a meaningful opportunity to present a defense. He was permitted to introduce expert testimony to support his insanity defense, and the jury was instructed on that defense. *Id.* at 752. The Constitution does not require that a defendant be allowed to use mental health evidence for a dual purpose—both to prove insanity and to negate mens rea—when the state has chosen to define insanity as the exclusive vehicle for such evidence. *See id.* at 752–53.

III. ARIZONA’S RULE DOES NOT VIOLATE DUE PROCESS AS APPLIED TO CLARK’S CASE

Even assuming, arguendo, that due process includes some right to present expert mental health evidence to negate mens rea, Arizona’s rule was rationally related to a legitimate state interest and did not arbitrarily deprive Clark of a fair trial. *See Heller v. Doe*, 509 U.S. 312, 319–20 (1993). The Arizona Supreme Court’s decision upholding the rule was neither contrary to, nor an unreasonable application of, clearly established federal law. *See 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)(1)*.

Clark’s reliance on *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), is misplaced. In *Washington*, the Court held that the Sixth Amendment Compulsory Process Clause required that a defendant be permitted to compel the testimony of a co-defendant. *Id.* at 23. That case involved a defendant’s right to present exculpatory testimony from a witness who was physically available to testify. *Id.* Here, by contrast, Clark sought to use expert testimony for a purpose—negating mens rea—that Arizona law did not recognize as a valid defense. The rule did not prevent Clark from presenting any witness or evidence; it merely limited the permissible inferences from the expert testimony he did present. *See Clark*, 548 U.S. at 752–53.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
