# ARGUMENT

## I. The Controlled Substances Act's Temporary Scheduling Provision Supplies an Intelligible Principle and Therefore Does Not Unconstitutionally Delegate Legislative Power.

Article I vests "all legislative Powers" in Congress, but that provision has never been read to forbid Congress from enlisting executive assistance in filling in the details of a regulatory scheme. As this Court explained in *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394 (1928), Congress does not unconstitutionally delegate its power when it "lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *Id.* at 409. So long as Congress supplies that principle, the resulting statute "is not a forbidden delegation of legislative power." *Id.*

The temporary scheduling provision of the Controlled Substances Act, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), readily satisfies this standard. Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check; it specified the substantive criteria he must weigh—the drug's "history and current pattern of abuse," the "scope, duration, and significance of abuse," and the "risk, if any, to the public health," 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4)-(6)—and it required a finding that scheduling is necessary "to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety." § 811(h)(1). Congress further cabined the delegated authority procedurally, mandating Federal Register notice and consideration of the Secretary of Health and Human Services' views, § 811(h)(4), and temporally, limiting any temporary schedule to one year with a single six-month extension. § 811(h)(2). These are precisely the sort of "basic policy" determinations that satisfy the intelligible-principle requirement.

The contrast with the two decisions in which this Court struck down delegations is instructive. In *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), the Court invalidated a delegation because the statute "did not declare any policy" governing the President's authority to prohibit interstate shipment of contraband oil, and established no "criterion to govern the President's course." *Id.* at 415. Similarly, in *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), the Court found the National Industrial Recovery Act's code-approval scheme unconstrained by any legislative standard. Section 811(h) suffers from no comparable defect: it enumerates specific statutory factors, imposes procedural checks, and bounds the duration of the exercise of authority.

Other considerations this Court has treated as relevant to the delegation inquiry likewise favor the Government. The temporary scheduling power is subject to a defined and generally accepted body of pharmacological and law-enforcement standards, akin to the "well-known and generally acceptable standards" that supported delegation in *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). And because designer drugs can be synthesized and distributed before a formal rulemaking could be completed, Congress was entitled to provide an expedited mechanism responsive to the "rapidity of changes in the matter being regulated," much as this Court recognized in *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944), when it upheld broad wartime price-control delegations because of the need for swift administrative response. Finally, unlike the delegations in *Panama Refining* and *Schechter Poultry*, § 811(h) operates against the backdrop of settled expectations concerning judicial oversight of agency compliance with statutory criteria, a factor this Court found significant in *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952).

Petitioners' suggestion that a more demanding standard governs delegations touching criminal penalties finds no footing in the constitutional text or in this Court's decisions. *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), which first articulated the nondelegation principle as necessary to preserve "the integrity and maintenance of the system of government ordained by the Constitution," *id.* at 692, imposed no separate, heightened test for statutes with criminal consequences. Congress has long delegated authority whose exercise triggers criminal liability—for example, tariff and customs schedules under review in *Field v. Clark* itself—without offending Article I so long as an intelligible principle constrains the delegate's discretion.

## II. The Attorney General Was Statutorily Authorized to Subdelegate Temporary Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Act, including the temporary scheduling power, when it enacted 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). Where, as here, Congress itself has granted the delegate express authority to redelegate, subdelegation to a subordinate agency is unremarkable. This Court has long recognized that an executive officer entrusted with congressionally delegated authority may, absent a contrary statutory command, assign the exercise of that authority to a subordinate component of the same department. *Cf. Fleming v. Mohawk Wrecking & Lumber Co.*, 331 U.S. 111 (1947) (sustaining redelegation of price-control authority to subordinate administrators pursuant to statutory delegation authority). Nothing in the Controlled Substances Act suggests that Congress meant the Attorney General alone, rather than the DEA he oversees and which possesses the relevant expertise, to exercise the temporary scheduling function. The Attorney General's 1987 regulation assigning that function to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), was therefore a proper and authorized exercise of the redelegation power Congress itself provided.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
