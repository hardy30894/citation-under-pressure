**ARGUMENT**

**I. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of federal authority over navigable waters within the boundaries of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) does not withdraw federal regulatory jurisdiction over navigable waters within conservation system units (CSUs) simply because the submerged lands beneath those waters are state-owned. *See Sturgeon v. Frost*, 829 F.3d 1066, 1073 (9th Cir. 2016). This interpretation is consistent with longstanding Supreme Court precedent recognizing that the federal government retains paramount authority over navigable waters under the Commerce Clause, even where state title to submerged lands exists. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 28 (1935) ("The power of the United States over navigable waters is absolute and paramount."); *Hynes, Regional Director, Fish & Wildlife Service v. Grimes Packing Co.*, 337 U.S. 86, 114 (1949) (federal authority over navigable waters "is not diminished by state ownership of the underlying lands").

The Court has repeatedly affirmed that federal regulatory authority over navigable waters is not displaced by state ownership of the bed or banks. *Collins v. Yosemite Park & Curry Co.*, 304 U.S. 518, 529 (1938) ("The United States may regulate the use of navigable waters within a national park even though the lands under the waters are privately owned."); *Curtin v. Benson*, 222 U.S. 78, 86 (1911) (federal authority over national parks includes regulation of activities on private lands where necessary to protect park resources). This principle applies with equal force in Alaska, where ANILCA expressly preserves federal management of CSUs. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (ANILCA § 103(c)).

**II. The hovercraft ban is a permissible regulation of conduct within a national park preserve, not an impermissible land-use restriction.**

The regulation at issue does not target state-owned land per se; it prohibits the operation of hovercraft on all waters within the preserve, regardless of underlying ownership. *See* 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e). This is a valid exercise of the Secretary’s authority under the National Park Service Organic Act to "regulate the use of Federal areas known as national parks." 16 U.S.C. § 1. The Supreme Court has upheld similar regulations that restrict activities within national parks to protect natural resources, even where those activities occur on non-federal land. *Colorado v. Toll*, 268 U.S. 228, 230–31 (1925) (upholding NPS authority to regulate automobile traffic in Rocky Mountain National Park despite state ownership of some roads).

Moreover, the regulation is consistent with ANILCA’s preservation mandate. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3101(c) (declaring it national policy to preserve the natural integrity of Alaska’s parks). The Court has recognized that the federal government may impose restrictions on activities within national parks to prevent ecological harm, even where those activities are otherwise lawful under state law. *Ward v. Race Horse*, 163 U.S. 504, 516 (1896) ("The power of a State to control and regulate the taking of game cannot be questioned... but this power must yield where it conflicts with the exercise of federal authority under a treaty or statute.").

**III. ANILCA § 103(c) does not divest the federal government of jurisdiction over navigable waters within CSUs.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly rejected Sturgeon’s argument that § 103(c)—which provides that "the management of the public lands in Alaska is vested in the Secretary [of the Interior]"—somehow limits federal authority over navigable waters. *Sturgeon*, 829 F.3d at 1073. The Supreme Court has long held that federal regulatory authority over navigable waters is not diminished by state ownership of submerged lands. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 28; *Hynes*, 337 U.S. at 114. Section 103(c) addresses land management, not water rights or federal regulatory jurisdiction. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c).

The Court has also rejected the notion that state ownership of submerged lands automatically preempts federal regulation. *Organized Village of Kake v. Egan*, 369 U.S. 60, 72 (1962) ("The mere fact that the State has title to the submerged lands does not necessarily preclude federal regulation."). This principle is particularly apt in Alaska, where ANILCA expressly preserves federal management of CSUs. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3101 et seq.

**IV. The NPS’s interpretation of ANILCA and its own regulations is entitled to deference.**

The Ninth Circuit properly deferred to the NPS’s reasonable interpretation of ANILCA and its regulations. *Sturgeon*, 829 F.3d at 1074. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that federal agencies’ interpretations of their own regulations and enabling statutes are entitled to substantial deference. *Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co.*, 325 U.S. 410, 414 (1945); *Udall v. Tallman*, 380 U.S. 1, 16 (1965). This deference is particularly appropriate where, as here, the regulation concerns the conservation of natural resources within a national park. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 1 (NPS Organic Act).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of federal authority over navigable waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, and ANILCA § 103(c) does not divest the federal government of jurisdiction over those waters.
