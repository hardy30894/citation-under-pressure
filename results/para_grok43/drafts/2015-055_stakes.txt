**ARGUMENT**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively on collateral review. Under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), “new substantive rules generally apply retroactively.” *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351 (2004). A rule is substantive when it “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Id.* at 353.

*Johnson* held that the ACCA residual clause, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), “denies fair notice to defendants and invites arbitrary enforcement by judges” and is therefore void. 576 U.S. at 597. That holding “narrow[ed] the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms,” *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620 (1998), and thereby “place[d] certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal-law-making authority to proscribe,” *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311 (plurality opinion) (internal quotation marks omitted). After *Johnson*, a defendant whose prior conviction qualified as a violent felony only under the residual clause is no longer subject to the ACCA’s fifteen-year mandatory minimum. The decision therefore “prohibit[s] a certain category of punishment for a class of defendants because of their status or offense.” *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 330 (1989).

Because *Johnson* is substantive, it applies retroactively without regard to whether the conviction became final before or after the decision. *See Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190, 198 (2016) (“Substantive rules … are not subject to the Teague bar.”). Petitioner’s 1996 Florida robbery conviction was counted solely under the residual clause; *Johnson* therefore invalidates his ACCA sentence.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
