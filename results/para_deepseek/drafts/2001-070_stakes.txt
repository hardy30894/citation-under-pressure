**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition must draw its meaning from “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). Those standards are not measured by judicial preference, but by objective evidence, principally state legislation and jury sentencing practices. *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 370 (1989).

**I. There is no national consensus against executing mentally retarded offenders.**

This Court considered the identical question in *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), and held that “the execution of mentally retarded criminals is categorically prohibited by the Eighth Amendment” is not the law. *Id.* at 340. At that time, only two States had enacted categorical bans. *Id.* at 334. The Court therefore declined to impose a categorical constitutional rule.

The objective evidence has not changed sufficiently to warrant overruling *Penry*. Many States continue to permit capital punishment for mentally retarded offenders, and juries in those States continue to impose such sentences. Where the “clearest and most reliable objective evidence of contemporary values is the legislation enacted by the country’s legislatures,” *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 331, Atkins cannot point to the requisite national consensus. The existence of a legislative debate is not itself a constitutional command.

**II. Mental retardation is an individualized mitigating circumstance, not a categorical exemption.**

The Eighth Amendment requires “consideration of the character and record of the individual offender and the circumstances of the particular offense as a constitutionally indispensable part of the process of inflicting the penalty of death.” *Woodson v. North Carolina*, 428 U.S. 280, 304 (1976) (plurality opinion). That requirement is satisfied when the sentencer is free to consider mental retardation as a mitigating factor. *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978) (plurality opinion); *Eddings v. Oklahoma*, 455 U.S. 104, 114 (1982).

Atkins, however, asks this Court to replace that individualized inquiry with a conclusive presumption that no mentally retarded person can ever be sufficiently culpable to deserve death. That proposition is irreconcilable with *Woodson* and its progeny. Mental retardation varies in severity and in its effect on a particular defendant’s moral culpability. The sentencer is best positioned to weigh the evidence of retardation against the circumstances of the offense. A categorical bar would preclude the very “particularized assessment” that the Eighth Amendment demands. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 319.

This Court has recognized a categorical exemption only for those who are unaware of the punishment and its purpose, *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 409-10 (1986). Mental retardation does not, by itself, place a defendant in that category.

**III. The jury’s verdict in this case is constitutionally adequate.**

Atkins presented expert testimony regarding his intellectual functioning, and the jury heard and considered that evidence. The jury was not prevented from giving effect to any mitigating evidence. Its death sentence reflects a judgment that, notwithstanding Atkins’s asserted limitations, his culpability was sufficient. The Eighth Amendment does not require more. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
