ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in affirming Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) because the trial court's jury instruction on "true threats" did not require proof of Elonis' subjective intent to threaten. The First Amendment protects speech unless it falls within a narrow exception, such as "true threats." To determine whether a statement is a "true threat," the court must consider whether the speaker subjectively intended to threaten.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects speech, even if it is unpopular or disturbing. In Terminiello v. City of Chicago, 337 U.S. 1 (1949), the Court held that speech that "invites dispute" or "induces a condition of unrest" is still protected under the First Amendment. Similarly, in Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), the Court emphasized that the First Amendment protects speech that may be "offensive" or "provocative." These cases demonstrate that the First Amendment's protection of speech is not limited to speech that is inoffensive or uncontroversial.

In Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), the Court considered a statute that prohibited "knowingly and willfully" making a threat to harm the President. The Court held that the statute required proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten, emphasizing that "a statute such as this one, which makes criminal a form of pure speech, must be interpreted with the commands of the First Amendment clearly in mind." Id. at 707. This reasoning is applicable to § 875(c), which also criminalizes a form of pure speech.

The requirement of subjective intent is also supported by the Court's decision in Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494 (1951), where the Court emphasized the importance of considering the speaker's intent when evaluating the constitutionality of a statute regulating speech. In Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242 (1937), the Court held that a statute that prohibited "inciting insurrection" required proof of the defendant's subjective intent to incite violence.

Furthermore, in Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359 (1931), the Court struck down a statute that prohibited the display of a red flag as a symbol of opposition to organized government, emphasizing that the statute was overly broad and failed to distinguish between speech that was intended to incite violence and speech that was not. Similarly, in Fiske v. Kansas, 274 U.S. 380 (1927), the Court held that a statute that prohibited "criminal syndicalism" required proof of the defendant's subjective intent to advocate violence.

The Third Circuit's decision to the contrary relies on an objective standard, which is inconsistent with the First Amendment's protection of speech. As the Court held in De Jonge v. Oregon, 299 U.S. 353 (1937), "the right of peaceable assembly is a right cognate to those of free speech and free press, and is equally fundamental." Id. at 364. An objective standard would criminalize speech that is not intended to threaten, but is nonetheless perceived as threatening by the listener.

In Gitlow v. New York, 268 U.S. 652 (1925), the Court held that a statute that prohibited "advocating" the overthrow of the government required proof of the defendant's subjective intent to advocate violence. The Court emphasized that the statute must be construed narrowly to avoid infringing on the defendant's First Amendment rights. Similarly, § 875(c) must be construed to require proof of Elonis' subjective intent to threaten.

Requiring proof of subjective intent to threaten is essential to protecting the First Amendment rights of individuals like Elonis, who engage in speech that may be perceived as threatening, but is not intended to be taken literally. The Third Circuit's decision to the contrary undermines the First Amendment's protection of speech and should be reversed.
