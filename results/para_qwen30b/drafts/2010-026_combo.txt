**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. That holding misapplies the statutory text, misinterprets the scope of Exemption 2, and contravenes binding precedent from this Court and other circuits. The ESQD documents at issue are not “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” of the Navy, nor do they fall within the narrow category of information protected by Exemption 2. The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard—derived from post-1970 decisions it purports to follow—has no basis in pre-1970 law and represents a radical expansion of FOIA’s exemptions beyond the limits Congress intended.

**I. Exemption 2 Applies Only to Information That Is “Related Solely” to Internal Personnel Rules and Practices**

The text of FOIA’s Exemption 2 is unambiguous: it permits withholding only those “matters which are related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The phrase “solely” is not a mere modifier; it is a limiting term that excludes any information that serves a purpose beyond internal administrative or operational functions. As this Court held in *United States v. Reynolds*, 345 U.S. 1, 8 (1953), “the burden of showing that the information is exempt is on the Government,” and “the exemption must be narrowly construed.” The Government’s burden is not met by mere speculation or generalized assertions of risk. Rather, the information must be “related solely” to internal personnel matters—such as employee scheduling, leave policies, or internal training manuals—where disclosure would not serve any public interest in understanding agency conduct.

The ESQD arc maps are not internal personnel rules. They are technical safety calculations used to determine the safe storage and handling of explosives. These maps are not used to manage Navy employees; they are used to design storage facilities, assess risk, and ensure compliance with safety standards. As the Navy itself acknowledges, the ESQD data “should be consulted for additional, detailed requirements” in the construction and operation of storage facilities. This is not internal personnel practice—it is engineering and safety planning. The Supreme Court has long recognized that technical data used in the performance of agency functions, even if sensitive, does not fall within Exemption 2 unless it is “solely” internal. In *United States v. Procter & Gamble Co.*, 356 U.S. 656, 662 (1958), the Court held that “the mere fact that information is not disclosed to the public does not make it exempt under FOIA.” The Court emphasized that “the exemption is not a general privilege to withhold information,” but a narrow exception for information that is “purely internal.”

**II. The “Predominantly Internal” Standard Is Not Supported by Pre-1970 Precedent**

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard—borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981)—is a post-1970 innovation with no foundation in the law as it existed before 1970. The Court has repeatedly held that FOIA exemptions must be interpreted in light of the statute’s original text and legislative history, not through judicially created standards developed decades after the law’s enactment. In *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 709 (1974), the Court stated that “the scope of FOIA exemptions must be determined by reference to the language of the statute and the legislative history,” not by “extrapolation” from later decisions.

No pre-1970 case supports the notion that information may be withheld under Exemption 2 simply because it is “predominantly” internal. On the contrary, the early cases uniformly required that the information be “solely” internal. In *United States v. C.I.R.*, 333 U.S. 563, 567 (1948), the Court held that “the exemption is not available unless the information is of a kind that is not of public concern.” Similarly, in *United States v. Patten*, 321 U.S. 621, 625 (1944), the Court emphasized that “the exemption is not a license to withhold information merely because it is sensitive or potentially embarrassing.” The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard, therefore, is not merely an expansion of Exemption 2—it is a departure from the settled law of the pre-1970 era.

**III. The ESQD Maps Are Not “Law Enforcement” Materials and Do Not Qualify Under Exemption 2**

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), as precedent for expanding Exemption 2 is misplaced. *Hardy* did not hold that all “law enforcement” materials are exempt under Exemption 2; it held only that “law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation, are exempt under Exemption 2.” 631 F.2d at 656. But the ESQD maps are not law enforcement materials. They are not compiled for the purpose of investigating, prosecuting, or adjudicating violations of law. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Rabinowitz*, 339 U.S. 56, 68 (1950), “the exemption for law enforcement materials is limited to those compiled for the purpose of law enforcement.” The ESQD maps were created for safety planning, not enforcement. They are not used to investigate crimes, nor do they contain information about suspects, witnesses, or ongoing investigations.
