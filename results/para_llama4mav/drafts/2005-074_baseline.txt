The reinstatement statute, Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date in 1997. Even if it does, its application to such aliens is impermissibly retroactive.

First, the plain language and legislative history of the INA do not indicate that Congress intended the reinstatement statute to apply retroactively to pre-enactment re-entries. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), the Court established a two-part test for determining whether a statute applies retroactively. The first step is to determine whether Congress has clearly expressed its intent regarding the statute's temporal reach. Id. at 280. If it has, that intent controls. Here, the statute is silent on its applicability to pre-enactment re-entries, and the legislative history does not provide clear guidance.

The Court has held that "a statute has retroactive effect when it 'takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability, in respect to transactions or considerations already past.'" Id. at 269 (quoting Society for Propagation of the Faith v. Town of New Haven, 41 U.S. 210, 223 (1842)). Applying the reinstatement statute to pre-enactment re-entries would attach a new disability to Fernandez-Vargas' past actions, namely, his re-entry into the United States in 1982.

Furthermore, even if the statute were deemed to apply to pre-enactment re-entries, its application would be impermissibly retroactive. The Court has held that a statute's retroactive application is impermissible if it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." Id. at 270. In this case, Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States in 1982 and lived here for nearly two decades, forming connections and building a life. Applying the reinstatement statute to him would deprive him of the opportunity to adjust his status, a consequence that is directly related to his pre-enactment re-entry.

The Tenth Circuit's holding that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" is not supported by the case law. The Court has recognized that an alien's settled expectations can be a relevant factor in determining whether a statute's application is retroactive. See INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 321-24 (2001). Fernandez-Vargas had lived in the United States for nearly two decades and had taken steps to adjust his status, demonstrating a settled expectation that he would be able to do so.

In conclusion, the reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date, and even if it did, its application would be impermissibly retroactive. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
