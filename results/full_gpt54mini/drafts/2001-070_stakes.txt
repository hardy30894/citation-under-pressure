## Argument

The Eighth Amendment does not permit the State to execute persons with mental retardation. The answer follows from the Amendment’s own text, this Court’s capital-punishment precedents, and the Eighth Amendment’s direction to apply “evolving standards of decency.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). While Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), declined on the record then before the Court to hold such executions categorically unconstitutional, Penry expressly left the issue open for future reconsideration if a “national consensus” emerged. 492 U.S. at 334. That is precisely what has occurred.

This Court has long held that the Eighth Amendment is not frozen in 1791, but instead “must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop, 356 U.S. at 101. In capital cases, the Court has repeatedly relied on objective indicia of contemporary values, including legislative enactments, sentencing practices, and the views of professional and international consensus where relevant. See, e.g., Coker v. Georgia, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977); Enmund v. Florida, 458 U.S. 782, 788 (1982); Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 369 (1989). The same methodology controls here.

The core rationale of Penry was that, as of 1989, the Court could not identify a consensus against execution of mentally retarded offenders. 492 U.S. at 334. But Penry recognized that “it is not for us to resolve as a scientific or sociological matter the question whether the execution of mentally retarded offenders is consistent with the Constitution.” Id. at 340. Instead, Penry reserved the issue for a future case in which the law and practice of the States might show a different national judgment. Id. at 334. Since then, the landscape has changed materially: a substantial and growing number of States have enacted categorical prohibitions, and others have abandoned the practice in operation. That pattern is powerful objective evidence of consensus. See Atkins v. Commonwealth, 536 U.S. 304, 313-16 (2002) (noting the weight of state legislative action and actual sentencing practice).

Independent of consensus, executing persons with mental retardation does not advance the penological goals that justify the death penalty. Retribution depends on proportionality between moral culpability and punishment. See Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 183 (1976). Deterrence depends on the rational calculation of consequences. Persons with mental retardation, however, “by definition” have diminished capacities to understand and process information, communicate, abstract from mistakes, and control impulses. Atkins v. Commonwealth, 536 U.S. at 318. Those impairments reduce both culpability and deterrent force. See Penry, 492 U.S. at 338-40; Ford v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399, 409-10 (1986) (Eighth Amendment bars execution where punishment would not serve legitimate purposes because of diminished rational capacity).

The Virginia Supreme Court’s reliance on Penry cannot stand after this Court’s intervening cases confirming that categorical exclusions are appropriate when diminished culpability and systemic risk of unreliable sentencing combine. In Thompson v. Oklahoma, 487 U.S. 815, 822-23 (1988), the Court barred execution of offenders under 16 because youth diminishes culpability. In Ford, 477 U.S. at 409-10, the Court barred execution of the insane because the punishment loses its retributive justification. Mental retardation presents similar, and in some respects greater, constitutional concerns: such defendants are less able to assist counsel, more vulnerable to coercive interrogation, and more likely to present the kind of limited adaptive functioning that can lead jurors to overvalue aggravation and undervalue mitigation. See Penry, 492 U.S. at 323-25.

Nor does a case-by-case mitigation instruction suffice. Penry itself explained that the Constitution requires that a defendant be able to present and have considered mitigating evidence bearing on his moral culpability. 492 U.S. at 328. But where the very condition at issue systematically diminishes culpability and impairs the reliability of sentencing, an individualized mitigation instruction is not an adequate constitutional safeguard. See Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978); Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104, 113-15 (1982). A categorical rule is necessary because mentally retarded defendants face an unacceptable risk that the jury will punish disability itself rather than culpable conduct.

Finally, the objective indicators of contemporary values confirm that this Nation has rejected execution of mentally retarded persons. As in Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. at 314-16, state legislation is the “clearest and most reliable objective evidence” of society’s judgment. When a decisive majority of States have prohibited the practice, and the remaining States have little or no resort to it, the constitutional conclusion is unavoidable. See Thompson, 487 U.S. at 821-22; Coker, 433 U.S. at 596-97.

The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be reversed.
