The Due Process Clause does not require a separate, heightened “reasoned choice” competency standard for a defendant who pleads guilty or waives counsel. The Constitution requires only that the defendant be competent under the basic standard articulated in Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), as applied through the procedural safeguards of a competency inquiry when the trial court has reason to doubt competence. The Ninth Circuit erred by constitutionalizing a stricter standard that this Court has never adopted.

This Court has consistently defined trial competence in functional terms: whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and has “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). That standard governs competence to stand trial, and nothing in this Court’s decisions creates a higher constitutional threshold for pleading guilty or waiving counsel. In fact, Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993), rejects the Ninth Circuit’s premise that competence to plead guilty or waive counsel is distinct from competence to stand trial. The Court held that “the competence that is required of a defendant seeking to plead guilty is the competence to stand trial,” and that the Constitution does not require a “higher standard” of competence for such waivers. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389, 399, 401 (1993). The same is true for waiver of counsel. Id. at 400-01.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” formulation cannot be squared with this Court’s precedents. In Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), and Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), this Court recognized that a waiver of counsel must be knowing and intelligent, but those cases did not create a separate competency standard. Godinez made that explicit, explaining that the competency inquiry and the waiver inquiry are distinct: competency asks whether the defendant has the capacity to make the decision; waiver asks whether the decision was made knowingly and voluntarily. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389, 400-01 (1993). A court may not “import the competency standard into the inquiry into whether the waiver was knowing and voluntary.” Id. at 401.

That distinction is dispositive here. The Nevada trial court had already found Moran competent to stand trial after a psychiatric examination. Once that competence was established, due process did not require a further constitutional inquiry into whether Moran possessed a heightened level of decisionmaking ability. The trial court still had to ensure that Moran’s guilty pleas and waiver of counsel were knowing, voluntary, and intelligent under Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), and Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975). But the Federal Constitution does not convert the validity of the waiver into a separate “reasoned choice” competence test.

The Ninth Circuit relied on its own earlier cases, including Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), Evans v. Raines, 800 F.2d 884 (9th Cir. 1986), and Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853 (9th Cir. 1987). But those decisions cannot control where they conflict with this Court’s later clarification in Godinez. Indeed, Godinez expressly rejected the notion that the Constitution requires a more demanding competence standard for pleading guilty or waiving counsel than for standing trial. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389, 399-400 (1993).

Nor does the record here support the Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that Moran’s plea colloquy was constitutionally invalid for lack of a heightened competency finding. The Due Process Clause requires a trial court to conduct a competency hearing when the evidence raises a bona fide doubt about competence. Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 180 (1975). But the record showed Moran had been examined and found competent to stand trial, and the state court later relied on that evidence in rejecting his postconviction claim. The question is not whether Moran made an ideal decision, but whether the Constitution required the trial court to apply a special competence standard. It did not.

This Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and hold that the Due Process Clause requires no heightened “reasoned choice” standard for competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. The governing constitutional rule remains the Dusky standard, together with the separate requirements that any waiver be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993); Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam); Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969); Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975).
