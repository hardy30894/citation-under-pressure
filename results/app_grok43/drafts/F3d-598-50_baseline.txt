ARGUMENT

I. The District Court’s Erroneous Admission of Testimony from Two Assistant District Attorneys and a Police Lieutenant Requires a New Trial.

The District Court permitted two ADAs and a lieutenant to opine on Ramos’s and Rivera’s credibility, to state that probable cause existed for the arrests and prosecution, and to assess whether particular evidence strengthened or weakened Appellants’ case. This testimony violated three settled evidentiary rules.

First, witnesses may not vouch for the credibility of other witnesses. See Nimely v. City of New York, 414 F.3d 381, 398 (2d Cir. 2005) (“[E]xpert witnesses may not … bolster the credibility of other witnesses.”). Second, witnesses may not testify in the form of legal conclusions. See Hygh v. Jacobs, 961 F.2d 359, 363 (2d Cir. 1992) (“[E]xpert witnesses may not … offer opinions as to relevant legal standards.”). Third, lay or expert witnesses may not interpret evidence that jurors are equally capable of assessing themselves. See United States v. Scop, 846 F.2d 135, 142 (2d Cir. 1988) (testimony that “track[s] the language of the applicable statute” and “state[s] a legal conclusion” is inadmissible).

These errors were not harmless. The testimony spoke directly to the two issues the jury had to decide: the officers’ credibility and the existence of probable cause. See Hynes v. Coughlin, 79 F.3d 285, 291 (2d Cir. 1996) (reversal required where erroneously admitted evidence “bore on the central issue in the case”). Because the verdict “cannot be said to have been unaffected” by the improper testimony, a new trial is required. Id.

II. The Remaining Issues Warrant Instructions on Remand.

The photographs do not “blatantly contradict[]” Appellees’ account, Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 380 (2007), and therefore do not entitle Appellants to judgment as a matter of law. The District Court should, however, instruct the jury that, under New York law, obstructing governmental administration requires interference with a lawful official function, see People v. Smallwood, 187 Misc. 2d 280, 282 (N.Y. Crim. Ct. 2001), and should submit the question of punitive damages if the evidence permits an inference of malice, see Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983).
