**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court’s Prisoner-Release Order Fully Comports with the Prison Litigation Reform Act.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”) does not bar a remedial order that is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct a constitutional violation, and gives appropriate weight to public safety. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order satisfies every requirement of the Act.

**A. The Order Is the Least Intrusive Means of Remedying an Ongoing Constitutional Violation.**

Where prison conditions inflict needless suffering and death, a court must order relief that is “necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right.” *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ.*, 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971). Here, the district court found that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of systemic Eighth Amendment violations resulting in the death of one prisoner every six to seven days. That finding is entitled to substantial deference. *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409, 414 (1941). The population cap, set at 137.5% of design capacity, is not a fixed ceiling but a proportional remedy tailored to the magnitude of the violation. This Court has long recognized that “[t]he scope of a district court’s equitable powers to remedy past wrongs is broad.” *Swann*, 402 U.S. at 15.

If California could have remedied the violations through less restrictive means, it was obligated to propose them. The State did not. Having failed to offer an alternative that would cure the constitutional infirmity, the State cannot now complain that the court’s remedy is too broad. *See Brown v. Bd. of Educ.*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955) (requiring “practical flexibility” in shaping equitable remedies).

**B. The Order Is Narrowly Drawn and Extends No Further Than Necessary.**

The PLRA demands that relief be “narrowly drawn” and the “least intrusive means” to correct the violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order satisfies this standard because it permits the State to choose which prisoners to release, subject only to a population ceiling. This approach affords maximum discretion to prison administrators, as the order does not dictate specific release decisions. *Cf. Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978) (upholding order limiting punitive isolation to 30 days where it “left the other aspects of the conditions of confinement to the discretion of prison administrators”). The court’s order is thus far less intrusive than the detailed injunction upheld in *Finney*.

**C. The Order Gives Appropriate Weight to Public Safety.**

The PLRA requires that prospective relief “give appropriate weight to public safety.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court expressly considered public safety, requiring that the population reduction be implemented in a manner that does not endanger the public. It ordered California to accelerate the release of prisoners near the end of their sentences and those with low-risk profiles—the same categories that states traditionally release through parole and early release programs. *Cf. Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 249 (1949) (recognizing state’s broad discretion over parole and release decisions).

Moreover, the court’s order does not require the release of violent or dangerous inmates. The population cap is an outer limit, and the State retains full authority to prioritize the retention of high-risk prisoners. Where the alternative is a continued constitutional violation resulting in avoidable deaths, the PLRA does not require a court to accept the risk of continued harm as a condition of protecting speculative public safety concerns.

**II. The Three-Judge Court Properly Found That No Other Remedy Would Suffice.**

Before ordering a population cap, the PLRA requires a showing that “no other relief will remedy the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The court below made this finding after an exhaustive trial. California had been under court supervision for years, during which it admitted it could not comply with earlier remedial orders. The court appointed a receiver to oversee medical care, and still the State failed to provide constitutionally adequate care. This history demonstrates that “the remedial powers of equity” were exhausted. *Swann*, 402 U.S. at 15.

Where a State has “persistently and flagrantly” violated a federal right, the court may impose a remedy that directly addresses the root cause of the violation. *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. at 687. Overcrowding was that root cause. The district court considered and rejected alternatives such as construction of new facilities because they would take years to implement and would not alleviate the immediate crisis. A court need not “engage in a futile gesture” by ordering relief that is plainly inadequate. *Griffin v. County School Bd.*, 377 U.S. 218, 234 (1964).

**III. The PLRA Does Not Curtail a Court’s Inherent Equitable Power to Remedy Constitutional Violations.**

The PLRA’s limitations operate within the framework of a court’s inherent equitable authority. This Court has long held that “where a constitutional violation has been found, a court’s remedial power is to be exercised on the basis of what will achieve ‘effective relief.’” *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. at 686 n.9. The PLRA does not strip courts of that power; it merely requires that relief be narrowly tailored. That standard is satisfied here.

A contrary reading would suggest that Congress intended to leave prisoners without judicial recourse for ongoing constitutional violations, a result that would itself raise serious constitutional questions. This Court has repeatedly affirmed that the “power of the federal courts to enforce federal constitutional rights” is not lightly curtailed. *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 165 (1908). Where a State’s failure to act results in “the needless infliction of pain and suffering,” the courts must act. *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 103 (1976).

**CONCLUSION**

The three-judge court’s order complies fully with the PLRA. It is the least intrusive means of correcting a constitutional violation of the first magnitude. The order is narrow, proportionate, and protective of public safety. The judgment below should be affirmed.
