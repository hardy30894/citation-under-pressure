**ARGUMENT**

Respondent Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on the ground that the statute exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers and violates the Tenth Amendment. The Tenth Amendment “is not a source of any substantive rights for individuals”; rather, it is “a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered.” *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 156-57 (1992) (internal quotation marks omitted). The Amendment reserves powers to the States, but it does not confer a personal right that a private party may invoke to invalidate a federal conviction.

The Supreme Court squarely held in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. TVA* that private parties lack standing to raise a Tenth Amendment claim. 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939). There, utility companies argued that federal electricity sales violated the Tenth Amendment. The Court rejected that contention, ruling that “absent the states or their officers, [private parties] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Id.* at 144. That holding—that only a State may assert its own sovereign interests under the Tenth Amendment—remains binding. The decision was not dictum; the Court “dismissed the Tenth Amendment claim after analyzing both the standing issue and the merits,” making the standing holding an alternative ground. *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (citing *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978)).

Consistent with *Tennessee Electric*, every court of appeals to address the question except two has held that private parties lack standing to assert Tenth Amendment challenges. *See United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 33-36; *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004). The Third Circuit correctly followed this majority approach.

Bond argues that as a criminal defendant she has a personal stake in challenging the statute under which she is convicted. But the Tenth Amendment claim she presses does not involve any personal right. It is a structural federalism objection that belongs to the States, not to individuals. Bond’s conviction injures no sovereign interest of Pennsylvania; the Commonwealth has not objected to the federal prosecution. As the First Circuit explained, “the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state’s policy of acquiescence.” *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35. Bond cannot circumvent that principle by asserting the State’s rights as her own.

Bond’s reliance on *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693 (7th Cir. 1999), and *Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. U.S. Dep’t of Energy*, 666 F.2d 1359 (11th Cir. 1982), is misplaced. Those outlier decisions are inconsistent with *Tennessee Electric* and have been undermined by subsequent authority in their own circuits. *See, e.g., Dillard v. Chilton County Comm’n*, 495 F.3d 1324, 1335 (11th Cir. 2007) (denying private party standing under Tenth Amendment). Moreover, even if Bond could show an individual interest, the Tenth Amendment does not create a right to be free from federal prosecution for conduct that may also be subject to state law. The Constitution “leaves to the States the primary responsibility for the administration of the criminal law,” *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945), but that principle does not give a defendant standing to object when the federal government validly enforces a statute within its delegated powers. The question here is not the scope of federal authority under the Treaty Power; it is whether Bond is the proper party to raise that question. She is not.

Because standing is a threshold jurisdictional requirement, *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101-02 (1998), this Court cannot reach the merits of Bond’s Tenth Amendment claim. The court of appeals correctly held that Bond lacks standing, and its judgment should be affirmed.
