**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT ESQD ARC MAPS ARE “PREDOMINANTLY INTERNAL” AND RISK CIRCUMVENTION OF AGENCY REGULATIONS UNDER FOIA EXEMPTION 2.

A. Exemption 2’s Text and Supreme Court Precedent Limit Its Scope to “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices” That Risk Circumvention of Agency Regulation.

FOIA Exemption 2 exempts from disclosure “matters that are related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has emphasized that FOIA exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be “narrowly construed.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360–61 (1976) (quoting S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 3 (1965)). Exemption 2 applies only to “internal personnel rules and practices,” not to all internal documents. *Id.* at 369.

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test contravenes this narrow construction. The Supreme Court in *Rose* expressly rejected a broad reading of Exemption 2, noting that the exemption applies only to “personnel rules or practices” and not to all internal documents. *Id.* at 369–70. The Court cautioned that FOIA’s exemptions must be “given a fair reading” and that agencies cannot rely on “vague, if not wholly illusory, claims of harm” to withhold documents. *Id.* at 363.

B. The ESQD Arc Maps Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices.”

The ESQD arc maps are not “internal personnel rules and practices” under *Rose*. They are operational safety tools used to design and arrange ammunition storage facilities to minimize risk. Commander Whitbred’s declaration confirms that the maps are used to “design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” These are not personnel rules or practices; they are operational safety measures.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. *Hardy* involved a law enforcement manual detailing how to execute search warrants—clearly an internal personnel practice. *Id.* at 655. The ESQD arc maps, by contrast, are not personnel rules or practices; they are technical safety data used to protect the public and agency personnel from explosions.

C. The ESQD Arc Maps Do Not Risk Circumvention of Agency Regulation.

Even if the ESQD arc maps were “internal,” they do not risk circumvention of agency regulation. The Supreme Court in *Rose* held that circumvention risk requires a showing that disclosure would “frustrate enforcement efforts or circumvent agency regulation.” *Id.* at 379. The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test dilutes this requirement, allowing agencies to withhold documents merely because they are internal, regardless of whether disclosure would circumvent regulation.

Here, disclosure of the ESQD arc maps would not circumvent agency regulation. The maps are used to design storage facilities and organize operations—disclosure would not enable anyone to bypass safety regulations. The Navy’s concern that the maps could be used to plan an attack is speculative and insufficient to justify withholding under Exemption 2. *See Maricopa Audubon Soc. v. U.S. Forest Serv.*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085 (9th Cir. 1997) (rejecting agency’s speculative claims of harm).

II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE ESQD ARC MAPS ARE EXEMPT UNDER FOIA EXEMPTION 7(F).

A. Exemption 7(F) Requires That the Documents Be “Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes.”

Exemption 7(F) exempts documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(F). The Supreme Court has held that Exemption 7(F) applies only to documents compiled for law enforcement purposes. *United States Dep’t of Justice v. Reporters Comm. for Freedom of the Press*, 489 U.S. 749, 773 (1989).

The ESQD arc maps were not compiled for law enforcement purposes. Commander Whitbred’s declaration states they are used for “design, array, and construct[ion]” of storage facilities and “organize[ation of] ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” These are safety and operational purposes, not law enforcement. *See Church of Scientology v. U.S. Dep’t of Defense*, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (D.C. Cir. 1979) (Exemption 7(F) applies only to “adjudicative or enforcement purpose[s]”).

B. The Navy’s Claim That Disclosure Could Endanger Life or Safety Is Speculative and Insufficient.

The Navy’s claim that disclosure could endanger life or safety is speculative. The Ninth Circuit has held that agencies must provide specific evidence of harm, not generalized assertions. *Lahr v. National Transp. Safety Bd.*, 569 F.3d 964, 973 (9th Cir. 2009). The Navy has not shown that disclosure of the ESQD arc maps would endanger anyone. The maps are already publicly available for other bases, and no harm has resulted.

III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION DISTORTS CONGRESS’S BALANCE IN FAVOR OF OVERBROAD EXEMPTIONS.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision expands Exemption 2 beyond its statutory limits, undermining FOIA’s “careful balance between the interests of the public in greater access to information and the needs of the Government to protect certain kinds of information from disclosure.” *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 157, 157 (1989). The Supreme Court has cautioned that FOIA exemptions must be “narrowly construed” and “explicitly exclusive.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361; *Tax Analysts v. Internal Revenue Serv.*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision allows the Navy to withhold operational safety data under Exemption 2, despite the Supreme Court’s clear instruction that Exemption 2 applies only to “internal personnel rules and practices.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. This misreading of Exemption 2 distorts FOIA’s balance and should be reversed.
