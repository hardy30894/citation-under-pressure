The issue presented in this case is whether a District Court's failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal, as required by the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, provides ground for habeas relief when the defendant was aware of his right and elected not to appeal. The respondent respectfully submits that it does not.

The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, specifically Rule 32(a)(2), mandate that the court advise a defendant of his right to appeal after sentencing. However, the failure to comply with this rule does not automatically entitle a defendant to habeas relief. As the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit correctly held, a Rule 32 violation is subject to harmless-error review, a principle supported by precedent. (See United States v. Daddato, 160 F.2d 695, 697 (7th Cir. 1947); United States v. Paglia, 190 F.2d 445, 448 (2d Cir. 1951)).

The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that a defendant is aware of his right to appeal. (See United States v. Bazzano, 146 F.2d 923, 925 (3d Cir. 1945)). When a defendant is already aware of this right, as Peguero was, the rule's purpose is served. The District Court's finding that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing is consistent with this principle. (See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), where the Court emphasized the importance of a defendant's knowing and intelligent waiver of his rights).

The harmless-error doctrine, as established in Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946), holds that an error is harmless if it does not affect the outcome of the case. In this instance, Peguero's awareness of his right to appeal and his decision not to exercise it render the District Court's failure to advise him harmless. The Supreme Court has long recognized that not all procedural errors require reversal. (See Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 116-17 (1934); Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 83 (1942)).

Furthermore, the Court has emphasized the importance of showing prejudice resulting from an error to be entitled to relief. (See Glasser, 315 U.S. at 83). Peguero has not demonstrated any prejudice resulting from the District Court's failure to advise him of his right to appeal. The absence of prejudice, combined with Peguero's awareness of his right to appeal, supports the conclusion that the District Court's error was harmless.

In Coppedge v. United States, 369 U.S. 438, 442 (1962), the Court noted that the right to appeal is "fundamental" and that a defendant's knowledge of this right is crucial. Here, Peguero's knowledge of his right to appeal, despite the District Court's failure to advise him, satisfies this fundamental requirement.

In conclusion, the respondent submits that the District Court's failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal does not provide ground for habeas relief when Peguero was aware of his right and elected not to appeal. The Court of Appeals' decision affirming the District Court's denial of habeas relief should be upheld, as it is consistent with the harmless-error doctrine and the principle that a defendant's knowledge of his rights can cure a procedural error.
