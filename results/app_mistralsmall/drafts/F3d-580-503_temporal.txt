**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Denying Appellant’s Motion for a Fingerprint Expert and a Continuance, Violating His Sixth Amendment Right to a Fair Trial and Due Process of Law.**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a criminal defendant the right to present evidence and to confront the witnesses against him. *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 22 (1967). This right includes the opportunity to secure and present evidence in his defense, *id.*, and to have a reasonable opportunity to prepare for trial. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 57 (1932). The denial of a continuance or the refusal to allow the presentation of evidence may violate these rights if the denial is arbitrary or unreasonable. *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964).

Here, the district court denied Appellant’s motion for a fingerprint expert and a continuance just five days before trial, despite the fact that the government had already disclosed that its expert would testify that no latent fingerprints of value were found on the evidence. The court’s denial was arbitrary and unreasonable, as it deprived Appellant of the opportunity to challenge the government’s fingerprint evidence—a critical issue in the case. The court’s reasoning—that the motion was “woefully late”—does not justify the denial of Appellant’s constitutional rights. *See Ungar*, 376 U.S. at 589 (holding that a continuance may be denied only for “weighty reasons” and that the denial must not impair the defendant’s right to a fair trial).

Moreover, the denial of the fingerprint expert violated Appellant’s right to due process, as it prevented him from presenting a meaningful defense. *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 57. The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of a fair trial includes the right to present evidence that may impeach the government’s case. *Washington*, 388 U.S. at 22. By refusing to allow Appellant to examine the evidence for fingerprints or to present expert testimony on the issue, the court denied him the opportunity to challenge a key piece of evidence against him.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Support Appellant’s Conviction for Possession of a Firearm in Furtherance of a Drug Trafficking Crime Under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c).**

To convict a defendant under § 924(c), the government must prove that the defendant “willfully” possessed a firearm “in furtherance of” a drug trafficking crime. *United States v. Feliz-Cordero*, 356 F.2d 88, 90 (2d Cir. 1966). The government must show a nexus between the firearm and the drug trafficking crime, such as evidence that the firearm was used to protect drugs or drug proceeds, or to facilitate the drug transaction. *Id.* at 91.

Here, the government’s evidence was insufficient to establish that Appellant possessed the firearm in furtherance of a drug trafficking crime. The firearm was recovered from Appellant’s jacket, which was found near the scene of his arrest, but there was no evidence that the firearm was used in connection with the drug transaction or that it was possessed for the purpose of facilitating drug trafficking. The government’s theory—that the firearm was possessed in furtherance of drug trafficking because it was found near drugs—is insufficient under *Feliz-Cordero*. The government must show more than mere proximity; it must demonstrate a connection between the firearm and the drug trafficking crime. *Id.* at 91.

The government’s evidence was also insufficient to prove that Appellant “willfully” possessed the firearm in furtherance of a drug trafficking crime. The evidence showed that Appellant fled from police and discarded the firearm, but there was no evidence that he possessed the firearm for the purpose of facilitating drug trafficking. The government’s theory—that Appellant possessed the firearm because he was a drug dealer—is insufficient to establish the requisite intent under § 924(c). *See United States v. Garmia*, 344 F.2d 725, 727 (2d Cir. 1965) (holding that mere possession of a firearm by a drug dealer is not enough to establish a violation of § 924(c)).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant’s conviction and sentence should be reversed. The district court’s denial of Appellant’s motion for a fingerprint expert and a continuance violated his Sixth Amendment rights, and the evidence was insufficient to support his conviction under § 924(c).
