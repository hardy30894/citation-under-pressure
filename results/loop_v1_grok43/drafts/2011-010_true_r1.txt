ARGUMENT

The text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage only when injury occurs as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf. That language imposes a geographic limitation on the place of injury. It is a well settled principle that the words of a statute if they be plain and unambiguous must be taken in their ordinary and usual sense. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 413 (1819). The words of the statute are the best evidence of legislative intent. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 188 (1824). Nothing in the provision authorizes compensation for an injury sustained on land miles from the Shelf.

Congress enacted the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act against the settled rule that federal maritime remedies stop at the water’s edge. Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205, 217-18 (1917). The statute therefore cannot be read to displace state compensation schemes for purely onshore accidents. The Constitution does not permit the federal government to extend its maritime jurisdiction over injuries occurring on land. Knickerbocker Ice Co. v. Stewart, 253 U.S. 149, 160 (1920).

The same principle of strict geographic demarcation appears throughout the Court’s pre-OCSLA decisions. Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 55 (1932). The Act is limited to injuries occurring upon the navigable waters. Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc., 314 U.S. 244, 247 (1941) likewise confined coverage to the precise situs described by Congress. Davis v. Department of Labor & Industries, 317 U.S. 249, 252-53 (1942) reaffirmed that any extension beyond navigable waters requires explicit statutory language.

When Congress wished to enlarge remedies, it did so with unmistakable words. See, e.g., The Propeller Genesee Chief v. Fitzhugh, 53 U.S. (12 How.) 443, 453 (1852) (admiralty jurisdiction extended only after express statutory amendment). Section 1333(b) contains no comparable language removing the traditional situs requirement. General expressions in every opinion are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used. Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399 (1821).

The Ninth Circuit’s substantial nexus test therefore contravenes the statute’s plain terms and the uniform line of authority limiting federal maritime remedies to the precise geographic area Congress designated. The judgment should be reversed.
