**ARGUMENT**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the plain-error standard in concluding that, even if the Government failed to prove Greer’s knowledge of his felon status at the time of possession—a requirement newly established in *Rehaif v. United States*, 583 U.S. ___ (2019)—the error did not affect Greer’s substantial rights or undermine the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The Court should affirm because the appellate court properly exercised its discretion under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) by reviewing matters beyond the jury’s record—specifically, the full trial record and Greer’s prior convictions—to assess whether the alleged error was prejudicial. This approach is consistent with settled precedent and essential to meaningful plain-error review.

Under Rule 52(b), a federal appellate court may consider unpreserved errors only when they constitute “plain error” that affects “substantial rights.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). To satisfy this standard, the defendant must show: (1) an error; (2) that is clear or obvious; (3) that affects substantial rights; and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Id. The third and fourth prongs are closely related: “a defendant must demonstrate that the error actually affected his substantial rights,” which requires showing “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different.” *Id.* at 736–37.

Crucially, the Supreme Court has long recognized that appellate courts may look beyond the jury’s verdict to determine whether an error affected substantial rights. In *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985), the Court held that “in determining whether an error affected substantial rights, we may consider the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury.” Similarly, in *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 390, 396 (2002), the Court emphasized that “the plain-error inquiry necessarily involves a review of the entire record,” noting that “the determination of whether an error affected substantial rights depends on the facts of the case and the totality of circumstances.” These precedents confirm that appellate courts are not confined to the narrow scope of evidence submitted to the jury when assessing prejudice under Rule 52(b).

Here, the Eleventh Circuit did precisely what *Cotton* and *Young* permit: it reviewed the full trial record—including Greer’s plea colloquy, the factual basis for his guilty plea, and his extensive criminal history—to evaluate whether the failure to instruct the jury on knowledge of status likely influenced the outcome. The district court had accepted Greer’s stipulation that he was a felon and had admitted five prior Ohio aggravated burglary convictions—each of which involved violent conduct and demonstrated a pattern of serious criminal behavior. The plea agreement explicitly acknowledged that Greer was subject to the Armed Career Criminal Act based on those convictions. The record further shows that Greer was represented by counsel throughout the process, entered his plea knowingly and voluntarily, and received a sentence within the advisory guidelines range.

Given this context, the Eleventh Circuit reasonably concluded that Greer could not establish prejudice. As the Court explained in *Olano*, “the burden of demonstrating prejudice rests squarely on the defendant.” 507 U.S. at 734. Greer offered no evidence that he was unaware of his felony status at the time of possession—indeed, his own criminal history suggests otherwise. He had been convicted of multiple serious offenses, including violent crimes, and had previously been sentenced as an armed career criminal. The fact that he pleaded guilty to being a felon in possession of a firearm, without contesting the factual basis, strongly indicates that he knew he was a prohibited person. See *United States v. Smith*, 582 F.3d 1202, 1207 (11th Cir. 2009) (“A guilty plea, especially one supported by a detailed factual basis, carries strong indicia of truthfulness and awareness.”).

Moreover, the Government’s failure to prove knowledge of status did not alter the nature of the offense or the sentencing consequences. Under *Rehaif*, the prosecution must prove both possession and knowledge of status. But here, Greer’s plea agreement and the factual basis for his conviction included explicit admissions that he knew he was a felon. The district court relied on these admissions in imposing sentence. Thus, even if the jury instruction error were considered, the record demonstrates that the judge—and the defendant—were fully aware of the relevant facts. The error, therefore, did not affect the outcome.

Finally, the Eleventh Circuit did not exceed its authority by considering extrajudicial materials. The Court has repeatedly upheld the use of background information in plain-error review. In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 60 (2002), the Court affirmed that “an appellate court may consider the entire record, including the defendant’s criminal history, when evaluating whether an error affected substantial rights.” That principle applies equally here. The lower court’s analysis was neither speculative nor arbitrary—it was grounded in the totality of the circumstances, including Greer’s own admissions and the nature of his prior convictions.

For these reasons, the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the plain-error standard. It properly looked beyond the jury’s record to assess prejudice, and concluded that Greer failed to meet his burden of proving that the error affected his substantial rights or undermined the fairness of the proceeding. The judgment should be affirmed.
