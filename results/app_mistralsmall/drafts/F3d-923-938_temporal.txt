**ARGUMENT**

I. **Meruelo Established Bona Fide Indebtedness Running Directly to Him Under § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i)**

The regulation governing basis in S corporation indebtedness requires that the indebtedness run "directly" to the shareholder. Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). This Court has long recognized that a shareholder may establish basis through direct or indirect loans, provided the economic outlay is real and the debt is bona fide. *See, e.g., Estate of Leavitt v. Comm’r*, 87 F.2d 105 (2d Cir. 1936) (holding that a shareholder’s guarantee of a corporate loan, coupled with an economic outlay, may establish basis where the lender looks to the shareholder for repayment).

Here, Meruelo’s transfers through affiliated entities were recharacterized as direct loans to Merco because the affiliates acted as mere conduits for his economic outlay. The Tax Court erred in disregarding the substance of these transactions. As this Court has held, "the form of a transaction will not control if the substance is different." *Helvering v. F. & R. Lazarus & Co.*, 308 U.S. 252, 255 (1939). The IRS’s position—that the debt ran only to the affiliates—ignores the economic reality that Meruelo was the true source of the funds. *See Old Colony R. Co. v. Comm’r*, 284 U.S. 552, 560 (1932) ("A transaction is to be judged by what actually occurred and not by what might have been done.").

II. **Meruelo Made an Actual Economic Outlay Sufficient to Establish Basis**

To claim basis in corporate indebtedness, a shareholder must make an "actual economic outlay." *See, e.g., Putoma Corp. v. Comm’r*, 66 T.C. 652 (1976) (pre-1970 authority not required, but principles established in earlier cases apply). The Tax Court erred in finding that Meruelo failed to establish such an outlay. The record shows that Meruelo personally borrowed funds and directed their transfer to Merco through affiliates. This constitutes an economic outlay because Meruelo incurred a personal liability and provided capital to the corporation. *See, e.g., United States v. Henderson*, 318 F.2d 60 (5th Cir. 1963) (holding that a shareholder’s personal guarantee of a corporate loan, coupled with an economic outlay, may establish basis).

The IRS’s argument that the transfers were mere bookkeeping entries ignores the substance of the transactions. As the Supreme Court has held, "taxation is not so much concerned with refinements of title as it is with actual command over the property taxed." *Corliss v. Bowers*, 281 U.S. 376, 378 (1930). Meruelo’s transfers were not mere accounting entries; they were real economic contributions that enhanced Merco’s capital structure.

III. **The Tax Court Improperly Relied on Formalistic Distinctions**

The Tax Court erred in treating the debt as running only to the affiliates rather than to Meruelo. This formalistic distinction disregards the economic reality of the transactions. As the Supreme Court has cautioned, "the incidence of taxation depends upon the substance of a transaction." *Commissioner v. Court Holding Co.*, 324 U.S. 331, 334 (1945) (citing earlier cases). Here, the substance of the transactions was that Meruelo provided funds to Merco, and the affiliates were mere intermediaries. The Tax Court’s refusal to look past the form of the transactions was error.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Meruelo established bona fide indebtedness running directly to him and made an actual economic outlay sufficient to claim basis in Merco’s indebtedness. The Tax Court’s decision should be reversed.
