The execution of mentally retarded persons is not "cruel and unusual punishment" prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. As the Virginia Supreme Court correctly held in affirming Atkins' death sentence, Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), controls this case. In Penry, this Court considered whether the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of mentally retarded persons and held that it does not. Id. at 329-30. Although the Court acknowledged that there was a national trend against executing the mentally retarded, it concluded that the trend was not sufficiently established to warrant a holding that the practice is unconstitutional. Id. at 334-35.

Atkins argues that the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on "cruel and unusual punishment" should be interpreted to bar the execution of mentally retarded persons. However, as this Court held in Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361 (1989), the Eighth Amendment is to be interpreted in light of "evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." Id. at 369 (quoting Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958)). At the time of Atkins' trial, the national consensus against executing the mentally retarded was not yet fully formed.

Moreover, the Virginia Supreme Court's decision to follow Penry was consistent with the principle that a lower court is bound to follow the precedent of a higher court. See Rodriguez de Quijas v. Shearson/American Express, Inc., 490 U.S. 477, 484 (1989). As the Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied the binding precedent of Penry, Atkins' Eighth Amendment claim is without merit.

The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
