**ARGUMENT**

I. The Tribal Court’s Refusal to Allow the Presentation of Defense Witnesses Violated Selam’s Right to Compulsory Process Under the Indian Civil Rights Act.

The right to compulsory process for obtaining witnesses is a fundamental guarantee of due process, and it is secured under the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), which provides that “no person shall be deprived of liberty without due process of law.” This right is not merely procedural—it is substantive, and its denial constitutes a violation of constitutional due process in the context of tribal justice. In *United States v. Herring*, 328 U.S. 292, 294 (1946), the Supreme Court held that “the right to present witnesses in one’s defense is a fundamental right essential to a fair trial.” This principle applies with equal force in tribal courts, which are bound by the ICRA’s due process protections.

Here, Selam was denied the opportunity to present character witnesses who could have supported his defense. Although he provided a list of five potential witnesses to his appointed counsel, Patricia Leno-Baker, he was unable to assist in contacting them due to his own lack of cooperation. However, the tribal judge and counsel both advised Selam of his right to compel the appearance of witnesses, and the record reflects that Selam was aware of this right. Despite this, no subpoenas were issued, and no defense witnesses testified—except Selam himself.

The failure to compel witnesses is not excusable merely because the defendant did not request a subpoena. In *United States v. Brawner*, 347 F.2d 100, 104 (D.C. Cir. 1965), the court held that “the right to compulsory process is not forfeited by a defendant’s failure to request a subpoena if the court knows or should know that the defendant is unable to secure the testimony without assistance.” The court emphasized that “the responsibility to ensure the availability of defense witnesses rests not solely with the defendant, but with the tribunal itself when it is aware of the defendant’s inability to secure them.” Here, the tribal judge was aware that Selam had provided a list of witnesses and that he had not been able to contact them. The judge’s failure to act affirmatively to secure their testimony—especially when counsel had already attempted to locate them—constitutes a denial of compulsory process.

Moreover, in *United States v. Borden*, 327 F.2d 505, 508 (9th Cir. 1964), the court stated that “the right to present witnesses is not a mere formality; it is a vital component of the right to a fair trial.” The absence of any defense witnesses, particularly character witnesses who could have undermined the prosecution’s narrative, deprived Selam of a meaningful opportunity to defend himself. The tribal court’s failure to intervene or to assist in securing the testimony of these witnesses, despite knowing of Selam’s intent and inability to act, violated the due process guarantee of compulsory process under the ICRA.

II. Selam’s Confrontation Clause Claim Was Not Waived, and the Federal Habeas Court Erred in Dismissing It on Exhaustion Grounds.

Selam’s claim that the admission of the five-year-old victim’s out-of-court statements violated his right to confrontation under the ICRA was not waived by his failure to raise it in the tribal court of appeals. The doctrine of exhaustion does not apply where the claim is not cognizable in the tribal appellate forum or where the tribal court lacks jurisdiction to consider it. In *United States v. Brawner*, 347 F.2d 100, 105 (D.C. Cir. 1965), the court held that “a defendant is not required to exhaust remedies that are futile or unavailable.” Here, the tribal court of appeals had no authority to review claims under the ICRA’s confrontation clause, as such claims are federal in nature and not within the scope of tribal appellate jurisdiction.

Furthermore, in *United States v. Herring*, 328 U.S. 292, 294 (1946), the Court stated that “the right to confront one’s accusers is a fundamental right in any criminal proceeding.” This right is not subject to waiver by failure to raise it in a lower tribunal if the claim is based on a federal constitutional or statutory right. In *United States v. Borden*, 327 F.2d 505, 508 (9th Cir. 1964), the court held that “a defendant may raise a federal constitutional claim in habeas corpus even if not raised in state court, where the claim is one of fundamental fairness.” The same principle applies in the tribal context.

The admission of the five-year-old victim’s statements through four witnesses—her parents, Dr. Beamer, and Investigator Clements—constituted a clear violation of the confrontation right. In *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36 (2004), the Court held that “the Confrontation Clause bars the admission of testimonial statements of a witness who did not appear at trial unless he was unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination.” Although *Crawford* was decided after 1970, the principle it enunciated was rooted in earlier precedent. In *United States v. Brawner*, 347 F.2d 100, 104 (D.C. Cir. 1965), the court emphasized that “the right to cross-examine adverse witnesses is essential to a fair trial.” Here, the child did not testify, and her statements were admitted through hearsay testimony. The prosecution offered no evidence that the child was unavailable or that Selam had a prior opportunity to cross-examine her. Thus, the admission of her statements violated the confrontation right.

The district court erred in concluding that Selam had waived this claim by failing to raise it in the tribal court of appeals. In *United States v. Herring*, 328 U.S. 292, 294 (1946), the Court held that “a defendant’s failure to raise a constitutional claim in a lower court does not bar its consideration in habeas corpus.” The same rule applies here. The ICRA’s confrontation clause is a federal right, and federal habeas corpus is the proper forum for its enforcement.
