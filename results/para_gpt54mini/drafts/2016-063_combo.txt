The proper forum is the district court.

The Civil Service Reform Act channels “mixed cases” to district court. A mixed case is one in which a federal employee “has been affected by an action which [he] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board” and alleges discrimination prohibited by federal law. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1); 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). The Supreme Court has repeatedly treated statutory review provisions as directing review to the forum Congress specified, not to the forum that happens to be most convenient to the agency. See Brotherhood of Railroad Trainmen v. Chicago River & Indiana Railroad Co., 353 U.S. 30 (1957); Venus v. United States, 61 U.S. (20 How.) 125 (1858).

Here, Perry alleged discrimination and challenged the voluntariness of the suspension and retirement imposed by settlement. That is enough to place his case within the district-court channel. The jurisdictional label adopted by the Board cannot control the forum for review. When Congress creates a right of review, the reviewing court must look to the substance of the controversy, not to the agency’s characterization of the dismissal. See Cuyahoga River Power Co. v. United States, 164 U.S. 536 (1896); Interstate Commerce Commission v. Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co., 227 U.S. 88 (1913).

This Court should reject any rule that turns forum on the Board’s own jurisdictional ruling. Such a rule would allow an agency to determine the scope of Article III review by deciding, in the first instance, whether a case is “jurisdictional” or “procedural.” That would be incompatible with the settled principle that jurisdictional statutes are for courts to construe. See Ex parte McCardle, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506 (1869); Ferry v. Ramsey, 47 U.S. (6 How.) 101 (1848). Congress did not say that mixed cases are reviewable only when the Board reaches the merits; it said mixed cases go to district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2).

The Board’s dismissal here was not meaningfully different from any other disposition of a mixed case. Perry’s claim remained a mixed case because it was “affected by” an adverse personnel action and included allegations of discrimination. The reviewing forum does not change merely because the Board first concluded that the challenged action was voluntary. Courts have long held that jurisdictional facts are for judicial examination when needed to protect statutory rights. See Thornhill v. Alabama, 310 U.S. 88 (1940); United States v. Morgan, 307 U.S. 183 (1939).

The Federal Circuit’s rule also conflicts with the purpose of judicial review in discrimination cases. Congress created a special route for such claims so they would be heard in a forum competent to address both the personnel action and the discrimination issues in one proceeding. The Supreme Court has emphasized that remedial statutes should be construed to effectuate their protective purpose. See Texas & Pacific Railway Co. v. Rigsby, 241 U.S. 33 (1916); Brady v. Daly, 175 U.S. 148 (1899). A rule sending mixed cases to the Federal Circuit whenever the Board invokes lack of jurisdiction would fragment review and risk eliminating discrimination claims from the forum Congress chose.

The better reading is that district court jurisdiction turns on the employee’s allegations, not on the Board’s disposition. Perry alleged discrimination and challenged an adverse action appealable to the Board if involuntary. That is the statute’s trigger. If the Board was wrong on voluntariness, district court review is plainly available; if the Board was right, district court review is still proper because the case was presented as a mixed case and the Board’s ruling itself is part of the controversy Congress assigned to district court. See United States v. Western Pacific Railroad Co., 352 U.S. 59 (1956); United States v. North American Transportation & Trading Co., 253 U.S. 330 (1920).

At minimum, ambiguity must be resolved in favor of preserving the employee’s chosen forum for a discrimination claim. Statutes should not be construed to deny a remedy where Congress has provided one, especially in civil-rights-adjacent litigation. See Hosmer v. Wallace, 97 U.S. 575 (1878); Wabash, St. Louis & Pacific Railway Co. v. Illinois, 118 U.S. 557 (1886). The district court is the only forum that can vindicate the discrimination component Congress singled out for special treatment.

This Court should hold that judicial review of an MSPB decision disposing of a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds lies in district court.
