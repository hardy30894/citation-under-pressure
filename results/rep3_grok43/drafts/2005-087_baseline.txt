ARGUMENT

Arizona’s rule confining mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense comports with the Due Process Clause. A state may allocate the burden of proving affirmative defenses and may channel relevant evidence to the issue on which the defendant bears that burden. Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 207-08 (1977); Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 799-800 (1952). The Clause “does not require the States to conform to any particular model of criminal procedure.” Id. at 799.

The rule does not relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Arizona still must establish the requisite culpable mental state; the defendant may rebut that showing with any competent evidence other than expert testimony on mental disease. The limitation therefore operates as a rule of evidence, not a redefinition of the offense. Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37, 41-43 (1996) (plurality opinion).

States possess “broad latitude” to adopt such evidentiary restrictions when they serve legitimate purposes. Id. at 58 (plurality); see also Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973) (recognizing that evidentiary rules may exclude evidence without violating due process). Arizona’s restriction prevents the misuse of psychiatric testimony to circumvent the statutory allocation of the insanity burden, a concern this Court has treated as valid. See Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354, 368-69 (1983). Because the rule is neither arbitrary nor disproportionate to its purpose, it does not transgress the fundamental fairness guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment. Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 437, 445-46 (1992).

Petitioner’s proposed right would require every jurisdiction to admit mental-disease evidence on mens rea whenever it is admitted on insanity. No precedent imposes that obligation. The Constitution leaves the integration of these defenses to legislative judgment.
