**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONSTITUTION PRESCRIBES THE EXCLUSIVE QUALIFICATIONS FOR CONGRESSIONAL SERVICE, AND STATES MAY NOT ALTER THEM.**

The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution establish the exclusive criteria for membership in Congress. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the state when elected. Article I, Section 3, Clause 3 sets analogous qualifications for Senators: age thirty, citizenship for nine years, and in-state inhabitancy. By specifying these qualifications, the Framers intended them to be exclusive, precluding state-imposed additions.

In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969), this Court held that the Qualifications Clauses were intended to be "an exclusive constitutionally prescribed set of qualifications." The Court explained that the Framers viewed the power to set qualifications as "a fundamental decision about the nature of the federal government" that could not be altered by individual states. *Id.* at 540. This principle was reaffirmed in *Cook v. Gralike*, 531 U.S. 510, 523 (2001), where the Court struck down a Missouri ballot measure that penalized candidates who failed to support term limits, holding that "the Constitution's qualifications clauses were intended to be exclusive."

Arkansas Amendment 73 directly contravenes this settled precedent. Section 3 of the Amendment provides that any person who has served three or more terms in the House or two or more terms in the Senate "shall not be certified as a candidate and shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot." This is not a mere ballot-access regulation; it is a substantive disqualification. As the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized, "the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp." *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill*, 316 Ark. 251, 265, 872 S.W.2d 349, 356 (1994). The practical effect is identical: a candidate who has served the specified number of terms is rendered ineligible for federal office.

This Court has consistently rejected state efforts to impose additional qualifications through indirect means. In *Tashjian v. Republican Party of Connecticut*, 479 U.S. 208, 225 (1986), the Court emphasized that states may not "circumvent the constitutional scheme" by regulating federal elections in ways that conflict with federal constitutional requirements. Similarly, in *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, 730 (1974), the Court recognized that while states have broad authority to regulate ballot access, that authority "must be exercised in a manner consistent with the Constitution."

**II. THE FRAMERS' REJECTION OF TERM LIMITS CONFIRMS THE EXCLUSIVITY OF THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES.**

The historical record demonstrates that the Framers considered and rejected term limits for Congress. During the Constitutional Convention, delegates debated proposals to limit the number of terms a member could serve. *See The Federalist No. 53* (Madison) (arguing against term limits as unnecessary and harmful). The Convention ultimately declined to include any such restriction, leaving qualifications to the three enumerated criteria. This decision reflects a deliberate choice that "the people should be free to choose their representatives as they see fit." *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 540.

In *Term Limits v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 800 (1995), this Court definitively resolved the question presented here. The Court held that "the Constitution's qualifications clauses were intended to be exclusive" and that "states cannot, by law, add to or alter the qualifications for federal office." *Id.* at 798. The Court further explained that the Framers "intended the Constitution to be the exclusive source of qualifications for members of Congress." *Id.* at 803. Arkansas Amendment 73 is therefore unconstitutional.

**III. THE SEVERABILITY CLAUSE CANNOT SAVE THE UNCONSTITUTIONAL PROVISIONS.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Section 3 of Amendment 73 is not severable from Sections 1 and 2, which impose term limits on state officials. The test for severability is "whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion." *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 748, 375 S.W.2d 809, 811 (1964). Here, the record contains no evidence that voters would have approved term limits for state officials alone. The Amendment was presented as an "all or nothing" package, with state and federal officials "lumped together" in the Preamble. *Hill*, 316 Ark. at 265, 872 S.W.2d at 356. As the Arkansas Supreme Court observed, "there was not a straightforward, up-or-down vote on term limits for state officeholders." *Id.* at 266, 872 S.W.2d at 357.

This Court has held that severability is a question of state law. *See Leavitt v. Jane L.*, 518 U.S. 137, 139 (1996). The Arkansas Supreme Court's determination that Sections 1 and 2 are not severable is entitled to deference. *See id.* at 140. Moreover, the Court has recognized that "the inclusion of a severability clause does not conclusively resolve the severability question." *Id.* at 139. Here, the Arkansas Supreme Court properly concluded that the unconstitutional Section 3 infected the entire Amendment.

**IV. THE STATE'S POWER TO REGULATE ELECTIONS DOES NOT EXTEND TO ALTERING FEDERAL QUALIFICATIONS.**

While states possess broad authority to regulate the "time, place, and manner" of federal elections under Article I, Section 4, Clause 1, that authority does not include the power to impose additional qualifications. *See Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355, 366 (1932) (holding that state election regulations must not "conflict with the provisions of the Constitution"). In *Roudebush v. Hartke*, 405 U.S. 15, 24 (1972), the Court emphasized that state authority over elections is "subject to the paramount authority of Congress." Arkansas Amendment 73 exceeds that authority by effectively adding a durational residency requirement—a qualification not found in the Constitution.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
