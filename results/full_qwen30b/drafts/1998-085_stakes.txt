**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous” independent of its state of mind to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—requires this Court to interpret the plain language of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which amended Title VII to authorize punitive damages for intentional discrimination. The answer is clear: **no**. Congress did not require that the employer’s conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” in a manner independent of its mental state. Rather, punitive damages are available under § 1981a(b)(1) when a plaintiff demonstrates that the employer acted with *malice* or *reckless indifference* to the plaintiff’s federally protected rights. The lower court’s refusal to instruct the jury on punitive damages was error, and the judgment must be reversed.

### I. Congress Rejected the “Egregious Conduct” Standard in Favor of a Mental-State-Based Test

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 amended Title VII to provide for compensatory and punitive damages in cases of intentional discrimination. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(a). The statute explicitly authorizes punitive damages “if the [plaintiff] demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This language is unambiguous: the focus is on the *defendant’s state of mind*, not on the objective severity of the conduct.

The legislative history confirms that Congress intended to adopt a standard rooted in culpability, not in the degree of harm or the outwardly shocking nature of the conduct. The House Report stated that punitive damages under § 1981a “should be available only when the employer’s conduct was so egregious that it warranted punishment beyond mere compensation.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, at 12 (1991). But this language was not adopted in the final version of the Act. The Senate version, which became law, replaced the “egregious” standard with the *malice or reckless indifference* standard. See S. Rep. No. 102-166, at 10 (1991). The final statute reflects Congress’s decision to prioritize *intent* and *awareness* over the subjective perception of the conduct’s “outrageousness.”

The Court has long recognized that punitive damages under federal statutes are not awarded for the mere occurrence of harm, but for conduct that reflects a culpable state of mind. In *BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 574 (1996), the Court held that punitive damages must be “proportional to the harm” and “guided by the principle that the defendant’s conduct should be blameworthy.” But the Court emphasized that “the defendant’s mental state is a critical factor in determining whether punitive damages are appropriate.” Id. at 575. The same principle applies here: *malice* and *reckless indifference* are not mere legal fictions—they are mental states that reflect a defendant’s awareness of the wrongfulness of its conduct.

### II. The “Egregious Conduct” Standard Is Inconsistent with the Statutory Text and Precedent

The Court has repeatedly held that when a statute uses clear language, courts must give effect to that language, even if the result appears harsh or inconsistent with prior practice. *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989). The phrase “with malice or with reckless indifference” is not a vague standard; it is a well-established legal concept. *Malice* in this context means “an intentional wrongdoing with a bad motive or ill will,” while *reckless indifference* means “a conscious disregard of a known risk of harm.” *See, e.g.,* *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002) (defining “reckless indifference” in the context of the Americans with Disabilities Act).

The Court has also rejected the notion that punitive damages require “egregious” conduct independent of the defendant’s state of mind. In *Parratt v. Taylor*, 451 U.S. 527, 540 (1981), the Court held that punitive damages are not available simply because the government’s conduct was “unwise” or “unfair,” but only when the conduct reflects a “deliberate or reckless disregard” of constitutional rights. The same principle applies under Title VII: the focus is on the employer’s *intent*, not on whether the conduct would shock the conscience of a reasonable observer.

The lower court’s reliance on the House Report’s reference to “egregious” conduct is misplaced. The House Report was not adopted. The final statute, which reflects the compromise between the House and Senate versions, uses the *malice or reckless indifference* standard. The Court has long held that legislative history cannot override the plain language of a statute. *United States v. Ron Pair*, 489 U.S. at 242. Moreover, the Senate Report explicitly rejected the idea that punitive damages should be limited to “extraordinarily egregious” cases. S. Rep. No. 102-166, at 10 (“The Senate believes that punitive damages should be available in cases of intentional discrimination, even if the conduct is not ‘egregious’ in the traditional sense.”).

### III. The Lower Court’s Error Was Harmless Only If the Jury Was Instructed on the Correct Standard

The District Court erred in denying Kolstad’s request to instruct the jury on punitive damages. The jury had already found that ADA intentionally discriminated against Kolstad on the basis of sex. The only remaining issue was whether ADA acted with *malice* or *reckless indifference*. The jury was not instructed on this standard, and thus could not properly assess whether punitive damages were warranted.
