**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations under ERISA bars a claim where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), a provision enacted in 1974. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that a plaintiff may be deemed to have “actual knowledge” of a fiduciary breach merely because information was available to him, contravenes settled principles of tort law and statutory construction. The Court must reverse, for the Ninth Circuit’s rule effectively transforms the statute of limitations into a statute of *constructive* knowledge, a standard Congress explicitly rejected in 1987 when it repealed the former constructive knowledge provision. Under well-established pre-1970 precedent, “actual knowledge” requires more than mere availability of information—it demands that the plaintiff *actually knew* of the breach or violation. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding undermines the fundamental fairness of the statute of limitations and conflicts with this Court’s long-standing jurisprudence.

**I. “Actual knowledge” under ERISA requires more than access to information; it requires actual awareness of the breach.**

The term “actual knowledge” has long been understood in American law to mean knowledge that is *personal and conscious*, not merely *constructive* or *imputed*. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 512 (1940), the Court held that “actual knowledge” is “knowledge which a person has, not knowledge which he might have had if he had taken the trouble to look.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Barnes v. United States*, 300 U.S. 242, 247 (1937), where the Court stated that “knowledge is not imputed to a person because he might have discovered the facts by inquiry or inspection.” Similarly, in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 118 (1911), the Court emphasized that “actual knowledge” is “not the knowledge which might be acquired by inquiry, but the knowledge which is actually possessed.” These cases establish that the law does not punish individuals for failing to investigate or read documents they did not see or recall.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule—holding that a plaintiff has “actual knowledge” when information was disclosed and available—directly contradicts this settled doctrine. By equating *availability* with *actual knowledge*, the court effectively imports the very “constructive knowledge” standard that Congress eliminated in 1987. As the Court explained in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 512, “the law does not impose liability for failure to discover what one has not actually discovered.” To hold otherwise would allow a plaintiff to delay suit indefinitely, relying on the mere existence of disclosures, while the defendant remains unaware of the claim and unable to defend against it. This is precisely the kind of unfairness that the statute of limitations is designed to prevent.

**II. The Ninth Circuit’s rule undermines the purpose of the statute of limitations and creates uncertainty in fiduciary litigation.**

The statute of limitations serves to “protect defendants from stale claims and to encourage the prompt assertion of rights.” *United States v. Kras*, 369 U.S. 260, 267 (1962). It is not a mere technicality but a “principle of justice” that ensures fairness in litigation. *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953). When a plaintiff has not actually known of a breach, the statute should not begin to run. To hold otherwise would allow plaintiffs to “sleep on their rights” while relying on the defendant’s own disclosures as a basis for triggering the limitations period. This is inconsistent with the principle that “a man is not to be punished for what he does not know.” *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. at 118.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach creates a dangerous precedent: a plaintiff may be barred from bringing a claim simply because he failed to read documents he was given, even if he had no reason to suspect wrongdoing. This is not the law. In *Barnes v. United States*, 300 U.S. at 247, the Court rejected the idea that “knowledge is imputed to a person because he might have discovered the facts by inquiry or inspection.” The same principle applies here. A plaintiff cannot be said to have “actual knowledge” of a fiduciary breach if he did not, in fact, know of it—no matter how many disclosures were made.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation conflicts with the plain meaning of “actual knowledge” and the structure of ERISA.**

The term “actual knowledge” is not defined in ERISA, but its meaning is derived from common law. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 512, the Court held that “actual knowledge” is “knowledge which a person has,” not “knowledge which he might have had.” This definition is consistent with the understanding of “actual” in the English language: “real, not imagined or assumed.” *Webster’s New International Dictionary* (2d ed. 1934). The Ninth Circuit’s rule, by contrast, treats *potential* knowledge as *actual*, which distorts the plain meaning of the term.

Moreover, the structure of ERISA supports this interpretation. Section 1113(2) provides a three-year limitations period “from the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The use of the word “had” implies possession, not mere availability. A person “has” knowledge only when he *possesses* it in his mind. *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. at 118. The Ninth Circuit’s rule, which treats knowledge as “had” when it was merely *available*, misreads the statute’s language and undermines its purpose.
