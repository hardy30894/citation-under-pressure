**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause guarantees a defendant the right to present a complete defense, but this right is not absolute and must be balanced against the state’s interest in maintaining the integrity of the criminal trial process. In this case, the Arizona trial court did not violate Eric Michael Clark’s due process rights by limiting the use of his expert mental health testimony to the insanity defense. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a defendant’s right to present evidence is subject to reasonable rules of evidence and procedural constraints, and that the state may define the scope of affirmative defenses, including the burden and standard of proof. Clark’s claim—that he had a separate due process right to use expert evidence about his mental state to rebut the prosecution’s proof of criminal intent—misconstrues both the nature of the insanity defense and the limits of constitutional due process.

First, the Court has consistently held that the Constitution does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce evidence on a defense that the state has not recognized. *United States v. Scheffer*, 526 U.S. 493, 498 (1999). In *Scheffer*, the Court upheld a military rule excluding polygraph evidence, emphasizing that “the Constitution does not require the admission of evidence that is irrelevant or unreliable.” Similarly, Arizona’s decision to confine expert testimony on mental state to the insanity defense is a valid exercise of state authority to define the contours of its criminal law. The state is not required to permit a defendant to use mental health evidence to attack the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent unless the state has created a separate defense—such as diminished capacity—that allows such use. Arizona has not done so.

Second, the Court has repeatedly affirmed that the insanity defense is a distinct legal doctrine, separate from the issue of criminal intent. In *M’Naghten’s Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), the English common law established that a defendant may be acquitted if, at the time of the offense, he did not know the nature and quality of his act or did not know that it was wrong. This framework was adopted in the United States and remains the basis for modern insanity defenses. The Supreme Court reaffirmed in *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 410 (1986), that “the insanity defense is a legal, not a medical, determination.” The fact that a defendant may suffer from a mental illness does not automatically negate criminal intent; rather, the law requires a separate showing that the defendant lacked the capacity to understand the wrongfulness of his conduct. Thus, the state may properly limit the use of expert testimony to that specific legal inquiry.

Third, the Court has rejected the notion that due process requires a defendant to be allowed to introduce evidence on a defense that is not constitutionally mandated. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 797 (1952), the Court held that the Constitution does not require a state to recognize a defense of “diminished capacity” unless the state chooses to do so. The Court stated: “The Constitution does not require a State to recognize any particular defense.” This principle applies with equal force here: Arizona has not created a defense based on inability to form criminal intent, and thus Clark cannot claim a constitutional right to introduce expert evidence on that issue.

Fourth, the Court has emphasized that due process does not entitle a defendant to introduce evidence that is not relevant to the issues at trial. In *Huddleston v. United States*, 485 U.S. 681, 688 (1988), the Court held that “the trial judge has broad discretion to exclude evidence that is not relevant or that would confuse the jury.” Expert testimony about mental illness may be relevant to the insanity defense, but it is not automatically relevant to the issue of criminal intent unless the state has adopted a legal standard that incorporates such a defense. Arizona has not done so.

Fifth, the Court has recognized that states may impose procedural rules that limit the use of evidence, even when such rules affect the defendant’s ability to present a defense. In *United States v. Scheffer*, 526 U.S. at 498, the Court upheld the exclusion of polygraph evidence, stating that “the Constitution does not require the admission of evidence that is irrelevant or unreliable.” Similarly, in *Taylor v. Illinois*, 484 U.S. 400, 410 (1988), the Court upheld a state’s exclusion of evidence that was not timely disclosed, noting that “the trial court’s discretion to exclude evidence is not limited by the defendant’s right to present a defense.” The Arizona trial court’s ruling was consistent with these principles.

Sixth, the Court has held that due process does not require a state to allow a defendant to use expert testimony to attack the prosecution’s burden of proof unless the state has created a defense that permits such use. In *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 659 (1984), the Court stated that “the right to present a defense is not absolute” and that “the Constitution does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce evidence that is not relevant to the issues in the case.” Clark’s expert testimony, while relevant to his insanity claim, was not relevant to the issue of criminal intent under Arizona law.

Seventh, the Court has recognized that states may define the elements of crimes and the defenses thereto. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 440 (1978), the Court held that “the Constitution does not require a state to recognize a defense that it has not created.” Arizona has not created a defense based on inability to form criminal intent, and thus Clark cannot claim a constitutional right to introduce evidence on that issue.

Eighth, the Court has emphasized that due process does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce evidence that would undermine the state’s burden of proof unless the state has created a defense that permits such use. In *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667, 677 (1985), the Court held that “the Constitution does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce evidence that is not relevant to the issues in the case.” Clark’s expert testimony was not relevant to the issue of criminal intent under Arizona law.
