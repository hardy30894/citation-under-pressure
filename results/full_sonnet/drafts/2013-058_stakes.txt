# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF SECTION 522 EXEMPTS ONLY FUNDS SET ASIDE FOR THE DEBTOR'S OWN RETIREMENT

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code exempt "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under" specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The statute requires two conjunctive elements: the assets must be (1) "retirement funds" that are (2) held in a tax-exempt account. Petitioners' inherited IRA satisfies the second element but fails the first, because by the time of the bankruptcy filing the funds no longer bore any of the economic attributes that make money "retirement funds."

This Court has long recognized that statutory terms take their ordinary meaning at the time relevant to the statute's application, not a meaning fixed by the asset's history. As the Seventh Circuit correctly held below, "retirement funds" must be assessed by "how Heidi used the property, not how her mother used it." *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 563 (7th Cir. 2013). An inherited IRA cannot function as retirement savings for the beneficiary because federal tax law forbids it from doing so.

## II. AN INHERITED IRA LACKS THE DEFINING CHARACTERISTICS OF RETIREMENT FUNDS

The Internal Revenue Code imposes a fundamentally different regime on inherited, non-spousal IRAs than on ordinary retirement accounts. As the court below explained, "no new contributions can be made, and the balance cannot be rolled over or merged with any other account." *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 560 (citing 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C)). Critically, "the inherited IRA must begin distributing its assets within a year of the original owner's death," with payout sometimes required "in as little as five years." *Id.* (citing 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(11)(A), incorporating 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B)).

These features mean that, unlike a traditional IRA, the inherited account cannot be preserved until the beneficiary's own retirement. The beneficiary may withdraw the entire balance the day after inheriting it, without penalty, and spend it on anything at all. As the Seventh Circuit observed, "an inherited IRA is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner's retirement." *Id.* This is the precise opposite of what Congress meant by "retirement funds"—money genuinely set aside for the post-employment years.

The court's hypothetical is instructive: had Ruth Heffron withdrawn her IRA balance and simply handed the cash to her daughter, "[t]he money would have been 'retirement funds' while in Ruth's IRA, but not thereafter; in Heidi's bank account the money would be no different from any other assets she could save or spend at will." *Id.* at 562. Nothing in the statute suggests that the label "inherited IRA" transforms otherwise unrestricted, freely spendable funds into "retirement funds" simply because of their pedigree.

## III. PETITIONERS' RELIANCE ON THE WORD "INHERITED" CANNOT OVERCOME THE FUNDS' ACTUAL CHARACTER

Petitioners, echoing the reasoning of *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (B.A.P. 8th Cir. 2010), emphasize that Congress did not include the word "debtor's" in § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), unlike other subsections of § 522. But as the Seventh Circuit correctly recognized, this drafting choice serves a narrow, temporal function—it "gives debtors a break by omitting a temporal restriction," so that "new value added to a retirement fund during bankruptcy" remains exempt. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. That drafting choice says nothing about whether funds that have ceased to be anyone's retirement savings can qualify merely because they once were.

The court's homestead-exemption analogy confirms this point. Section 522(b)(3)(A) similarly omits the word "debtor's," yet no one would suggest that a debtor who inherits her mother's house and rents it out could claim the homestead exemption based on her mother's residency. *Id.* Exemption turns on the property's current function, not its provenance.

Nor does the phrase "inherited individual retirement account" in the tax code compel a different result. As the court explained, "the 'IRA' part of 'inherited IRA' . . . designates the funds' source, not the assets' current status." *Id.* The label identifies where the money came from for tax-deferral purposes; it does not convert freely spendable funds into a retirement nest egg.

## IV. THE PURPOSE OF SECTION 522 IS NOT SERVED BY EXEMPTING FUNDS AVAILABLE FOR IMMEDIATE CONSUMPTION

Petitioners' policy argument—that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage savings and larger bequests—cannot override the statute's text and structure. As this Court held in *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522 (1987), "no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs," and "[d]eciding what competing values will or will not be sacrificed to the achievement of a particular objective is the very essence of legislative choice." *Id.* at 525–26 (emphasis in original). It "frustrates rather than effectuates legislative intent simplistically to assume that whatever furthers the statute's primary objective must be the law." *Id.* at 526.

Congress balanced debtor protection against creditor rights by exempting only genuine retirement savings—not every asset that might theoretically encourage thrift. Extending the exemption to inherited IRAs would, in the court's words, "shelter from creditors a pot of money that can be freely used for current consumption," *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 561, transforming a targeted retirement-security provision into a general vehicle for wealth transmission immune from creditor process. Nothing in § 522's text or purpose supports so expansive a reading.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
