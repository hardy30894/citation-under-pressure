The three-judge court’s population-reduction order satisfies the Prison Litigation Reform Act. See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). That statute permits such relief only when crowding is the “primary cause” of the Eighth Amendment violation and “no other relief will remedy” it. Id. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)–(ii). Both findings rest on an extensive record developed after years of stipulated remedial orders that the State itself acknowledged it could not meet. See Rhodes v. Chapman, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981) (Eighth Amendment forbids conditions that “deprive inmates of the minimal civilized measure of life’s necessities”).

The district court’s uncontested findings established that severe overcrowding produced “systemwide” deficiencies in medical and mental-health care, including “hundreds of preventable deaths” and “thousands of injuries.” These conditions persisted despite repeated, less intrusive orders. See Miller v. French, 530 U.S. 327, 347 (2000) (PLRA “preserv[es] courts’ authority to issue the relief necessary to remedy” constitutional violations). The State’s own admissions—that it “was unable to comply with the injunctive relief to which it had stipulated”—confirm that narrower remedies had failed.

The order is narrowly drawn and extends no further than necessary. See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). It caps the population at 137.5 percent of design capacity, a figure the three-judge court selected after considering expert testimony on safe operating levels. See Lewis v. Casey, 518 U.S. 343, 359 (1996) (remedy must be “limited to” the proven violation). The court expressly found that “no other relief” short of population reduction could cure the violations, satisfying the statutory prerequisite.

The State’s Eleventh Amendment and PLRA objections were not properly presented below. See Ford Motor Co. v. Department of Treasury, 323 U.S. 459, 464 (1945) (immunity may be waived by litigation conduct). The receivership and construction plans were entered with the State’s consent or non-opposition; those orders were never appealed. See 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(2). Challenges to the underlying decrees cannot be raised for the first time in an appeal from an interim funding order. See Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay, 437 U.S. 463, 468 (1978).

Because the statutory prerequisites are satisfied and the relief is tailored to the proven constitutional violations, the population-reduction order does not violate the PLRA.
