**ARGUMENT**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (“TPS”) can never satisfy 8 U.S.C. § 1255’s threshold requirement that an applicant be “inspected and admitted or paroled.” The better reading of the Immigration and Nationality Act is that TPS places a noncitizen in a lawful immigration status that Congress expressly made relevant to adjustment of status, and that Congress did not exclude TPS recipients from § 1255’s remedial scheme. See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1254a(f)(4), 1255(a). At a minimum, the statutory text is ambiguous, and the Court should adopt the construction that gives effect to Congress’s decision to treat TPS recipients as “being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4); cf. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984).

First, TPS is not merely a waiver of removability; Congress directed that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for adjustment-of-status purposes. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). That command matters because § 1255 is the statute governing who may adjust status. Congress knew how to require a prior lawful entry when it wanted to do so. See 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A) (defining “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer”); 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). But Congress also knew how to create exceptions and alternate pathways within § 1255 itself. See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1255(h), (i), (k), (m). The presence of those tailored exceptions shows that § 1255 is not a rigid one-size-fits-all statute. See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987).

Second, the agency’s longstanding view supports Petitioners. Soon after Congress enacted TPS, the INS General Counsel concluded that TPS should not be construed as an admission, but the agency’s own regulatory framework did not foreclose adjustment for TPS recipients who satisfied § 1255 otherwise. See Temporary Protected Status and Eligibility for Adjustment of Status under Section [1255], INS Gen. Counsel Op. No. 91-27, 1991 WL 1185138 (Mar. 4, 1991); see also De Leon-Ochoa v. Attorney General, 622 F.3d 341, 353–54 (3d Cir. 2010) (TPS is not “a program of entry for an alien”). More importantly, the Board has recognized that an adjustment of status “generally constitutes an admission.” In re Espinosa-Guillot, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). That principle confirms that Congress does not treat “admission” and “status” as mutually exclusive in the way the Government urges. See Hanif v. Attorney General, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012).

Third, the Government’s reading makes § 1254a(f)(4) largely superfluous. If TPS merely confers a dead-end lawful status with no relevance to adjustment, Congress’s explicit instruction that TPS recipients be “considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” would do little work in the statute Congress enacted. Courts avoid constructions that render statutory language “inoperative or superfluous.” Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004). The better reading is that Congress created a status that counts for adjustment purposes when the remaining elements of § 1255 are satisfied.

Fourth, the decisions below improperly collapsed “admission” into physical entry. But the Court has repeatedly recognized that immigration status can change without a new physical entry. See Hanif, 694 F.3d at 485; Taveras v. Attorney General, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013). And because Congress used “lawful status” in § 1254a(f)(4), it is unreasonable to read that language as having no operative effect in the one statute—§ 1255—that governs adjustment of status.

Finally, the purpose of TPS supports Petitioners. Congress enacted TPS to protect nationals of countries “temporarily unable to handle adequately the return of their nationals,” see 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1), and the program “was designed to shield aliens already in the country from removal” after disaster or conflict, De Leon-Ochoa, 622 F.3d at 353–54. A construction that forces TPS recipients to remain forever ineligible for adjustment despite Congress’s express command that they be treated as lawfully present for adjustment purposes contradicts that humanitarian design.

For these reasons, this Court should hold that TPS may satisfy § 1255’s admission requirement, or at minimum that the statute does not categorically foreclose adjustment for TPS recipients.
