## ARGUMENT

**I. Rule 23(b)(3)'s predominance requirement demands admissible, rigorous proof—not mere assertion—that common questions actually predominate, including as to damages.**

Class treatment is an exception to the ordinary process of individual litigation, and it may bind absent parties only where their interests are genuinely and demonstrably aligned on the questions to be tried in common. See *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 41 (1940) (class members may be bound by a judgment only where they are, in fact, adequately represented on truly common questions). That principle applies with special force to predominance under Rule 23(b)(3), which asks whether *common* questions—not merely questions that are common in label—will actually predominate at trial. A court cannot answer that question by accepting an expert's assurance that a "methodology" exists; it must determine, on the actual evidentiary record, that the methodology is capable of proving the specific theory of liability on a classwide basis. Anything less converts Rule 23 into a pleading formality rather than a rigorous evidentiary gatekeeping function.

**II. Antitrust damages doctrine requires that classwide evidence of impact correspond to the certified theory of liability; a model untethered to that theory is not competent evidence of injury.**

This Court has long distinguished between the *fact* of antitrust damage, which must be proven with reasonable certainty, and the *amount*, which may be approximated. In *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931), the Court held that "while the damages may not be determined by mere speculation or guess, it will be enough if the evidence show the extent of the damages as a matter of just and reasonable inference, although the result be only approximate." But that latitude presupposes that the plaintiff has already shown, with evidence tied to the unlawful conduct, that the injury in fact flowed from that conduct. *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927) (damages recoverable only insofar as they flow from the defendant's unlawful conduct). Similarly, in *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 265 (1946), the Court explained that "the most elementary conceptions of justice and public policy require that the wrongdoer shall bear the risk of the uncertainty which his own wrong has created," but that rule relieves plaintiffs only of the burden of precise quantification—not the burden of showing that the *specific* wrong charged, and no other, caused the injury alleged.

This Court reaffirmed the distinction in *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100 (1969), holding that antitrust plaintiffs must still establish the fact of damage attributable to the unlawful conduct with evidence that rises above "speculation or guesswork," even though the amount may be estimated. *Id.* at 123–25. A damages model that lumps together injury from multiple theories of liability—several of which have been rejected—cannot satisfy even this threshold showing, because it cannot demonstrate that the injury it quantifies is the injury caused by the conduct actually at issue. The Third Circuit's own precedent recognizes the same causal requirement in the antitrust context. See *William Goldman Theatres, Inc. v. Loew's, Inc.*, 150 F.2d 738 (3d Cir. 1945) (damages must be causally connected to the specific unlawful practice charged, not to lawful competitive conduct occurring in the same market).

**III. Expert evidence offered to satisfy Rule 23(b)(3) must meet ordinary standards of reliability before a court may rely on it to certify a class.**

Courts have never permitted expert opinion to substitute for reliable methodology merely because it is offered by a qualified witness. See *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923) (expert deduction must be "sufficiently established to have gained general acceptance in the particular field in which it belongs" before a court may rely upon it). A finding that rests on an unreliable or mismatched methodology is not simply a matter of weighing conflicting evidence—it is legal error dressed as fact-finding, and the "clearly erroneous" standard does not immunize it. See *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (a finding is clearly erroneous where the reviewing court is "left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed"). A district court that certifies a class based on a damages model incapable of isolating the injury caused by the certified theory of liability has applied an incorrect legal standard to the predominance inquiry, not merely made a permissible factual choice between competing views of ambiguous evidence.

**IV. The class here was certified without admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a classwide basis consistent with the sole surviving theory of liability.**

The district court accepted a single regression model built to quantify overcharges flowing from four distinct theories of anticompetitive conduct, even after three of those theories were rejected and only the "overbuilder deterrence" theory survived. Under *Story Parchment*, *Eastman Kodak*, *Bigelow*, and *Zenith Radio*, respondents were required to show that the injury quantified by their model was, in fact, the injury caused by that surviving theory—not injury attributable to conduct no longer part of the case. Because the model made no such isolation, it could not constitute reliable common evidence under *Frye*, and the district court's reliance on it was not a permissible choice between two views of the evidence but an error of law under *Gypsum*. Nor could it satisfy the due-process foundation for binding absent class members recognized in *Hansberry*, since the "common" question of damages was, in truth, incapable of common proof. The Third Circuit's affirmance should be reversed.
