# ARGUMENT

## I. Federal Courts Possess Broad Equitable Authority to Fashion Structural Remedies Adequate to Eliminate an Established, Ongoing Constitutional Violation.

A court sitting in equity has always possessed the authority to mold its remedy to the scope of the wrong before it. As this Court explained in *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944), "the essence of equity jurisdiction has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case." *Id.* at 329. That flexible authority is not confined to individual relief; it extends to structural decrees that reach the systemic conditions producing the violation. *See In re Debs*, 158 U.S. 564, 593 (1895) (recognizing the broad power of a federal court to enter relief adequate to secure compliance with federal law).

Nor is such relief static once entered. In *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106 (1932), this Court confirmed that a court retains continuing power to supervise its own decree and to adjust it as circumstances require, so long as any modification remains tethered to the violation the decree was designed to correct. *Id.* at 114-15. The three-judge court's order here reflects precisely this traditional, calibrated exercise of equitable power: after extensive fact-finding, the court determined that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of the ongoing Eighth Amendment violations, considered alternatives, and retained jurisdiction to adjust the order as conditions changed. The PLRA's requirement that relief be "narrowly drawn" and the "least intrusive means" necessary does not displace this traditional framework—it codifies it. A remedy tailored, as this one is, to the specific overcrowding shown to cause the violation is the very embodiment of proportional equitable relief.

## II. This Court's Desegregation Precedents Confirm That District Courts May Order Specific, Systemic Relief When Necessary to Eliminate an Entrenched Constitutional Violation.

The State's contention that a population reduction order is inherently too intrusive cannot be squared with this Court's repeated approval of comparably far-reaching remedies where state officials failed for years to correct a proven constitutional wrong. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294 (1955) ("*Brown II*"), this Court remanded the desegregation cases "to take such proceedings and enter such orders and decrees consistent with this opinion as are necessary and proper to admit to public schools on a racially nondiscriminatory basis," *id.* at 301, entrusting the district courts with broad discretion to design remedies commensurate with the violation—even where compliance required restructuring the operation of state institutions.

That discretion was substantive, not nominal. In *Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, 377 U.S. 218 (1964), this Court held that continued resistance justified extraordinary remedial measures, explaining that "the time for mere 'deliberate speed' has run out." *Id.* at 234. And in *Green v. County School Board of New Kent County*, 391 U.S. 430 (1968), this Court confirmed that once a constitutional violation of this character is established, the responsible officials bear "an affirmative duty to take whatever steps might be necessary" to eliminate it "root and branch." *Id.* at 437-38. Likewise, in *Louisiana v. United States*, 380 U.S. 145 (1965), this Court held that a federal court confronting an entrenched constitutional violation "has not merely the power but the duty to render a decree which will so far as possible eliminate the discriminatory effects of the past as well as bar like discrimination in the future." *Id.* at 154.

These precedents establish a consistent principle: when a coordinate branch of state government has, over years of litigation, failed to remedy an adjudicated constitutional violation, a federal court does not exceed its authority by ordering specific, quantifiable relief—whether expressed as pupil assignment ratios, as in *Green*, or as a population ceiling calibrated to design capacity, as here. The order below is directly analogous to the remedial decrees this Court has consistently sustained, and it should likewise be sustained.

## III. The Order Is a Proportionate Response to an Ongoing Violation of the Eighth Amendment's Prohibition on Cruel and Unusual Punishment.

The gravity of the underlying violation confirms the propriety of the remedy. The Eighth Amendment, binding on the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962), draws its meaning from "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). This Court has long recognized that punishment inflicting needless suffering offends the Constitution regardless of its formal legality. *See Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 366-67 (1910). A prison system so overcrowded that inmates are systematically denied adequate medical and mental health care—as the district court found after extensive evidentiary hearings—inflicts exactly the kind of needless, unredressed suffering the Eighth Amendment forbids. Where, as in *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958), state officials' own persistent noncompliance has perpetuated a constitutional violation, "the constitutional rights of [the injured parties] are not to be sacrificed or yielded" to administrative or fiscal inconvenience. *Id.* at 16. The State cannot now claim that a remedy proportionate to the violation it allowed to fester for years exceeds the PLRA's bounds.

## CONCLUSION

The population order is narrowly drawn, reflects the least intrusive means available to correct a violation of constitutional dimension, and is fully consistent with this Court's settled equitable and Eighth Amendment jurisprudence. The judgment below should be affirmed.
