## ARGUMENT

### I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, viewing the record in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and drawing all reasonable inferences in that party's favor. *Torgerson v. City of Rochester*, 643 F.3d 1031, 1042 (8th Cir. 2011) (en banc). Summary judgment is proper only where "there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact." *Id.* At this stage, "[t]he evidence of the non-movant is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in his favor." *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). A court may not weigh the evidence or make credibility determinations; those functions belong to the jury. *Id.*; *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322–23 (1986).

### II. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on Appellants' FRSA Retaliation Claims

FRSA retaliation claims are analyzed under the burden-shifting framework borrowed from the Wendell H. Ford Aviation Investment and Reform Act for the 21st Century, 49 U.S.C. § 42121(b), as incorporated by 49 U.S.C. § 20109(d)(2)(A). *Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co.*, 768 F.3d 786, 789 (8th Cir. 2014). An employee must first show, by a preponderance of the evidence, that (1) he engaged in protected activity; (2) the employer knew he engaged in the protected activity; (3) he suffered an unfavorable personnel action; and (4) the protected activity was a contributing factor in the unfavorable action. *Id.* If the employee satisfies that showing, the burden shifts to the employer to demonstrate "by clear and convincing evidence" that it would have taken the same action in the absence of the protected activity. *Id.*

**A. Appellants engaged in activity protected under § 20109(a)(1)(C).**

Section 20109(a)(1)(C) protects an employee who reports, in good faith, "a hazardous safety or security condition." 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). Kline and Snyder's written statements to Trainmaster Dixon identifying the bridge's lack of side rails and lighting, and Foster's recorded statement to Claims Representative Murphy noting that BNSF had previously been told the crew-change location was dangerous, are textbook reports of a hazardous safety condition. BNSF does not seriously dispute that this conduct falls within the statute; the district court's error lies at the causation step.

**B. The record contains sufficient evidence that Appellants' safety reports were a contributing factor in BNSF's discipline.**

The "contributing factor" standard is deliberately forgiving. *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789. It requires only a showing of "any factor, which, alone or in connection with other factors, tends to affect in any way the outcome of the decision." *Araujo v. N.J. Transit Rail Operations, Inc.*, 708 F.3d 152, 158 (3d Cir. 2013). This Court has recognized that temporal proximity between protected activity and an adverse action, combined with other circumstantial evidence, can defeat summary judgment. *Blackorby v. BNSF Ry. Co.*, 849 F.3d 716, 723 (8th Cir. 2017); *Heim v. BNSF Ry. Co.*, 849 F.3d 723, 727 (8th Cir. 2017).

Here, the temporal sequence is stark: Foster gave her recorded statement identifying the known hazard on April 5; the very next day, BNSF issued notice that it was investigating the plaintiffs for rule infractions. Snyder and Kline gave further statements on April 12 and 13 reiterating the unsafe conditions, and thereafter, according to the record, both were subjected to heightened, undisclosed operations testing while the disciplinary proceeding remained pending. A reasonable jury could find that this sequence—investigation announced one day after an employee's safety complaint, followed by covert surveillance of the complaining employees—is far more than coincidence.

The record also reveals a substantial conflict of interest bearing directly on causation. Knutstrom and Dixon, the two witnesses whose testimony formed the basis for finding rule violations, were the same individuals responsible for directing the crew-change location that the plaintiffs identified as hazardous. Their account—that the crew change was to occur east of the bridge—directly contradicted the plaintiffs' account and shifted blame for the accident away from BNSF's own instructions and onto the employees who had reported the hazard. A biased decisionmaker's role in the chain of adverse action is a recognized basis for inferring retaliatory causation. *See Staub v. Proctor Hosp.*, 562 U.S. 411, 421 (2011) (an adverse action tainted by a biased supervisor's report can support liability even where a nominally independent decisionmaker formally imposes the discipline). Because Thompson's decision rested on the transcripts and exhibits generated by Dixon and Knutstrom, a jury could find that the taint of their conflicted testimony contributed to the discipline imposed on Foster, Kline, and Snyder.

Taken together—the one-day gap between Foster's report and the investigation notice, the subsequent covert testing of Kline and Snyder, and the self-interested testimony of the very officials whose instructions the plaintiffs' reports implicated—this evidence is more than sufficient for a reasonable jury to find that Appellants' protected safety reports were a contributing factor in BNSF's disciplinary decisions. The district court erred by resolving these disputed inferences against Appellants at summary judgment. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255.

### III. BNSF Cannot Show by Clear and Convincing Evidence That It Would Have Disciplined Appellants Absent Their Protected Reports

Even if BNSF could point to legitimate rule infractions—failure to use flashlights, improper headlight operation, or track fouling—the clear-and-convincing standard is exacting and factual disputes about the credibility and motivations of BNSF's witnesses preclude resolution on summary judgment. *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 791. Where, as here, the very witnesses supplying the basis for discipline had reason to deflect blame from their own safety instructions, a jury—not the court—must decide whether BNSF has met its heightened burden.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
