# ARGUMENT

## FEDERAL COURTS DO NOT HAVE EXCLUSIVE JURISDICTION OVER EVERY CASE IN WHICH A PATENT-LAW QUESTION IS EMBEDDED WITHIN A STATE-LAW CAUSE OF ACTION

Congress vested federal district courts with jurisdiction over civil actions "arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents," and made that jurisdiction exclusive of the state courts. This grant of jurisdiction, however, has never been construed to sweep within it every dispute that happens to touch upon a patent-law issue. This Court's precedents make clear that the "arising under" inquiry demands a searching and discriminating analysis—not a mechanical inquiry into whether patent law is mentioned in the pleadings.

### A. This Court Has Long Required a Substantial and Actually Contested Federal Question Before Federal Jurisdiction Attaches

The touchstone of "arising under" jurisdiction is not whether federal law is merely relevant to a case, but whether the case "really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). A suit does not arise under federal law "unless it really and substantially" turns on that law; the mere presence of a federal ingredient is not enough.

This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), explaining that federal jurisdiction requires courts to engage in "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Id.* at 117-18. Justice Cardozo's opinion for the Court cautioned against a jurisdictional test so permissive that it would "expand the jurisdiction of the district courts" beyond what Congress intended whenever a federal issue is lurking somewhere in the case. *Id.* The federal question, this Court held, must be "an essential element of the plaintiff's cause of action," and it must be disclosed upon the face of the complaint, "unaided by anything alleged in anticipation of avoidance of defenses." *Id.* at 112-13.

Here, Minton's legal malpractice claim is a creature of Texas law, requiring proof of duty, breach, causation, and damages under state tort principles. That resolving a hypothetical patent-law defense—the experimental-use exception—may be necessary to prove the "case within a case" of causation does not transform this state tort action into one arising under federal patent law. The patent question is not "an essential element" of the cause of action in the sense *Gully* requires; it is, at most, a hypothetical inquiry into what a federal tribunal would have done in a suit that has already concluded.

### B. Federal Jurisdiction Must Not Disturb the Congressionally Approved Balance Between State and Federal Judicial Responsibility

Even where a federal issue is embedded in a state-law claim, this Court has recognized that jurisdiction cannot be permitted to upset the settled division of labor between the state and federal judiciaries. In *Gully*, this Court emphasized that if any federal ingredient were sufficient to confer jurisdiction, "the courts of the United States. . . might be found to embrace every case in which title to property might be traced back to a patent, a treaty, or a statute of the United States." 299 U.S. at 116. Such a result would be intolerable, for it would federalize vast swaths of state tort, contract, and property law merely because a federal instrument happened to appear somewhere in the chain of title or proof.

Legal malpractice actions are a paradigm of state judicial responsibility. States possess sovereign authority to regulate the practice of law within their borders, to define the duties attorneys owe their clients, and to provide remedies for the breach of those duties. Nothing in the patent laws suggests that Congress intended to strip the states of this traditional authority merely because an attorney's alleged negligence occurred in the course of federal patent litigation. Indeed, in *Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800 (1988), this Court reaffirmed that a claim arises under patent law for purposes of §1338(a) only where "the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, in that patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims." *Id.* at 808-09. The word "substantial" was not surplusage. It was designed to screen out precisely the sort of backward-looking, case-within-a-case inquiry presented here, where the federal patent question is significant only to the parties before the court and carries no precedential consequence for the develop­ment or uniformity of patent law itself.

### C. The Patent Issue in a Legal Malpractice Suit Is Hypothetical, Backward-Looking, and Devoid of the Prospective Significance That Justifies Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction

The entire rationale for centralizing patent adjudication in the federal courts is to secure nationwide uniformity in the substantive law of patents—a body of law that directly affects the scope of a property right good against the world. But when a state court adjudicates a legal malpractice claim, it does not adjudicate the validity or scope of any patent. It does not create, destroy, or modify any patent right. It asks only what a hypothetical, "case within a case" federal court would have done, on the record as it existed, had the attorney raised a particular defense. This retrospective and case-specific inquiry lacks the broader consequence that has always justified departing from the ordinary state-court forum.

This Court's decision in *Shulthis* is instructive by analogy: there, the Court held that a suit did not arise under federal law simply because federal statutes formed part of the chain of title to the property at issue, for the case turned "upon the construction of a will and the effect of certain conveyances," not upon "the disputed construction of the acts of Congress." 225 U.S. at 570. So too here: this case turns on the construction of Texas tort law, not upon any prospective construction of the Patent Act that would bind future litigants or affect the rights of patentees generally. The patent-law issue is confined to the four corners of the malpractice dispute between Minton and his former counsel, and a decision resolving it will have no binding effect in any subsequent patent case.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be affirmed, and this Court should hold that state courts retain jurisdiction over legal malpractice claims of this kind, notwithstanding the embedded patent-law issue.
