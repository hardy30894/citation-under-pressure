**Argument**

The challenged Florida statute burdens core political speech and association by forcing lobbyists and principals to disclose sweeping information about their advocacy and by banning contingency-fee compensation for legislative and executive lobbying. The First Amendment does not permit such restraints absent a constitutionally adequate justification. *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976); *First Nat’l Bank of Boston v. Bellotti*, 435 U.S. 765 (1978); *Citizens Against Rent Control v. City of Berkeley*, 454 U.S. 290 (1981). Because the statute reaches substantially protected activity and imposes facially invalid burdens on association, the disclosure provisions cannot survive, and the contingency-fee prohibition should be invalidated as well.

I. **The disclosure provisions burden protected speech and association and are facially overbroad.**

Political advocacy directed at government officials lies at the heart of the First Amendment. *Meyer v. Grant*, 486 U.S. 414 (1988); *Riley v. Nat’l Fed’n of the Blind of N.C., Inc.*, 487 U.S. 781 (1988). Lobbying is a quintessential form of petitioning and political expression, not mere commercial conduct. See *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612 (1954). Florida’s scheme compels disclosure of “all lobbying expenditures,” including expenditures made by the lobbyist or the principal, together with the “source of funds” for those expenditures and detailed categorical reporting. Such compelled disclosure is constitutional only where it is sufficiently tailored to an important governmental interest. *Buckley*, 424 U.S. at 64-68; *McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm’n*, 514 U.S. 334 (1995).

Here, the breadth of the statute is the problem. It does not merely require disclosure of direct payments to influence specific legislation; it sweeps in all lobbying expenditures and their funding sources. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that compelled disclosure can chill association and speech by exposing speakers to harassment or reprisals. *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449 (1958); *Buckley*, 424 U.S. at 74; *Americans for Prosperity Found. v. Bonta*, 141 S. Ct. 2373 (2021). Florida’s law is especially vulnerable because it applies to all lobbying activity without a narrow focus on corruption prevention, and the availability of an advisory opinion procedure does not cure overbreadth in the statute’s substantive command. See *City of Lakewood v. Plain Dealer Publ’g Co.*, 486 U.S. 750 (1988).

The State may invoke transparency, but “more disclosure” is not always constitutionally better. *Brown v. Socialist Workers ’74 Campaign Comm.*, 459 U.S. 87 (1982), recognized that disclosure may be impermissible where the record shows a reasonable probability of threats, harassment, or reprisals. Florida’s statutory breadth forces disclosure first and leaves privacy concerns to be vindicated only after the fact. That inversion is incompatible with the First Amendment. *McIntyre*, 514 U.S. at 341-42; *Bonta*, 141 S. Ct. at 2386-87.

Because the statute regulates a large amount of protected lobbying speech and association in relation to any legitimate interest, it is facially overbroad. *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601 (1973); *Virginia v. Hicks*, 539 U.S. 113 (2003).

II. **The contingency-fee ban is unconstitutional.**

The Florida Supreme Court may have upheld similar restrictions long ago, but the federal constitutional question is governed by Supreme Court precedent protecting the right to engage counsel and advocates on terms chosen by the parties. In *Meyer*, 486 U.S. 414, the Court held that the State may not unduly burden political advocacy by restricting the means of expression. In *Riley*, 487 U.S. 781, the Court struck down regulations that burdened compensated solicitation and advocacy. Those cases underscore that Florida’s categorical ban on compensation contingent upon successful lobbying impermissibly restricts the market for advocacy services.

Although the State may rely on *Providence Tool Co. v. Norris*, 69 U.S. (2 Wall.) 45 (1864), and *Hazelton v. Sheckels*, 202 U.S. 71 (1906), those decisions are antiquated and cannot control in light of the Court’s modern First Amendment jurisprudence. To the extent they survive, they do not address a statute that directly burdens political advocacy and petitioning. The Court has repeatedly invalidated laws that burden expressive conduct by limiting how speakers may organize and finance advocacy. *Buckley*, 424 U.S. 1; *Meyer*, 486 U.S. 414; *Riley*, 487 U.S. 781.

Nor can the ban be sustained as an anti-corruption measure. The Court has recognized that laws aimed at corruption must be closely drawn to prevent quid pro quo arrangements. *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010); *McCutcheon v. FEC*, 572 U.S. 185 (2014). A blanket ban on contingency compensation sweeps far beyond any demonstrable corruption risk by prohibiting an entire compensation structure for all lobbying, regardless of whether any improper influence exists.

III. **Facial invalidation is warranted.**

A facial challenge succeeds where a statute lacks any plainly legitimate sweep or is unconstitutional in a substantial number of applications. *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739 (1987); *Washington State Grange v. Washington State Republican Party*, 552 U.S. 442 (2008). Here, the disclosure provisions reach far beyond any narrow anticorruption rationale, and the contingency-fee ban suppresses a protected mode of political advocacy in all its applications. The district court therefore erred in entering summary judgment for the State.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
