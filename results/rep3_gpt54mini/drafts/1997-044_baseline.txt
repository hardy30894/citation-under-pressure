**ARGUMENT**

I. **Section 523(a)(2)(A) bars discharge of the entire debt resulting from fraud, not merely the amount initially obtained.**

The Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by” “false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). This Court has recognized that exceptions to discharge are designed to prevent the Bankruptcy Code’s fresh start from becoming a shield for dishonest conduct. Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 287 (1991). The question is not whether the debtor can discharge a debt he created through fraud, but whether the debt “result[ed] from” fraud in the first place. Cohen’s debt plainly did.

The courts below held that Cohen committed “actual fraud” when he charged rents in excess of lawful limits, and that ruling is not challenged here. Once fraud is established, § 523(a)(2)(A) reaches the resulting debt in full. Nothing in the statute limits nondischargeability to the precise dollar amount the debtor first acquired. The text bars discharge of “any debt” for money or property obtained by fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). As this Court later confirmed in **Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. 213 (1998)**, “[o]nce it is established that specific money or property has been obtained by fraud,” “any debt” arising from that fraud is excepted from discharge. Id. at 218-19.

That reading is consistent with the structure of the Code. Section 523(a)(2)(A) focuses on the debt, not merely the original transfer. Id. at 219-20. A contrary rule would let a debtor discharge the very liabilities that state law imposes because of his fraud, including treble damages and fees, even though those liabilities arise directly from the fraudulent conduct. The Code does not require such an artificial distinction.

II. **Damages, treble damages, fees, and costs arising from fraud are nondischargeable because they are part of the debt “for” fraud.**

Cohen argues that only the tenants’ overcharge claim is nondischargeable, while statutory enhancements, attorney’s fees, and costs are dischargeable. But this Court has squarely rejected that limitation. In **Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. 213 (1998)**, the Court held that “any debt” arising from fraud includes “treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs.” Id. at 223. The Court explained that once fraud has been shown, § 523(a)(2)(A) prevents discharge of all liability arising from it, not just compensatory damages. Id. at 218-19.

That conclusion follows from the ordinary meaning of “debt,” which the Code defines broadly as “liability on a claim.” 11 U.S.C. § 101(12). A state-law judgment for treble damages and fees is a liability on the same fraud claim. As this Court observed in **Cohen**, the statute’s phrase “to the extent obtained by” modifies the money or property originally obtained, not the later measure of the creditor’s recovery. 523 U.S. at 220-21. Once the debtor obtained money by fraud, all debt arising from that fraud is included. Id.

III. **The fresh-start policy does not protect the dishonest debtor.**

Cohen invokes bankruptcy’s fresh-start policy, but that policy aids only the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991). The Court has repeatedly declined to permit discharge where doing so would undermine the Code’s express exceptions for fraud. Id. at 287; see also **Bullock v. BankChampaign, N.A., 569 U.S. 267, 275 (2013)** (bankruptcy exceptions reflect Congress’s judgment that certain debts should survive discharge despite the fresh start).

Here, Cohen was found to have committed actual fraud under § 523(a)(2)(A). The Bankruptcy Code does not convert such a debtor into the beneficiary of a clean slate for the very liabilities his fraud generated. As this Court later summarized in **Cohen**, “[a]ll liability arising from fraud is excepted from discharge.” 523 U.S. at 220-21. That rule best fits the statutory text and the equitable principle that bankruptcy is not intended to reward deceit.

IV. **The judgment should be affirmed.**

The courts below correctly held that Cohen’s fraud-related liabilities are nondischargeable. Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge not only the amount initially obtained by fraud, but the full debt arising from that fraud, including statutory treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs. Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. 213, 223 (1998). The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
