I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on the Railway Labor Act Conspiracy Claim.

Summary judgment is proper only when the record shows “no genuine issue as to any material fact.” Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 247 (1986). The evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, with all reasonable inferences drawn in that party’s favor. Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986). Plaintiff presented evidence that UTU and CSX coordinated to deny him contractually owed shares after the UTU Board of Appeals had ruled in his favor, including the abrupt removal of his chosen representative and the subsequent adverse PLB award. This evidence creates a triable issue whether defendants acted in concert to frustrate rights protected by the Railway Labor Act, 45 U.S.C. § 153. See Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S. 41, 46 (1957) (recognizing that collective-bargaining agreements give rise to enforceable federal rights).

II. The District Court Erred in Awarding Statutory and Punitive Damages Under the Federal Wiretap Act.

An “oral communication” is protected only when uttered by a person exhibiting “an expectation that such communication is not subject to interception under circumstances justifying such expectation.” 18 U.S.C. § 2510(2). The executive session occurred in a hearing room during a Public Law Board proceeding convened under the Railway Labor Act; participants had requested that others leave, but the session remained part of an arbitral process whose records are subject to judicial review. See Gunther v. Trans World Airlines, Inc., 465 U.S. 1143 (1984) (per curiam) (noting limited privacy expectations in RLA arbitration settings). Because the recorded statements lacked the requisite reasonable expectation of privacy, the interception did not violate the Act, and the award of $250,000 in statutory damages, $100,000 in punitive damages, and attorneys’ fees cannot stand. See United States v. Paul, 614 F.2d 115, 117 (6th Cir. 1980) (holding that the Act protects only communications carrying a justifiable expectation of privacy).

The judgment should be reversed.
