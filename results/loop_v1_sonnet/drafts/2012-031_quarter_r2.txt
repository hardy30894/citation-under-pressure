**ARGUMENT**

**A district court may not certify a class under Rule 23(b)(3) without admissible common evidence establishing that damages are capable of measurement on a class-wide basis.**

Class certification is not a mere procedural formality; it determines whether individuals who never appeared before the court will nonetheless be bound by its judgment. Because absent class members are bound without personally litigating their claims, due process requires that their interests be genuinely aligned with, and adequately represented by, the named plaintiffs on every element of the claim, including damages. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32 (1940). That guarantee traces to the earliest representative suits in equity, which permitted one party to sue on behalf of a class only where the class shared a truly common right or interest in the subject of the litigation. *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288 (1853). A class predicated on an antitrust damages theory that cannot actually be proved with common evidence fails this basic requirement, because it forces individualized inquiries into the back door of a supposedly unitary proceeding.

The predominance inquiry cannot be satisfied by evidence going only to *amount* of damages while ignoring whether the *fact* of common injury can be shown class-wide. This Court has long distinguished between proof of the fact of damage and proof of the extent of damage, holding that while the latter may be estimated, the former must rest on a sound evidentiary foundation and not on speculation. *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555 (1931). Uncertainty in quantifying the amount of harm is tolerable only after the plaintiff has shown, through competent proof, that the wrong caused a discernible injury common to the class. *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359 (1927). Even where courts permit a jury to estimate damages using the best available evidence once the fact of injury is established, that estimate must still be tethered to a methodology that actually measures the injury the plaintiffs allege—not one that measures something else, or that sweeps in harms attributable to conduct never certified as a basis for class treatment. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946). This Court reaffirmed in *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100 (1969), that antitrust plaintiffs must still supply proof connecting the alleged violation to the claimed injury before any relaxed standard for calculating amount comes into play. And in evaluating whether a plaintiff's theory of injury holds together, courts must examine the plaintiffs' evidence as an integrated whole rather than crediting isolated fragments; but that principle, articulated in *Continental Ore Co. v. Union Carbide & Carbon Corp.*, 370 U.S. 690 (1962), presupposes that the evidence, taken together, in fact supports the theory of liability actually pressed—not a broader or different theory that the evidence happens to address.

Here, the courts below inverted that analysis. The District Court accepted a damages model built by Plaintiffs' expert, Dr. McClave, that purported to quantify harm from four distinct theories of anticompetitive conduct, even though three of those theories were later stripped from the case and only the “overbuilder deterrence” theory survived to support certification. Behrend v. Comcast Corp., 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). No admissible evidence was offered to isolate what portion, if any, of the model's aggregate damage figure was attributable to the single surviving theory as opposed to the discarded ones. That is precisely the sort of speculation that *Story Parchment* and *Eastman Kodak* forbid: a model that may reflect *some* injury does not establish that the injury common to the class is the injury actually pleaded. A method that cannot distinguish injury flowing from the certified theory from injury flowing from rejected theories is not evidence of common damages at all; it is guesswork dressed in the language of statistics.

The Third Circuit compounded this error by reviewing the District Court's acceptance of the damages model under a deferential standard without first confirming that the model was legally capable of proving what Rule 23(b)(3) requires it to prove. Even under the deferential clear-error standard that ordinarily governs factual findings, a court of appeals may set aside a finding that lacks adequate evidentiary grounding even though some evidence nominally supports it. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). A damages methodology that cannot be tied to the certified theory of liability is not merely a debatable inference from conflicting evidence—it is an evidentiary gap of the kind that permits reversal under the standard this Court articulated in *Gypsum*. *Id.*

Because Rule 23(b)(3) demands proof, not assumption, that damages can be proved on a class-wide basis consistent with the class's own theory of liability, and because the model approved below cannot make that showing, the judgment below rested on an impermissible substitution of aggregation for admissible common proof. The decisions of this Court in *Story Parchment*, *Eastman Kodak*, *Bigelow*, *Zenith Radio*, and *Continental Ore* establish that antitrust damages, however imprecise in amount, must be causally and evidentially connected to the wrong alleged. That connection was never established here. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
