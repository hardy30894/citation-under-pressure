**Argument**

The Sixth Amendment does not bar the government from relying on Bryant’s prior tribal-court convictions as predicates under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). This Court’s decision in *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), supplies the governing principle: a valid uncounseled misdemeanor conviction may be used later to increase punishment. Id. at 748–49. Section 117(a) does not punish Bryant for the tribal offenses themselves; it punishes the new domestic assault offense, while using the prior convictions only to define recidivist status. The Supreme Court has repeatedly explained that recidivist laws “penalize only the last offense committed by the defendant.” *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747; see also *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948).

Bryant’s theory wrongly conflates the constitutional rules governing the prior proceedings with the distinct question whether those convictions may be used later. In *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367 (1979), the Court held that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is triggered when imprisonment is actually imposed. Id. at 373–74. *Nichols* then held that a prior uncounseled conviction valid under *Scott* may be considered at sentencing notwithstanding the absence of counsel. 511 U.S. at 743–49. Nothing in *Nichols* confines that rule to sentencing only in the formal sense; its reasoning is broader. The Court rejected the notion that the Constitution forbids all later use of a valid uncounseled conviction. Id. at 746–49. Section 117(a)’s recidivist element is such a later use.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with *Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654 (2002). *Shelton* held that a suspended sentence that may lead to imprisonment cannot be imposed without counsel. Id. at 662–67. But the Court was careful to distinguish the validity of the conviction from the validity of the sentence: a constitutionally defective sentence does not erase the underlying conviction. See id. at 674. Here, Bryant’s tribal convictions were valid when entered under the governing tribal procedures. The Sixth Amendment does not apply of its own force to tribal-court prosecutions. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384–85 (1896); *Duro v. Reina*, 495 U.S. 676, 693 (1990), superseded by statute on other grounds as recognized in *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 203–06 (2004). The proper constitutional inquiry is therefore not whether those convictions would have complied with the Sixth Amendment in federal court, but whether Congress may use valid tribal convictions as predicates in a federal recidivist statute. *Lara*, 541 U.S. at 200–07, confirms Congress’s broad authority over Indian affairs.

That result is also consistent with the Court’s treatment of recidivism enhancements. In *Parke v. Raley*, 506 U.S. 20 (1992), the Court upheld the use of prior convictions in a recidivist proceeding and recognized the “presumption of regularity” that attaches to final judgments. Id. at 29–34. In *Custis v. United States*, 511 U.S. 485 (1994), the Court held that a defendant generally may not collaterally attack prior convictions used to enhance a federal sentence, except for the “failure to appoint counsel” in violation of *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963). 511 U.S. at 496–97. Bryant asks for a rule even broader than *Custis*: not merely the ability to challenge the predicate convictions, but a constitutional prohibition on Congress’s reliance on them at all. The Court has never adopted such a rule.

Finally, Bryant’s reliance on *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is misplaced. Whatever force *Ant* once had, it predates *Nichols*, *Custis*, *Parke*, and *Lara*, and it cannot override Supreme Court authority. The better reading of § 117(a) is the one adopted by the Eighth and Tenth Circuits: a valid tribal-court conviction may serve as a predicate for a federal recidivist offense even though the Sixth Amendment did not require appointed counsel in the tribal proceeding. *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 995–1000 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 600–05 (8th Cir. 2011).

The judgment below should be reversed.
