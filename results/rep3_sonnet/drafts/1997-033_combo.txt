**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Mandatory Text of Section 1407(a) Forecloses Self-Transfer Under Section 1404(a).**

Section 1407(a) provides that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand a transferred action "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." That command is unambiguous, and this Court has long held that when statutory language is plain, "the duty of interpretation does not arise, and the rules which are to aid doubtful meanings need no discussion." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Congress did not write that the Panel *may* remand, or that remand is discretionary once pretrial proceedings conclude; it wrote that the Panel "shall" do so. As this Court explained in construing an analogous mandatory command directed at judicial action, such language is "the language of command." *Escoe v. Zerbst*, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935). Congress's choice of "shall" rather than "may" was deliberate, and courts are not free to treat mandatory statutory directives as merely permissive suggestions. *Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947) (distinguishing mandatory from permissive statutory language according to the term Congress actually chose).

There is no textual exception in Section 1407(a) permitting the transferee court to avoid remand by instead invoking Section 1404(a) to transfer the case to itself. Nothing in the statute's words suggests that a transferee judge may substitute a self-directed venue transfer for the remand Congress required. As this Court observed in a different but instructive context, "[t]here is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Here, the words Congress chose leave no room for indefinite retention of a case by the transferee court under the guise of a Section 1404(a) transfer.

**II. Section 1404(a)'s General Transfer Authority Cannot Override Section 1407(a)'s Specific Remand Command.**

Section 1404(a) is a general venue provision, authorizing transfer of any civil action "[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice." It was enacted, and has been construed, as a general "housekeeping" tool for federal courts—not as a vehicle for circumventing more specific statutory schemes governing particular categories of transfer. This Court has described Section 1404(a) as "a federal housekeeping measure," permitting "easy change of venue within a unified federal system." *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 613 (1964). Section 1404(a)'s purpose is to facilitate convenient litigation of cases already properly before a court on their merits, not to confer upon a transferee court acting under a separate and more specific multidistrict statute the power to retain a case for trial that Congress commanded be remanded.

Where a specific statutory provision governs a particular subject, that provision controls over a more general provision addressing the same general area. *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222 (1957) (holding that a specific venue statute governing a defined category of actions is not displaced by resort to a general venue provision). Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the procedure by which multidistrict cases are transferred and returned; Section 1404(a) is the general venue-transfer statute applicable to ordinary civil litigation. Under the reasoning of *Fourco*, the specific remand command of Section 1407(a) must control. To permit a Section 1407(a) transferee court to invoke the general authority of Section 1404(a) to transfer a case to itself is to allow the general provision to swallow the specific one—precisely the interpretive error this Court's precedents forbid.

Moreover, this Court has recognized that Section 1404(a)'s convenience-based transfer authority operates within the ordinary course of civil litigation, where the transferor court retains discretion to weigh the convenience of parties and witnesses in the interest of justice as the case proceeds toward trial in its originating posture. *Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960). That discretion presumes a case properly situated for ordinary adjudication—not a case whose presence in the transferee district exists solely by virtue of a coordinate, purpose-limited statute that already commands its return. Nothing in *Continental Grain* or *Ex parte Collett v. Nashville Bridge Co.*, 337 U.S. 55 (1949)—which confirmed that Section 1404(a) applies according to its terms to actions properly filed and pending for adjudication—suggests that Section 1404(a) may be used to negate the express remand obligation of a wholly separate transfer statute.

**III. The Self-Transfer Practice Undermines the Statutory Scheme Congress Enacted.**

Section 1407(a) reflects a considered congressional balance: cases may be consolidated for pretrial efficiency, but the plaintiff's chosen forum, and the venue in which the case will ultimately be tried, is preserved by the mandatory remand requirement. Permitting transferee courts to invoke Section 1404(a) to assign cases to themselves for trial defeats that balance and converts a limited pretrial assignment into a permanent appropriation of the case—a result the plain text forecloses under *Caminetti*, *American Trucking Ass'ns*, and *Escoe*, and one inconsistent with the specific-governs-general principle of *Fourco*. The judgment below should be reversed.
