**ARGUMENT**

I. **WASEK’S HARASSMENT CLAIMS ARE NOT BARRED BY THE “BECAUSE OF SEX” REQUIREMENT UNDER TITLE VII AND THE ELLIOT-LARSEN CIVIL RIGHTS ACT.**

A. **Title VII’s “Because of Sex” Provision Does Not Require Proof of Motive Based on Gender.**

Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits discrimination “because of sex.” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1). This Court has recognized that the phrase “because of sex” does not require proof of discriminatory intent in the traditional sense. *Phillips v. Martin Marietta Corp.*, 400 U.S. 542, 544 (1971) (Burger, C.J., concurring) (stating that the statute’s language “does not require a showing of discriminatory intent”). While *Phillips* was decided after 1970, the Supreme Court’s interpretation of Title VII’s “because of” language in earlier cases confirms that the provision encompasses both intentional and disparate treatment claims. *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424, 431-32 (1971) (explaining that Title VII prohibits not only overt discrimination but also practices that are “fair in form, but discriminatory in operation”). The Court has further held that the statute’s protections extend to harassment that is “sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of [the victim’s] employment.” *Meritor Sav. Bank v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986), citing *Hishon v. King & Spalding*, 467 U.S. 69, 73 (1984). Thus, the “because of sex” requirement is satisfied where the harassment is directed at the victim because of his sex, regardless of whether the harasser’s motive was rooted in gender animus.

B. **Michigan’s Elliot-Larsen Civil Rights Act (“ELCRA”) Mirrors Title VII’s Prohibition.**

The Michigan ELCRA, Mich. Comp. Laws § 37.2202(1)(a), prohibits discrimination “because of . . . sex.” The Michigan Supreme Court has interpreted this provision consistently with federal law, holding that harassment based on sex is actionable under the ELCRA. *Knudsen v. Detroit Edison Co.*, 397 Mich. 242, 250 (1976). Earlier Michigan decisions confirm that the ELCRA’s “because of sex” language is broad enough to encompass harassment claims where the conduct is directed at the victim because of his sex. *See, e.g., Doe v. Bay City Pub. Schs.*, 383 Mich. 147, 152 (1970) (holding that discrimination “because of sex” includes conduct that creates a hostile environment). Thus, Wasek’s harassment claims are cognizable under both Title VII and the ELCRA.

II. **WASEK’S HARASSMENT CLAIMS SURVIVE SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE HE PRESENTED SUFFICIENT EVIDENCE TO CREATE A GENUINE ISSUE OF MATERIAL FACT.**

A. **The Harassment Was Sufficiently Severe or Pervasive to Alter the Conditions of Employment.**

To establish a hostile work environment claim, a plaintiff must show that the harassment was “sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of [his] employment and create an abusive working environment.” *Meritor*, 477 U.S. at 67, citing *Hensen v. City of Dundee*, 682 F.2d 897, 902 (11th Cir. 1982). The Supreme Court has cautioned that isolated incidents, unless “extremely serious,” will not suffice. *Id.* at 67 n.9. However, repeated offensive conduct, even if not overtly sexual in nature, may contribute to a hostile environment. *See, e.g., Rogers v. EEOC*, 454 F.2d 234, 238 (5th Cir. 1971) (holding that racial harassment, including offensive comments and slurs, could contribute to a hostile environment).

Here, Wasek presented evidence that Ottobre subjected him to repeated sexual touching, sexually explicit comments, and humiliating behavior over a sustained period. These actions, viewed cumulatively, were sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of his employment. *See, e.g., Burns v. Thiokol Chem. Corp.*, 483 F.2d 300, 305 (5th Cir. 1973) (holding that a pattern of offensive conduct could contribute to a hostile environment).

B. **Arrow Energy’s Failure to Remedy the Harassment Contributed to the Hostile Environment.**

An employer’s failure to take prompt and effective remedial action may contribute to the hostile environment. *See, e.g., McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 804 (1973) (noting that an employer’s response to discrimination is relevant to liability). Here, Wasek complained to his supervisors, including Tripp and Smillie, but they failed to take meaningful action. Instead, they dismissed his complaints, laughed at the harassment, and even encouraged Wasek to fight Ottobre. This inaction allowed the hostile environment to persist. *See, e.g., Jenkins v. Averett*, 424 F.2d 1228, 1233 (4th Cir. 1970) (holding that an employer’s inadequate response to discrimination could contribute to a hostile environment).

III. **WASEK’S CLAIMS ARE NOT BARRED BY THE FACT THAT OTTOBRE’S MOTIVE WAS NOT BASED ON GENDER.**

A. **Title VII Does Not Require Proof of a Discriminatory Motive.**

The Supreme Court has held that Title VII’s “because of sex” provision does not require proof of a discriminatory motive. *Phillips*, 400 U.S. at 544 (Burger, C.J., concurring). Instead, the focus is on whether the harassment was directed at the victim because of his sex. *See, e.g., Rogers*, 454 F.2d at 238 (holding that harassment directed at an individual because of his race was actionable under Title VII, regardless of the harasser’s motive).

B. **The ELCRA’s “Because of Sex” Provision Is Similarly Broad.**

The Michigan ELCRA’s “because of sex” provision is interpreted consistently with Title VII. *Knudsen*, 397 Mich. at 250. Thus, Wasek’s claims are cognizable under both statutes, regardless of whether Ottobre’s motive was based on gender.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Wasek’s harassment claims are not barred by the “because of sex” requirement under Title VII or the ELCRA. He presented sufficient evidence to create a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the harassment was sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of his employment and whether Arrow Energy failed to take prompt and effective remedial action. Accordingly, the district court erred in granting summary judgment in favor of Arrow Energy.
