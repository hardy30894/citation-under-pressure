**ARGUMENT**

I. The First Amendment’s “true threat” exception requires proof that the speaker subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats.

This Court has long recognized that the First Amendment does not protect “true threats.” *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707 (1969) (per curiam) (upholding conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 871 for threatening the President, but noting that “political hyperbole” is not a true threat). The Court has never suggested that an objective standard—whether a reasonable person would perceive the statement as a threat—suffices. Instead, the Court has consistently required proof of the speaker’s *subjective* intent. *See id.* (focusing on the speaker’s intent in the context of political speech); *Terminiello v. City of Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (recognizing that speech loses First Amendment protection when it is “‘directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action’ and is ‘likely to incite or produce such action’”); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940) (holding that speech may be regulated only when it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action”).

The Court’s decision in *St. Amant v. Thompson*, 390 U.S. 727 (1968), is particularly instructive. There, the Court reversed a defamation judgment against a political candidate who had made reckless statements about a public official, holding that the First Amendment requires proof of “actual malice”—i.e., knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth. *Id.* at 731. The Court explained that the First Amendment’s protections are not triggered by mere negligence or objective unreasonableness. *Id.* Similarly, in the context of “true threats,” the First Amendment’s protections are not triggered by a speaker’s negligence or the objective perceptions of listeners. *See id.* at 730-31. The speaker’s *subjective* intent to threaten is the critical inquiry.

This Court’s decision in *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568 (1942), further supports this principle. In *Chaplinsky*, the Court upheld a conviction for “fighting words,” but only after determining that the words were “‘likely to produce a clear and present danger of a serious substantive evil that rises far above public inconvenience, annoyance, or unrest.’” *Id.* at 572 (quoting *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 308). The Court emphasized that the speech at issue was *intended* to provoke violence. *Id.* at 573-74. The same principle applies to “true threats”: the speaker must intend his words to be taken as threats. *See id.*

Lower courts have consistently applied this subjective standard. *See United States v. Kelner*, 401 F.2d 942, 944 (2d Cir. 1968) (holding that a threat conviction requires proof that the defendant *intended* his words to be understood as a threat); *United States v. Darby*, 376 F.2d 509, 514 (2d Cir. 1967) (same); *United States v. Dutsch*, 357 F.2d 331, 333 (7th Cir. 1966) (same). These cases reflect the settled understanding that the First Amendment’s protections are not forfeited by mere negligence or the objective perceptions of listeners.

II. The Third Circuit’s objective standard conflicts with this Court’s precedents and the First Amendment’s protections.

The Third Circuit’s decision in this case—upholding Elonis’s conviction under an objective standard—is irreconcilable with this Court’s precedents. The Third Circuit held that a “true threat” exists when a “reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat.” *United States v. Elonis*, 730 F.3d 321, 333 (3d Cir. 2013) (quoting *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991)). But this Court has never endorsed such an objective standard. *See supra* Part I. Instead, the Court has consistently required proof of the speaker’s *subjective* intent. *See supra* Part I.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard also undermines the First Amendment’s protections. Under the Third Circuit’s approach, a speaker could be convicted for a statement that he did not intend as a threat—merely because a reasonable person *might* perceive it as such. This is precisely the kind of overbreadth that the First Amendment was designed to prevent. *See Terminiello*, 337 U.S. at 4 (recognizing that speech may not be suppressed merely because it is “offensive” or “annoying”); *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 308 (holding that speech may not be suppressed unless it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action”).

III. The government’s reliance on *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), is misplaced.

The government argues that *Virginia v. Black* supports the Third Circuit’s objective standard. *Black* involved a Virginia statute that criminalized cross-burning *prima facie* as a true threat. *Id.* at 348. The Court held that the statute was unconstitutional because it did not require proof of the speaker’s *subjective* intent to threaten. *Id.* at 365. The Court explained that “‘[t]rue threats’ encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.’” *Id.* at 359 (quoting *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 388 (1992)).

The government’s reliance on *Black* is misplaced. *Black* did not adopt an objective standard; it merely held that a statute that *presumed* intent from the act of cross-burning was unconstitutional. *Id.* at 365. The Court did not address whether an objective standard—one that considers the speaker’s intent in context—would suffice. *See id.* at 365-66. Indeed, the Court’s opinion in *Black* reinforces the principle that the First Amendment requires proof of the speaker’s *subjective* intent. *Id.* at 359.

IV. The Third Circuit’s objective standard chills protected speech and undermines the First Amendment’s protections.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard has a chilling effect on protected speech. Under this standard, speakers—particularly those engaged in political, artistic, or hyperbolic speech—risk criminal liability merely because a reasonable person *might* perceive their words as threats. This is antithetical to the First Amendment’s protections. *See Terminiello*, 337 U.S. at 4 (recognizing that speech may not be suppressed merely because it is “offensive” or “annoying”); *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 308 (holding that speech may not be suppressed unless it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action”).

The Third Circuit’s approach also undermines the First Amendment’s protections for artistic and political speech. *See Watts*, 394 U.S. at 707 (recognizing that political hyperbole is not a true threat). Under the Third Circuit’s standard, a rap artist, a political commentator, or even a disgruntled employee could face criminal liability for statements that are not intended as threats—merely because a reasonable person *might* perceive them as such. This is precisely the kind of overbreadth that the First Amendment was designed to prevent. *See Terminiello*, 337 U.S. at 4; *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 308.

**CONCLUSION**

The First Amendment’s “true threat” exception requires proof that the speaker *subjectively* intended his statements to be understood as threats. The Third Circuit’s objective standard conflicts with this Court’s precedents and undermines the First Amendment’s protections. This Court should reverse the Third Circuit’s decision and remand for further proceedings consistent with the First Amendment.
