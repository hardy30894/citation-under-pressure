# ARGUMENT

## I. Specific Jurisdiction Requires Only That Claims "Arise Out of or Relate to" Forum Contacts—Not That the Contacts Cause the Injury.

Since *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), this Court has held that due process permits a state to exercise jurisdiction over a defendant whose activities in the forum render the suit consistent with "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *Id.* at 316. The touchstone is not mechanical causation but whether the defendant has "purposefully avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State." *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). Where a defendant does so, and the resulting suit "arise[s] out of or relate[s] to" those activities, jurisdiction is proper. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472 (1985) (quoting *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984)).

That standard is disjunctive. This Court has never collapsed "relate to" into "arise from," nor imposed a strict causal test requiring that the specific forum contact be the mechanism of injury. In *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770 (1984), the Court sustained jurisdiction in New Hampshire over a libel claim even though the plaintiff's own contacts with the forum, not the defendant's, were minimal, because the defendant "circulated magazines regularly" there and could "reasonably anticipate being haled into court" on claims connected to that regular business. *Id.* at 774, 781. The defendant's general course of forum-directed conduct—not a discrete transaction causing the particular injury—supplied the constitutional link.

Ford's proposed rule—that jurisdiction requires proof the defendant's forum contacts *caused* the plaintiff's claim—finds no footing in this Court's precedents and cannot be squared with the "relate to" half of the settled formulation. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 472. It would rewrite decades of doctrine to require exactly the kind of "but-for" causal nexus this Court has consistently declined to impose.

## II. Ford Purposefully Availed Itself of Montana, and Gullett's Claims Relate Directly to That Availment.

Ford is not a stranger to Montana. It advertises extensively there, maintains dealerships that sell and service Ford Explorers—the very model at issue—and cultivates a resale and repair market for its vehicles throughout the State. This is precisely the kind of systematic and continuous course of conduct through which a company "avail[s] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws." *Hanson*, 357 U.S. at 253. Ford profits every day from Montanans who buy, drive, service, and rely on Ford Explorers on Montana roads.

Gullett's claims—that a defectively designed Explorer tire failed on a Montana highway—are not some random accident unconnected to that Montana business. They "relate to" the very activity Ford purposefully cultivated in the forum: the marketing, sale, and support of the Explorer model line to Montana residents. As the Montana Supreme Court correctly recognized, by "advertising and selling parts within the state of Montana," Ford "availed itself of the privilege of doing business" there in a manner squarely connected to this suit.

This conclusion follows the logic of *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286 (1980), which held that jurisdiction is proper where a defendant "delivers its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State." *Id.* at 297–98. Ford does far more than place a product into an undirected stream; it deliberately markets Explorers to Montana consumers, expecting—indeed relying upon—their use on Montana roads. Where a defendant, "purposefully avail[ing] itself of the forum," could reasonably foresee "being haled into court" in a state whose citizens it serves, that "reasonable anticipation" itself satisfies due process. *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297.

Nor does *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 137 S. Ct. 1773 (2017), compel a different result. There, out-of-state plaintiffs sued over conduct occurring entirely outside the forum, and this Court found no "adequate link" between the forum and those particular claims. *Id.* at 1781. Here, by contrast, the plaintiff is a Montana resident, the injury occurred on a Montana highway, and the vehicle was marketed and eventually possessed within Montana's borders. The forum-claim connection *Bristol-Myers* found lacking is present here in full measure.

## III. Montana's Exercise of Jurisdiction Comports with Fair Play and Substantial Justice.

Finally, jurisdiction here honors the "fair play and substantial justice" that due process demands. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316. Montana has a manifest interest in adjudicating a case involving the death of its own citizen on its own highway. *See Keeton*, 465 U.S. at 776 ("A State . . . has a special interest in exercising judicial jurisdiction over those who commit torts within its territory."). Ford, a global manufacturer that markets vehicles in every State, cannot claim surprise or unfairness in defending a products-liability suit in the very State where its product was sold, used, and caused a fatal injury.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
