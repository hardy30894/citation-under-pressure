ARGUMENT

The Sixth Amendment preserves the common-law jury trial as it existed in 1791. Sparf v. United States, 156 U.S. 51, 102 (1895). That guarantee extends only to facts that constitute the offense itself. Callan v. Wilson, 127 U.S. 540, 549 (1888). Sentencing factors and exemptions, including mental capacity, lie outside that historical core. Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2, 123 (1866).

Florida’s scheme therefore satisfies the Sixth Amendment. The jury determines guilt of first-degree murder; the trial judge then weighs statutory aggravators and mitigators to select the penalty. This division accords with the common-law practice under which judges exercised sentencing discretion after a guilty verdict. Patton v. United States, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930); Thompson v. Utah, 170 U.S. 343, 350 (1898).

The Eighth Amendment likewise imposes no requirement of jury unanimity or jury adjudication of mental retardation. The Amendment forbids only those punishments that were cruel and unusual at common law. Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581, 602 (1900). Non-unanimous jury recommendations and judicial sentencing were unknown to that tradition and therefore fall outside its prohibition. Schick v. United States, 195 U.S. 65, 71 (1904).

Mental retardation is a mitigating circumstance, not an element of capital murder. Its determination may constitutionally be assigned to the court. District of Columbia v. Clawans, 300 U.S. 617, 628 (1937). Florida permits the defendant to present retardation evidence to both judge and jury; the judge’s ultimate finding satisfies due process. Balzac v. Porto Rico, 258 U.S. 298, 313 (1922).

Nor does the Constitution demand that every factual predicate for a death sentence be found unanimously by the jury. Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740, 748 (1948) (recognizing that common-law juries returned general verdicts of guilt). Florida’s requirement of a majority jury recommendation, followed by independent judicial sentencing, preserves the essential features of the common-law jury trial while affording the defendant additional procedural safeguards.

Because the Florida procedure respects the historical limits of the jury-trial right and imposes no punishment unknown to the common law, the judgment should be affirmed.
