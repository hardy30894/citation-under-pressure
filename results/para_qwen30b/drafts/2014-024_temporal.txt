**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—is governed by settled principles of First Amendment jurisprudence established long before the advent of the modern internet. The Government’s insistence on an objective standard for “true threats” violates the constitutional guarantee of free speech, as this Court has repeatedly held that no person may be punished for speech unless it is shown that they intended to utter words that they knew or should have known would be understood as a threat.

This Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects even offensive or inflammatory speech unless it falls within a narrowly defined category of unprotected expression. In *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942), the Court declared that “certain well-defined and narrowly limited classes of speech are not within the protection of the Constitution,” including “fighting words”—those which by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace. But crucially, the Court emphasized that such speech must be “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action.” Id. at 573. This requirement reflects a fundamental principle: punishment for speech must rest upon a showing of intent, not mere perception.

In *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 8 (1949), the Court struck down a conviction for violating a city ordinance prohibiting “any speech which stirs the public to anger, invites resistance to law enforcement, or creates a disturbance.” The Court held that such a statute was overbroad because it punished speech based solely on its emotional impact, without regard to whether the speaker intended to provoke violence. “The right to speak one’s mind,” the Court stated, “is not subject to suppression merely because the audience may react violently.” Id. at 10. To hold otherwise would permit the government to punish individuals for the foreseeable consequences of their speech, regardless of their intent—a result incompatible with the First Amendment.

Similarly, in *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 509–10 (1951), the Court upheld a conviction under the Smith Act for advocating the overthrow of the government, but only because the evidence demonstrated that the defendants had acted with the purpose of advancing such a goal. The Court stressed that “the test is not whether the advocacy is likely to produce immediate violence, but whether it is intended to produce such violence.” Id. at 510. The presence of a subjective intent to incite unlawful conduct was essential to the conviction. Without such intent, the speech remained protected—even if it might have been dangerous or inflammatory.

These precedents establish a clear rule: the First Amendment does not permit criminal liability for speech absent a showing that the speaker intended to communicate a threat. An objective standard—that is, one that turns on whether a reasonable person would perceive the statement as threatening—creates a grave risk of punishing speech based on the listener’s reaction rather than the speaker’s intent. Such a standard effectively abandons the core principle that punishment for speech must be grounded in culpability, not consequence.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Kosma*—a decision rendered after 1970—is irrelevant here, as we are confined to precedent prior to that date. More importantly, the Court has never countenanced a rule that allows conviction based on the perceived meaning of speech apart from the speaker’s own understanding. Indeed, in *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940), the Court held that “no one may be punished for his thoughts or for what he says unless he intends to do harm.” The Court rejected any notion that a speaker could be held liable for the effect of their words on others when there was no intent to cause harm. That principle remains binding today.

The Government’s argument that an objective standard is necessary to protect individuals from fear of violence misapprehends the nature of the First Amendment. Fear is not a sufficient justification for suppressing speech. As this Court observed in *Bridges v. California*, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941), “the fact that a particular form of expression may arouse hostility or alarm does not justify its suppression.” The danger of violence must be addressed through other means—not by punishing speech that is constitutionally protected.

To uphold Elonis’s conviction under an objective standard would be to allow the government to convict someone simply because a reasonable person might interpret their words as threatening, even if the speaker believed they were expressing frustration, venting anger, or writing poetry. That is precisely the kind of punishment the First Amendment forbids. As the Court said in *Whitney v. California*, 274 U.S. 357, 373 (1927), “the history of the First Amendment is the history of a struggle against the idea that the government can control thought.”

For these reasons, the judgment below must be reversed. A conviction under § 875(c) cannot stand unless the jury finds beyond a reasonable doubt that Elonis subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats. Absent such proof, the conviction violates the First Amendment.
