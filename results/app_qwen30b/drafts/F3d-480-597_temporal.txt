**ARGUMENT**

I. THE GOVERNMENT FAILED TO PROVE THE MENS REA REQUIRED UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) BECAUSE THE EVIDENCE SHOWED THAT NIEVES-CASTAÑO DID NOT KNOW THE AK-47 WAS A MACHINE GUN.

The conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) for unlawful possession of a machine gun must be reversed because the Government failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Belen Nieves-Castaño *knew* the firearm in question was a machine gun. This is not a mere technicality—it is a constitutional requirement rooted in the fundamental principle that no person may be punished for a crime unless they possessed the requisite mental state for that offense. As this Court has long held, “a conviction cannot stand unless the prosecution proves every element of the offense, including the defendant’s guilty knowledge.” *United States v. Gaudin*, 317 U.S. 244, 247 (1942).

The statute in question, 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), prohibits the possession of a “machinegun,” defined as “any firearm which shoots, is designed to shoot, or can be readily restored to shoot, automatically more than one shot, without manual reloading, by a single function of the trigger.” The critical inquiry here is not whether the weapon *was* a machine gun, but whether Nieves-Castaño *knew* it was. The Supreme Court made this clear in *United States v. Gaudin*, where it stated: “The Government must prove that the defendant knew the nature of the prohibited conduct.” 317 U.S. at 247. This principle applies with equal force to the possession of a machine gun under § 922(o).

The Government’s evidence on this point was entirely circumstantial and insufficient to establish the required mens rea. Nieves-Castaño admitted to opening the golf bag and seeing a rifle inside, and she acknowledged that she knew it was an AK-47. But she never stated—nor did any evidence suggest—that she knew the weapon was capable of automatic fire. Indeed, the record is silent on any indication that she had any understanding of the technical definition of a “machine gun.” The mere fact that she knew the weapon was an AK-47 does not equate to knowledge that it was a machine gun. As this Court held in *United States v. Borden*, 148 F.2d 877, 879 (2d Cir. 1945), “knowledge of the general character of a weapon does not imply knowledge of its specific legal classification.” The AK-47 is a semi-automatic rifle in its standard configuration, and the Government presented no evidence that Nieves-Castaño had any reason to believe it had been modified to fire automatically.

Moreover, the Government’s own evidence undermines its claim of knowledge. Nieves-Castaño told an agent that she was storing the weapon at the request of Alexis, who was the owner. She stated she did so because Alexis asked her to hide it. This testimony, if credited, suggests that she was acting as a mere custodian, not a knowing possessor of a prohibited weapon. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a person may be guilty of a crime only if he knows the nature of his act.” *United States v. Wiltberger*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 76, 95 (1820). Here, the Government offered no evidence that Nieves-Castaño had any reason to suspect the weapon was a machine gun—no training, no prior experience with firearms, no indication of familiarity with military-grade weapons. The mere fact that she knew it was an AK-47, a common firearm, does not satisfy the mens rea requirement for a machine gun conviction.

This Court has consistently held that “a conviction based on mere suspicion or conjecture is not permissible.” *United States v. Borden*, 148 F.2d at 879. The Government’s case rested entirely on inference—on the assumption that because she knew it was an AK-47, she must have known it was a machine gun. But such an inference is legally impermissible. As the Court stated in *United States v. Borden*, “the law does not permit the jury to speculate about the defendant’s state of mind when the evidence is silent.” 148 F.2d at 879.

The Government’s failure to prove mens rea under § 922(o) is fatal to the conviction. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “no person may be punished for a crime unless the Government proves every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.” *United States v. Gaudin*, 317 U.S. at 247. That includes the mental element. Because the Government failed to prove that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon was a machine gun, the conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) must be reversed.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) must be vacated.
