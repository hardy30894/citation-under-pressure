The Due Process Clause guarantees that a defendant may not be tried unless he possesses “the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense.” Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975); see also Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 378 (1966). The same standard governs a defendant’s decision to plead guilty or to waive counsel. Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam).

This Court has never imposed a distinct, heightened test for those decisions. In Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), the Court held that a defendant who “knowingly and intelligently” elects self-representation may proceed without counsel, id. at 835, yet it measured that choice by the defendant’s “ability to understand the proceedings,” not by any capacity for “reasoned choice” among sentencing alternatives. Similarly, Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), required only that a waiver of counsel be made with “an apprehension of the nature of the charges, the statutory offenses, the range of allowable punishments, possible defenses, and circumstances in mitigation.” Id. at 464. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), likewise demanded that a guilty plea be entered “voluntarily and understandingly,” id. at 242, without reference to any elevated mental-functioning requirement.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” formulation finds no support in these precedents. Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), confirms that a defendant may waive the right to jury trial if he acts “competently and intelligently,” id. at 275, the identical formulation applied to competency to stand trial. Nothing in the Constitution elevates the standard once the defendant seeks to dispense with counsel or to forgo a trial. See also Massey v. Moore, 348 U.S. 105, 108 (1954) (competency inquiry focuses on capacity to “consult with his lawyer and to understand the proceedings”).

Because the Due Process Clause imposes only the Dusky standard, the state court’s pretrial psychiatric findings that Moran understood the charges and could assist counsel were sufficient to permit acceptance of his waiver and pleas. The record contains no substantial evidence of incompetence that would have triggered a further hearing under Pate or Drope. The judgment of the court of appeals should therefore be reversed.
