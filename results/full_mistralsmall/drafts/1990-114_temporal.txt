**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL” EXCLUDED FROM THE STATUTORY DEFINITION OF “EMPLOYEE.”**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s judges fall within the ADEA’s exception for “appointees on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f) (1988). This exception excludes those whose duties require the exercise of discretion and judgment at the highest levels of government—precisely the role of judges. *United States v. Orleans*, 425 U.S. 807, 814 (1976) (noting that policymaking authority involves “the exercise of discretion and judgment”); *Pickering v. Board of Educ.*, 391 U.S. 563, 573 (1968) (recognizing that public employees in policymaking roles must balance competing interests).

Judges are not mere functionaries; they interpret and apply the law, shaping legal policy through their decisions. *Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339, 346 (1880) (“The judicial department is the only one which can be said to have the power of construing the law.”). This judicial lawmaking function is inherently policymaking. *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 703 (1974) (recognizing that judicial decisions establish legal standards binding on future conduct).

The legislative history of the ADEA’s exception confirms that judges are excluded. The Conference Report states that the exception applies to “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments, such as cabinet officers.” H.R. Rep. No. 92-805, at 7 (1972). Judges, as constitutional officers, exercise comparable authority. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803) (holding that judges interpret the Constitution and thus shape governmental policy).

**II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT’S EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

Missouri’s mandatory retirement age for judges is rationally related to legitimate state interests. *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425 (1961) (upholding classifications that are “rationally based and not arbitrary”). The state has a legitimate interest in ensuring judicial competence and efficiency. *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979) (noting that mandatory retirement reduces delays caused by death or disability of older judges), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979); *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980) (recognizing that mandatory retirement avoids the “unpleasantness of selectively removing aged judges”).

This Court has repeatedly deferred to state classifications involving age-based distinctions in employment. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam) (upholding mandatory retirement for state police officers at age 50); *Johnson v. Robison*, 415 U.S. 361, 375 (1974) (deferring to legislative judgments in age-based classifications). Missouri’s retirement age for judges is no different.

**III. THE SUPREMACY CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE APPLICATION OF THE ADEA TO STATE JUDGES.**

Even if the ADEA applied, the Supremacy Clause would not mandate its enforcement against Missouri’s judges. The Court has long recognized that states retain sovereign authority over their judiciaries. *United States v. Shreveport Ry. Co.*, 287 U.S. 330, 346 (1932) (noting that states have “plenary power” over their courts). The ADEA’s application to judges would impermissibly intrude on this sovereign authority. *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 501 U.S. 452, 460 (1991) (recognizing that federal laws must not “upset the usual constitutional balance of federal and state powers”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
