# ARGUMENT

Respondent Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc. respectfully submits that the Occupational Safety and Health Administration lacked authority to cite Rig 52 because Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act withdraws OSHA's jurisdiction wherever, as here, the Coast Guard already exercises statutory authority over the working conditions of seamen aboard vessels in navigation—without regard to whether the vessel happens to be "inspected" or "uninspected."

## I. The Coast Guard's Exclusive Authority Over Vessel Safety Reflects a Century-Old Admiralty Commitment to Uniformity That Congress Did Not Disturb

This Court has long recognized that matters of maritime safety and discipline demand a single, nationally uniform body of regulation rather than the layered, potentially conflicting regulatory schemes that would result if multiple federal agencies could simultaneously govern the same vessel. In *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205 (1917), the Court held that even state legislation of general applicability must yield when it "works material prejudice to the characteristic features of the general maritime law or interferes with the proper harmony and uniformity of that law in its international and interstate relations." *Id.* at 216. That principle did not merely police the boundary between federal and state power; it announced a structural rule that the regulation of vessels and those who work aboard them must remain uniform and administered by a single competent authority.

The Court reaffirmed and extended this rule in *Knickerbocker Ice Co. v. Stewart*, 253 U.S. 149 (1920), holding that Congress itself could not delegate to the States the power to supplant the uniform maritime rules governing vessel operations, because the need for such uniformity is constitutionally rooted rather than a matter of legislative convenience. And in *Panama Railroad Co. v. Johnson*, 264 U.S. 375 (1924), the Court sustained Congress's own choice to create a single, coherent remedial scheme for seamen—the Jones Act—precisely because Congress recognized that fragmented, inconsistent regulation of shipboard conditions was intolerable in the maritime context.

Section 4(b)(1) codifies this same insistence on a single regulator. It excludes OSHA jurisdiction wherever "other Federal agencies... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Read against the backdrop of *Jensen*, *Knickerbocker Ice*, and *Panama Railroad*, that provision confirms what Congress must be understood to have intended: the Coast Guard, not a land-based agency with no history of shipboard regulation, remains the exclusive source of safety standards for vessels such as Rig 52.

## II. The Special Federal Concern for Seamen's Welfare Has Always Been Administered as a Uniform Body of Law, Not Piecemeal Regulation

The Court's decision in *The Osceola*, 189 U.S. 158 (1903), illustrates why Congress would not have wanted to graft OSHA's general industrial standards onto vessels already subject to comprehensive federal maritime obligations. There, the Court catalogued the well-established maritime duties owed to seamen, explaining that "the vessel and her owners are liable, in case a seaman falls sick, or is wounded, in the service of the ship, to the extent of his maintenance and cure." *Id.* at 175. These duties were not haphazard; they were part of "the general maritime law," administered uniformly across the federal admiralty system. *Id.* The Coast Guard's safety regulations occupy the same uniform space that *The Osceola* described—rules tailored to the particular hazards of a vessel in navigation, developed and enforced by the agency with maritime expertise, rather than standards designed for stationary, land-based industrial facilities.

## III. Nothing About a Vessel's "Uninspected" Status Removes It From the Coast Guard's Exclusive Maritime Domain

Petitioner's effort to confine the Coast Guard's exclusivity to "inspected" vessels finds no support in this Court's admiralty jurisprudence, which has never conditioned the scope of maritime regulation on a vessel's inspection status. In *Grant Smith-Porter Ship Co. v. Rohde*, 257 U.S. 469 (1922), the Court held that work performed aboard a vessel under construction on navigable waters was maritime in character and subject to admiralty jurisdiction even though the vessel was not yet a completed, inspected vessel. *Id.* at 476–77. The Court's analysis turned not on the vessel's regulatory classification but on the essentially maritime nature of the activity and the location where it occurred—precisely the considerations present here. Rig 52 was a drilling barge operating on navigable waters, performing quintessentially maritime work; its uninspected status is a Coast Guard licensing classification, not a jurisdictional line separating maritime regulation from industrial regulation.

Taken together, *Jensen*, *Knickerbocker Ice*, *Panama Railroad*, *The Osceola*, and *Grant Smith-Porter* establish that the regulation of vessels and seamen's working conditions has always been treated as a unified federal maritime concern administered by a single competent sovereign authority. Section 4(b)(1) preserves that tradition. The Fifth Circuit correctly held that this exclusivity extends to uninspected vessels like Rig 52, and this Court should affirm.
