**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred by Admitting Prejudicial Opinion Testimony That Vouched for Witnesses, Stated Legal Conclusions, and Interpreted Evidence**

The admission of testimony from two Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant—who opined on the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, the existence of probable cause, and the strength of Appellants’ case—violated fundamental evidentiary rules and deprived Appellants of a fair trial. A witness may not “vouch” for another witness’s credibility, *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 18 (1985), nor testify to legal conclusions that invade the province of the jury, *United States v. Scop*, 846 F.2d 135, 139 (2d Cir. 1988). Moreover, a lay witness may not offer opinions that “merely tell the jury what result to reach,” *United States v. Bilzerian*, 926 F.2d 1285, 1294 (2d Cir. 1991). Here, the ADAs and lieutenant directly told the jury that Ramos and Rivera were credible, that probable cause existed, and that certain evidence undermined Appellants’ claims. These statements were not harmless. The central disputes at trial were precisely whether the officers fabricated their story and whether they had probable cause. By allowing ostensibly neutral government officials to opine on these very issues, the court “usurp[ed] the jury’s role” and “taint[ed] the entire trial.” *United States v. Mejia*, 545 F.3d 179, 197 (2d Cir. 2008). Because the evidence against Appellants was not overwhelming, the error cannot be deemed harmless. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946). A new trial is required.

**II. The District Court Erred in Denying Appellants’ Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law**

Appellants also contend that the security-camera photographs so “blatantly contradict” the officers’ account that no reasonable jury could credit Appellees’ version. *See* Appellants’ Br. 31. While the photographs do not definitively resolve every factual dispute, they undermine the officers’ core narrative. However, because the district court correctly noted that the photographs do not “definitively rule out either side’s version,” the denial of judgment as a matter of law was not an abuse of discretion. *See Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150 (2000). Appellants preserve this issue for further review but acknowledge that the evidentiary errors alone warrant reversal.

**III. The District Court Erred in Refusing to Instruct the Jury That Obstructing Governmental Administration Requires a Lawful Arrest**

Under New York law, the offense of obstructing governmental administration, N.Y. Penal Law § 195.05, requires that the officer be “engaged in a lawful arrest.” *People v. Lupinacci*, 191 A.D.2d 579, 580 (2d Dep’t 1993). Appellants requested an instruction to that effect, but the district court denied it. Because Appellants’ theory of the case was that the officers lacked probable cause to arrest Cameron, the lawfulness of the arrest was a central issue. Without the instruction, the jury could have convicted Higgenbottom of obstruction even if the underlying arrest was unlawful. This error was prejudicial. *See United States v. Dove*, 916 F.2d 41, 45 (2d Cir. 1990). On remand, the court should give the requested instruction.

**IV. The District Court Erred in Denying a Punitive Damages Instruction**

Appellants presented evidence that Ramos and Rivera knowingly fabricated their account, which would support a finding of malice. Under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, punitive damages are available when a defendant acts “with evil motive or intent, or with reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others.” *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). Appellants’ version of events—that the officers lied to justify an arrest—satisfies this standard. The district court’s refusal to give a punitive damages instruction was therefore error. *See Ismail v. Cohen*, 899 F.2d 183, 186 (2d Cir. 1990). On remand, the instruction should be given if the evidence supports it.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial consistent with this Court’s instructions.
