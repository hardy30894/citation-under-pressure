**ARGUMENT**

Section 103(c) of ANILCA, 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c), states that non-federal lands “shall be deemed to be excluded from the boundaries” of conservation system units and that “no provision of this Act shall be construed as permitting any such regulation” that applies solely to those lands. The hovercraft prohibition, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), is not such a provision. It governs the operation of a specific class of vehicle throughout the National Park System, wherever the NPS possesses statutory authority to protect park resources. See 54 U.S.C. § 100751(a) (authorizing “necessary and appropriate” rules “for the use of” System units).

The statute’s text therefore supplies no textual barrier to the regulation’s application on the Nation River. “When the words of a statute are unambiguous, then, this first canon is also the last: judicial inquiry is complete.” Connecticut Nat. Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 254 (1992) (internal quotation marks omitted). Because the regulation is not limited to public lands, § 103(c) leaves it untouched.

This conclusion accords with the settled principle that federal land-management authority extends to conduct on non-federal inholdings when necessary to fulfill statutory duties. See Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529, 539 (1976) (Property Clause power “entrusts Congress with the task of choosing the appropriate means” to protect federal lands). ANILCA did not repeal that authority; to the contrary, § 1319 expressly preserves “existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to … exercise … regulatory functions.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207.

Sturgeon’s contrary reading would require the Court to insert the word “any” before “regulation,” converting a narrow non-applicability clause into a broad exemption. Courts may not “add words to the law to produce a desired result.” EEOC v. Abercrombie & Fitch Stores, Inc., 575 U.S. 768, 775 (2015). The Ninth Circuit correctly declined to do so.

The judgment should be affirmed.
