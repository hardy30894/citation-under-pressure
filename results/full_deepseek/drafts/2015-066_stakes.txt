**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sixth Amendment Prohibits the Government from Using an Uncounseled Tribal Court Conviction to Satisfy an Essential Element of a Federal Felony Offense.**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right is fundamental to the integrity of our adversarial system, ensuring that a defendant receives a fair trial. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963). While the Court has recognized that the right to counsel does not attach to misdemeanor prosecutions that result in no term of imprisonment, *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 373 (1979), a conviction obtained in violation of this right is presumptively unreliable and cannot be used to support a subsequent deprivation of liberty. *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967).

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the government’s use of Mr. Bryant’s uncounseled tribal court convictions as the *sine qua non* of a federal felony prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment. This case is not about sentencing enhancement; it is about the essential elements of a crime. Section 117(a) criminalizes a domestic assault only when the defendant “has a final conviction on at least 2 separate prior occasions in Federal, State, or Indian tribal court proceedings.” 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The prior convictions are not merely a sentencing factor; they are an element of the offense that the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt to secure a conviction. *See In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970).

The government’s position, adopted by the dissenting judges below, rests on a fundamental misreading of *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994). In *Nichols*, the Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid under *Scott* because it did not result in imprisonment, could be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines. *Id.* at 746-49. The Court emphasized the distinction between the guilt and punishment phases of a criminal proceeding, noting that the sentencing scheme at issue “accommodated” reliability concerns because a defendant could challenge the reliability of the prior conviction and the government’s burden of proof was a preponderance of the evidence. *Id.* at 747-48.

*Nichols* does not sanction the use of an uncounseled conviction to prove an element of a new offense. The Court in *Nichols* explicitly reaffirmed the principle that a conviction obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment is invalid for use in a future prosecution. *Id.* at 748-49 (citing *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 115). The Court’s holding was narrow, addressing only the use of such convictions at sentencing, where the “accommodations” of the Guidelines mitigate reliability concerns. *Id.* at 747. Here, those accommodations are absent. The government must prove the prior convictions beyond a reasonable doubt, and the defendant has no mechanism to challenge their reliability before they are used to establish his guilt. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), correctly applied this principle, holding that the government may not use tribal court convictions that do not provide an equivalent right to counsel as evidence of guilt in a subsequent federal prosecution. *Id.* at 1396.

**II. The Fact That the Convictions Occurred in Tribal Court Does Not Immunize Them from Sixth Amendment Scrutiny.**

The government and the dissenting judges argue that because the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal court proceedings, the convictions are “valid” and may be used for any purpose. This argument conflates the validity of a tribal court judgment for purposes of tribal sovereignty with its reliability for purposes of a federal criminal prosecution. While it is true that the Bill of Rights does not directly constrain tribal governments, *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896), the federal government’s use of those convictions to create a new federal crime is a different matter. The Sixth Amendment governs *federal* prosecutions, and it prohibits the federal government from relying on unreliable evidence to secure a conviction.

The Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303, provides certain procedural protections to tribal defendants, but it does not guarantee a right to appointed counsel. *See United States v. Bryant*, 769 F.3d 671, 675 (9th Cir. 2014). Congress has recognized this deficiency. In the Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010, Congress expanded tribal court jurisdiction but conditioned that expansion on the provision of an “effective assistance of counsel” equivalent to the Sixth Amendment right. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(c). Similarly, in the Violence Against Women Reauthorization Act of 2013, Congress required tribal courts exercising special domestic violence jurisdiction over non-Indians to provide “all other rights whose protection is necessary under the Constitution of the United States,” including the right to counsel. 25 U.S.C. § 1304(d). These enactments demonstrate Congress’s understanding that the reliability of tribal court convictions is tied to the provision of counsel.

The government’s approach would permit the federal government to do indirectly what it cannot do directly: use an uncounseled conviction to deprive a person of liberty. This is precisely what the Sixth Amendment forbids. *See Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 115. The Ninth Circuit’s decision respects tribal sovereignty by not invalidating the tribal court judgments themselves; it merely holds that the federal government cannot bootstrap those judgments into a new federal felony without ensuring the defendant had the benefit of counsel. This is a modest and constitutionally sound result.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Does Not Frustrate the Purpose of § 117(a) and Is Consistent with Congressional Intent.**

The dissenting judges below argued that the panel’s decision “wiped this important statute off the books” and left tribal communities vulnerable to domestic violence. This is hyperbole. The decision does not invalidate § 117(a); it simply requires that tribal court convictions used as predicates be obtained with the protections of counsel. Congress has already moved in this direction, as evidenced by the Tribal Law and Order Act and the Violence Against Women Reauthorization Act. The decision is consistent with Congress’s dual interest in respecting tribal sovereignty and ensuring due process for tribal defendants. It does not express contempt for tribal courts; it recognizes that the Sixth Amendment’s core interest in reliability applies with full force when the federal government seeks to use a prior conviction to prove an element of a new offense.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
