**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant convicted under a federal statute has standing to challenge that statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise a constitutional challenge to 18 U.S.C. § 229 on federalism grounds because she is not a party with a legally cognizable interest in the balance of power between the federal government and the states. This Court has long held that a private individual cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal legislation absent a showing of injury to a state’s sovereign interests, and Bond has not alleged any such injury.

The Supreme Court has made clear that “the Tenth Amendment is not a source of individual rights.” *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 558 (1995). Rather, it functions as a “limitation on the power of the Federal Government,” *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 156 (1992), and “does not confer individual rights,” *Heller v. Connecticut*, 389 U.S. 902, 903 (1967). The Tenth Amendment’s purpose is to preserve the structure of federalism by preventing Congress from compelling states to enforce federal regulatory schemes. *Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898, 935 (1997). But this structural protection is not available to private parties who are merely prosecuted under federal law.

In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939), the Court rejected a Tenth Amendment challenge brought by private utility companies that alleged the federal government’s sale of electricity violated state sovereignty. The Court held that “the private companies, absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. This holding remains binding precedent. The Court did not treat the standing issue as mere dicta; rather, it was an alternative ground for decision, and the Court’s resolution of the jurisdictional question precluded consideration of the merits. *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005). The Court’s reasoning is sound: the Tenth Amendment protects state sovereignty, not individual rights. A private citizen prosecuted under federal law has no standing to assert a state’s sovereign interests.

Bond’s argument that she is a “victim of federal overreach” does not overcome this principle. She claims that § 229 “brings citizens into the federal criminal area for conduct not properly the subject of federal prosecutors,” but this is not a personal injury. It is a generalized grievance about the scope of federal power—a concern shared by all citizens, but not one that confers standing. *See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 94 (1998) (holding that “a plaintiff must show that he personally has suffered some actual or threatened injury” to have standing under Article III). Bond has not alleged that her prosecution deprived her of any right protected by the Constitution, nor has she shown that the federal government’s exercise of power under § 229 has impaired a state’s ability to govern itself.

Moreover, the Third Circuit correctly recognized that the federal government’s authority to implement treaties under the Necessary and Proper Clause is not subject to challenge by private defendants. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920), established that “if a treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.” This principle is not limited to cases where states are parties; it applies whenever Congress acts pursuant to a valid treaty. The Chemical Weapons Convention is a binding international agreement, and § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to enforce it. *See United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004) (recognizing that treaty power may “override” state authority when necessary to fulfill international obligations).

Bond’s claim that § 229 is unconstitutionally vague also fails. The statute defines “chemical weapon” and “toxic chemical” with sufficient precision to provide fair notice. *See United States v. Powell*, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975) (holding that a statute is not unconstitutionally vague if it “gives a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that his contemplated conduct is forbidden by the statute”). The statute prohibits the use of toxic chemicals for non-peaceful purposes, and Bond, as a trained laboratory technician, was fully aware of the dangers of potassium dichromate and 10-chloro-10H-phenoxarsine. Her conduct—repeatedly applying toxic substances to Haynes’s property—was plainly within the statute’s scope.

Finally, Bond’s guilty plea, which preserved her right to appeal, does not undermine the validity of the statute. *Haring v. Prosise*, 462 U.S. 306, 313 (1983), holds that a guilty plea does not waive a defendant’s right to challenge the constitutionality of a statute under which he was prosecuted. But that right is not unlimited. A defendant may not challenge a statute on grounds that do not affect his own rights. *See United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284 (10th Cir. 2004) (denying standing to challenge a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds where the defendant was not a state or state official).

For these reasons, Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
