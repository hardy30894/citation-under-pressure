The district court erred in holding that the releases bar appellants’ WARN Act claims. The Act mandates sixty days’ advance written notice of a plant closing or mass layoff, 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a), and creates an individual right to that notice that “affect[s] the public interest.” Brooklyn Sav. Bank v. O’Neil, 324 U.S. 697, 704 (1945). Rights of this character “may not be waived or released if such waiver or release contravenes the policy of the statute.” Id.; accord D.A. Schulte, Inc. v. Gangi, 328 U.S. 108, 114 (1946).

The same principle precludes waiver of WARN rights. Like the FLSA provisions at issue in Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight System, Inc., 450 U.S. 728, 740 (1981), the WARN Act “confers a federal statutory right” that “is independent of the collective-bargaining process.” A union therefore cannot bargain away individual employees’ statutory entitlement to notice. Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Co., 415 U.S. 36, 51-52 (1974) (Title VII rights “cannot be waived prospectively” through collective bargaining).

The releases here are not saved by the rule of Gilmer v. Interstate/Johnson Lane Corp., 500 U.S. 20 (1991), or 14 Penn Plaza LLC v. Pyett, 556 U.S. 247 (2009). Those decisions permit arbitration of statutory claims only when the agreement “clearly and unmistakably” requires arbitration of the specific claim. Id. at 274. The releases at issue contain no arbitration clause and purport to extinguish the WARN claim entirely, not merely channel it to another forum.

Even if waiver were permissible, the releases are invalid for lack of consideration. The severance payments were exchanged for the very notice rights the Act guarantees; they therefore supplied no new consideration. See North Star Steel Co. v. Thomas, 515 U.S. 29, 32 (1995) (WARN liability is measured by wages and benefits the employer would have paid during the notice period). The agreements also fail to satisfy the Act’s requirement of “good faith” efforts to provide notice. Watson v. Michigan Indus. Holdings, Inc., 311 F.3d 760, 765 (6th Cir. 2002); United Steelworkers of Am. v. North Star Steel Co., 5 F.3d 39, 43 (3d Cir. 1993).

Because the releases are unenforceable, summary judgment must be reversed.
