**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as the *MR. BELDON*, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation preempts OSHA’s authority under § 4(b)(1) of the Act, regardless of whether the vessel is inspected or uninspected. That holding contradicts the plain language of the OSH Act, this Court’s precedent on statutory construction and preemption, and the principle that Congress does not intend to create overlapping or conflicting regulatory schemes unless clearly expressed.

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act “shall not apply to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision does not automatically bar OSHA from regulating any workplace where another federal agency has authority; rather, it creates a narrow exception for cases where another agency has *exclusive* statutory authority over the subject matter. The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the Coast Guard has *exclusive* jurisdiction over the working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation, including uninspected vessels, rests on an overbroad reading of *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), both of which are distinguishable and do not support the broad preemption the court enunciated.

In *Clary*, this Court held that OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation. 609 F.2d at 1122. However, the Court did not purport to define the scope of the Coast Guard’s authority over *uninspected* vessels, nor did it address whether such vessels fall within the ambit of the OSH Act. The *MR. BELDON* was not a self-propelled vessel, and the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels—such as drilling barges—is limited to specific safety equipment and operational standards, not comprehensive occupational health and safety regulation. See 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. The Coast Guard has not promulgated a comprehensive set of occupational safety and health standards for uninspected vessels, nor does it regulate the day-to-day working conditions of employees on such vessels. The absence of such regulation is not a basis for inferring preemption.

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of § 4(b)(1) as creating a per se bar to OSHA jurisdiction over any vessel, regardless of inspection status, is inconsistent with this Court’s settled principle that preemption must be “clearly expressed.” *American Textile Manufacturers Institute, Inc. v. Donovan*, 452 U.S. 490, 500 (1981). Congress did not intend to create a regulatory vacuum in which workers on uninspected vessels are left without federal protection. The OSH Act was enacted to “assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions.” 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). This mandate applies to all workplaces, including those on offshore drilling rigs, even if they are not inspected by the Coast Guard.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Donovan* is similarly misplaced. In *Donovan*, this Court reaffirmed that OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation. 720 F.2d at 826. But the Court did not hold that the Coast Guard has exclusive authority over *all* working conditions on *all* vessels, nor did it purport to define the boundaries of that authority. The Court’s reasoning was grounded in the need to avoid “anomaly” and “confusion” from overlapping regulation. *Id.* at 827, 829. Yet, where one agency (the Coast Guard) regulates only certain safety equipment and procedures, and another (OSHA) regulates broader occupational health and safety standards—including training, hazard communication, and emergency response—there is no conflict, only complementary oversight. The existence of concurrent jurisdiction does not constitute preemption.

This Court has long recognized that federal agencies may regulate the same workplace under different statutory schemes so long as there is no conflict. *United Air Lines, Inc. v. Division of Industrial Safety*, 454 U.S. 944, 945 (1981) (per curiam). In *United Air Lines*, the Court rejected a claim that FAA regulation preempted state occupational safety laws, noting that “the federal and state schemes may coexist so long as they do not conflict.” Id. Similarly, the Coast Guard’s regulation of vessel safety and OSHA’s regulation of workplace safety are not mutually exclusive. The Coast Guard may regulate the structural integrity of a barge, while OSHA may regulate the training and protective equipment used by workers on that barge. The two agencies serve different purposes and operate under different statutory mandates.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision creates a dangerous precedent: it allows employers to avoid OSHA scrutiny simply by operating uninspected vessels, even when those vessels are engaged in high-risk, industrial operations. This undermines Congress’ intent to provide comprehensive protection to all workers. The *MR. BELDON* was not a mere vessel in navigation; it was a fixed offshore drilling rig, a workplace where employees performed complex, hazardous tasks. The fact that it was not inspected by the Coast Guard does not strip it of its status as a “workplace” under § 4(a) of the OSH Act. *Atlas Roofing Co. v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n*, 430 U.S. 442, 450 (1977) (defining “workplace” as “any establishment or other area where work is performed by employees”).

The Court should reverse the Fifth Circuit’s judgment. OSHA has jurisdiction to issue citations to uninspected vessels like the *MR. BELDON* when those vessels constitute workplaces where employees are exposed to recognized hazards.
