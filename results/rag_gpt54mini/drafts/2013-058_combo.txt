Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) do not exempt Heidi Heffron-Clark’s inherited IRA because the account no longer contains “retirement funds” within the ordinary meaning of that term. The Seventh Circuit correctly held that the Bankruptcy Code protects assets held for retirement, not assets that merely once served that purpose. Inherited IRAs are materially different: no additional contributions may be made, the account may not be rolled over, and required distributions must begin long before the beneficiary’s own retirement. See 26 U.S.C. §§ 408(d)(3)(C), 402(c)(11)(A), 401(a)(9)(B); In re Clark, Nos. 12-1241 & 12-1255, slip op. at 2–5 (7th Cir. Apr. 23, 2013).

The statutory text matters. Congress exempted “retirement funds” in an account exempt from taxation under specified Internal Revenue Code provisions. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). That phrase is naturally read to refer to money set aside for retirement, not to any tax-favored account that once held retirement savings. The Court has long rejected readings that detach bankruptcy provisions from their function. In United States v. Embassy Restaurant, Inc., 359 U.S. 29, 32 (1959), the Court construed a bankruptcy priority according to its “manifest purpose,” not an abstract literalism. Likewise, in Joint Industry Board of Electrical Industry v. United States, 391 U.S. 224 (1968), the Court held that contributions payable only to trustees and not providing immediate support were outside the priority designed to protect workers’ current wages. Those decisions confirm that bankruptcy exemptions are defined by the role the asset plays, not by a label alone.

That principle defeats the Clarks’ position. An inherited IRA is not a retirement reservoir for the new owner. As the Seventh Circuit explained, it is “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” In re Clark, slip op. at 3. Because mandatory distributions begin upon inheritance, the beneficiary may consume the account long before retirement age. That is the opposite of the retirement function Congress protected. See also Bruning v. United States, 376 U.S. 358, 360–61 (1964) (bankruptcy law does not turn on formal labels divorced from the statute’s operation).

This Court’s exemption cases likewise look to present use and legal incidents, not to the asset’s remote history. In Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622, 628–29 (1943), the Court allowed a homestead exemption because the debtor complied with state-law requirements before the exemption question was finally fixed. But the Court’s analysis turned on the property’s status at the relevant time, not on its prior character. Here, by the petition date, the inherited IRA no longer functioned as Heidi’s retirement savings. The same is true under Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294, 299 (1903), which recognized that exempt property is treated differently because it is exempt at bankruptcy, not because it once had some protected quality.

The Clarks’ contrary theory also conflicts with the structure of bankruptcy exemptions. In Kaufman v. Tredway, 195 U.S. 271, 276–77 (1904), the Court emphasized that property must actually “become a part of the debtor’s estate” before it is administered for creditors. And in Bronx Brass Foundry, Inc. v. Irving Trust Co., 297 U.S. 230, 234 (1936), the Court refused to let a creditor manipulate bankruptcy procedure to obtain an unwarranted advantage. Those decisions reflect a basic bankruptcy premise: courts look to what property is in the estate, in the debtor’s hands, at the time of filing. An inherited IRA held by a debtor is not “retirement funds” in that sense because it is immediately available for current consumption subject only to tax rules.

The Clarks’ reading would also create a windfall inconsistent with the Code’s careful balance. The Bankruptcy Act has never been understood to shelter assets merely because they once enjoyed a protected status. See Boteler v. Ingels, 308 U.S. 57, 60–61 (1939) (bankruptcy provisions are applied according to their operative function); Schrader v. Manufacturers’ Nat. Bank of Chicago, 133 U.S. 67, 71–72 (1890) (property treatment depends on the legal relation at issue). And where the Court has protected exempt assets, it has done so because they remained exempt in the debtor’s hands. McKee v. Paradise, 299 U.S. 119, 122–24 (1936), for example, recognized preferential treatment only after tracing actual trust funds into the bankruptcy estate. There is no comparable tracing here from Heidi’s own retirement savings to an account she inherited with mandatory payout rules.

Finally, the policy argument cuts against the Clarks. If inherited IRAs were exempt, retirement savings could pass from generation to generation insulated from creditors, turning tax deferral into effective exemption. The Code does not say that. As the Seventh Circuit correctly held, “inherited IRAs do not qualify, because they are not savings reserved for use after their owners stop working.” In re Clark, slip op. at 8. The judgment should be affirmed.
