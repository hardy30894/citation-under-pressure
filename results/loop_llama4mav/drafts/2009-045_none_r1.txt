The question presented is whether a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a court may award a reasonable attorneys' fee to a prevailing party, taking into account the value of the services rendered and the results obtained.

The principle that a court may consider the quality of representation and the results obtained when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee is rooted in early Supreme Court decisions. In Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1882), the Court established that a court may award a reasonable attorneys' fee to a prevailing party, considering the "character of the services rendered" and the "results obtained." Id. at 532-33. This principle was reaffirmed in Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), where the Court held that a court may consider the "skill and ability" of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 124-25.

The importance of considering the results obtained when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee was further emphasized in Hartford Fire Insurance Co. v. Bradley, 142 U.S. 651 (1892). The Court noted that a court should take into account "the amount involved, the character of the services, and the results obtained." Id. at 655. This principle is also reflected in the English common law, as seen in Copp v. Cheshire, 1 Vern. 259 (1683), where the English Court of Chancery recognized that a court may award a "liberal allowance" to a solicitor who has rendered exceptional service. Id. at 260.

The Supreme Court has continued to recognize the importance of considering the quality of representation and the results obtained when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee. In Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court held that a court has the power to award attorneys' fees in a case where a party has created, preserved, or protected a fund or property for the benefit of others. The Court noted that the fee award should be based on the "value of the services rendered" and the "results obtained." Id. at 166-67. Similarly, in Ex parte Peterson, 253 U.S. 300 (1920), the Court recognized that a court has the authority to allow a reasonable fee to a party who has incurred expenses in litigating a matter that benefits others, considering the "character of the services rendered" and the "results obtained." Id. at 316-17.

The Sixth Circuit has also recognized the importance of considering the results obtained and the skill and ability of counsel when determining a reasonable fee, as seen in G. & C. Merriam Co. v. Saalfield, 241 F. 682 (6th Cir. 1917). The court held that a court may consider these factors when determining a reasonable fee, id. at 685, and this principle is consistent with the longstanding practice of considering the quality of representation and the results obtained when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that a district court has the discretion to enhance a fee award based on exceptional performance and results. The lodestar figure is not always a sufficient measure of the value of the services rendered, and a court may consider other factors, including the quality of representation and the results obtained, when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee. The district court's decision to enhance the lodestar figure in this case was not an abuse of discretion, as it was based on detailed findings and specific evidence on the record. The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court's decision, and the longstanding principles established by the Supreme Court and other courts support the conclusion that a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based on the quality of performance and results obtained.
