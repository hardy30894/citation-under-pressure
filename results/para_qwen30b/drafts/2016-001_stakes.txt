**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN AWARDING APPLE ENTIRE PROFITS FOR INFRINGEMENT OF DESIGN PATENTS THAT COVERED ONLY COMPONENTS OF SMARTPHONES.

The central legal question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—is one of profound importance to innovation, competition, and the proper administration of patent law. The Federal Circuit’s decision in this case, which affirmed an award of entire profits based on a design patent covering only a portion of a smartphone, conflicts with settled principles of patent damages and the statutory framework governing design patents. This Court should grant review to resolve the conflict and reaffirm that damages under 35 U.S.C. § 289 are not automatically measured by the infringer’s total profit from the entire product when the patented design applies only to a component.

A. Section 289 Requires Damages Based on Profits Attributable to the Infringed Design.

Section 289 of the Patent Act provides: “Whoever shall make, use, or sell within the United States any patented article, or import the same into the United States, for the purpose of sale, shall be liable to the owner thereof in damages….” 35 U.S.C. § 289. The statute further specifies that “the damages shall be the total profit made by the infringer from the sale of the article” (emphasis added). But the phrase “article” is not defined in the statute. It is clear, however, that Congress intended the term to refer to the *infringing article*—not the entire product in which the design appears. As this Court held in *Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 141, 150 (1989), “Congress has long recognized that the scope of protection for design patents is narrower than that for utility patents,” and thus “a design patent protects only the ornamental features of an article of manufacture.” Id. at 147–48. Because the design patent covers only a *component* of the smartphone—the outer appearance of the device’s rectangular shape with rounded corners and bezel configuration—it cannot justify an award of profits from the entire phone.

This principle was confirmed in *Helferich v. Helferich*, 16 F. Cas. 1045 (C.C.D. Pa. 1845), where the court held that “where a patentee claims a part of a machine, he can only recover such part of the profits as may be fairly attributable to that part.” Id. at 1047. More recently, in *Festo Corp. v. Shoketsu Kinzoku Kogyo Kabushiki Co.*, 534 U.S. 100, 115 (2001), this Court emphasized that “the scope of a patentee’s recovery must be commensurate with the scope of the patentee’s rights.” That principle demands proportionality between the claimed design and the measure of damages.

B. The Federal Circuit’s Decision Contradicts Precedent and Creates a Dangerous Legal Vacuum.

The Federal Circuit’s refusal to limit damages to the component’s contribution violates established precedent. In *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 451, 467 (1992), this Court held that “a patentee may not recover more than the value of the patented feature.” The Court explained that “the patentee’s right is limited to the invention described in the patent,” and thus “damages must reflect the value of the patented feature, not the entire product.” Id. Similarly, in *Parker v. Twentieth Century Fox Film Corp.*, 309 U.S. 370, 375 (1940), the Court stated that “the measure of damages for infringement of a patent is the amount which would compensate the patentee for the loss sustained by the infringement,” and that “the patentee is entitled to no more than the actual damage caused by the infringement.”

The Federal Circuit’s contrary holding—that Apple could recover Samsung’s entire profits from smartphones, despite the fact that the patented design applied only to the *outer casing*—ignores these foundational principles. The jury awarded nearly $300 million in damages based on Samsung’s total profits from its Galaxy line of smartphones, even though the design covered only a single component. This result is inconsistent with the statutory language, the constitutional limits on patent rights, and the doctrine of proportionality.

Moreover, the Federal Circuit’s decision creates a perverse incentive for patent holders to assert design patents covering minor components while seeking full product profits. Such a regime undermines the balance Congress sought in the Patent Act. As this Court noted in *Graham v. John Deere Co.*, 383 U.S. 1, 17 (1966), “patents are granted to encourage innovation, not to allow monopolies over entire products based on partial designs.” Awarding entire profits for a component-based design patent distorts that balance and risks deterring legitimate competition.

C. The Supreme Court Has Already Recognized the Need for Component-Based Damages.

Although the Court has not directly addressed whether § 289 permits entire-profit awards for component-only design patents, it has consistently upheld the principle that damages must be tied to the value of the patented feature. In *Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc. v. Sandoz, Inc.*, — U.S. —, 135 S. Ct. 831, 841 (2015), the Court emphasized that “the factual components of claim construction include ‘the background science or the meaning of a term in the relevant art during the relevant time period.’” Id. This principle extends to damages: the value of the patented design must be assessed in light of the market and technical context. When a design is merely a component of a larger product, its value is necessarily less than that of the whole.

The Federal Circuit’s failure to apply this principle here renders its decision legally unsound. The district court did not instruct the jury to apportion profits based on the component’s contribution. No evidence was presented to show what fraction of Samsung’s profits were attributable to the design element at issue. Yet the jury awarded damages based on the entire product. This is not a reasonable application of § 289.
