### ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause does not require Arizona to allow a defendant to use mental-health expert testimony, separately from an insanity defense, to negate mens rea. The Constitution guarantees the State’s burden to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt, but it has never required States to adopt a particular evidentiary theory of diminished capacity or to admit all mental-state evidence for all purposes. See *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970); *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952).

This Court’s decisions recognize that a State may define how insanity is litigated and may place burdens on the defendant without violating due process. In *Leland*, the Court upheld Oregon’s rule requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, even though the prosecution still had to prove premeditation, deliberation, malice, and intent beyond a reasonable doubt. 343 U.S. at 798-99. The Court explained that “the prosecution was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every element of the crime charged,” yet the State could still channel the use of insanity evidence within its chosen framework. *Id.* at 798. That holding is fatal to petitioner’s claim, because if due process permits a State to make insanity a separate issue and to impose a stringent burden of proof on the defendant, it necessarily permits the State to regulate the manner in which expert testimony relevant to insanity may be used. See *id.* at 798-99.

Likewise, *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), and *Moore v. Duckworth*, 443 U.S. 713 (1979), confirm that sanity and mens rea are distinct questions that are handled according to the governing law of the forum. In *Moore*, the Court rejected a due process challenge to the sufficiency of the State’s proof of sanity and reiterated that, under state law, expert testimony does not control the jury’s determination. 443 U.S. at 714-15. And in *Leland*, the Court specifically treated insanity as a defense separate from the elements of the offense, even while acknowledging that evidence offered on insanity may bear on mental state. 343 U.S. at 798-99. That is precisely Arizona’s approach here: it did not bar Clark from introducing psychiatric evidence; it confined that evidence to the insanity issue, while leaving the State to prove intent by other evidence.

Petitioner’s position would constitutionalize a diminished-capacity defense that this Court has never held the Fourteenth Amendment requires. The Court has repeatedly recognized that States retain substantial authority over criminal defenses and the allocation of proof. In *Jones v. United States*, 463 U.S. 354, 366-67 (1983), the Court again cited *Leland* for the proposition that a defendant may be required to prove insanity by a higher standard. *Jones* also observed that a defendant who raises insanity is already invoking a doctrinal system separate from ordinary mens rea adjudication. *Id.* at 367. The logic of *Jones* cuts against petitioner: if due process tolerates a special insanity regime with different burdens and consequences, it does not compel Arizona to convert every psychiatric opinion into admissible evidence on intent.

Nor does *Clark* present the kind of exclusion of all defense evidence that would raise a serious constitutional problem. The Court has condemned the arbitrary denial of a fair opportunity to defend oneself where the defendant is denied basic process, such as a hearing on competence to stand trial. See *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105, 108-09 (1954); *Blackburn v. Alabama*, 361 U.S. 199, 206-09 (1960). But Arizona did not deprive Clark of a defense. He was permitted to present his insanity claim and expert evidence on that issue. The State simply required him to use that evidence for the purpose Arizona law assigned to it. That is an evidentiary limitation, not a denial of due process. See *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798-99.

Petitioner’s reliance on the fact that the prosecution had to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt is misplaced. Due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of the elements the State has defined, but it does not dictate that every category of defense evidence must be admissible on every element. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364; *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798-99. Arizona’s rule preserves the State’s burden of proof while refusing to recognize a separate mental-disease excuse from intent outside the insanity framework. That choice lies within the State’s authority. See *Rivera v. Delaware*, 429 U.S. 877, 878-79 (1976) (Powell, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (recognizing the State’s ability to characterize insanity doctrine); *Lynch v. Overholser*, 369 U.S. 705, 712-13 (1962) (describing insanity as a distinct legal category).

The Court should therefore reject petitioner’s proposed due process right. The Constitution does not compel Arizona to admit expert testimony on mental disease to negate mens rea when the State has chosen to limit that testimony to the insanity defense. *Leland*, 343 U.S. 790; *Jones*, 463 U.S. 354.
