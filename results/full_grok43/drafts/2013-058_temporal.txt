The purpose of the bankruptcy laws is to afford the honest debtor a fresh start by relieving him of pre-existing debts while ensuring that exempt property is limited to assets serving the debtor's own needs at the time of filing. Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). This principle requires that any claimed exemption be determined by the character of the property in the debtor's hands when the petition is filed. White v. Stump, 266 U.S. 310, 313 (1924).

An inherited individual retirement account does not qualify as exempt retirement funds under these settled rules. Once the original owner dies, the account ceases to be reserved for anyone's retirement and instead becomes subject to mandatory distribution within a fixed period. The funds therefore lose the attributes that once rendered them exempt. See Burlingham v. Crouse, 228 U.S. 459, 473 (1913) (property that passes out of an exempt status becomes part of the estate). The same result follows when funds are withdrawn from a retirement vehicle before bankruptcy: they are no longer protected. Holden v. Stratton, 198 U.S. 202, 214 (1905).

The absence of any requirement that the funds be the debtor's own retirement savings does not enlarge the exemption. Exemptions have always been personal to the debtor and measured by the use to which the debtor puts the property. See Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622, 626 (1943). Just as a homestead loses its exempt character when the debtor no longer occupies it as a residence, an inherited account that must be consumed before the debtor's retirement cannot be treated as retirement funds. See White, 266 U.S. at 313.

Allowing the exemption would convert a time-limited tax deferral into a permanent shield for assets available for current consumption, contrary to the limited scope of statutory exemptions recognized before the modern Code. See Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294, 299 (1903). The trustee is therefore entitled to administer the account for the benefit of creditors.
