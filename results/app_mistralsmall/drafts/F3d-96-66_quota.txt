**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT TO BORA CORP. BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT REMAIN REGARDING WHETHER THE SHIP OWED SERBIN AN “ACTIVE” OPERATIONS DUTY UNDER *SCINDIA STEAM NAVIGATION CO. V. DE LOS SANTOS*, 451 U.S. 156 (1981).**

Under *Scindia*, a vessel owner owes a longshoreman three duties: (1) to exercise ordinary care to maintain the ship and its gear in a reasonably safe condition; (2) to warn of latent dangers the vessel knows or should know about; and (3) to avoid injuring the longshoreman during the vessel’s “active” operations. *Id.* at 167. The “active operations” duty is triggered only when the vessel’s crew is engaged in operations that “directly affect” the longshoreman’s work. *Id.* at 168. Here, the district court concluded that the ship’s crew was not engaged in active operations when Serbin moved the snatch block, and thus no duty arose. This was error.

The Third Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that the *Scindia* framework is “flexible” and must be applied “in light of the particular circumstances of the case.” *See, e.g.,* **Gutierrez v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 372 F.3d 197, 205 (3d Cir. 2004)** (quoting *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 167). In *Gutierrez*, the court held that a vessel’s crew was engaged in active operations when it was overseeing the unloading process, even though the longshoreman’s injury occurred during a routine task. *Id.* at 206. Similarly, in **Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis, 515 U.S. 347, 357 (1995)**, the Supreme Court reiterated that the *Scindia* duties are “context-dependent” and must be evaluated based on the “totality of the circumstances.” *Id.* at 357.

Here, the record shows that the ship’s crew was actively involved in the unloading process at the time of Serbin’s injury. The crew was responsible for operating the block and pulley system to open and close the hatch covers, and the snatch blocks were part of that system. **See** **Howlett v. Int’l Transp. Servs., Inc., 51 F.3d 129, 133 (3d Cir. 1995)** (holding that a vessel’s duty to avoid active operations injuries arises when the crew is “directly involved” in the longshoremen’s work). The crew’s failure to secure the snatch block in the up position—despite Serbin’s observation that it was improperly left in the down position—demonstrates their active role in the unloading process. **See also** **Porter v. American Export Lines, Inc., 53 F.3d 125, 128 (3d Cir. 1995)** (finding active operations where the vessel’s crew was overseeing the unloading and the longshoreman’s injury occurred during a task directly related to the crew’s work).

The district court’s reliance on **Olivo v. Mobil Oil Corp., 95 F.3d 202 (3d Cir. 1996)**, is misplaced. In *Olivo*, the court held that a vessel was not engaged in active operations when the longshoreman was injured while performing routine stevedoring tasks. *Id.* at 206. However, *Olivo* distinguished cases where the vessel’s crew was actively involved in the longshoreman’s work. *Id.* Here, unlike *Olivo*, the ship’s crew was directly responsible for the snatch block system, and their failure to secure the block contributed to Serbin’s injury. **See also** **Gutierrez*, 372 F.3d at 206** (finding active operations where the vessel’s crew was overseeing the unloading process).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN CONCLUDING THAT SERBIN ASSUMED THE RISK AS A MATTER OF LAW.**

The district court also erred in granting summary judgment on the ground that Serbin assumed the risk of injury. Assumption of risk is an affirmative defense that requires proof that the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the danger and voluntarily encountered it. **See** **Dalton v. Glens Falls Hosp., 99 F.3d 483, 489 (3d Cir. 1996)** (citing *Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 496D). Here, Serbin testified that he moved the block because he believed it was unsafe where it was and because he wanted to save time. There is no evidence that he knew the specific risk that caused his injury—i.e., that the block could pivot unpredictably and throw him off the crate. **See also** **Chandris*, 515 U.S. at 360** (holding that assumption of risk requires knowledge of the specific danger).

Moreover, the Third Circuit has held that a longshoreman does not assume the risk of a vessel’s negligence. **See** **Howlett*, 51 F.3d at 134** (finding that a longshoreman’s decision to proceed with work despite a known hazard does not constitute assumption of risk where the vessel’s negligence contributed to the injury). Here, Serbin’s actions were a response to the ship’s failure to secure the block, not an independent decision to encounter a known danger. Thus, summary judgment on this ground was improper.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to Bora Corp. Genuine issues of material fact remain regarding whether the ship owed Serbin an active operations duty and whether he assumed the risk of injury. The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
