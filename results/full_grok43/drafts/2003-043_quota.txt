ARGUMENT

The IRS may collect a tax only from the taxpayer against whom it has made a timely assessment. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Because the individual partners are distinct taxpayers, the assessments against the partnership did not extend the ten-year collection period to them. Neilson v. Chang (In re First T.D. & Inv., Inc.), 253 F.3d 520, 526 (9th Cir. 2001).

Section 6203 requires that an assessment record “the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The Code defines “taxpayer” to include both a partnership and an individual. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The regulation implementing § 6203 likewise demands “identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. An assessment against one taxpayer therefore cannot extend the limitations period applicable to another. United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961).

The IRS’s reliance on Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. There the partner had already paid the tax; the court held only that he could not recover a refund. Id. at 910. The opinion expressly recognized that collection without an individual assessment or judgment would require “resort to court action.” Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959), aff’d, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). No such action was brought here within three years. Goldston v. United States (In re Goldston), 104 F.3d 1198, 1200-01 (10th Cir. 1997).

United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is distinguishable for the same reason: the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. United States v. Wright, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only question presented was whether the partnership’s bankruptcy tolled the partners’ limitations period—an issue not raised here.

Even if state law renders partners jointly and severally liable, California requires a separate judgment against the partner before partnership assets may be reached from personal assets. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The IRS obtained no such judgment, and the three-year period for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The bankruptcy court therefore properly disallowed the claims as “unenforceable against the debtor.” 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1); Gruntz v. County of Los Angeles (In re Gruntz), 202 F.3d 1074, 1084 n.9 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc); Beaupied v. Chang (In re Chang), 163 F.3d 1138, 1140 (9th Cir. 1998).

The district court’s judgment should be affirmed.
