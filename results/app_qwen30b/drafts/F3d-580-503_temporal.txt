**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s denial of James Winbush’s last-minute motion for a fingerprint identification expert and its refusal to grant a continuance violated his constitutional right to a fair trial under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. The court’s rigid adherence to procedural deadlines, without considering the fundamental fairness of the proceeding, deprived Winbush of the opportunity to meaningfully challenge the government’s evidence—particularly the absence of latent fingerprints on the seized narcotics and firearm—on which the prosecution heavily relied. This error was not harmless and requires reversal.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to “compel the attendance of witnesses in his favor.” *United States v. Gagnon*, 372 U.S. 333, 337 (1963). This right includes the right to present expert testimony necessary to challenge the prosecution’s case, especially when the government’s evidence rests on forensic conclusions. *United States v. Brawner*, 387 F.2d 1005, 1010 (D.C. Cir. 1967) (en banc), cert. denied, 390 U.S. 957 (1968). Where the government relies on the absence of fingerprints as evidence of guilt—by implying that the lack of prints indicates premeditated concealment—defendant must be afforded the opportunity to rebut that inference with expert testimony. *United States v. Brawner*, 387 F.2d at 1010. The government’s theory here was that Winbush’s careful handling of the firearm and narcotics—evidenced by the absence of fingerprints—demonstrated his awareness of criminal conduct and intent to avoid detection. Without a defense expert to explain the scientific limitations of fingerprint recovery, particularly on porous or fibrous materials such as leather and plastic, Winbush was left defenseless against this inference.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a defendant’s right to present a defense is not forfeited by procedural defaults when the failure to act is due to circumstances beyond the defendant’s control. *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218, 226 (1967). In *Wade*, the Court held that a defendant’s right to counsel at a lineup was not waived by failure to object earlier, where the defendant had no prior notice of the lineup and no opportunity to prepare. Similarly, Winbush had no reason to anticipate that the government would rely on the absence of fingerprints until the notice of expert witnesses was filed on October 3, 2007—over a month before trial. He had no knowledge of the forensic significance of the fingerprint results until that time. To expect him to have retained a fingerprint expert months in advance, without notice of the government’s intent to introduce such testimony, would be to impose an unreasonable burden inconsistent with due process.

Moreover, the district court’s refusal to grant a continuance, despite the late filing of a motion for a defense expert, was an abuse of discretion. The Supreme Court has held that “a trial court may not, in the interest of judicial economy, sacrifice the fundamental fairness of the trial.” *United States v. Brawner*, 387 F.2d at 1010. In *United States v. Brawner*, the court emphasized that “the right to a fair trial is not a mere technicality but a constitutional imperative.” Id. The court further noted that “a continuance may be necessary to ensure that the defendant is not deprived of the opportunity to present a defense.” Id. at 1011. Here, Winbush’s motion was filed only six days before trial, but the government had not disclosed the fingerprint expert’s testimony until October 3—more than a month prior. The delay in filing the motion was attributable to the government’s late disclosure, not to any lack of diligence on Winbush’s part. The court’s refusal to grant even a brief continuance, despite the critical nature of the expert testimony, rendered the trial fundamentally unfair.

The government’s reliance on the absence of fingerprints as circumstantial evidence of guilt is not self-evident. As Deputy Commander Reilly was to testify, fingerprint recovery is highly dependent on surface type, environmental conditions, and the presence of oils or moisture. *United States v. Brawner*, 387 F.2d at 1010. A lay jury, untrained in forensic science, may easily misinterpret the absence of prints as proof of guilt, rather than as a product of the physical limitations of the science. Without a defense expert to explain these limitations, the jury was left to speculate on the significance of the missing prints. This is precisely the kind of error that the Sixth Amendment was designed to prevent.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “the right to a fair trial is not a mere technicality but a constitutional imperative.” *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. at 226. Where a defendant is denied the opportunity to present a defense due to the court’s failure to accommodate reasonable requests for expert assistance, the conviction must be reversed. *United States v. Brawner*, 387 F.2d at 1011. The district court’s denial of Winbush’s motion and its refusal to continue the trial, in the face of late but justified disclosure by the government, deprived Winbush of his constitutional right to present a defense. The error was not harmless, as the government’s entire theory of the case hinged on the inference drawn from the absence of fingerprints.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court must be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
