**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Cindy Moll’s hostile work environment claim under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), by narrowly construing the statute of limitations to exclude non-sexually offensive conduct occurring outside the limitations period. The court’s error stemmed from its failure to apply the well-established principle that a hostile work environment claim is evaluated in its totality, with the statute of limitations beginning to run only when the last act of harassment occurs. See *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775, 788 (1998) (“The statute of limitations for a hostile work environment claim begins to run when the plaintiff knows or should have known of the discriminatory conduct.”). The district court’s rigid focus on whether individual acts were “sexually offensive” within the limitations period ignored the cumulative and pervasive nature of the harassment, which is central to such claims.

Under Second Circuit precedent, a hostile work environment claim may be based on a combination of facially sex-neutral and sexually offensive acts, so long as the conduct is “sufficiently severe or pervasive to create an objectively hostile or abusive work environment.” *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002). The Second Circuit has made clear that “the offensive nature of the conduct need not be explicitly sexual” and that “a pattern of discriminatory conduct, even if not overtly sexual, may still constitute a hostile environment if it is motivated by sex.” *Id.* at 376. Here, Moll alleged a pattern of conduct—including being denied promotions, excluded from social events, subjected to arbitrary work restrictions, and retaliated against after filing a complaint—all of which were directed at her because of her sex. These acts, when viewed together, created a hostile environment that persisted well beyond the limitations period.

The district court erred in treating the 1998–1999 notes and hotel room incidents as the sole basis for the hostile environment claim. While those acts were sexually offensive, the court failed to consider the broader context of discriminatory treatment that continued through 2003 and beyond. *See* *Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986) (“A hostile environment claim is not limited to conduct that is explicitly sexual in nature.”). Moll’s allegations that male colleagues were promoted during a so-called “freeze” while she was denied advancement, that she was barred from working from home while male peers were permitted to do so, and that she was denied vacation time despite male colleagues receiving similar requests, all constitute sex-based disparate treatment that contributed to a hostile environment. *See* *Burlington Indus., Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742, 760 (1998) (hostile environment claims may include “non-sexual but discriminatory conduct” that is “sufficiently severe or pervasive” to alter the conditions of employment).

Moreover, the transfer to Syracuse in December 2004—after Moll filed her EEOC charge and lawsuit—was a clear act of retaliation, which further contributed to the hostile environment. *See* *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 804 (1973) (retaliation claims are cognizable under Title VII). The transfer was not a neutral business decision, as Verizon offered Moll only three options: transfer, find another job, or accept a severance package—none of which were viable alternatives. The court’s failure to consider this retaliatory transfer as part of the hostile environment claim was error. *See* *Barnes v. Costle*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (D.C. Cir. 1977) (retaliation and hostile environment claims may be interrelated and should be considered together).

The district court also erred in dismissing Moll’s claim based on the “sham issue of fact” doctrine. The court held that Moll could not rely on a non-party witness’s testimony that contradicted her prior deposition, but this misapplies the doctrine. The “sham issue of fact” doctrine applies only when a party creates a false factual dispute through perjured or fabricated testimony. *See* *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 250 (1986) (“The mere existence of a scintilla of evidence in support of the plaintiff’s position will not defeat a motion for summary judgment.”). The doctrine does not bar the court from considering credible, non-party testimony that is consistent with the record. *See* *Harris v. City of New York*, 822 F.3d 706, 712 (2d Cir. 2016) (non-party testimony may be considered if it is not “contrived” or “inconsistent with the record”).

Finally, the district court’s dismissal of Moll’s claim based on the statute of limitations ignored the “continuing violation” doctrine. *See* *Barnes v. Gen. Motors Corp.*, 897 F.2d 1254, 1258 (2d Cir. 1990) (a hostile environment claim may be based on a series of related acts, even if some occurred outside the limitations period). The Second Circuit has held that “a hostile work environment claim is not time-barred if the discriminatory conduct is part of a continuing pattern.” *Id.* at 1259. Moll’s allegations of disparate treatment, exclusion from social events, and retaliatory transfer all formed part of a continuing pattern of sex-based discrimination that culminated in her disability leave in 2004.

For these reasons, the district court erred in dismissing Moll’s hostile work environment claim. The claim must be evaluated in its totality, and the statute of limitations should not be applied in a manner that excludes conduct that contributes to a pervasive and hostile work environment. *See* *Faragher*, 524 U.S. at 788; *Alfano*, 294 F.3d at 375; *Barnes*, 897 F.2d at 1259.
