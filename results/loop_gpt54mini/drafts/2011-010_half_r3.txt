**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act does **not** extend Longshore Act coverage to any employee injured anywhere so long as the injury has some relationship to offshore operations. The statutory text cabins coverage to injuries “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That language ties coverage to injuries occurring in the course of offshore operations, not to every downstream consequence of those operations ashore. The court below properly rejected the Benefits Review Board’s cramped situs-of-injury rule, but the respondent’s broader claim still cannot be sustained. The injury here occurred at an onshore processing facility, not on the Shelf; the statute’s ordinary meaning, read in light of settled interpretive principles, does not reach so far.

Text matters. Where “the words of a law” are clear, courts must apply them as written. **Lake County v. Rollins, 130 U.S. 662, 670 (1889)**. Courts must give effect to statutory language, not extend it by policy preference. **Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917)**. And where Congress uses words of limitation, courts must respect them. See **Brown v. Duchesne, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 183, 194 (1856)**. The point is reflected in older authority: statutes are not to be enlarged beyond their terms. **United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 347-50 (1971)**.

The phrase “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” requires a genuine operational nexus to shelf activity, not a mere but-for chain untethered to the Shelf itself. Respondent’s position would erase the geographic component Congress chose to include and convert a maritime/offshore compensation scheme into a general employment statute for all shoreside injuries connected to offshore work. In **South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 260-61 (1940)**, the Court focused on the statutory limits Congress placed on coverage. That decision confirms that compensation statutes are bounded by the conditions Congress specified, not by an open-ended causal theory.

Respondent’s broad theory also conflicts with the long-settled rule that remedial statutes, though liberally administered, cannot be rewritten. **United States v. Merriam, 263 U.S. 179, 187-88 (1923)**. Even where a statute is humane in purpose, courts must still apply its terms. **Hebert v. Louisiana, 272 U.S. 312, 316-17 (1926)**. And when Congress legislates against an established legal backdrop, it is presumed to know the distinction between location-based coverage and broader causal formulations. **Ex parte McNiel, 13 Wall. 236, 242 (1871)**.

Finally, the respondent’s attempt to transform OCSLA into a sweeping compensatory regime cannot be reconciled with the statute’s structure. Congress extended Longshore benefits only for specified offshore injuries. **Ocean Accident & Guarantee Corp. v. Washington, 6 F.2d 103, 105 (9th Cir. 1925)**. That structure presupposes an identifiable place and event of offshore origin. The injury here occurred when Mr. Valladolid was working at an onshore oil-processing facility, not while conducting operations on the Shelf. Extending OCSLA to that setting would erase the statutory boundary Congress enacted.

For those reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
