ARGUMENT

Arizona’s rule confining mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense does not violate the Due Process Clause. A state may “define the elements of its crimes and the defenses to them,” Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 210 (1977), and may “channel” evidence bearing on mental condition into a single affirmative defense on which the defendant bears the burden of proof, Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 799-800 (1952). The Clause “does not require the states to conform to any particular model of criminal procedure,” id. at 798, and therefore permits Arizona to treat evidence of mental disease exclusively as proof of legal insanity rather than as rebuttal to mens rea.

This Court has repeatedly upheld analogous restrictions. In Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37 (1996), a plurality sustained a statute barring voluntary-intoxication evidence offered to negate intent, explaining that the Due Process Clause does not “set[] a floor” on the categories of evidence a state must admit. Id. at 43. The same principle governs here: Arizona may exclude mental-disease testimony from the mens-rea stage without denying the defendant “a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense.” Crane v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986). The defendant remains free to introduce lay testimony, his own statements, and circumstantial evidence bearing on intent; only the specialized expert evidence is channeled to the insanity issue.

Nor does the rule impermissibly shift the burden of proof. The State must still prove every element, including intent, beyond a reasonable doubt. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Arizona’s scheme merely withholds one species of evidence from that inquiry, a limitation the Court has found consistent with due process when the evidence is instead admitted under an affirmative defense. See Patterson, 432 U.S. at 207-08; see also Fisher v. United States, 328 U.S. 463, 476-77 (1946) (upholding District of Columbia rule limiting mental-defect evidence to insanity).

Because the Arizona Court of Appeals correctly applied these settled principles, the judgment should be affirmed.
