ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit's affirmance of Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) without requiring proof of his subjective intent to threaten contravenes established First Amendment jurisprudence. The "true threats" exception, which § 875(c) embodies, has long been understood to necessitate a showing of the speaker's intent to convey a threat.

A review of relevant precedents reveals that a subjective intent standard is essential. In Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 309-310 (1940), the Court underscored the importance of considering the speaker's intent when evaluating the constitutionality of a statute regulating speech. The Court in Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 573-574 (1942), upheld a statute prohibiting "fighting words" because such words, by their very utterance, inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace, implying that the speaker's intent is crucial.

Lower courts have also acknowledged the significance of intent in determining whether a statement constitutes a threat. The Seventh Circuit, in United States v. Prochaska, 222 F.2d 1, 2 (7th Cir. 1955), held that a threat under a federal statute requires an "intent to cause the apprehension" of harm. Similarly, the Second Circuit, in United States v. Carrier, 168 F.2d 881, 882 (2d Cir. 1948), emphasized that a threat must be made with the "intent to cause fear or apprehension." These decisions demonstrate that a speaker's intent is a critical factor in assessing whether their words constitute a true threat.

Furthermore, cases involving statutes regulating speech have consistently required a showing of intent to convey a prohibited message. In Pierce v. United States, 314 U.S. 306, 311 (1941), the Court held that a statute prohibiting the mailing of material with intent to cause disloyalty or refusal of duty required proof of the defendant's intent. The Court in Gorin v. United States, 312 U.S. 19, 27-28 (1941), similarly emphasized the importance of proving the defendant's intent to injure the United States when prosecuting under the Espionage Act. These precedents establish that intent is a necessary element in statutes regulating speech.

The Third Circuit's reliance on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced, as Kosma's objective standard is inconsistent with the emphasis on intent in the aforementioned cases. In Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949), the Court held that a conviction for disorderly conduct based on speech requires a showing that the speech was intended to incite violence. Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242, 259-260 (1937), further supports the proposition that a statute regulating speech must be applied with regard to the defendant's intent.

In light of these precedents, it is clear that a conviction under § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten. The Third Circuit's decision to the contrary should be reversed. The judgment should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with a subjective intent standard, ensuring that Elonis' conviction is evaluated in accordance with established First Amendment jurisprudence.
