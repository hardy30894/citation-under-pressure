The judgment below should be affirmed because the IRS cannot extend the ten-year collection period under 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) by assessing only the partnership and then, years later, proceed directly against individual partners who were never assessed. The Internal Revenue Code makes assessment taxpayer-specific. Section 6203 requires assessment “by recording the liability of the taxpayer,” and § 6502(a) extends collection time only after “the assessment of any tax” has been made within the period prescribed. 26 U.S.C. §§ 6203, 6502(a). As the Ninth Circuit explained, “the individual or entity assessed must be a separately identified ‘taxpayer.’” United States v. Galletti, 322 F.3d 666, 673 (9th Cir. 2003). That interpretation is consistent with the settled understanding that a partnership is distinct from its partners. Forsyth v. Woods, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 484, 495 (1871) (“The partnership is a distinct thing from the partners themselves”).  

This Court’s partnership cases confirm that distinctness. In Liberty Nat. Bank of Roanoke, Va. v. Bear, 276 U.S. 215, 222-23 (1928), the Court recognized separate administration of partnership and individual estates, with partnership assets applied to partnership debts and individual estates to individual debts. Francis v. McNeal, 228 U.S. 695, 699-700 (1913), likewise treated partnership and individual liability as different, warning against “an anomaly” in conflating them. And Meek v. Centre County Banking Co., 264 U.S. 499, 501-02 (1924), and Meek v. Centre County Banking Co. Dale v. Same. Breeze v. Same, 268 U.S. 426, 429-30 (1925), reflect that partnership bankruptcy and partner liability are not interchangeable concepts. Those cases do not authorize the IRS to collapse the statutory distinction between a partnership assessment and an assessment against each partner.

The Ninth Circuit’s older tax precedents point the same way. In Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960), the court upheld a refund denial where the partner had already paid, but it did not hold that a partnership assessment alone authorizes collection from unassessed partners. To the contrary, the district court had recognized that assessment of the partnership was “unnecessary and superfluous” only to “create liability,” not to eliminate the need for a proper collection mechanism. Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959), aff’d, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458, 462-63 (9th Cir. 1961), is even more directly supportive of respondent’s position, because the court treated the absence of an individual assessment as fatal to the government’s attempt to reach a partner’s property.  

The government’s reliance on state partnership law is no better. California law permits collection from a partner’s assets only after a judgment against that partner. Galletti, 322 F.3d at 676; see Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). A claim is disallowable in bankruptcy if it is “unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor.” 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). Because the IRS neither assessed nor obtained judgment against these partners within the limitations period, its claims are unenforceable.  

The government’s contrary rule would rewrite § 6502(a). A timely assessment against the partnership would forever preserve collection against every present and former partner, even if the IRS never identified them as taxpayers and never took action within the ordinary limitations period. That is inconsistent with the Code’s text and structure and with the long-recognized separateness of partnership and individual obligations. See Guaranty Trust Co. v. Commissioner, 303 U.S. 493, 496-97 (1938); Heiner v. Mellon, 304 U.S. 271, 279-80 (1938); Commissioner v. Tower, 327 U.S. 280, 287-88 (1946); Commissioner v. Culbertson, 337 U.S. 733, 742-43 (1949); Lusthaus v. Commissioner, 327 U.S. 293, 297-98 (1946). The judgment should be affirmed.
