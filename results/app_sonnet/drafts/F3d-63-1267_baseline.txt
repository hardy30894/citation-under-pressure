# ARGUMENT

## I. STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL DAMAGES CAUSALLY ATTRIBUTABLE TO THE ALLEGED PRICE DISCRIMINATION, AND THE JUDGMENT UNDER SECTION 4 OF THE CLAYTON ACT MUST BE VACATED.

A violation of section 2(a) of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 13(a), does not, without more, entitle a plaintiff to recover treble damages under section 4, 15 U.S.C. § 15(a). The Supreme Court has made clear that "the antitrust plaintiff must make some showing of actual injury attributable to something the antitrust laws were designed to prevent." *J. Truett Payne Co. v. Chrysler Motors Corp.*, 451 U.S. 557, 562 (1981). Proof of the statutory violation establishes only that discriminatory prices existed; it does not establish that Stelwagon suffered any cognizable injury as a result. *Id.* at 561-62 ("the injury the plaintiff suffered, if any, is not the injury the antitrust laws were designed to prevent").

Stelwagon's damages case rested entirely on a comparison of the prices Tarmac charged Stelwagon against the prices charged to Standard Roofing Company and Celotex Corporation, multiplied across Stelwagon's total volume of purchases. That approach conflates the *existence* of a price differential with *injury flowing from* the differential. Nothing in the record isolates what portion, if any, of Stelwagon's lost sales or reduced margins was caused by the price gap, as opposed to general market conditions, Stelwagon's own pricing decisions, or competition unrelated to Tarmac's conduct. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that antitrust damages "may not be based on speculation or guesswork." *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931). While a plaintiff need not prove damages with mathematical precision, and "the jury may make a fair and reasonable approximation," *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946), that latitude presupposes a evidentiary foundation connecting the wrong to the harm. Where, as here, the plaintiff's expert simply multiplied a raw price differential by volume without accounting for causation, competitive effect, or alternative explanations for Stelwagon's financial performance, the resulting figure is not a "reasonable estimate" but pure conjecture. *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100, 114 (1969) (damages must rest on "a reasonable basis for computation," not "speculation or guesswork").

This deficiency is fatal under the Robinson-Patman Act specifically because secondary-line injury requires proof that the price differential actually harmed competition between Stelwagon and the favored purchasers in the marketplace. *Falls City Industries, Inc. v. Vanco Beverage, Inc.*, 460 U.S. 428, 434-35 (1983) (setting forth the elements of a secondary-line claim, including a showing of competitive injury). A showing that Tarmac charged different prices to different distributors, standing alone, satisfies only the prima facie elements of section 2(a); it says nothing about whether that differential translated into actual, quantifiable pecuniary loss to Stelwagon. Because Stelwagon offered no method for the jury to distinguish injury caused by the discriminatory pricing from injury caused by other market forces, its evidence "amounts to only speculation and conjecture," *J. Truett Payne*, 451 U.S. at 566, and the treble-damages judgment cannot stand. This Court should vacate the judgment under section 4 of the Clayton Act.

## II. THE CONTRACT DAMAGES AWARD LIKEWISE RESTS ON IMPERMISSIBLY SPECULATIVE PROOF AND SHOULD NOT BE SUSTAINED AT THE REMITTED AMOUNT.

Even after the district court's remittitur, the $74,242 contract award suffers from the same infirmity. Under Pennsylvania law, which governs this diversity contract claim, lost profits must be proven "with reasonable certainty," and a plaintiff cannot recover where the claimed loss is "a matter of pure speculation or guesswork." *AM/PM Franchise Ass'n v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 526 Pa. 110, 121, 584 A.2d 915, 921 (1990). Pennsylvania courts likewise require that lost-profit damages be supported by evidence of "the amount of damages ... with a fair degree of probability," not conjecture about what might have occurred absent the breach. *Delahanty v. First Pa. Bank, N.A.*, 318 Pa. Super. 90, 108, 464 A.2d 1243, 1257 (1983).

Stelwagon's contract damages theory assumed that, absent Tarmac's sales to competing distributors, Stelwagon would have captured all of the diverted volume at its own historical margins. That assumption ignores the undisputed evidence that Stelwagon had limited warehouse capacity, a defined customer base of small and medium-sized contractors, and no demonstrated ability to absorb the additional volume it claims it lost. As in *AM/PM Franchise*, where the Pennsylvania Supreme Court rejected a lost-profits calculation built on "unsubstantiated assumptions" about market capture, 526 Pa. at 122, 584 A.2d at 921, Stelwagon's model rests on an unproven premise rather than record evidence of actual capacity and demand.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should vacate the judgment awarding treble damages under section 4 of the Clayton Act, and should further reduce or vacate the contract damages award as unsupported by evidence of reasonably certain loss.
