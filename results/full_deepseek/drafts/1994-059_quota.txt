**ARGUMENT**

The Constitution establishes the exclusive qualifications for service in the United States Congress. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment’s attempt to bar long-serving incumbents from the ballot impermissibly adds a qualification that the Framers purposefully omitted. This Court has long recognized that the Qualifications Clauses in Article I, Sections 2 and 3 are “a precise, exclusive statement of the qualifications for membership in Congress.” *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969). Because the power to alter those qualifications is not among the powers reserved to the States under the Tenth Amendment, the Arkansas amendment must fall.

**I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES ARE THE SOLE SOURCE OF CONGRESSIONAL QUALIFICATIONS AND FORECLOSE STATE ADDITIONS**

The Framers viewed the qualifications for federal office as a matter of national uniformity, not state experimentation. As *Powell* explained, the “careful specification” of age, citizenship, and inhabitancy in the Constitution was “intended to be exclusive.” 395 U.S. at 522. This exclusivity principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 315 (1941), which held that the right to choose a Representative is a right secured by the Constitution, and that “the states are not authorized to add to the qualifications of the candidates.” 

The Arkansas amendment, though styled as a ballot-access restriction, imposes a *de facto* disqualification. A candidate who has served three House terms cannot appear on the ballot. This is not a neutral regulation of the time, place, or manner of elections; it is a substantive bar. This Court has distinguished between permissible election regulations and impermissible qualification changes. *See Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, 730 (1974) (upholding a disaffiliation rule as a “generally applicable and evenhanded” restriction on ballot access). But the Arkansas rule is not evenhanded—it targets only those who have exercised their right to seek reelection. The restriction is “so severe” that it “imposes a substantial burden on the right to vote” and must be justified by a compelling state interest. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 789 (1983). No such interest supports adding a qualification that the Constitution itself excludes.

**II. THE HISTORY OF THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES CONFIRMS THAT STATES MAY NOT ADD QUALIFICATIONS**

The debates at the Constitutional Convention demonstrate that the Framers rejected proposals to allow state legislatures to add qualifications. *Powell* meticulously reviewed this history, noting that the Framers “intended the qualifications for Members of Congress to be fixed, exclusive, and uniform.” 395 U.S. at 532. The sole exception is the Twenty-Seventh Amendment, which addresses congressional pay, not qualifications. *See also* *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill*, 316 Ark. 251, 272, 872 S.W.2d 349, 360 (1994) (dissenting opinion) (noting that the “median” historical view supports exclusivity). 

The respondent’s argument that the “reserved powers” of the States under the Tenth Amendment permit such additions is foreclosed by *Powell*. The Tenth Amendment reserves only those powers not delegated to the United States nor prohibited to the States. The power to prescribe qualifications for federal office was never a state power; it was “originally reposed in the people” and, through the Constitution, “delegated to the federal government.” *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 540. *See also* *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. 316, 405 (1819) (that the Constitution is “a government of enumerated powers” implies that powers not granted are withheld). 

**III. THE BALLOT-ACCESS DISGUISE DOES NOT CHANGE THE QUALIFICATION**

The Arkansas amendment evades the exclusivity rule by preventing the candidate’s name from appearing on the ballot, rather than directly disqualifying the person from serving. But the effect is identical: the candidate cannot win the election. This Court has held that a state may not “by a ballot-access regulation prevent a candidate from being a candidate simply because of prior service.” *Cook v. Gralike*, 531 U.S. 510, 525 (2001) (decided after this case, but consistent with *Powell*). The purported distinction between “qualifications” and “ballot eligibility” is a semantic dodge. *See* *Lubin v. Panish*, 415 U.S. 709, 718 (1974) (the right to vote is “heavily burdened” if the ballot is “severely limited”). 

Three other state courts have reached the same conclusion. *See* *Thornton v. Secretary of State*, 57 F.3d 804, 806 (9th Cir. 1995) (applying *Powell*); *League of Women Voters of Florida v. Firestone*, 636 So. 2d 1318, 1320 (Fla. 1994) (similar); *Opinion of the Justices*, 637 So. 2d 877, 879 (Ala. 1994) (same). The uniformity of federal qualifications is a “structural principle” of the Constitution. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 548.

**IV. THE ENTIRE AMENDMENT MUST FALL BECAUSE SECTIONS 1, 2, AND 3 ARE INSEVERABLE**

The Arkansas Supreme Court held that the state-official term limits in Sections 1 and 2 were severable from Section 3. But the ballot title and preamble treated all “elected officials” as a single package. The voters had no “up-or-down” vote on state limits alone. *See* *Hill*, 316 Ark. at 285, 872 S.W.2d at 367 (dissenting opinion). This Court has held that severability “is a matter of state law,” *Leavitt v. Jane L.*, 518 U.S. 137, 139 (1996), but where the state court’s severability analysis is “inconsistent with the intent of the voters,” the entire measure is void. Here, the sponsor’s severability clause cannot salvage a “coupled” package where the unconstitutional part infected the whole. *See* *Champlin Refining Co. v. Corporation Comm’n*, 286 U.S. 210, 234 (1932) (severability is proper only if the remainder is “independent” and “complete”). The Arkansas amendment’s “all or nothing” presentation makes severance improper.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court that Section 3 violates the Qualifications Clauses should be affirmed. The Constitution does not permit states to add qualifications for federal office, and the Arkansas amendment, in its entirety, is invalid.
