### ARGUMENT

#### I. The disclosure provisions are facially invalid because they sweep far beyond the State’s permissible regulatory interests and burden core political speech and association.

The lobbying provisions compel disclosure of “all lobbying expenditures,” including expenditures made by the principal, and require itemization by broad categories such as “food and beverages, entertainment, research, communication, media advertising, publications, travel, and lodging.” Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a). Because lobbying is “protected speech” at the heart of the petitioning process, compelled disclosure must be justified by a constitutionally sufficient governmental interest and narrowly drawn. See Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 64-68 (1976) (upholding disclosure only where it served important interests in informing the electorate, deterring corruption, and gathering data for enforcement). Where a disclosure law reaches beyond those interests, it cannot stand. See Brown v. Socialist Workers ’74 Campaign Comm. (Ohio), 459 U.S. 87, 91-92 (1982) (recognizing that compelled disclosure may be unconstitutional where there is a “reasonable probability” of harassment and the State’s interest is insufficient); Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60, 64-65 (1960) (striking down compelled identification requirements that burden anonymous political advocacy).

Florida’s statute is not limited to contributions made to candidates or electioneering expenditures tied to ballot access. It reaches every dollar spent in the course of lobbying, including internal research, communications, travel, and lodging, regardless of whether the expenditure creates any realistic risk of corruption. That breadth matters. The Supreme Court has repeatedly distinguished between disclosure rules justified by the electoral process and restrictions on political advocacy more generally. See Citizens Against Rent Control/Coalition for Fair Hous. v. City of Berkeley, 454 U.S. 290, 298-300 (1981) (invalidating contribution limits in the initiative context and emphasizing that not all political spending raises the same corruption concerns); First Nat’l Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765, 790 (1978) (invalidating restrictions on corporate advocacy and warning against suppressing speech because of the speaker’s identity).

The State may argue that disclosure helps inform the public and deter corruption. But Buckley permits compelled disclosure only where the regulation is closely related to those interests. 424 U.S. at 66-68. Florida’s sweeping reporting regime goes well beyond that. It requires disclosure of a lobbyist’s ordinary operating costs and of the source of funds for those costs, even where the expenditures have no direct relation to a corrupt transaction. Such a regime burdens association by exposing clients, strategies, and political affiliations to public scrutiny. The Court has recognized that compelled disclosure can chill association and advocacy where it deters participation. See NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson, 357 U.S. 449, 462-63 (1958). Because the statute reaches far more broadly than the governmental interests recognized in Buckley, it is overbroad on its face.

#### II. The contingency-fee ban cannot be sustained because later First Amendment doctrine has undermined the old cases on which the State relies.

Section 11.047 bars lobbyists from receiving compensation contingent on legislative or executive success. The State may invoke the Supreme Court’s older decisions condemning contingency-fee arrangements in lobbying. See Providence Tool Co. v. Norris, 69 U.S. (2 Wall.) 45, 54-55 (1864) (invalidating a contract for contingent compensation for procuring government action); Hazelton v. Sheckells, 202 U.S. 71, 79-80 (1906) (same). But those decisions predate the Court’s modern recognition that political advocacy is protected expression and that government may not lightly burden the incentive structures that facilitate it. See Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414, 421-25 (1988) (striking down prohibition on paying petition circulators and holding that compensation rules burden political speech by limiting the number of voices and the strength of advocacy); Riley v. Nat’l Fed’n of the Blind of N.C., Inc., 487 U.S. 781, 795-801 (1988) (invalidating restrictions on fund-raising fees as compelled speech burdens and rejecting paternalistic limits on compensation structures).

Contingency fees in lobbying operate as a market-based form of compensation that enables access to public officials for those who otherwise could not afford full upfront representation. The First Amendment protects not only the right to speak, but also the means necessary to make speech effective. See Meyer, 486 U.S. at 424-25. A flat ban on contingent compensation reduces the quantity and effectiveness of lobbying speech by preventing clients and lobbyists from structuring representation around success. That burden is especially acute where lobbying involves petitioning government, a core First Amendment activity. See United Mine Workers of Am., Dist. 12 v. Illinois State Bar Ass’n, 389 U.S. 217, 222-23 (1967) (recognizing petitioning as a protected form of collective activity).

Florida cannot justify the ban by invoking generalized anti-corruption concerns. In the campaign-finance context, the Court has upheld restrictions only where the legislature demonstrated a sufficiently important interest and the law was carefully tailored. See Buckley, 424 U.S. at 25-29. Here, the State has not shown that a contingent fee in lobbying necessarily produces corruption, only that it creates a stronger incentive for success. But incentives to advocate forcefully are not themselves unconstitutional; they are the ordinary engine of effective petitioning. See Meyer, 486 U.S. at 424.

At minimum, the Court should not extend Providence Tool and Hazelton beyond their facts in light of the First Amendment’s later development. Those cases were decided before the modern doctrine that protects political advocacy from burdens on its funding and structure. See Bellotti, 435 U.S. at 776-77; Meyer, 486 U.S. at 421-25. Because the contingency-fee ban suppresses protected advocacy more than it prevents concrete corruption, it is unconstitutional.

#### III. At the very least, the statute cannot survive facial review because it is substantially overbroad.

A facial challenger need not show that every application is invalid, only that the statute reaches a substantial amount of protected conduct relative to its plainly legitimate sweep. See Broadrick v. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973). Florida’s law burdens lawful lobbying speech in ordinary, noncorrupt contexts and imposes disclosure duties far broader than those approved in Buckley. It therefore chills protected advocacy on a substantial scale. The district court’s judgment should be reversed.
