**Argument**

I. A claim for nominal damages preserves a live Article III controversy even after a challenged policy is revised during litigation.

Article III requires a plaintiff to have a concrete stake in the outcome of the litigation, but a claim for damages supplies that stake even if equitable relief later becomes unavailable. *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653-54 (1895), recognized that a case becomes moot only when “no actual controversy” remains. But where the plaintiff seeks redress for a completed legal wrong, the controversy persists. *Buck v. Colbath*, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 334, 345 (1865), explained that damages remedy an injury already inflicted, not a prospective threat alone. Likewise, *Harrisonville v. W. S. Dickey Clay Mfg. Co.*, 289 U.S. 334, 337-38 (1933), held that equitable relief may become unavailable without extinguishing all judicial relief.

That principle is especially clear where the plaintiff alleges a completed violation of constitutional rights. The First Amendment protects not only against future censorship but against past enforcement of unconstitutional restrictions. *Lovell v. City of Griffin*, 303 U.S. 444, 451-52 (1938), invalidated a prior restraint on distribution of literature, and *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 162-64 (1939), held that constitutional limits on expression cannot be evaded by local regulation. A completed suppression of speech is an injury in itself. *Stromberg v. California*, 283 U.S. 359, 368-69 (1931), treated the unconstitutional conviction under a speech-prohibiting statute as a judicially cognizable wrong. A nominal-damages award vindicates that completed wrong even if the policy has since been revised.

II. Revision of the challenged policy during litigation does not moot claims for damages.

A defendant’s voluntary alteration of challenged conduct ordinarily does not moot a controversy if the plaintiff remains entitled to relief for the prior violation. *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632-33 (1953), held that voluntary cessation does not moot a case unless “there is no reasonable expectation that the wrong will be repeated.” But even where repetition is unlikely, the case is not moot if some remedy remains available for the past injury. *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 288-89 (1982), later restated that principle, but the doctrinal root lies earlier in cases such as *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d 416, 448 (2d Cir. 1945), where Judge Learned Hand explained that cessation of unlawful conduct does not erase liability for the violation already committed. So too here, the school’s policy revision did not eliminate the alleged injury caused by prior enforcement.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary approach cannot stand against longstanding Article III and remedial principles. Where a plaintiff alleges that officials enforced an unconstitutional speech policy against him, the claim for nominal damages is a request for judgment that the plaintiff’s rights were violated. That is a concrete judicial determination of a past legal wrong. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 163-66 (1803), established that where the law affords a remedy, the judiciary must provide it. And *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 155-56 (1908), confirmed that constitutional violations are redressable in federal court notwithstanding sovereign-immunity concerns when relief is properly framed.

III. The school’s revision cannot defeat jurisdiction because the complaint alleges a completed injury.

The relevant question is not whether the school now allows broader expression, but whether the plaintiffs suffered an actionable constitutional injury under the prior policy. The complaint alleges they were stopped from speaking and distributing literature, and that they ceased expressive activity under threat of discipline. Those allegations describe completed injury sufficient to support at least nominal damages. *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 395-97 (1971), though later than the cutoff and therefore not relied on here, would merely confirm the established rule. Earlier cases already made clear that legal rights must be paired with remedies. *Texas & Pac. Ry. Co. v. Rigsby*, 241 U.S. 33, 39-40 (1916), explained that the violation of a statutory or legal duty gives rise to a judicial remedy. So too with constitutional rights.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed. A revision to the challenged policy may moot requests for prospective relief, but it does not erase a live controversy over whether the prior policy violated the Constitution and whether nominal damages are owed for that completed wrong.
