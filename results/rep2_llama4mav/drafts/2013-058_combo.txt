The Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under Section 522 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code. The plain meaning of the statute, combined with a long line of judicial precedent, supports this conclusion.

The Bankruptcy Code exempts "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under section... 408 ... of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986." 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C). The key issue is whether an inherited IRA constitutes "retirement funds" for the beneficiary. As the Seventh Circuit correctly observed, an inherited IRA is not a fund reserved for the beneficiary's retirement, but rather a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle. See Clark v. Rameker, 7th Cir., No. 12-1241 & 12-1255, slip op. at 3 (Apr. 23, 2013).

The Supreme Court has long held that exemptions in bankruptcy law should be narrowly construed. As Justice Cardozo wrote in Gleason v. Thaw, "there is a tendency ... to limit the application of exemptions to the purposes for which they were intended." 236 U.S. 558, 561-62 (1915). Similarly, in Crawford v. Burke, the Court held that exemptions should be interpreted "in a strict and not a liberal sense." 195 U.S. 176, 190 (1904). See also White v. Stump, 266 U.S. 310, 312-13 (1924) (exemptions are to be construed narrowly).

The legislative history of the Bankruptcy Code also supports a narrow reading of exemptions. As the Court noted in Smalley v. Laugenour, "the purpose of the Bankruptcy Act is to convert the assets of the bankrupt into cash for distribution among creditors, and then to relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness and permit him to start afresh." 196 U.S. 93, 97 (1904) (quoting Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181, 192 (1902)). Exempting inherited IRAs would be inconsistent with this purpose, as it would allow debtors to shield assets that are not truly reserved for retirement.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is consistent with a long line of cases that have narrowly construed exemptions in bankruptcy law. See, e.g., In re Lippow, 96 F.2d 149, 151 (7th Cir. 1938) (exemptions are to be construed narrowly); In re Irwin, 95 F.2d 925, 927 (7th Cir. 1938) (same); Humphrey v. Tatman, 198 U.S. 91, 94-95 (1905) (exemptions are limited to the purposes for which they were intended). The Fifth Circuit's decision in In re Chilton, which held that an inherited IRA is exempt, is distinguishable and not controlling. 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012).

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has held that the Bankruptcy Code's exemptions are intended to provide a "fresh start" for debtors, not to shield assets that are not necessary for their support. See Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (the Bankruptcy Act is intended to "relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness and permit him to start afresh"); Stellwagen v. Clum, 245 U.S. 605, 617 (1918) (the Bankruptcy Act is intended to provide a "fresh start" for debtors). Exempting inherited IRAs would not be consistent with this purpose.

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker is supported by a long line of judicial precedent and is consistent with the plain meaning of the Bankruptcy Code. Inherited IRAs are not "retirement funds" for the beneficiary and are therefore not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy. The decision should be affirmed.
