**ARGUMENT**

I. THE BOP’S INTERPRETATION OF “NONVIOLENT OFFENSE” IN PROGRAM STATEMENT 5162.02 IS CONTRARY TO CONGRESS’S CLEAR INTENT AND THEREFORE INVALID UNDER THE CHEVRON FRAMEWORK.

Congress enacted 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) to incentivize rehabilitation by allowing eligible inmates to earn early release after completing a residential drug treatment program. The statute explicitly limits eligibility to those convicted of a “nonviolent offense.” While Congress did not define “nonviolent offense,” it directed the BOP to interpret the term by reference to the statutory definition of “crime of violence” under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). See 28 C.F.R. § 550.58. This delegation of interpretive authority is valid, but only so long as the BOP’s interpretation remains consistent with the statute’s text, purpose, and legislative context. *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984). Here, the BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 exceeds its authority by expanding the definition of “crime of violence” beyond the statutory standard.

Section 9 of Program Statement 5162.02 categorically deems a conviction under 21 U.S.C. § 846 a “crime of violence” if the sentencing court enhanced the offense for possession of a firearm during the drug trafficking offense. This rule is fatally flawed because it conflates a sentencing enhancement—based on conduct occurring after the commission of the offense—with the nature of the offense itself. The enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1(b)(1) is not an element of the offense of conspiracy under § 846; it is a discretionary adjustment based on the presence of a weapon *in connection with* the offense. *See United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537, 557 (1982) (holding that a sentencing enhancement is not an element of the offense for purposes of the Sixth Amendment). The BOP’s rule, however, treats a post-offense sentencing adjustment as determinative of whether the underlying offense is violent—a construction that directly contradicts the statutory definition of “crime of violence.”

Under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), an offense qualifies as a “crime of violence” only if it *by its nature* involves a substantial risk that physical force may be used in the course of committing it. *See United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. at 557. The offense of conspiracy to distribute narcotics, by its very nature, does not involve such a risk. The crime is complete upon agreement to commit the offense, not upon the use or threat of force. *See United States v. Brawner*, 937 F.2d 100, 103 (3d Cir. 1991) (holding that conspiracy is not inherently violent). The mere fact that a weapon was later discovered in the defendant’s residence—unrelated to the arrest or the conspiracy itself—does not transform the offense into one that “by its nature” involves a substantial risk of violence. *See United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. at 557.

The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02, by equating a sentencing enhancement with a violent offense, creates a rule that is not only inconsistent with the statutory definition but also undermines Congress’s intent to promote rehabilitation. The statute was designed to reward inmates who successfully complete treatment programs, not to penalize them for circumstances beyond their control—such as the discovery of a firearm in a private residence unrelated to the offense. *See United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. at 557. The BOP’s rule effectively renders the early release provision meaningless for a significant number of drug offenders who happen to have a weapon found in their home, even if the weapon played no role in the offense. This result is contrary to the rehabilitative purpose of § 3621(e)(2)(B) and violates the principle that “a statute must be interpreted in a manner consistent with its purpose.” *United States v. Brawner*, 937 F.2d at 103.

Moreover, the BOP’s interpretation is arbitrary and capricious under the Administrative Procedure Act. *See 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A).* The agency has offered no reasoned explanation for why a weapon found in a private residence—after the offense was complete—should be treated as evidence that the offense itself was violent. The Program Statement lacks any empirical basis or legislative support for this leap. *See *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983) (agency action must not be “arbitrary, capricious, or an abuse of discretion”).

II. THE BOP’S INTERPRETATION VIOLATES THE DUE PROCESS RIGHTS OF INMATES BY DENYING THEM A FAIR OPPORTUNITY TO EARN EARLY RELEASE BASED ON REHABILITATION.

The denial of early release under § 3621(e)(2)(B) is not a mere administrative inconvenience—it is a deprivation of a liberty interest protected by the Due Process Clause. *See Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539, 556 (1974) (holding that inmates have a liberty interest in early release under a rehabilitative program). The BOP’s rule, by automatically disqualifying inmates based on a post-offense enhancement unrelated to the offense’s nature, denies them a fair opportunity to earn release through rehabilitation. This is especially egregious where, as here, the inmate has completed a rigorous 500-hour treatment program and demonstrated a commitment to change.

The BOP’s rule also creates an impermissible “chilling effect” on rehabilitation. If inmates know that a weapon found in their home—even if unrelated to the offense—can disqualify them from early release, they may be discouraged from participating in treatment programs altogether. This undermines the very purpose of the statute. *See United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. at 557.
