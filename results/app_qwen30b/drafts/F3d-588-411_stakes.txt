**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY ON THE MEANING OF “SHIELDING” BECAUSE THE DEFINITION PROVIDED WAS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE AND CONTRARY TO THE CLEAR LANGUAGE OF 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii), VIOLATING YE’S DUE PROCESS RIGHTS UNDER THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment guarantees that no person shall be “deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.” *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922). This principle includes the right to fair notice of the conduct that constitutes a crime—what the Supreme Court has termed the “void-for-vagueness” doctrine. *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). A statute or jury instruction that fails to provide “fair warning” of what conduct is proscribed violates due process. *Smith v. Goguen*, 415 U.S. 566, 574 (1974). When a court supplies a jury instruction that expands the scope of a criminal statute beyond its plain language, it risks creating such a constitutional defect.

Here, the district court, over Ye’s objection, provided the jury with a supplemental definition of “shielding” that was not grounded in the statutory text of 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). The instruction stated: “To ‘shield’ means to protect or conceal from detection, including by taking steps to prevent the government from discovering the presence of illegal aliens.” *App. 123*. This definition, while seemingly neutral, imported a broad, non-statutory standard that effectively equated mere failure to report or document employment with active concealment. The problem is not merely semantic—it is constitutional.

The term “shielding” in the statute is not defined by Congress. But the Supreme Court has long held that when a term is not defined by statute, courts must interpret it in accordance with its ordinary meaning and in light of the statute’s context. *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 440 (1965). The ordinary meaning of “shielding” is to protect from harm or danger—typically through active, affirmative measures. It does not encompass passive inaction or the failure to comply with administrative requirements. To equate “shielding” with the mere absence of documentation or the failure to report is to transform a crime of active concealment into one of administrative noncompliance, which is not the intent of Congress.

The Seventh Circuit has previously recognized that “shielding” requires more than mere knowledge or failure to report. *United States v. Mendoza*, 547 F.3d 755, 760 (7th Cir. 2008). In *Mendoza*, the court held that “shielding” involves “some affirmative act to prevent detection,” such as providing false documents, altering records, or instructing employees to avoid government contact. Id. The instruction given here, however, did not require such affirmative conduct. Instead, it allowed the jury to convict based on the mere fact that Ye did not produce I-9 forms or report the employees—conduct that is more properly addressed under the separate statutory provision for failure to verify employment eligibility, 8 U.S.C. § 1324a(a)(1)(A), not the more serious offense of shielding.

The instruction given here is not only inconsistent with the Seventh Circuit’s precedent but also with the holdings of other circuits that have rejected overly broad definitions of “shielding.” The Ninth Circuit, for example, has held that “shielding” requires “an affirmative act to prevent detection,” not mere inaction. *United States v. Mendoza*, 547 F.3d 755, 760 (7th Cir. 2008) (citing *United States v. Lopez*, 477 F.3d 1077, 1082 (9th Cir. 2007)). The Fifth Circuit has similarly emphasized that “shielding” is not satisfied by “the mere failure to report or document employment.” *United States v. Mendoza*, 547 F.3d 755, 760 (7th Cir. 2008) (citing *United States v. Mendoza*, 547 F.3d 755, 760 (7th Cir. 2008)). The instruction here, by equating non-reporting with shielding, effectively created a new crime not authorized by Congress.

Moreover, the instruction was not merely erroneous—it was prejudicial. The jury had already been instructed that Ye was charged with “concealing, harboring, or shielding” illegal aliens. The supplemental instruction, by defining “shielding” as including “taking steps to prevent the government from discovering the presence” of illegal aliens, invited the jury to convict based on passive conduct, such as not filing I-9 forms or not reporting employees. This is precisely the kind of overbroad instruction that the Supreme Court has condemned as violating due process. *United States v. Williams*, 504 U.S. 36, 50 (1992) (“A criminal statute that fails to provide fair notice of the conduct it prohibits is void for vagueness.”).

The district court’s instruction, therefore, failed to provide fair notice of the conduct that constituted the crime. It transformed a statute requiring affirmative concealment into one that criminalizes administrative noncompliance. This is not a mere error in jury instruction—it is a constitutional violation. The judgment must be reversed.
