The federal courts do **not** have exclusive jurisdiction over every state-law case that touches patent law. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), exclusive jurisdiction extends only to civil actions “arising under” federal patent law, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that this category is narrow. Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988); Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 313-14 (2005).

Christianson held that a case “arises under” patent law only where federal patent law creates the cause of action or where the plaintiff’s right to relief “necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” 486 U.S. at 808-09. But Christianson also warned that patent jurisdiction cannot be triggered merely because patent law is one possible issue in a broader dispute; the patent issue must be necessary to the claim actually pleaded. Id. at 809. That principle controls here. Minton’s claim is for legal malpractice, a state-law cause of action that exists independently of patent law. Under Texas law, malpractice requires duty, breach, causation, and damages. Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., Inc., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). The federal patent question arises only as part of the “case within a case” causation inquiry. Schaeffer v. O’Brien, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied).
