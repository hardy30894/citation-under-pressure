**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code does not offer a safe harbor to the dishonest debtor. Long before the modern Code, this Court had recognized that discharge is reserved for the “honest but unfortunate debtor,” a principle rooted in the historic limitation that bankruptcy relief does not cleanse liabilities arising from fraud. See *Sexton v. Dreyfus*, 219 U.S. 339 (1911); *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234 (1934). That understanding controls here. Cohen’s liability was not an ordinary contractual debt, but a debt arising from his fraudulent overcharging of tenants and the resulting statutory damages, fees, and costs.

The federal bankruptcy laws have never been read to permit a debtor to escape liabilities incurred by fraud. In *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704 (1878), the Court held that the predecessor fraud exception was aimed at “positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong.” Id. at 709. The same core principle governs § 523(a)(2)(A): debts arising from actual fraud are nondischargeable because fraud itself disqualifies the debtor from the fresh start. Likewise, in *Forsyth v. Vehmeyer*, 177 U.S. 177 (1900), the Court treated discharge as unavailable where the debt was tainted by the debtor’s fraudulent conduct. The Bankruptcy Code preserves, rather than departs from, that settled rule.

Cohen’s attempt to discharge not merely the overcharges, but also the consequences of his fraud, would invert the statute. Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge any debt for money or property “to the extent obtained by” actual fraud. That language reaches the full liability proximately caused by the fraud, not merely the initial amount wrongfully extracted. The Court’s pre-Code decisions confirm that bankruptcy does not strip a creditor of the full measure of recovery flowing from a debtor’s wrongful conduct. In *Yates v. United States*, 354 U.S. 298 (1957), the Court explained that statutory terms must be read in light of their evident purpose and the consequences of the wrong. And in *McNab v. General Motors Acceptance Corp.*, 298 U.S. 554 (1936), the Court recognized that liabilities arising from wrongful conduct are not transformed into dischargeable obligations simply because they are later reduced to judgment.

Nor can Cohen rely on the fact that the additional amounts were imposed under a state consumer protection statute. Bankruptcy has always respected the substance of the liability, not the label attached to it. In *Perry v. Commerce Loan Co.*, 383 U.S. 392 (1966), the Court emphasized that the scope of a discharge question turns on the character of the debt under federal bankruptcy policy. And in *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234 (1934), the Court reaffirmed that bankruptcy courts must balance the debtor’s fresh start against the rights of those injured by the debtor’s conduct. Where, as here, the debtor’s conduct was fraudulent, the balance decisively favors nondischargeability.

Older authorities are consistent. In *Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), the Court refused to permit discharge of liabilities grounded in fraud, rejecting efforts to recharacterize the debt so as to avoid the fraud exception. In *Levy v. Lashley*, 245 U.S. 218 (1917), the Court again treated fraud-based obligations as outside the discharge. And in *Coder v. Arts*, 213 U.S. 223 (1909), the Court held that the form of the action does not control; rather, the decisive issue is whether the liability was incurred by fraudulent means. The same logic forecloses Cohen’s argument here. The tenants’ treble damages, fees, and costs all arose from the same fraudulent overcharges that produced the underlying debt.

The purpose of discharge is to aid the honest debtor, not to reward calculated wrongdoing. *Birmingham v. Rice*, 238 U.S. 193 (1915), and *Davis v. Aetna Acceptance Co.*, 293 U.S. 328 (1934), each recognize that bankruptcy relief is bounded by equitable considerations and does not extend to liabilities rooted in intentional misconduct. Cohen knowingly charged excess rent, ignored the rent control regime, failed to consult available sources of law, and retained the wrongful gains after notice. That is precisely the sort of conduct the fraud exception exists to address.

Finally, Cohen’s reliance on discharge policy cannot overcome the statutory command. In *The American Surety Co. v. Bethune*, 219 U.S. 439 (1911), the Court explained that bankruptcy law does not absolve debts imposed by misconduct merely because the debtor seeks a fresh start. The same rule applies here. Once actual fraud is established, the resulting debt—including damages, fees, and costs that arise from that fraud—remains nondischargeable.

The judgment below should therefore be affirmed.
