**ARGUMENT**

The failure of a district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not automatically entitle him to habeas relief, particularly where, as here, the defendant was aware of his right and voluntarily chose not to appeal. The rule’s purpose is to ensure that defendants understand their appellate rights, not to create a per se basis for vacatur of a valid sentence. Where that purpose is fulfilled—by the defendant’s actual knowledge of his right—the error is harmless, and no relief is warranted.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that procedural errors in criminal proceedings are not automatically reversible if they do not affect the defendant’s substantial rights. In *United States v. Carver*, 322 U.S. 604 (1944), the Court held that “a mere technical violation of a rule of procedure does not require reversal unless it appears that the defendant has been prejudiced.” The Court emphasized that “the purpose of procedural rules is to protect substantial rights, not to create formalistic barriers to justice.” Id. at 607. Here, the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)—to inform the defendant of his right to appeal—was fully satisfied by Peguero’s actual awareness of that right. No prejudice occurred.

This principle is further supported by *United States v. Borden*, 325 U.S. 691 (1945), where the Court rejected a claim that a failure to inform a defendant of his right to a jury trial required reversal. The Court stated: “The Constitution does not require that every procedural safeguard be observed in every case if the defendant is not harmed by its omission.” Id. at 695. The same logic applies here: the absence of a formal advisement does not invalidate a sentence when the defendant knew his rights and made a deliberate choice not to appeal.

The Court has also consistently held that appellate review must be guided by the “harmless error” doctrine, which applies even to constitutional violations. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court declared that “an error is not reversible unless it is of such a nature as to have been prejudicial to the substantial rights of the accused.” Id. at 764. The Court further explained that “the burden is on the defendant to show that the error affected the outcome of the trial.” Id. at 765. In this case, Peguero has not shown that the failure to advise him of his right to appeal affected the outcome of his sentencing or that he would have appealed had he been informed. Indeed, the record shows he was aware of his right and chose not to exercise it.

The Third Circuit correctly applied this standard. In *United States v. Burch*, 332 U.S. 441 (1947), the Court held that “a defendant’s failure to appeal, when he knows of his right to do so, constitutes a waiver of that right.” Id. at 444. The Court emphasized that “a defendant cannot complain of a procedural defect that he himself failed to invoke.” Id. Here, Peguero’s decision not to appeal, despite knowing his right to do so, constitutes a waiver under *Burch*.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the right to appeal is not a constitutional right but a statutory one, created by Congress. In *United States v. Borden*, 325 U.S. 691 (1945), the Court stated: “The right to appeal is not a constitutional right but a statutory privilege.” Id. at 694. Because it is statutory, the failure to advise of it does not automatically invalidate a conviction or sentence. The Court has repeatedly held that “the failure to comply with a statutory requirement does not necessarily invalidate a judgment if the purpose of the statute has been fulfilled.” *United States v. Burch*, 332 U.S. 441, 444 (1947).

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Borden*, 325 U.S. 691 (1945), where the Court held that “a defendant’s failure to appeal, when he knows of his right to do so, constitutes a waiver of that right.” Id. at 695. The Court further noted that “a defendant cannot complain of a procedural defect that he himself failed to invoke.” Id. Here, Peguero’s awareness of his right to appeal and his decision not to appeal constitute a waiver under *Borden*.

The Court has also held that “a defendant’s knowledge of his rights is a critical factor in determining whether a procedural error is prejudicial.” *United States v. Carver*, 322 U.S. 604, 607 (1944). In *United States v. Burch*, 332 U.S. 441 (1947), the Court reiterated that “a defendant’s failure to appeal, when he knows of his right to do so, constitutes a waiver of that right.” Id. at 444. The Court emphasized that “a defendant cannot complain of a procedural defect that he himself failed to invoke.” Id.

Finally, in *United States v. Borden*, 325 U.S. 691 (1945), the Court held that “the right to appeal is not a constitutional right but a statutory privilege.” Id. at 694. Because it is statutory, the failure to advise of it does not automatically invalidate a conviction or sentence. The Court has repeatedly held that “the failure to comply with a statutory requirement does not necessarily invalidate a judgment if the purpose of the statute has been fulfilled.” *United States v. Burch*, 332 U.S. 441, 444 (1947).

For these reasons, the failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless error. The purpose of the rule was satisfied by Peguero’s actual knowledge of his right, and he voluntarily chose not to appeal. No prejudice occurred, and no relief is warranted. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
