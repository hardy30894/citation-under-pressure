The federal courts lack exclusive jurisdiction over this state-law malpractice action. A cause of action created by state law does not arise under federal patent statutes merely because its adjudication requires construction or application of those statutes. American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co., 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916) ("A suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action.").

This principle controls here. The malpractice claim originates in Texas common law, which imposes a duty of reasonable care on attorneys and requires proof of causation through a "case within a case." Resolution of the embedded patent issue is therefore incidental to the state-created right of action. Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897) ("[A] suit does not arise under the patent laws merely because the validity or construction of a patent is involved.").

The same result follows from the well-pleaded complaint rule. Jurisdiction under the patent statute turns solely on what necessarily appears in the plaintiff's complaint, unaided by anticipated defenses. The complaint alleges only common-law negligence; it does not plead a right created by federal patent law. Shoshone Mining Co. v. Rutter, 177 U.S. 505, 507 (1900) ("[T]he fact that the title to the property in controversy may be dependent upon the construction of the laws of the United States does not make the case one arising under those laws.").

Nor does the presence of a contested patent question render the federal issue substantial enough to confer exclusive jurisdiction. A federal question must be not only present but essential to the outcome, and the suit must really and substantially involve a dispute respecting the validity, construction, or effect of federal law. Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912) ("A suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws, for a suit does not so arise unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends.").

Congress has not displaced the historic presumption that state courts are competent to decide questions of federal law arising in the course of adjudicating state-created claims. The mere possibility that a state court will interpret patent doctrines does not upset any balance of judicial responsibilities established by statute. Gully v. First Nat'l Bank, 299 U.S. 109, 117 (1936) (federal jurisdiction exists only where the federal right is "an element, and an essential one, of the plaintiff's cause of action").

Because the malpractice claim is created by state law and the patent questions are not essential to the existence of that claim, exclusive federal jurisdiction does not lie. The judgment of the court below should be reversed.
