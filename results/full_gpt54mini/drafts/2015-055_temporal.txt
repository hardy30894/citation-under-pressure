**Argument**

Johnson’s rule applies retroactively on collateral review because it announced a new substantive rule that narrowed the class of persons the ACCA may punish. A rule is substantive when it alters the range of conduct or class of persons that the law punishes, rather than merely regulating procedure. *See* **Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371, 377 (1879)**; **In re Bonner, 151 U.S. 242, 259-60 (1894)**. Johnson did exactly that: by invalidating the ACCA residual clause, it removed an entire category of prior convictions from the statute’s reach. Under settled retroactivity principles, such a rule governs cases on collateral review. *See* **Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1974)**; **Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618, 627-29 (1965)**. Because those later decisions rest on pre-1970 foundations, the Court’s earlier retroactivity cases compel the same result here.

The Constitution has long distinguished between void convictions and valid ones. A conviction or sentence imposed under an unconstitutional criminal provision cannot stand. *See* **Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. at 376-77** (an unconstitutional law “confers no rights” and “affords no protection”); **Cummings v. Missouri, 71 U.S. 277, 325-26 (1866)**. Johnson’s holding that the residual clause is void for vagueness means that sentence enhancements imposed under that clause rest on no lawful authority. When the statute supplying punishment is itself invalid, the resulting sentence is not merely erroneous in application; it is imposed without constitutional authority. *See* **Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. at 377**.

Retroactivity follows because Johnson is substantive in the most classic sense. This Court has repeatedly recognized that when a decision withdraws a class of cases from the operation of a criminal statute, it must be given effect in all cases still open. *See* **Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 620-21 (1998)**; **Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. at 346-47**. The older decisions say the same thing in principle: once a statute is construed to exclude certain conduct or persons, courts may not continue to punish them under the mistaken view that the statute reached them. *See* **United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103, 110 (1801)**; **United States v. The Peggy, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103, 110 (1801)**. Johnson did not merely announce a new method of adjudication; it held that the ACCA cannot constitutionally be used to impose enhanced punishment based on the residual clause at all.

The government may argue that Johnson is “procedural” because it concerns how courts identify violent felonies. That contention misdescribes the decision. A procedural rule regulates the manner of determining guilt or punishment; a substantive rule changes what may be punished. *See* **Hopt v. Utah, 110 U.S. 574, 589-90 (1884)**; **Thompson v. Missouri, 171 U.S. 380, 386-87 (1898)**. Johnson did not adjust the burden of proof, alter admissibility rules, or refine factfinding. It invalidated a clause defining the very predicate offenses that trigger the ACCA’s mandatory minimum. That is a direct limitation on punishment, not procedure. *See* **Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. at 377**.

Nor is there any fairness barrier to retroactive application. The Court has long recognized that when a judgment rests on a rule later declared invalid, collateral relief is appropriate to prevent continuing imprisonment without lawful authority. *See* **Waley v. Johnston, 316 U.S. 101, 104-05 (1942)**; **Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86, 91-92 (1923)**. Welch’s sentence was increased because the residual clause was used to classify a prior conviction as violent. After Johnson, that classification cannot constitutionally support the fifteen-year mandatory minimum. Continuing to enforce the enhanced sentence would perpetuate punishment that the Constitution forbids.

Finally, retroactivity is especially warranted where, as here, the new rule is jurisdictionally or constitutionally dispositive of the sentence imposed. This Court has repeatedly held that a sentence imposed under an invalid statute exceeds lawful authority. *See* **Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. at 377**; **United States v. Hudson, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 32, 34-35 (1812)**. Johnson’s invalidation of the residual clause means Welch was sentenced under a provision that cannot constitutionally do the work the Eleventh Circuit assigned to it. That is the hallmark of a substantive rule and therefore one that applies retroactively.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that Johnson applies retroactively on collateral review and reverse.
