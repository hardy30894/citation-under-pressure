**ARGUMENT**

I. **Congress did not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General**

The Controlled Substances Act satisfies the Constitution’s “intelligible principle” requirement. Congress may delegate to another branch so long as it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372-79 (1989). The Court has repeatedly upheld broad delegations where Congress identified the goals to be pursued, the factors to be considered, and the limits on discretion. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374-79; *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 423-27 (1944).

Section 811(h) easily meets that standard. Congress authorized temporary scheduling only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” and directed the Attorney General to consider specified factors concerning abuse, public-health risk, and related matters. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). Congress also required notice in the Federal Register and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). And the temporary order is limited in duration to one year, with only a six-month extension. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). Those restrictions are far more definite than many delegations the Court has upheld. See *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374-79; *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 425-27; *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947).

Petitioners’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved sweeping delegations with virtually no standards to guide the Executive. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415-16; *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 541-42. Here, by contrast, Congress specified the danger to be addressed, the factors to be weighed, the consultation required, and the temporary nature of the authority. The CSA therefore falls comfortably within the modern nondelegation cases that have sustained comparable or broader grants of authority. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373-79; *Whitman v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 531 U.S. 457, 474-76 (2001).

Nor does the fact that temporary scheduling can support criminal prosecutions make the delegation unconstitutional. Congress routinely legislates in the criminal context through standards that leave implementation to executive officials. See *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517-22 (1911); *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 423-27. The critical question is whether Congress itself made the basic policy judgment and supplied the rule of decision. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372-79. It did so here.

II. **The Attorney General was authorized to subdelegate temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator**

Petitioners’ second question is whether the Act permits the Attorney General to delegate temporary scheduling authority to the DEA. It does. Congress provided in 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) that “[t]he Attorney General may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” That text is broad and explicit. A subdelegation to the DEA Administrator, an officer within the Department of Justice at the time, falls squarely within the statute’s terms. See *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 514-23 (1974) (recognizing that where Congress authorizes delegation within an agency, the statutory text controls the scope of permissible subdelegation).

The Third Circuit correctly observed that Congress did not leave the Attorney General without subdelegation authority. A contrary reading would ignore § 871(a)’s plain authorization and would improperly read into the Act an unstated limitation on internal departmental management. Courts do not infer restrictions where Congress spoke broadly and directly. *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 363-65 (1942).

III. **The Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA was a valid internal subdelegation**

Even apart from the statute, the Attorney General’s regulation assigning functions to the DEA Administrator was a permissible exercise of administrative authority. The Attorney General remains responsible for the exercise of the delegated power; the DEA Administrator acts as his subordinate officer. Internal delegation for efficient administration is a commonplace feature of federal governance. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372-79; *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 694-96 (1974). Nothing in the CSA suggests that Congress intended the Attorney General personally to perform every temporary scheduling decision.

Petitioners identify no structural principle requiring the Attorney General himself to sign every temporary scheduling order. To the contrary, Congress enacted the CSA against the backdrop of a complex and rapidly changing drug market. The temporary scheduling provision was designed to enable prompt enforcement where delay would create an imminent hazard. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). Allowing subdelegation to the DEA, the agency with specialized enforcement expertise, is fully consistent with that design. See *Whalen v. Roe*, 429 U.S. 589, 603-04 (1977); *Weinberger v. Bentex Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 412 U.S. 645, 653-54 (1973).

Because Congress supplied an intelligible principle and expressly authorized departmental delegation, the judgment below should be affirmed.
