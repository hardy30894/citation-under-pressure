**ARGUMENT**

A federal habeas petitioner may establish cause for the procedural default of a constitutional claim by demonstrating that the default resulted from the ineffective assistance of appellate counsel, even if that ineffective-assistance claim was itself defaulted in state post-conviction proceedings. This rule follows directly from the cause-and-prejudice framework first articulated in Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977), and refined in subsequent decisions.

In Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), the Court held that “the exhaustion doctrine generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default.” Id. at 488. The Court simultaneously recognized that constitutionally ineffective assistance of counsel constitutes cause because “the prisoner has no other means of vindicating” the underlying right. Id. at 488. Nothing in Carrier requires that the ineffective-assistance claim itself be free of procedural default before it may serve this function; the opinion addresses only the requirement of fair presentation.

Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), confirmed that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim may still supply cause when the default is attributable to counsel’s constitutionally deficient performance on direct appeal. There the Court reiterated that “if the procedural default is the result of ineffective assistance of counsel, the Sixth Amendment itself requires that responsibility for the default be imputed to the State.” Id. at 754. The imputation occurs because the State bears the obligation to furnish effective appellate counsel; any default flowing from counsel’s inadequacy is therefore external to the prisoner. Id.

The Sixth Circuit correctly applied these principles. Carpenter’s sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was defaulted solely because his appellate lawyer failed to raise it. That failure, if constitutionally ineffective, is chargeable to the State under Coleman. The fact that Carpenter’s subsequent attempt to assert the ineffective-assistance claim was itself dismissed as untimely does not sever the causal link established in Carrier and Coleman. To hold otherwise would insulate the State from responsibility for the very constitutional violation that prevented timely presentation of the underlying claim.

Accordingly, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
