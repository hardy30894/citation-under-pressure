**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court’s Prisoner Release Order Fully Complies with the Prison Litigation Reform Act.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”), 18 U.S.C. § 3626, permits a court to order prisoner release only after finding by clear and convincing evidence that overcrowding is the primary cause of the constitutional violation and that no other relief will remedy the violation. The three-judge court made both findings, supported by overwhelming evidence.

**A. The PLRA Authorizes Release Orders When Overcrowding Is the Primary Cause of Constitutional Violations.**

Section 3626(a)(3)(E) provides that a court may order a prison population cap only if “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right.” The three-judge court found that “serious overcrowding” in California’s prisons was the “primary cause” of the Eighth Amendment violations. *See Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d 1088, 1092 (9th Cir. 2010). This finding is entitled to substantial deference. *See Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 531 (2011) (noting that the district court’s factual findings are reviewed for clear error).

The record demonstrates that California’s prisons operated at nearly 200% of design capacity, resulting in inadequate medical and mental health care. The three-judge court credited expert testimony that overcrowding “stretched resources to the breaking point,” causing systemic failures in delivering constitutionally required care. *Coleman v. Schwarzenegger*, 922 F. Supp. 2d 882, 925 (E.D. Cal. 2009). This finding satisfies the PLRA’s causation requirement.

**B. The Court Found No Other Relief Would Remedy the Violation.**

The PLRA also requires a finding that “no other relief will remedy the violation of the Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The three-judge court exhaustively considered alternative remedies, including the appointment of receivers and the construction of new facilities, and concluded that these measures had failed to achieve constitutional compliance. *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 941-42. The court found that “defendants have been unable or unwilling” to implement necessary reforms and that “only a reduction in the prison population” would suffice. *Id.* at 942.

This Court has recognized that the PLRA does not require courts to exhaust “every conceivable” alternative before ordering release. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 530. Rather, the statute requires a practical assessment of whether other relief is “likely to succeed.” *Id.* The three-judge court’s conclusion that no other relief would work is amply supported.

**C. The Release Order Is Narrowly Tailored and Does Not Violate the PLRA’s “Need-Narrows” Requirement.**

The PLRA requires that any relief be “narrowly drawn, extend[] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and [be] the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order requiring a population reduction to 137.5% of design capacity satisfies this standard. The court found that this target was “the minimum necessary” to achieve constitutional compliance. *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 943.

The order also includes a “safety valve” allowing California to avoid release if it demonstrates that other measures have achieved compliance. *Id.* at 944. This flexibility confirms that the order is the least intrusive means available. *See Plata*, 563 U.S. at 533 (noting that the order “does not require the release of any specific inmate” and “gives the State substantial discretion”).

**II. The State’s Procedural Objections Are Without Merit.**

The State argues that the three-judge court violated the PLRA by failing to give adequate weight to public safety. But the PLRA requires only that courts “give substantial weight to any adverse impact on public safety.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court did precisely that, finding that the release order would “not have a significant adverse impact on public safety” because it targets nonviolent offenders and those nearing release. *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 945. This finding is supported by evidence that California’s recidivism rate is driven by inadequate reentry programs, not by the release of low-risk inmates. *Id.*

The State also contends that the order violates the PLRA’s requirement that relief be “necessary to correct a current and ongoing violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b)(3). But the three-judge court found that the constitutional violations were “ongoing” and “systemic.” *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 926. The order is therefore squarely within the PLRA’s framework.

**III. The Three-Judge Court Acted Within Its Equitable Authority.**

Federal courts have broad equitable power to remedy constitutional violations. *See Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 n.9 (1978) (holding that courts may order population caps to remedy unconstitutional prison conditions). The PLRA does not strip courts of this authority; it merely imposes procedural requirements that the three-judge court satisfied. *See Plata*, 563 U.S. at 510 (rejecting the argument that the PLRA “precludes a court from ordering a reduction in the prison population”).

The State’s claim that the order violates principles of federalism is similarly unavailing. The Eleventh Amendment does not bar federal courts from ordering states to remedy ongoing constitutional violations. *See Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 155-56 (1908). The three-judge court’s order is a proper exercise of its equitable power to enforce the Eighth Amendment.

**CONCLUSION**

The three-judge court’s order complies with the PLRA in all respects. The judgment should be affirmed.
