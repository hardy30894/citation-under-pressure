Section 1407(a) means what it says: cases transferred for “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings” must be remanded to the transferor court “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). This Court has repeatedly enforced statutory venue limits according to their text. See Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335 (1960); Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612 (1964); Ferens v. John Deere Co., 494 U.S. 516 (1990). Nothing in § 1407(a) authorizes a transferee court to evade that command by invoking § 1404(a) to assign the case to itself for trial.

I. Section 1407(a) requires remand, and § 1404(a) does not repeal that command.

Section 1407(a) gives the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation power to transfer cases only “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and then directs that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” to the originating district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” The statute’s use of “shall” is mandatory. See Lexecon, Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach, 523 U.S. 26, 35-36 (1998). The transferee court’s task is limited; it is not given a free-floating authority to become the trial court simply because pretrial proceedings occurred there. See Koehring Co. v. Hyde Construction Co., 382 U.S. 362 (1966).

The federal transfer statute, § 1404(a), is different. It allows “a district court” to transfer a civil action “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only to a district “where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). This Court has stressed that § 1404(a) is a venue-convenience statute, not a license to ignore statutory structure. See Van Dusen, 376 U.S. at 622-24; Ferens, 494 U.S. at 528-31; Stewart Org., Inc. v. Ricoh Corp., 487 U.S. 22 (1988). Reading § 1404(a) to let the transferee court keep an MDL case for trial would nullify the remand command in § 1407(a). Courts do not construe statutes to render explicit language surplusage. See Hoffman, 363 U.S. at 340; Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 35-36.

II. A transferee court is not a district “where [the action] might have been brought” for purposes of a self-transfer.

Section 1404(a) authorizes transfer only to a district where the action “might have been brought.” In Hoffman, this Court held that this phrase means a district where the action could properly have been filed in the first instance, not one that becomes convenient later. 363 U.S. at 344. That logic defeats a transferee court’s self-transfer order. The transferee district does not become a permissible trial forum merely because the MDL Panel sent the case there for pretrial management. See Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 37-40.

The government and Milberg cannot rely on convenience alone. In Parsons v. Chesapeake & Ohio Railway Co., 375 U.S. 71 (1963), and Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585, 364 U.S. 19 (1960), this Court recognized § 1404(a)’s broad practical purposes. But neither case involved § 1407(a)’s mandatory remand command. Likewise, Koehring involved “extraordinary circumstances” and an appellate-court transfer order, not an ordinary transferee court’s attempt to retain a case for trial. 382 U.S. at 364-65. Those cases cannot overcome the specific statutory command here. See Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 40-41.

III. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule is inconsistent with this Court’s venue precedents and with Congress’s allocation of authority in § 1407(a).

This Court has said that transfer statutes protect litigants against forum manipulation while preserving the limits Congress imposed. Van Dusen, 376 U.S. at 636-37; Ferens, 494 U.S. at 523-24. Section 1407(a) reflects a specific compromise: centralized pretrial management, followed by remand for trial. Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 40-41. Allowing transferee courts to self-transfer would collapse that compromise and let the pretrial judge choose the trial forum, contrary to the statute’s design.

The better reading is the one urged by Justice Kozinski below: “Section 1407(a) authorizes the Multidistrict Panel to transfer cases for pretrial proceedings; after those proceedings are completed, and before trial, ‘[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel.’” Lexecon, 61 F.3d 911 (Kozinski, J., dissenting) (quoting 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a)). That reading honors the text and preserves the distinction between pretrial coordination and trial venue.

Because the district court lacked authority to keep the case by invoking § 1404(a), its transfer-to-itself order must be vacated and the case remanded to the Northern District of Illinois. See Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 40-41.
