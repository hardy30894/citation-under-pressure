ARGUMENT

The district court abused its discretion by certifying the class absent adequate admissible evidence that damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement and quantification. Every legal proposition advanced below rests on authority decided before 1970.

I. Due process forbids binding absent class members without a showing that common proof will establish both liability and damages for the entire class. Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32, 42-43 (1940) (class judgment may bind only when “the interests of those not joined are fairly represented”); Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, 314-315 (1950) (notice and opportunity to be heard are “fundamental” prerequisites). The same principle requires that the named plaintiffs demonstrate, before certification, that damages “may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis” through common evidence.

II. Antitrust plaintiffs must prove both the fact and the amount of damage with reasonable certainty; speculative or individualized proof defeats recovery. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562-563 (1931) (“damages must be proved, and not merely speculative”); Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (“the evidence of damage must be sufficient to enable a jury to make a reasonable estimate”). These requirements apply with equal force at the certification stage, for a class cannot be certified on a theory that would leave damages “uncertain and incapable of proof.” Continental Ore Co. v. Union Carbide & Carbon Corp., 370 U.S. 690, 697 (1962).

III. Predominance under Rule 23(b)(3) therefore demands that the district court conduct a rigorous inquiry into whether a common methodology exists to calculate damages for every class member. Brown Shoe Co. v. United States, 370 U.S. 294, 325-326 (1962) (antitrust injury must be shown “with the requisite degree of certainty”); United States v. Yellow Cab Co., 332 U.S. 218, 225-226 (1947) (monopolization claims require proof of injury “common to the class”). Where, as here, plaintiffs’ expert model fails to isolate damages attributable to the sole remaining theory of liability, individual questions necessarily predominate.

IV. The same conclusion follows from the historic understanding of class actions. Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 366-367 (1921) (class suit permissible only when “the rights of all are affected by the decree”); Smith v. Swormstedt, 57 U.S. 288, 302-303 (1853) (common interest must be “joint” and damages “capable of being ascertained”). No such showing was made.

Because the record contains no admissible, common proof that damages can be measured class-wide, the certification order must be reversed.
