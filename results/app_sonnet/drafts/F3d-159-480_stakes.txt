# ARGUMENT

## I. Congress Exceeded Its Commerce Power In Applying § 1951 To This Purely Local Robbery

The Hobbs Act reaches only those robberies that affect interstate commerce, because that effect is itself a jurisdictional element of the offense. *Stirone v. United States*, 361 U.S. 212, 218 (1960) (Hobbs Act reaches interference with "the flow of commerce"). This Court has sustained the Act against Commerce Clause challenge only because proof of an effect on commerce is required in every case. *United States v. Bolton*, 68 F.3d 396, 399 (10th Cir. 1995). But *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 559 (1995), makes clear that Congress may not regulate purely local, non-economic conduct merely because it has some attenuated relationship to commerce. The government's proof here—that a single sandwich shop purchased some out-of-state supplies and that robbing its register might reduce future purchases—rests on the same "depletion of assets" theory that treats every local theft as a federal crime. That theory cannot be squared with *Lopez*'s admonition that the Commerce Clause has "outer limits," 514 U.S. at 557, and Mr. Pearson's conviction under § 1951 must be reversed.

## II. The District Court Violated Mr. Pearson's Right To Present A Defense By Excluding His Alibi Evidence

"[T]he right of an accused in a criminal trial to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor" is a fundamental element of due process. *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973). Preclusion of a defendant's own evidence is not automatically forbidden, but it is a "disfavored" remedy that requires the trial court to weigh the reasons for the delay, the importance of the testimony, and the prejudice to the opposing party before excluding critical defense proof. *Taylor v. Illinois*, 484 U.S. 400, 414-15 (1988); *Michigan v. Lucas*, 500 U.S. 145, 152 (1991) (preclusion permissible only after "a case-by-case" evaluation, not as an automatic sanction). The district court made no such findings here. It refused to permit Mr. Pearson's alibi—corroborated by an independent jewelry-store employee and by disinterested pager records—without ever assessing whether a lesser sanction would have cured any prejudice to the government. Given that the alibi evidence went to the heart of Mr. Pearson's defense, exclusion cannot be reconciled with *Taylor*'s requirement that preclusion be reserved for willful, blatant violations after the "less severe" alternatives have been considered. 484 U.S. at 413.

## III. The Evidence Was Insufficient To Support Conviction

Under *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979), a conviction may stand only if "any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." The government's identification proof does not meet that standard. Ms. Starks could not identify Mr. Pearson from a photo array taken shortly after the robbery; she identified him only after seeing his picture broadcast on television and after he sat as the sole young black male in the courtroom. Such procedures raise the very reliability concerns this Court must weigh under *Manson v. Brathwaite*, 432 U.S. 98, 114 (1977) (identification testimony must be excluded, or discounted, where "the corrupting effect of the suggestive identification" outweighs its reliability), and echo the suggestive-confrontation concerns identified in *Simmons v. United States*, 390 U.S. 377, 383 (1968). What remains is the testimony of two accomplices—Meyer and Martin—each testifying pursuant to a plea agreement and each with obvious incentive to minimize his own culpability. On this record, no rational jury could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt as to Mr. Pearson's participation.

## IV. The Felony-Murder Instruction Misstated The Elements Of The Offense

A conviction cannot stand where a jury instruction "relieve[s] the State of the burden of proof … on the critical question" of an element of the offense. *Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510, 521 (1979). Review of jury instructions asks not merely whether the instruction was "undesirable, erroneous, or even universally condemned," but whether, taken as a whole, it "so infected the entire trial that the resulting conviction violates due process." *Cupp v. Naughten*, 414 U.S. 141, 146-47 (1973). The instruction given here permitted the jury to convict without finding that Mr. Pearson possessed the requisite connection to the killing itself, effectively collapsing the distinct elements of the underlying robbery and the resulting death into a single inquiry. That omission removed from the jury's consideration a fact the government was constitutionally required to prove, and it requires reversal under *Sandstrom* and *Cupp*.

## V. The District Court Erred In Refusing Mr. Pearson's Lesser-Included-Offense Instructions

"[A] defendant is entitled to an instruction on a lesser included offense if the evidence would permit a jury rationally to find him guilty of the lesser offense and acquit him of the greater." *Keeble v. United States*, 412 U.S. 205, 208 (1973). Denial of such an instruction, where warranted, forces the jury into an impermissible "all-or-nothing choice," undermining the reliability of the verdict. *Beck v. Alabama*, 447 U.S. 625, 637 (1980). Here, the evidence—including testimony that Mr. Pearson was unarmed and that the fatal shot was fired accidentally by Martin—would have permitted a rational jury to convict Mr. Pearson of robbery while acquitting him of felony murder. Under *Keeble* and *Beck*, the refusal to instruct on that lesser offense was reversible error.
