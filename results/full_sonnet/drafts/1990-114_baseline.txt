# ARGUMENT

## I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ADEA

### A. The Plain Statement Rule Requires Congress to Speak Clearly Before Federal Law Intrudes on a State's Power to Determine the Qualifications of Its Own Judges

This Court has long recognized that "certain constitutional guarantees ... require clear statutory guidance." *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). Because the states' constitutional structure and their power to determine the qualifications of their own officials, including judges, lie "at the heart of representative government," any congressional intent to displace such structures must be "unmistakably clear in the language of the statute." Determining who is qualified to serve as a state judge is a decision of the most fundamental sort concerning a State's power to "define the qualifications of officeholders." *Sugarman v. Dougall*, 413 U.S. 634, 647 (1973). Missouri's decision to require its judges to retire at seventy is exactly such a decision.

Nothing in the ADEA clearly extends its coverage to state judges. The Eighth Circuit correctly held that because "the tenure of state judges is a matter of considerable importance to a state, and one that traditionally has been left to each state to regulate," the ADEA should not be read to displace Missouri's constitutional retirement provision "unless we find that Congress clearly has expressed its intention to bring state judges into the ambit of the federal statute." *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 599 (8th Cir. 1990). No such clear expression exists here.

### B. Missouri's Appointed Judges Are "Appointee[s] on the Policymaking Level" Excluded From ADEA Coverage

The ADEA excludes from its definition of "employee" any "appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). This exception encompasses Missouri's judges.

The function of judging is not merely mechanical application of settled rules; it inherently involves policymaking. As the Eighth Circuit recognized, adopting the reasoning of the First Circuit, "'[p]olicymaking' is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents." *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 602 (quoting *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988)). Judicial decisions "necessarily will resolve issues previously unsettled and thus will create law." *Id.* Missouri's own courts confirm this reality: the Missouri Supreme Court has overturned settled tort doctrine and adopted comparative negligence wholesale, see *Gustafson v. Benda*, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), and has resolved profound questions of medical ethics and constitutional right, see *Cruzan v. Harmon*, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988) (en banc). These are policymaking functions of the highest order.

Moreover, Missouri's judges exercise substantial administrative and supervisory policymaking authority independent of case adjudication. The Missouri Supreme Court promulgates the rules governing professional conduct for the entire state judiciary and bar, and possesses general supervisory power over the lower courts. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 602-03; Mo. Const. art. V, § 4. When judicial factions within a circuit could not agree on court rules, the Missouri Supreme Court intervened directly to resolve the impasse. *See In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc). This is quintessential policymaking.

Petitioners' contrary reading would produce an implausible result. If the "policymaking level" exception does not encompass appointed judges, then none of the ADEA's four statutory exemptions would apply to any member of the judicial branch in a state that appoints, rather than elects, its judges—even though elected judges performing identical functions are entirely exempted. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 603. Congress is unlikely to have intended so anomalous a result, under which the constitutionally sensitive question of who may serve as a state judge would be treated differently for ADEA purposes based solely on the state's chosen method of selection, without any difference in judicial function or responsibility. *Id.*

## II. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE

This Court's precedents establish that age is not a suspect classification and that mandatory retirement provisions for public employees are subject only to rational basis review. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). This Court has already held that "this Court's decisions give no support to the proposition that a right of governmental employment per se is fundamental." *Id.* Petitioners here can point to no fundamental right burdened by Missouri's retirement provision; they possess no entitlement to continued judicial service past age seventy. *See also San Antonio Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1, 35 (1973).

Under rational basis review, Missouri's mandatory retirement provision easily survives. Multiple legitimate state interests support a mandatory retirement age for judges. First, "the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode." *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979). Second, mandatory retirement "reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges," a risk that increases with age. *Id.* at 629. Third, a fixed retirement age "eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged ... judges" through individualized, potentially embarrassing competency proceedings. *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980). As the Massachusetts high court explained, such a rule "eliminates the anguish, time, delay, expense, and embarrassment of the supervision and removal of older judges of failing competence pursuant to an evaluation process," while also "mak[ing] it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary." *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988).

Rational basis review does not require Missouri to have chosen the empirically optimal age, nor does it permit courts to substitute their own policy judgments for the State's. Petitioners may disagree with Missouri's line-drawing "on either empirical or philosophical grounds," but such disagreement does not establish that the State "lacks a rational basis for its mandatory retirement rule for judges." *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 605. Because Missouri's provision is rationally related to legitimate state interests in judicial competence, administrability, and diversity, it comports with the Equal Protection Clause.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
