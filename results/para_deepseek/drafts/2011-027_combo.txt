**ARGUMENT**

The constitutional test for determining navigability for title purposes requires a trial court to assess whether the relevant stretch of a river was navigable at the time of statehood. The Montana courts correctly applied this settled standard, and this Court should affirm.

**I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Demands Navigability at Statehood**

Under the equal footing doctrine, “the title to the beds of the rivers” passes to a newly admitted state “upon its admission into the Union” if the rivers were navigable at that time. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894). This principle flows from *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 224 (1845), which held that states “are admitted into the Union on an equal footing with the original states” and therefore acquire title to submerged lands. The precise temporal anchor is statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931) (“the title to the beds of the Humboldt and Salt rivers depends upon the navigability of these rivers at the time of the admission of the State of Utah into the Union”). *Accord United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (“the test of navigability must be applied as of the time of the admission of the state into the Union”).

This Court has consistently applied that rule. *Packer v. Bird*, 137 U.S. 661, 669 (1891) (title to riverbeds “passes to the State upon its admission” if navigable at statehood). *Ex parte Boyer*, 109 U.S. 629, 631 (1884) (navigability at statehood is the “true test”). The inquiry is fixed; subsequent changes in the river’s condition do not alter the title. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75-76.

**II. The Montana Courts Correctly Applied the Historical Test**

The Montana Supreme Court evaluated historical evidence of the rivers’ use in 1889, precisely as required. Navigability is a question of fact, to be determined by evidence of “the condition of the river as a navigable highway at the time of statehood.” *The Montello*, 87 U.S. 430, 440 (1874). *Barney v. Keokuk*, 94 U.S. 324, 338 (1876) (navigability for title “must be determined by the condition of the river at the time the State was admitted”). The court properly relied on historical records, including ships’ logs, newspaper accounts, and government surveys, all of which are admissible under the ancient-documents and public-records exceptions. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870) (defining navigable waters as those “used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce”); *The Montello*, 87 U.S. at 442 (historical use can demonstrate navigability). The evidence showed that the Clark Fork, Missouri, and Madison rivers were used by steamboats and other commercial vessels in 1889, satisfying the test.

**III. PPL’s Contrary Argument Lacks Support**

PPL appears to argue that the test requires a different standard—perhaps a modern or a segment-by-segment inquiry that ignores the statehood date. But this Court has repeatedly rejected such approaches. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82 (navigability is assessed “in its natural and ordinary condition” at statehood). *Packer v. Bird*, 137 U.S. at 670 (same). The federal test for navigability for title is distinct from the broader commerce-clause test; it is tied to the state’s historic entitlement upon admission. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. at 563. And the Montana courts correctly applied the standard that “the navigability of a river for title purposes is determined by its condition at the time of statehood.” *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14.

**Conclusion**

The constitutional test is clear: navigability for title must be determined as of the date of statehood. The Montana courts faithfully applied that test, relying on historical evidence that the rivers were navigable in 1889. The judgment below should be affirmed.
