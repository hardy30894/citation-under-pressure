The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Clause protects a defendant from being "subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." Although the Clause does not explicitly mention sentencing determinations, the Supreme Court has interpreted it to apply in certain contexts.

In Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), the Court held that a defendant who was initially sentenced to life imprisonment for first-degree murder and later retried and sentenced to death after a successful appeal was not subjected to double jeopardy. However, the Court noted that the sentencing proceeding was "in substance and effect a trial" because the jury had the discretion to impose either a life sentence or the death penalty.

Similarly, in this case, the trial of the prior conviction allegation under California's "three-strikes" law has many characteristics of a trial on guilt or innocence. The prosecution must prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt, and the defendant has the right to confront witnesses and present evidence. Although the Supreme Court has not directly addressed the issue of double jeopardy in noncapital sentencing proceedings, the principles enunciated in Stroud suggest that the Clause may apply in this context.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause is not limited to protecting against retrial for the same offense, but also protects against multiple punishments for the same offense. In Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. 163 (1873), the Court held that a defendant who had already been sentenced to imprisonment and had served part of the sentence could not be resentenced to a different punishment.

The trial of the prior conviction allegation in this case is akin to a trial on guilt or innocence, and the Double Jeopardy Clause should apply to bar retrial on the allegation after the Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient to support the trial court's true finding. The State's attempt to retry the prior conviction allegation would subject Monge to a second "jeopardy" for the same "offense," violating his constitutional rights under the Fifth Amendment.

The California Supreme Court's decision to allow retrial of the prior conviction allegation is inconsistent with the principles of double jeopardy. By holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings, the court below erred. This Court should reverse the California Supreme Court's decision and hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial on the prior conviction allegation.
