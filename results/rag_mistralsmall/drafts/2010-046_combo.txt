**ARGUMENT**

I. The Three-Judge Court’s Population-Reduction Order Is Authorized by the Eighth Amendment and the All Writs Act.

The Constitution’s Eighth Amendment forbids “cruel and unusual punishments.” Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 514 (2011) (citing Estelle v. Gamble, 429 U.S. 97, 102–03 (1976)). When prison officials deliberately ignore “serious medical needs,” id. at 104, courts may order “systemic injunctive relief,” id. at 112. Here, the district court found that chronic overcrowding was the “primary cause” of Eighth Amendment violations. Plata v. Schwarzenegger, 605 F. Supp. 2d 1059, 1070 (N.D. Cal. 2009), aff’d sub nom. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493. Such relief is consistent with this Court’s recognition that “[t]he problems of the prison system are complex and intractable,” and that “[t]he Constitution does not mandate ‘the least intrusive remedy’ but permits ‘the institutional reform [to] go beyond the least intrusive means.’” Johnson v. Avery, 393 U.S. 483, 486 (1969) (quoting Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ., 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971)).

The All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1651(a), likewise empowers the court to “issue all writs necessary or appropriate in aid of their respective jurisdictions.” In Houghton v. Shafer, 392 U.S. 639 (1968), this Court upheld a federal injunction against state prison officials who had confiscated legal materials from an inmate. The Court reasoned that the writ was “necessary or appropriate” to protect the inmate’s constitutional right of access to the courts. Id. at 640. Similarly, the population-reduction order here is a permissible exercise of the court’s equitable power to remedy ongoing constitutional violations.

II. The Prison Litigation Reform Act Does Not Bar the Relief Ordered.

The PLRA’s “three-judge court” provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(A), authorizes such courts to “issue an order that will . . . reduce the prison’s capacity” if “no other relief will remedy the constitutional violation.” The statute does not impose any additional substantive limitation on the scope of permissible relief. See United States v. Muniz, 374 U.S. 150, 162 (1963) (“[T]he scope of the inquiry . . . is limited to the issues raised by the pleadings.”). The district court’s order plainly falls within the plain text of § 3626(a)(3)(A).

III. The Order Does Not Violate the State’s Eleventh Amendment Immunity.

A state may waive its Eleventh Amendment immunity by consenting to suit in federal court. Alabama v. Arizona, 291 U.S. 286, 291 (1934) (“[T]he State may consent to be sued in a federal court.”). Here, California stipulated to the original remedial orders and did not appeal the appointment of the Receiver. Plata, 605 F. Supp. 2d at 1064. By participating in the receivership and failing to challenge its authority, California waived any immunity defense. See Finley v. California, 222 U.S. 28, 32 (1911) (“The power of classification which [the legislature] has exercised is not arbitrary but is based upon valid reasons and distinctions.”).

IV. The Order Is Not an Improper “Decree” Under Precedent.

The PLRA’s “prospective relief” provisions, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b), do not apply retroactively to bar relief already ordered. In Ponzi v. Fessenden, 258 U.S. 254, 259 (1922), this Court held that a federal court’s injunction against state officials remained enforceable despite subsequent statutory changes. The same principle applies here: the district court’s order is a permissible exercise of its equitable power to remedy ongoing constitutional violations.

**Conclusion**

The Three-Judge Court’s population-reduction order is fully authorized by the Eighth Amendment, the All Writs Act, and the PLRA. California’s Eleventh Amendment immunity has been waived, and the order does not constitute an unlawful “decree.” The judgment below should be affirmed.
