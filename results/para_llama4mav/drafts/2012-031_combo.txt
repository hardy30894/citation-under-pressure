## Argument

The District Court erred in certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The court's decision to certify the class was based on an overly lenient standard, which is contrary to the requirements of Rule 23(b)(3) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a class action is an exception to the usual rule that litigation is conducted by and on behalf of the individual named parties only. See Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32, 41 (1940). To justify a departure from that rule, "some showing" must be made that a class action "will fairly and adequately protect the interests of the absent members of the class." Id. at 42-43. In this case, the District Court failed to ensure that the class members' interests would be adequately protected.

The District Court's certification decision was based on its finding that the plaintiffs had demonstrated by a preponderance of the evidence that they could prove antitrust impact and damages on a class-wide basis. However, this finding is not supported by the evidence. The court relied heavily on the testimony of the plaintiffs' expert, Dr. James McClave, who presented a damages model that purported to measure damages on a class-wide basis. However, the model was flawed and did not account for individual variations in damages.

The Supreme Court has held that a district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" of the evidence before certifying a class. See Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin, 84 S.Ct. 1420, 1426 (1964) (Eisen III). This requires the court to "evaluate carefully the legitimacy of the named plaintiff's plea that he is a proper class representative under Rule 23(a)." Id. at 1427. In this case, the District Court failed to conduct a sufficiently rigorous analysis of the evidence.

The Third Circuit has also emphasized the importance of a rigorous analysis in class certification decisions. See Philadelphia Elec. Co. v. Anaconda Am. Brass Co., 43 F.R.D. 452, 457 (E.D. Pa. 1968) (noting that the court must make a "definitive determination" that the requirements of Rule 23 are met). The District Court's decision to certify the class without adequate evidence of class-wide damages is contrary to this principle.

Furthermore, the District Court's decision is inconsistent with the principles of antitrust law. The Supreme Court has held that antitrust plaintiffs must prove that they have suffered an "injury of the type that the antitrust laws were intended to forestall." See Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc., 88 S.Ct. 502, 506 (1967) (quoting Wyandotte Transp. Co. v. United States, 88 S.Ct. 383, 389 (1967)). In this case, the plaintiffs must prove that they suffered antitrust injury as a result of Comcast's alleged anticompetitive conduct.

The District Court's certification decision also raises concerns about the manageability of the class action. The Supreme Court has noted that a class action is not "superior to other available methods for the fair and efficient adjudication of the controversy" if it is unmanageable. See Eisen III, 84 S.Ct. at 1430. In this case, the District Court failed to consider the manageability implications of certifying a class with millions of members.

In conclusion, the District Court erred in certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The court's decision is contrary to the requirements of Rule 23(b)(3) and the principles of antitrust law. The certification decision should be reversed.
