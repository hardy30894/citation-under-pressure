**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Smoot’s Railway Labor Act conspiracy claim and in awarding statutory, punitive, and fee relief on the Wiretap Act counterclaims.

I. The executive-session recording did not violate the Federal Wiretap Act.

An “oral communication” is protected only when uttered by a speaker who exhibits “an expectation that such communication is not subject to interception under circumstances justifying such expectation.” 18 U.S.C. § 2510(2). The Public Law Board hearing was a quasi-public arbitral proceeding conducted under the Railway Labor Act, 45 U.S.C. § 153. Participants could not reasonably expect that every word spoken in the presence of the parties’ representatives and a court reporter would remain immune from recording. See Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347, 351 (1967) (“What a person knowingly exposes to the public … is not a subject of Fourth Amendment protection.”). Because no protected communication was intercepted, the Act supplied no predicate for statutory damages, 18 U.S.C. § 2520(c)(2), punitive damages, or attorney’s fees.

Even if the communication were protected, the district court’s finding of intentional violation cannot stand on summary judgment. The record shows only that Smoot left recorders running during a recess; it contains no evidence that he acted with the “conscious or reckless disregard of the law” required for punitive relief. See Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). Absent such scienter, the award of $100,000 in punitive damages and $86,308.74 in fees must be vacated.

II. Genuine issues of material fact preclude summary judgment on the RLA conspiracy claim.

Section 153 First (q) permits judicial review of Public Law Board awards only for fraud or corruption. 45 U.S.C. § 153 First (q). Smoot alleged that UTU and CSX conspired to predetermine the outcome of his stock claim by removing his chosen representative and conducting an ex parte executive session. The district court credited the carriers’ and union’s denials without addressing the tape’s contents or the timing of Earley’s removal. On summary judgment the non-movant’s evidence must be credited and all reasonable inferences drawn in his favor. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). Because the record permits an inference that the arbitration was tainted by collusion, summary judgment on the RLA claim was improper.

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
