**ARGUMENT**

**I. The plain text of the FDCPA defines a “debt collector” as one who regularly collects debts “owed or due another,” and Santander was collecting its own debts when it engaged in the challenged conduct.**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (“FDCPA”) defines a “debt collector” as “any person who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts, or who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). The statute’s plain language establishes that the critical inquiry is whether the person collects debts on behalf of another—not whether the debt was in default when acquired. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (noting that the definition “includes a person ‘who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] . . . another’”) (alteration in original).

Here, the complaint alleges that Santander purchased the petitioners’ defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto on December 1, 2011, and thereafter attempted to collect on those loans for its own account. Petitioners do not allege that Santander was collecting on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto at the time of the challenged communications. To the contrary, the complaint states that Santander acquired the loans “for a few cents on the dollar,” making Santander the owner of the debt. As the owner, Santander was collecting its own debt, not a debt “owed or due another.” *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1315-16 (11th Cir. 2015) (“The statutory text is entirely transparent. . . . [A] person must regularly collect or attempt to collect debts for others in order to qualify as a ‘debt collector’ under the second definition of the term.”).

The FDCPA’s definition of “creditor” confirms this distinction. A “creditor” is “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). When Santander purchased the loans, it became the entity “to whom a debt is owed.” *Id.* The Act excludes from the definition of “creditor” only those who receive a debt “in default solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another.” *Id.* (emphasis added). Petitioners ignore the critical phrase “for another.” Because Santander purchased the debt for its own account—not to facilitate collection for CitiFinancial Auto—it falls squarely within the definition of “creditor,” not “debt collector.”

**II. The default status of a debt is irrelevant to whether a purchaser of debt qualifies as a “debt collector” under the threshold definition.**

Petitioners’ central argument—that the default status of a debt determines whether a purchaser is a “debt collector”—finds no support in the statute’s plain language. The default status appears only in an exclusion from the definition of “debt collector,” not in the definition itself. Section 1692a(6)(F)(iii) excludes from the term “debt collector” “any person collecting or attempting to collect any debt owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another to the extent such activity . . . concerns a debt which was not in default at the time it was obtained.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) (emphasis added). This exclusion applies only to persons who collect debts “owed or due another”—i.e., third-party collectors. It does not create an affirmative definition of “debt collector” for those who purchase defaulted debt.

As the Fourth Circuit correctly held, the structure of § 1692a(6) requires a court to “first determine whether the person satisfies one of the statutory definitions given in the main text of § 1692a(6) before considering whether that person falls into one of the exclusions.” *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA, Inc.*, 817 F.3d 131, 137 (4th Cir. 2016). If a person does not satisfy the main definition—i.e., does not regularly collect debts “owed or due another”—the exclusions are irrelevant. *Id.* (citing *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1314). Petitioners’ attempt to invert this structure by using an exclusion to create a definition is contrary to settled principles of statutory interpretation.

**III. Petitioners’ interpretation would produce absurd results inconsistent with Congress’s purpose.**

Congress enacted the FDCPA “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). The Act targets third-party collectors who engage in abusive practices on behalf of creditors, not creditors collecting their own debts. *See S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698* (stating that the term “debt collector” is intended “to cover all third persons who regularly collect debts for others”).

Petitioners’ interpretation would sweep into the FDCPA every entity that purchases defaulted debt, even if that entity’s principal business is lending or servicing its own loans. This would transform the FDCPA into a statute regulating ordinary creditors—a result Congress expressly disclaimed. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (excluding from the definition of “debt collector” any “officer or employee of a creditor while, in the name of the creditor, collecting debts for such creditor”). As the Fourth Circuit observed, under petitioners’ view, “a company such as Santander . . . would be subject to the FDCPA for all of its collection activities simply because one of its several activities involves the collection of debts for others.” *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 141. Congress did not intend this result.

**IV. The complaint fails to allege that Santander was acting as a “debt collector” at the time of the challenged conduct.**

A valid FDCPA claim requires the plaintiff to prove that the defendant was acting as a “debt collector” “at the time of and in connection with the prohibited conduct.” *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 141. Here, the complaint alleges that Santander purchased the loans on December 1, 2011, and thereafter engaged in the challenged collection activities. The complaint does not allege that Santander was collecting on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto at that time. To the contrary, it alleges that Santander was collecting on debts it owned. Because Santander was acting as a creditor, not a debt collector, the FDCPA does not apply.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
