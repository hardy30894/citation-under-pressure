**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that a plaintiff may defeat ERISA’s three-year statute of limitations merely by testifying that he did not read or could not recall having read disclosures that fully revealed the alleged breach. Section 1113(2) bars suit “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). “Actual knowledge” means knowledge of the underlying facts constituting the breach, not knowledge that those facts amount to a legal violation. A plaintiff who receives complete disclosure of those facts has actual knowledge, regardless of whether he chooses to read or later remembers the information.

**I. Actual Knowledge Requires Only Knowledge of the Facts, Not Legal Characterization**

This Court has long held that “actual knowledge” of a wrong means knowledge of the facts giving rise to the claim, not awareness of their legal significance. In *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 140 (1879), the Court explained that the statute of limitations for fraud runs from “the discovery of the facts constituting the fraud,” not from the plaintiff’s realization that those facts are actionable. *Accord Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. 342, 348 (1874) (limitations period begins when plaintiff “has knowledge of the facts” that would enable him to “ascertain the truth”). Similarly, in *Exploration Co. v. United States*, 247 U.S. 435, 448 (1918), the Court stated that “actual knowledge” of a claim requires “knowledge of the facts” that constitute the wrong, not “knowledge of the law.” *Soriano v. United States*, 352 U.S. 270, 276 (1957), applied the same principle to a federal limitations statute, holding that “actual knowledge” of a claim means “knowledge of the facts giving rise to the claim.” These cases establish a uniform rule: once a plaintiff knows the operative facts, the limitations period begins even if he does not appreciate their legal import.

The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that a plaintiff must also know that the defendant’s conduct was “imprudent” under ERISA § 1104 directly contradicts this settled principle. The court demanded “actual knowledge both that those investments occurred, and that they were imprudent.” That second element is a legal conclusion—whether the fiduciary’s actions satisfied the prudent-investor standard. This Court has never required knowledge of a legal conclusion to trigger a limitations period. *See United States v. Oregon Lumber Co.*, 260 U.S. 290, 300 (1922) (limitations period runs from “knowledge of the facts” even if plaintiff “did not know that the facts constituted a cause of action”). The Ninth Circuit’s rule would allow plaintiffs to indefinitely delay suit by claiming ignorance of the law, precisely what *Wood* and *Bailey* forbid.

**II. A Plaintiff Cannot Avoid the Limitations Period by Failing to Read or Recall Disclosed Information**

Intel provided Sulyma with Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and other documents that explicitly disclosed the alternative investments and the strategy behind them. Sulyma accessed some of these documents. He nevertheless testified that he was “unaware” of the investments and did not “recall” reading the disclosures. The Ninth Circuit held that this testimony created a genuine dispute of material fact. That holding is legally erroneous.

When a defendant fully discloses the facts constituting an alleged breach, the plaintiff has “actual knowledge” of those facts upon receipt of the disclosure. This Court has long recognized that a person is charged with knowledge of information that is made available to him, even if he fails to examine it. In *O’Neil v. Northern Colorado Irrigation Co.*, 242 U.S. 20, 26 (1916), the Court held that a party who signs a contract “is bound by its terms, even if he did not read them.” The same principle applies to disclosures: a plaintiff who receives a document containing the relevant facts cannot avoid the limitations period by claiming he did not read or remember its contents. *See Pufahl v. Parks’ Estate*, 299 U.S. 217, 225 (1936) (actual knowledge exists when a party “has been informed of the facts” even if he “does not fully comprehend their significance”). To hold otherwise would reward willful ignorance and undermine the statute’s purpose of ensuring prompt resolution of claims.

Moreover, Sulyma’s subjective lack of recall is insufficient to defeat summary judgment. *United States v. Wurts*, 303 U.S. 414, 416 (1938), held that “actual knowledge” is established when the evidence shows the plaintiff “knew or should have known” the facts. While the statute uses “actual knowledge,” the Court has consistently interpreted that term to include knowledge that a reasonable person would have gained from disclosed information. *See Urie v. Thompson*, 337 U.S. 163, 170 (1949) (limitations period begins when plaintiff “knows or should know” of the injury). Here, Intel’s disclosures were clear and comprehensive. Sulyma’s self-serving testimony that he does not recall reading them cannot create a genuine issue where the documentary evidence demonstrates that the facts were available to him.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Conflicts with the Statute’s Text and Purpose**

Congress repealed the constructive-knowledge provision in 1987, leaving only the actual-knowledge requirement. That repeal did not, however, transform “actual knowledge” into a subjective standard that turns on a plaintiff’s memory or reading habits. The term “actual knowledge” in § 1113(2) must be given its ordinary meaning: knowledge of the facts, not of the law. *Soriano*, 352 U.S. at 276. The Ninth Circuit’s addition of a “knowledge of imprudence” element reads a new requirement into the statute that Congress never intended. *See Wood*, 101 U.S. at 140 (courts should not “add to the statute” by requiring knowledge of legal consequences).

The purpose of a statute of limitations is to “promote justice by preventing surprises through the revival of claims that have been allowed to slumber until evidence has been lost.” *Bailey*, 88 U.S. at 349. Intel disclosed all relevant information in 2010, 2011, and 2012. Sulyma waited until 2015 to sue. Allowing him to proceed based on his failure to read or remember those disclosures would defeat the statute’s purpose and encourage plaintiffs to avoid examining plan documents. This Court should reverse.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
