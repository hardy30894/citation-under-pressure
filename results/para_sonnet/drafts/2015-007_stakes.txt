**ARGUMENT**

**I. Florida's Capital Sentencing Scheme Satisfies the Sixth Amendment Because a Jury Finds the Facts Necessary to Support a Death Sentence.**

This Court's decision in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), does not require reversal here. *Ring* invalidated Arizona's scheme because, under it, "the trial judge alone" found the aggravating circumstance necessary for a death sentence, "sitting without a jury." *Id.* at 588–89. Relying on *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), this Court held that "[b]ecause Arizona's enumerated aggravating factors operate as 'the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense,' the Sixth Amendment requires that they be found by a jury." *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609. The Court accordingly overruled *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), "to the extent that it allows a sentencing judge, sitting without a jury, to find an aggravating circumstance necessary for imposition of the death penalty." *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609.

Florida's scheme is materially different. Unlike Arizona's judge-only procedure, Florida requires the jury to hear the aggravating and mitigating evidence and to render a sentencing recommendation under § 921.141, Fla. Stat. Where, as here, the defendant is convicted of felony murder, the jury's own guilty verdict necessarily establishes—unanimously and beyond a reasonable doubt—the existence of the contemporaneous felony that the trial court applied as an aggravating circumstance. The Florida Supreme Court so held in *Bottoson v. Moore*, 833 So.2d 693 (Fla. 2002), and *King v. Moore*, 831 So.2d 143 (Fla. 2002), concluding that *Ring* does not disturb Florida's scheme where the jury's guilt-phase verdict itself supplies the aggravating fact. That jury fact-finding is precisely what *Ring* found lacking in Arizona.

Moreover, this Court has twice upheld Florida's system against Sixth Amendment attack. In *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447 (1984), the Court held that "the Sixth Amendment does not require jury sentencing, even where the sentence is death." *Id.* at 459. And in *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638 (1989) (per curiam), the Court expressly held that "the Sixth Amendment does not require that the specific findings authorizing the imposition of the sentence of death be made by the jury." *Id.* at 640–41. *Ring* did not overrule *Hildwin* or *Spaziano*; it addressed only Arizona's judge-alone procedure and left undisturbed schemes, like Florida's, in which the jury participates in the sentencing determination. Petitioner's jury heard the evidence, weighed the aggravating and mitigating circumstances, and recommended death by a vote of seven to five. That process, resting on a jury verdict of felony murder, satisfies the Sixth Amendment as construed in *Ring*.

**II. Neither the Sixth Nor Eighth Amendment Requires a Jury—Rather Than a Judge—to Determine Whether a Capital Defendant Is Intellectually Disabled.**

*Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), held that the Eighth Amendment bars execution of the intellectually disabled, but it expressly declined to dictate how States must implement that rule. Echoing its approach in *Ford v. Wainwright*, the Court stated that it would "leave to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction upon [their] execution of sentences." *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317. Florida did exactly that, enacting § 921.137, Fla. Stat., which assigns the trial judge the task of determining intellectual disability by clear and convincing evidence.

This Court confirmed that such judicial procedures are constitutionally adequate in *Schriro v. Smith*, 546 U.S. 6 (2005) (per curiam), summarily reversing a court of appeals that had required a jury determination of mental retardation. The Court reiterated that *Atkins* "leaves to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction," and that nothing in *Atkins* mandates jury fact-finding on that issue. *Id.* at 7. Petitioner's claim that the Sixth Amendment requires jury resolution of intellectual disability fails for the same reason his *Ring* claim fails: intellectual disability under Florida law operates as an eligibility bar decided by the court, not a fact increasing the statutory maximum punishment within the meaning of *Apprendi*. The judge in this case afforded petitioner a full opportunity to present intellectual-disability evidence and mitigation to the jury, and separately found, based on record evidence, that he had not proven intellectual disability. That procedure comports with both *Atkins* and *Schriro*.

**III. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require a Unanimous Jury Recommendation of Death.**

Finally, the Sixth Amendment does not require unanimity even in ordinary criminal verdicts. See *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404 (1972) (plurality opinion). *A fortiori*, it does not require unanimity in an advisory sentencing recommendation that, under *Spaziano* and *Hildwin*, need not be made by a jury at all. Just as a legislature may permit judges to find sentencing factors by a preponderance of the evidence, *see McMillan v. Pennsylvania*, 477 U.S. 79 (1986), Florida may permit its jury to recommend a sentence by majority vote. Petitioner's seven-to-five recommendation, following a unanimous guilty verdict establishing the underlying felony, satisfies both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
