**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings once a reviewing court has determined the evidence was insufficient to support the finding. The California Supreme Court erred in holding otherwise. The United States Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that sentencing proceedings that determine facts essential to punishment—such as recidivist enhancements—are subject to double jeopardy protections when they possess the hallmarks of a criminal trial. *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 438 (1981); *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 209-10 (1984). Because California statutorily affords defendants a jury trial on prior conviction allegations, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and the right to confrontation and cross-examination, *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993); *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal.4th 217, 224 (1996), the sentencing proceeding here bore the critical attributes of a criminal trial. Under these circumstances, retrial after reversal for insufficient evidence violates the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The Supreme Court has consistently applied double jeopardy principles to sentencing proceedings that resemble trials on guilt or innocence. In *Bullington*, the Court held that a capital sentencing proceeding in which the State bears the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt and the jury renders a life or death verdict is an “acquittal” for double jeopardy purposes, precluding retrial on the same aggravating factors after reversal. 451 U.S. at 438-40. The Court later extended this principle to noncapital sentencing in *Rumsey*, explaining that the critical inquiry is whether the proceeding “involve[d] the kind of determinations that are traditionally associated with the criminal trial process.” 467 U.S. at 209-10. The Court emphasized that the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence”—such as a burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt and the right to jury determination—trigger double jeopardy protections. *Id.* at 210.

California’s statutory scheme for adjudicating prior conviction allegations incorporates these hallmarks. Section 1025 of the California Penal Code guarantees a jury trial on prior conviction allegations, and this Court has interpreted that right to include proof beyond a reasonable doubt, *Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th at 566, the right to confrontation and cross-examination, *Reed*, 13 Cal.4th at 228, fn. 6, and the application of the rules of evidence, *People v. Myers*, 5 Cal.4th 1193, 1201 (1993). These procedural safeguards render the prior conviction proceeding indistinguishable from a trial on guilt or innocence for double jeopardy purposes. *See Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438-39 (focusing on the procedural protections of the sentencing phase); *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209-10 (same).

The California Supreme Court’s contrary conclusion misreads the Supreme Court’s precedents. The Court below relied on *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), for the proposition that double jeopardy does not apply to sentencing proceedings. However, *Stroud* predates *Bullington* and *Rumsey* and has been cabined to its facts. *See United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117, 134 (1980) (distinguishing *Stroud* as involving a sentencing scheme without procedural protections akin to a trial). Moreover, the Supreme Court has never held that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* are limited to capital cases. *See Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994) (merely noting *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases without disavowing their reasoning). The Court’s focus in *Bullington* and *Rumsey* was not the severity of the sentence, but the procedural protections of the sentencing phase. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438-39; *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209-10.

The Court below also erred in suggesting that statutory protections do not implicate double jeopardy. The Supreme Court has never required that procedural protections be constitutionally mandated to trigger double jeopardy. *See Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438 (applying double jeopardy despite Missouri’s statutory scheme). The critical inquiry is whether the proceeding possesses the hallmarks of a trial, not the source of the procedural protections. *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209-10.

Finally, the Court below’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. *Williams* upheld a sentencing scheme that afforded no procedural protections and permitted the sentencing judge to rely on untested, out-of-court information. *Id.* at 242-46. The Supreme Court has since cabined *Williams* to its facts, particularly in cases involving capital sentencing, *Gardner v. Florida*, 430 U.S. 349, 358 (1977), and has reaffirmed that sentencing proceedings with procedural protections akin to trials are subject to double jeopardy. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438-39; *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209-10.

Because the prior conviction proceeding here bore the hallmarks of a criminal trial—jury determination, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and the right to confrontation and cross-examination—the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial after reversal for insufficient evidence. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
