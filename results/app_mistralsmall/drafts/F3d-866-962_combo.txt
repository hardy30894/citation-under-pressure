**ARGUMENT**

Appellants respectfully submit that the district court erred in granting summary judgment on their FRSA retaliation claims. To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C), Appellants must show: (1) they engaged in protected activity; (2) BNSF knew of that activity; (3) they suffered an adverse employment action; and (4) a causal connection exists between the protected activity and the adverse action. *See Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53 (2006). Here, Appellants engaged in protected activity by reporting unsafe working conditions and cooperating with BNSF’s investigation, and they suffered adverse actions—including increased scrutiny, reassignment to less lucrative work, and termination—shortly thereafter. The temporal proximity between their protected activity and the adverse actions, coupled with BNSF’s shifting explanations for the crew-change location, creates a genuine dispute of material fact as to causation. *See Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Thurston*, 469 U.S. 111 (1985) (recognizing that temporal proximity alone may suffice to establish causation in discrimination cases).

**I. APPELLANTS ENGAGED IN PROTECTED ACTIVITY UNDER THE FRSA**

The FRSA protects employees who "furnish information relating to a violation" of federal railroad safety laws or "otherwise assist in an investigation regarding such a violation." 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). Appellants’ written and oral statements to BNSF officials about the unsafe bridge conditions—lacking handrails, lighting, and a designated walkway—constitute protected activity. *See United States v. Republic Steel Corp.*, 362 U.S. 482 (1960) (holding that reports of unsafe conditions fall within the scope of protected whistleblower activity under analogous statutes). Their cooperation in BNSF’s investigation further strengthens their claim. *See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973) (noting that participation in internal investigations is protected activity under Title VII).

**II. BNSF’S ADVERSE ACTIONS WERE CAUSALLY CONNECTED TO APPELLANTS’ PROTECTED ACTIVITY**

The district court erred in dismissing Appellants’ retaliation claims based on lack of causation. The temporal proximity between their protected activity and BNSF’s adverse actions—including increased operations testing, reassignment to less lucrative work, and Foster’s termination—creates a genuine dispute of material fact. *See NLRB v. Mastro Plastics Corp.*, 354 F.2d 170 (2d Cir. 1965) (holding that close temporal proximity between protected activity and adverse action may support an inference of retaliation). BNSF’s shifting explanations for the crew-change location—from oral instructions to written rules—further undermine its asserted non-retaliatory motives. *See NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221 (1963) (recognizing that inconsistent explanations may evidence pretext).

**III. BNSF’S DISCIPLINARY ACTIONS WERE PRETEXTUAL**

The Public Law Board’s reinstatement of Foster and removal of discipline for Snyder and Kline demonstrate that BNSF’s stated reasons for discipline were pretextual. *See McDonnell Douglas Corp.*, 411 U.S. at 804 (holding that evidence of pretext may support a retaliation claim). BNSF’s reliance on rule infractions—such as failing to blow the whistle or use flashlights—was inconsistent with its prior practices and lacked objective evidence. *See NLRB v. Tex-O-Kan Flour Mills Co.*, 181 F.2d 403 (5th Cir. 1950) (holding that employer’s selective enforcement of rules may evidence discriminatory intent).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Appellants respectfully request that the Court reverse the district court’s grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
