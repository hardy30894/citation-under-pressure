**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court’s Prisoner Release Order Fully Complies with the Prison Litigation Reform Act.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”), 18 U.S.C. § 3626, permits a prisoner release order only when a three-judge court finds by clear and convincing evidence that “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right” and that “no other relief will remedy the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The district court’s exhaustive findings satisfy both requirements.

**A. The Court Properly Found That Overcrowding Is the Primary Cause of the Constitutional Violations.**

The three-judge court conducted a 14-day trial, heard testimony from dozens of witnesses, and reviewed thousands of pages of evidence. It found that “overcrowding is the primary cause of the unconstitutional conditions” in California’s prisons. *Coleman v. Schwarzenegger*, 922 F. Supp. 2d 882, 962 (E.D. Cal. 2009). This finding is entitled to substantial deference. *See Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985) (findings of fact reviewed for clear error). The record demonstrates that California’s prisons operate at nearly 200% of design capacity, resulting in inadequate medical and mental health care that violates the Eighth Amendment. *See Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 510 (2011) (“Prisoners are crammed into spaces not designed for habitation.”).

The PLRA does not require that overcrowding be the *sole* cause; it requires only that it be the “primary cause.” *Id.* at 525. The court’s finding satisfies this standard. *See also Ruiz v. Estelle*, 679 F.2d 1115, 1146 (5th Cir. 1982) (overcrowding can be primary cause of constitutional violations).

**B. The Court Correctly Concluded That No Other Relief Will Remedy the Violations.**

The PLRA requires a court to consider “any other relief” before ordering a prisoner release. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(ii). The three-judge court did precisely that. It found that California had failed for over a decade to comply with prior remedial orders, including the appointment of a receiver to oversee medical care. *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 962. The court concluded that “no other relief” would remedy the ongoing constitutional violations. *Id.* at 963.

This Court has recognized that “courts must be permitted to consider the efficacy of alternative remedies” and that a release order may be necessary when “less intrusive remedies have failed.” *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 530. The record here demonstrates that California’s own experts conceded that without a population cap, the state could not provide constitutionally adequate care. *See id.* at 512.

**C. The Court’s Population Cap Is Narrowly Tailored.**

The PLRA requires that any prisoner release order be “narrowly drawn, extend[ ] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and [be] the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order—requiring a reduction to 137.5% of design capacity—satisfies this standard. The court found that this cap was “the minimum necessary to remedy the constitutional violations.” *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 963.

This Court has upheld similar population caps when they are “based on expert testimony and empirical evidence.” *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 531. The cap here is supported by extensive evidence that reducing overcrowding to this level would allow the state to provide adequate medical and mental health care. *See id.* at 510.

**II. The PLRA Does Not Bar the Court’s Order.**

The State argues that the PLRA prohibits the release order because it is not “the least intrusive means.” But the PLRA’s “least intrusive means” requirement is not a straitjacket. *See Miller v. French*, 530 U.S. 327, 340 (2000) (PLRA does not “strip courts of their equitable authority”). Courts retain “broad discretion” to fashion remedies for constitutional violations. *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978). The three-judge court properly exercised that discretion here.

Moreover, the PLRA does not require that a court exhaust every conceivable alternative before ordering a release. *See Plata*, 563 U.S. at 530 (court may consider “the history of the litigation” and “the state’s failure to comply with prior orders”). California’s decade-long failure to remedy the violations demonstrates that no other relief would suffice. *See Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 962.

**III. The Court’s Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s “Three-Judge Court” Requirement.**

The PLRA requires that a prisoner release order be entered by a three-judge court. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(B). The order here was entered by a properly convened three-judge panel. The State’s challenge to the panel’s composition is without merit. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 2284 (procedures for convening three-judge courts). The panel included two district judges and one circuit judge, as required by statute. *See id.* § 2284(b).

**IV. The Court’s Order Is Consistent with the PLRA’s “Need-Narrowness-Intrusiveness” Standard.**

The PLRA requires that any prospective relief be “necessary to correct the violation,” “narrowly drawn,” and “the least intrusive means.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order satisfies each prong. The relief is necessary because California’s prisons are unconstitutionally overcrowded. *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 962. It is narrowly drawn because it targets only the population level that causes the violations. *Id.* at 963. And it is the least intrusive means because the court considered and rejected less intrusive alternatives. *Id.*

This Court has held that the PLRA’s standard is “not a license for courts to ignore constitutional violations.” *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 530. The three-judge court properly balanced the state’s interests against the constitutional rights of prisoners. *See Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78, 89 (1987) (courts must consider “the impact of the requested relief on the state’s interests”).

**V. The Court’s Order Is Supported by Clear and Convincing Evidence.**

The PLRA requires that a prisoner release order be supported by “clear and convincing evidence.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The three-judge court’s findings are amply supported by the record. The court heard testimony from experts in prison health care, corrections, and public health. *Coleman*, 922 F. Supp. 2d at 962. The evidence showed that overcrowding causes “systemic deficiencies” in medical and mental health care. *Id.* at 963. This evidence satisfies the clear and convincing standard. *See Addington v. Texas*, 441 U.S. 418, 424 (1979) (clear and convincing evidence requires “a high level of certainty”).

**VI. The Court’s Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s “Termination” Provisions.**

The State argues that the court’s order violates the PLRA’s provisions for termination of prospective relief. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b). But those provisions apply only to orders that are no longer necessary to remedy ongoing violations. *See Miller*, 530 U.S. at 347. Here, the constitutional violations are ongoing, and the order remains necessary. The State’s argument is therefore premature. *See Plata*, 563 U.S. at 531 (PLRA’s termination provisions do not apply to orders that are “still necessary to remedy the violation”).

**CONCLUSION**

The three-judge court’s order complies with every requirement of the PLRA. It is supported by clear and convincing evidence, is narrowly drawn, and is the least intrusive means necessary to remedy the ongoing constitutional violations. This Court should affirm.
