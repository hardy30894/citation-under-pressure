Respondent Santander Consumer USA, Inc. is not a “debt collector” under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act when it collects debts it owns. The Act regulates “debt collectors,” not creditors collecting on their own behalf. 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692a(4), 1692a(6), 1692e; Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 292-93 (1995); Harris v. Liberty Community Management, Inc., 702 F.3d 1298, 1302 (11th Cir. 2012).

The statutory text controls. The FDCPA defines a “debt collector” primarily as “any person” whose “principal purpose” is debt collection, or who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due ... another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). By contrast, a “creditor” is a person to whom a debt is owed, excluding one who acquires defaulted debt “solely for the purpose of facilitating collection ... for another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). The Act thus draws the critical line between third-party collectors and entities collecting their own debts. See Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 136-41 (4th Cir. 2016), aff’d, 582 U.S. 79 (2017); Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 720 F.3d 1204, 1208-09 (9th Cir. 2013).

Santander falls on the creditor side of that line. The complaint alleges that CitiFinancial sold the receivables to Santander, and that Santander thereafter collected on debts it had purchased. Once Santander acquired the accounts, the debts were no longer “owed or due ... another” within the meaning of § 1692a(6). Henson, 817 F.3d at 139-41; Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1314-18 (11th Cir. 2015); Del Villar v. Preferred Credit Corp., 674 F. App’x 653, 654-55 (9th Cir. 2017). As the Fourth Circuit correctly recognized, the default status of the debt at the time of purchase does not transform a purchaser into a debt collector under the statute’s main definition. Henson, 817 F.3d at 137-41; see also Schlegel, 720 F.3d at 1209.

Petitioners’ contrary theory reads the FDCPA backwards. They rely on § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which excludes from “debt collector” a person collecting debts “owed or due another” that were not in default when obtained. But a statutory exclusion does not create liability where the threshold definition is not met. Henson, 817 F.3d at 137-40; Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1314-16. Section 1692a(6)(F)(iii) matters only after a defendant first falls within the Act’s baseline definition of “debt collector.” See Henson, 817 F.3d at 138-39; Schroyer v. Frankel, 197 F.3d 1170, 1174-75 (6th Cir. 1999).

Nor does the fact that Santander may have collected some accounts for others at one time make it a debt collector for all purposes forever after. The FDCPA asks whether the defendant was acting as a debt collector “at the time” of the challenged conduct. Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1318; Schlegel, 720 F.3d at 1209. A company that sometimes services or collects debts for others is not transformed into a debt collector when it later collects debts it owns for itself. Henson, 817 F.3d at 140-41; Bailey v. Security National Servicing Corp., 154 F.3d 384, 387-88 (7th Cir. 1998).

That interpretation also accords with Congress’s purpose. The FDCPA was enacted “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). It is not a general federal regulation of all creditors. Heintz, 514 U.S. at 292-93; Buckman v. American Bankers Insurance Co. of Florida, 115 F.3d 901, 903-04 (11th Cir. 1997). Petitioners’ reading would sweep ordinary creditors into the statute whenever they purchase defaulted accounts, contrary to the Act’s text and structure. Henson, 817 F.3d at 140-41; Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1315-18.

Because Santander attempted to collect debts it owned, not debts “owed or due another,” it was not acting as a “debt collector” under § 1692a(6). The judgment should be affirmed.
