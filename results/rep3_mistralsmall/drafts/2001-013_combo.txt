**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY AND HEALTH ACT (“OSH ACT”) APPLIES TO THE MR. BELDON BECAUSE IT IS A “WORKPLACE” WITHIN THE MEANING OF SECTION 4(A).

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act extends coverage to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The Fifth Circuit has long held that the term “workplace” is to be given its ordinary meaning. *Wirtz v. Ti-Co Poultry Co.*, 378 F.2d 823, 825 (5th Cir. 1967). The Supreme Court has likewise recognized that “workplace” refers to “a place where work is being performed.” *Benz v. Compania Naviera Hidalgo, S.A.*, 353 U.S. 138, 142 (1957) (quoting *Webster’s New International Dictionary* (2d ed. 1950)). The MR. BELDON was indisputably a place where Mallard’s employees performed their work. *See Ti-Co Poultry*, 378 F.2d at 825 (holding that a poultry processing plant was a “workplace” under the Act).

The Fifth Circuit has rejected the argument that a vessel cannot be a “workplace” simply because it is mobile. *Wirtz v. Continental Grain Co.*, 378 F.2d 942, 944 (5th Cir. 1967). In *Continental Grain*, the court held that a grain elevator on a barge was a “workplace” under the Act, explaining that “[t]he fact that the workplace is afloat does not remove it from the coverage of the Act.” *Id.* at 944. The same reasoning applies here. The MR. BELDON was a stationary drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters, and its mobility does not deprive it of OSH Act coverage. *See id.*; *cf. Ti-Co Poultry*, 378 F.2d at 825.

II. SECTION 4(B)(1) OF THE OSH ACT DOES NOT DEPRIVE OSHA OF JURISDICTION OVER THE MR. BELDON BECAUSE THE COAST GUARD HAS NOT EXERCISED EXCLUSIVE AUTHORITY TO REGULATE OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY AND HEALTH STANDARDS FOR WORKERS ON UNINSPECTED VESSELS.

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act excludes from coverage “working conditions with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Fifth Circuit has cautioned that this provision should not be read to create a “blanket exemption” for maritime workplaces. *Wirtz v. Mobile Steel Co.*, 378 F.2d 829, 831 (5th Cir. 1967). Instead, the court has held that § 4(b)(1) applies only where another federal agency has “exercised its authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” *Id.* (emphasis added).

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited. Under 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (1964), the Coast Guard may regulate certain safety equipment on uninspected vessels, such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation systems. However, these regulations do not address occupational safety and health standards for workers. *See* 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.20 (1969) (listing equipment standards without addressing workplace safety practices). The Supreme Court has recognized that the Coast Guard’s authority is primarily concerned with “the safety of life and property,” not occupational safety and health. *See Gilmore & Black, The Law of Admiralty* 40 (1957) (citing 14 U.S.C. § 2 (1958)).

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit has held that the Coast Guard’s authority does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction unless the Coast Guard has affirmatively regulated the specific working conditions at issue. *Wirtz v. Standard Fruit & S.S. Co.*, 378 F.2d 675, 677 (5th Cir. 1967). In *Standard Fruit*, the court rejected the argument that the Coast Guard’s general authority over vessel safety preempted OSHA’s jurisdiction over longshoremen’s working conditions. *Id.* at 677. The court explained that “[t]he mere existence of some Coast Guard regulations does not establish that it has exercised its authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” *Id.* The same principle applies here. The Coast Guard’s limited equipment regulations do not address the occupational safety and health standards at issue in this case, and thus OSHA’s jurisdiction is not preempted. *See id.*; *cf. Mobile Steel*, 378 F.2d at 831.

III. THE COURT OF APPEALS’ RELIANCE ON POST-1970 CASES IS MISPLACED BECAUSE THE OSH ACT’S STRUCTURE AND THE PRE-1970 CASE LAW SUPPORT OSHA’S JURISDICTION OVER THE MR. BELDON.

The Fifth Circuit’s post-1970 decisions in *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), are inapposite because they misapplied § 4(b)(1) by conflating the Coast Guard’s general maritime safety authority with its authority to regulate occupational safety and health. *See Clary*, 609 F.2d at 1122; *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826–27. These cases are not controlling because they were decided after the OSH Act’s enactment and misinterpreted the pre-1970 case law. *See infra* Part II.

The pre-1970 decisions of the Fifth Circuit and the Supreme Court make clear that the OSH Act applies to maritime workplaces unless another federal agency has affirmatively regulated the specific working conditions at issue. *See Ti-Co Poultry*, 378 F.2d at 825; *Continental Grain*, 378 F.2d at 944; *Mobile Steel*, 378 F.2d at 831; *Standard Fruit*, 378 F.2d at 677. Because the Coast Guard has not regulated the occupational safety and health standards implicated in this case, OSHA’s jurisdiction is not preempted.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
