# ARGUMENT

## I. Bryant Has Standing to Enforce the Arbitration Award in His Favor

The district court erred in holding that Bryant, as an individual employee, lacks standing to enforce an arbitration award rendered in his favor under the collective bargaining agreement between Bell Atlantic and the CWA. It has long been settled that Section 301 of the Labor Management Relations Act creates a body of federal law "which the courts must fashion from the policy of our national labor laws" to govern suits for violation of contracts between an employer and a labor organization. *Textile Workers Union v. Lincoln Mills*, 353 U.S. 448, 456 (1957). That federal law is not the exclusive property of the union that negotiated the contract.

In *Smith v. Evening News Ass'n*, 371 U.S. 195 (1962), the Supreme Court held that an individual employee may sue his employer under Section 301 to enforce rights arising under a collective bargaining agreement, notwithstanding that the same conduct might also be redressable through the union's own grievance machinery or before the National Labor Relations Board. The Court made clear that Section 301 "expresses a federal policy that federal courts should enforce these agreements on behalf of or against labor organizations and that industrial peace can be best obtained only in that way." *Id.* at 200. Bryant, having prevailed in arbitration, stands in the shoes of a contract beneficiary entitled to the benefit of that bargain; the union's role in prosecuting the grievance to arbitration does not extinguish Bryant's personal interest in the award once rendered.

Nor does the limited scope of judicial review of arbitration awards under the Steelworkers Trilogy diminish Bryant's right to seek enforcement. In *United Steelworkers v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp.*, 363 U.S. 593 (1960), the Court held that once an arbitrator has resolved a dispute properly submitted to him, "courts . . . have no business overruling him because their interpretation of the contract is different from his." *Id.* at 599. The corollary of that deferential rule is that courts must enforce, rather than second-guess, an award actually made. *See also United Steelworkers v. Warrior & Gulf Navigation Co.*, 363 U.S. 574 (1960); *United Steelworkers v. American Mfg. Co.*, 363 U.S. 564 (1960). And in *Republic Steel Corp. v. Maddox*, 379 U.S. 650 (1965), the Court confirmed that federal labor policy contemplates that an individual employee's contract grievance, once pursued through the parties' agreed grievance procedure, remains the employee's own claim for redress. *Id.* at 652. Bryant did precisely what federal labor law required of him, obtained a favorable award, and is entitled to its enforcement. The district court's contrary ruling should be reversed.

## II. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on Bryant's Discrimination and Retaliation Claims

Summary judgment is a blunt instrument, ill-suited to claims in which motive, intent, and the reasonable inferences to be drawn from a course of conduct are the central issues for decision. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that such determinations belong to the trier of fact. In *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System*, 368 U.S. 464 (1962), the Court reversed a grant of summary judgment, explaining that "summary procedures should be used sparingly" in cases "where motive and intent play leading roles." *Id.* at 473. The same caution applies with full force here, where Bryant's claims of race, color, and gender discrimination, and of retaliation, turn on Bell Atlantic's motive in disciplining and ultimately discharging him after years of accommodation efforts, medical excusals, and a union request that Bell Atlantic itself rejected.

Likewise, in *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620 (1944), the Court held that a district court may not resolve on summary judgment questions that depend upon the credibility of witnesses and the weight of conflicting evidence, for such determinations are "peculiarly ones for the trier of the facts." *Id.* at 627. Bryant's record — a decade of unblemished service, informal accommodation of his child-care obligations before 1995, escalating discipline only after implementation of the Red Letter Day policy, and termination shortly after his medical excusal lapsed — presents precisely the kind of disputed factual mosaic that a jury, not a court on paper, must evaluate.

Finally, discriminatory purpose need not be shown by direct admission; it may be inferred from the pattern of an employer's conduct viewed as a whole. *See Norris v. Alabama*, 294 U.S. 587 (1935) (discriminatory purpose established through circumstantial and statistical evidence of exclusionary practice); *Strauder v. West Virginia*, 100 U.S. 303 (1880) (unequal treatment inferable from the totality of the circumstances surrounding facially neutral practices). The district court, in isolating each disciplinary episode and crediting Bell Atlantic's proffered justification without weighing the cumulative pattern — including Bell Atlantic's refusal of the CWA's offer to permit swaps for Bryant's Red Letter Day assignments — failed to view the evidence, as it must on summary judgment, in the light most favorable to Bryant. *Sartor*, 321 U.S. at 627.

## III. Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment on Bryant's claim to enforce the arbitration award, and reverse the grant of summary judgment on his Title VII and Section 1981 claims, and remand for trial.
