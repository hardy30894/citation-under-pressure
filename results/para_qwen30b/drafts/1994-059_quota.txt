**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether states may alter the qualifications for U.S. Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution—must be answered in the negative. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment, by imposing additional, constitutionally impermissible qualifications on candidates for federal office, violates the Supremacy Clause and the exclusive nature of the qualifications set forth in Article I, Sections 2 and 3 of the U.S. Constitution. This Court has long held that the Constitution’s framers intended the qualifications for federal office to be exclusive, not subject to state addition or modification. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly concluded that Section 3 of Amendment 73 is unconstitutional, and that the entire amendment must fall because the unconstitutional provision is not severable from the valid state term limits.

**I. The Constitution’s Qualifications Clauses Are Exclusive and Supersede State Law**

The Constitution explicitly sets forth the qualifications for members of Congress in Article I, Section 2 and Section 3. These qualifications are not merely guidelines; they are the sole standards by which federal officeholders may be judged. The Framers rejected proposals to allow states to impose additional qualifications, and the final text reflects a deliberate decision to centralize control over federal eligibility. As this Court held in *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 788 (1995), “the Constitution’s framers intended the qualifications for federal office to be exclusive.” The qualifications for the House of Representatives—age, citizenship, and residence—are not subject to state addition or alteration. To permit states to impose additional qualifications would undermine the uniformity and national character of federal office, a principle this Court has consistently upheld.

In *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1875), this Court emphasized that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no state law can be valid if it conflicts with the Constitution.” The Arkansas Amendment directly conflicts with the Constitution by adding a new qualification—term limits—that is not found in Article I. The Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to impose state-imposed qualifications on federal officeholders. In *United States v. Ballin*, 144 U.S. 1, 10 (1892), the Court stated that “the qualifications for members of Congress are fixed by the Constitution, and no other qualifications can be added by the states.” This principle is not merely a matter of policy; it is a constitutional imperative.

**II. The Supremacy Clause Prohibits State Imposition of Additional Qualifications**

The Supremacy Clause, Article VI, Clause 2, establishes that the Constitution is the “supreme Law of the Land,” and that state laws “shall be in Conformity therewith.” *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 405 (1819). When a state law conflicts with a constitutional provision, it is void. The Arkansas Amendment does not merely regulate elections—it creates a new, constitutionally impermissible qualification. This is not a matter of election administration; it is a direct violation of the Constitution’s text. In *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 18 (1958), the Court declared that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no state law or state action may stand in the way of the Constitution.” The Arkansas Amendment, by barring individuals from appearing on the ballot based on prior service, imposes a qualification not found in the Constitution and thus violates the Supremacy Clause.

**III. The Framers’ Intent Was to Prevent State Interference with Federal Qualifications**

The debates at the Constitutional Convention make clear that the Framers rejected proposals to allow states to impose additional qualifications. In *The Federalist No. 57*, James Madison wrote that “the members of the House of Representatives are to be chosen by the people of the several States,” and that “the qualifications of the electors and of the elected” must be “uniform throughout the Union.” The Framers understood that uniformity was essential to the functioning of a national legislature. Allowing states to impose term limits would create a patchwork of qualifications, undermining the national character of Congress. In *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 790, the Court observed that “the Constitution’s framers rejected the idea of allowing states to impose additional qualifications,” and that “the Constitution’s framers intended the qualifications for federal office to be exclusive.”

**IV. Severability Is Not a Cure for Constitutional Infractions**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the severability clause in Section 4 does not save the amendment. The Court has long recognized that severability is not automatic. In *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 (1938), the Court stated that “a statute may be unconstitutional in its entirety even though it contains a severability clause.” The test is whether the valid portions of the law would have passed without the invalid ones. In *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750, 375 S.W.2d 809, 811 (1964), the Arkansas Supreme Court held that “the test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion.” Here, the amendment was presented as a single, unified package. The ballot title referred to “elected officials” without distinction between state and federal officeholders. The Preamble spoke of “entrenched incumbency” in general terms, and the voters were not given a choice between state and federal term limits. The amendment was not a “clean” severable package; it was a “one-size-fits-all” measure designed to appeal to a broad base of voters by bundling two distinct issues.

In *Hoban v. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 420, 316 S.W.2d 185, 188 (1958), the Arkansas Supreme Court refused to enforce a severability clause where the two provisions were “inextricably linked” and presented as a single proposition.
