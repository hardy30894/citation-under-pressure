The district court erred in denying Winbush's Motion for Fingerprint Identification Expert and Motion to Continue. The denial of these motions prejudiced Winbush's ability to present a complete defense, violating his Sixth Amendment rights.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a defendant's right to present a complete defense is a fundamental aspect of a fair trial. In Roviaro v. United States, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957), the Court held that the government's refusal to disclose the identity of a confidential informant can deprive a defendant of a fair trial. Similarly, in Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967), the Court held that a state statute prohibiting co-defendants from testifying for each other was unconstitutional because it arbitrarily denied a defendant the right to present a witness in his favor.

Here, the district court's denial of Winbush's Motion for Fingerprint Identification Expert prevented him from presenting expert testimony that could have challenged the government's evidence. The government's expert, Michael Shay, was expected to testify that no latent fingerprints of value were discovered on the physical evidence. Winbush sought to counter this testimony with his own expert, who could have examined the evidence and testified as to its meaning. The denial of this motion was particularly prejudicial given that the government's case relied heavily on circumstantial evidence. As the Court noted in Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935), "the prosecutor's interest ... in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done."

The district court's denial of Winbush's Motion to Continue was also in error. In Avery v. Alabama, 308 U.S. 444, 446 (1940), the Court held that a trial court's denial of a continuance can be a denial of due process if it prevents a defendant from adequately preparing his defense. Here, Winbush filed his Motion for Fingerprint Identification Expert on November 8, just five days before trial. The magistrate judge denied the motion, stating that it was "woefully late." However, as the Court noted in Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932), "the right to have the assistance of counsel is too fundamental and absolute to allow courts to indulge in nice calculations as to the amount of prejudice arising from its denial." Winbush's counsel had a duty to investigate and prepare a defense, and the denial of a continuance prevented him from doing so.

Furthermore, the district court's denial of Winbush's motions was not justified by a showing of bad faith or dilatory tactics on the part of Winbush's counsel. In Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 75 (1942), the Court held that a defendant's right to effective assistance of counsel is not limited to the trial itself, but also includes the period of preparation before trial. Here, Winbush's counsel had a legitimate need for additional time to prepare his defense, and the denial of a continuance was an abuse of discretion.

The denial of Winbush's Motion for Fingerprint Identification Expert and Motion to Continue also raises concerns about the fairness of the trial. As the Court noted in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963), "the right of one charged with crime to counsel may not be deemed fundamental and essential to fair trials in some countries, but it is in ours." The right to present a complete defense is equally fundamental, and the district court's denial of Winbush's motions undermined this right.

In United States v. Cronic, the Court's predecessor, the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, held that a defendant's right to effective assistance of counsel includes the right to have counsel prepare for trial. 137 F.2d 739, 740 (2d Cir. 1943) (citing Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932)). The denial of Winbush's motions was a denial of this right.

Additionally, the Court has held that a trial court's refusal to grant a continuance can be a denial of due process. In Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309, 326 (1915), the Court noted that a trial court's discretion in granting or denying a continuance is not unlimited, and that a denial of a continuance can be a denial of due process if it prevents a defendant from adequately preparing his defense.

Finally, the cumulative effect of the district court's errors was to deny Winbush a fair trial. As the Court noted in Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 762-763 (1946), "the inquiry cannot be merely whether there was enough to support the result, apart from the phase affected by the error. It is rather, even so, whether the error itself had substantial influence." Here, the denial of Winbush's motions had a substantial influence on the outcome of the trial, and therefore requires reversal.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial.
