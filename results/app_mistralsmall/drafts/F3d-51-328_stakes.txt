**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING A PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION BECAUSE SHAPIRO FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS OF HER CLAIM UNDER THE FAIR HOUSING AMENDMENTS ACT OF 1988.

The Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988 (“FHAA”), 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B), prohibits discrimination against individuals with disabilities by refusing to make “reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services, when such accommodations may be necessary to afford [a handicapped] person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling.” To prevail on a claim under this provision, a plaintiff must establish that: (1) she is disabled within the meaning of the FHAA; (2) the defendant is a covered entity; and (3) the defendant refused to make a reasonable accommodation that was necessary to afford the plaintiff equal opportunity to use and enjoy her dwelling. *United States v. California Mobile Home Park Mgmt. Co.*, 107 F.3d 1374, 1380 (9th Cir. 1997); *Housing Auth. of City of New Haven v. Housing Auth. of Town of Cheshire*, 92 F.3d 484, 490 (2d Cir. 1996).

Here, Shapiro has failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits because she cannot show that Cadman Towers’ refusal to provide her with an immediate parking space constitutes a refusal to make a “reasonable accommodation” under the FHAA. The FHAA does not require a housing provider to grant preferential treatment or waive neutral rules that apply to all residents. *See United States v. Overlook Gardens Apartments*, 852 F. Supp. 1235, 1240 (N.D. Ill. 1994) (holding that the FHAA does not mandate that a landlord “alter the fundamental nature of its operations or waive neutral rules that apply to all tenants”). Cadman Towers’ first-come, first-served parking policy is a neutral rule that applies equally to all residents, regardless of disability. Shapiro’s requested accommodation—immediate access to a parking space—would effectively exempt her from this neutral rule, thereby granting her preferential treatment not afforded to other residents. Such an accommodation is not “reasonable” within the meaning of the FHAA. *See id.* at 1241 (“A requested accommodation is not reasonable if it imposes undue financial or administrative burdens or requires a fundamental alteration in the nature of the provider’s operations.”).

Moreover, Cadman Towers has demonstrated that it is willing to accommodate Shapiro’s disability once she obtains a parking space through the normal waiting list process. The FHAA does not require housing providers to grant accommodations that would fundamentally alter their operations or impose undue burdens. *See id.* at 1240-41. Cadman Towers’ offer to reasonably accommodate Shapiro’s disability *after* she secures a parking space—such as by assigning her a space near her apartment—satisfies its obligations under the FHAA. *See id.* at 1241 (finding that a landlord’s offer to relocate a disabled tenant to a ground-floor unit after she obtained a parking space was a reasonable accommodation).

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN GRANTING THE PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION BECAUSE SHAPIRO FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE IRREPARABLE INJURY.

A plaintiff seeking a preliminary injunction must demonstrate that she will suffer irreparable injury if the injunction is not granted. *See Doran v. Salem Inn, Inc.*, 422 U.S. 922, 931 (1975). Here, the district court erred in finding irreparable injury because Shapiro has not shown that she will suffer any harm that cannot be remedied by monetary damages. Shapiro’s alleged injuries—difficulty finding parking, urinary accidents, and pain—are not irreparable; they are temporary and can be compensated through damages. *See United States v. City of Erie*, 885 F. Supp. 1296, 1305 (W.D. Pa. 1995) (finding that alleged injuries from lack of parking were not irreparable because they could be remedied by monetary damages).

Furthermore, the balance of equities does not favor Shapiro. Cadman Towers has a legitimate interest in enforcing its neutral parking policy, which applies equally to all residents. Granting Shapiro an immediate parking space would disrupt this policy and grant her preferential treatment not afforded to others. *See Overlook Gardens Apartments*, 852 F. Supp. at 1241. The public interest also weighs against granting the injunction, as it would set a precedent requiring housing providers to waive neutral rules for disabled individuals, potentially imposing undue burdens on those providers. *See id.*

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING THE PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION BECAUSE SHAPIRO FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON HER CLAIM THAT CADMAN TOWERS VIOLATED THE NEW YORK HUMAN RIGHTS LAW.

Shapiro’s state-law claim under the New York Human Rights Law (“NYHRL”), N.Y. Exec. Law § 296(2)(a), similarly fails because she cannot show that Cadman Towers’ refusal to provide her with an immediate parking space constitutes an unlawful discriminatory practice. The NYHRL, like the FHAA, requires reasonable accommodations for disabled individuals, but it does not mandate preferential treatment or waiver of neutral rules. *See Matter of New York State Ass’n for Retarded Children v. Carey*, 61 N.Y.2d 298, 305 (1984) (holding that the NYHRL does not require “affirmative action” or preferential treatment). Cadman Towers’ first-come, first-served parking policy is a neutral rule that does not discriminate against Shapiro on the basis of her disability. *See id.* at 306.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in granting the preliminary injunction. Cadman Towers respectfully requests that this Court vacate the injunction and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
