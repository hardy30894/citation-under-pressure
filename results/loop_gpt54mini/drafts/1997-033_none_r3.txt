The district court’s order should be reversed because § 1407(a) gives a transferee court authority only over pretrial proceedings, not trial. Congress provided that cases transferred for multidistrict coordination are to be handled “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and that the Panel “shall” remand each action “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The word “shall” is mandatory. United States v. Moore, 95 U.S. 760, 763 (1878); Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282, 289 (1929). Courts are not free to soften a mandatory statutory command because they believe a different arrangement would be more efficient. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

Section 1404(a) does not authorize what the district court did. That statute permits transfer “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only within the venue framework Congress established. Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58-59 (1949). Hoffman v. Blaski held that a transferee forum must be one where the case “might have been brought” originally; the transferee court cannot create authority to retain a case by invoking convenience after transfer. 363 U.S. 335, 343-44 (1960). Van Dusen v. Barrack confirmed that § 1404(a) does not alter the statutory venue regime or convert a transfer statute into a device for forum manipulation. 376 U.S. 612, 636-37 (1964). Norwood v. Kirkpatrick likewise recognized that § 1404(a) is a limited convenience provision, not a general grant of power to displace Congress’s chosen venue rules. 349 U.S. 29, 31-32 (1955).

The district court’s self-assignment also defeats § 1407’s structure and purpose. Multidistrict transfer centralizes common pretrial matters and then returns each action to its original district for trial. See Plumbing Fixture Cases, 298 F. Supp. 484, 495 (J.P.M.L. 1968). That statutory design reflects Congress’s judgment about venue and trial location, a judgment courts must respect. Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 507-08 (1947). The Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to treat venue rules as optional when convenience points elsewhere. United States v. National City Lines, Inc., 334 U.S. 573, 581-82 (1948); Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585, 364 U.S. 19, 26-27 (1960).

This Court has also long held that when statutory language is clear, courts must apply it as written, not rewrite it to achieve perceived practicality. Ex parte Falk, 180 U.S. 339, 344 (1901); United States v. Union Pacific R.R. Co., 98 U.S. 569, 604 (1879). Section 1407(a) leaves no room for a transferee court to retain a case for trial; it says the action “shall be remanded.” That textual command cannot be nullified by rebranding retention as a “transfer” under § 1404(a). Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 250-51 (1926). The district court’s order therefore was not a permissible exercise of discretion, but an unauthorized departure from the statute Congress enacted.

The judgment should be vacated and the case remanded to the Northern District of Illinois.
