**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue here—under which a non-unanimous jury may recommend death and a trial court may impose the death penalty without requiring the jury to find aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. This Court should affirm the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court, which correctly applied binding precedent and upheld a capital sentencing scheme that remains constitutionally sound under *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), and its progeny.

### I. THE FLORIDA SENTENCING SCHEME COMPLIES WITH THE SIXTH AMENDMENT AS INTERPRETED IN *RING*

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial “on the issue of guilt or innocence,” *Taylor v. United States*, 487 U.S. 326, 345 (1988), and *Ring* held that when a state makes an aggravating factor “an element of the greater offense,” that factor must be submitted to the jury and found beyond a reasonable doubt. 536 U.S. at 592. The Court emphasized that “the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find each fact necessary to impose a sentence of death.” Id. at 609.

But *Ring* did not hold that every fact relevant to the death penalty must be found by a unanimous jury. Rather, the Court focused on the *nature* of the aggravating factor: whether it is an “element” of the crime, not merely a “sentencing factor.” Id. at 592. In Florida, the aggravating circumstances are not elements of the offense of first-degree murder; they are *sentencing factors* that increase the penalty. See *Hurst v. State*, 18 So.3d 975, 995 (Fla. 2009) (citing *Harris v. United States*, 536 U.S. 545, 550 (2002)). The Florida Supreme Court has consistently held that the death penalty is not a mandatory punishment for first-degree murder, and that aggravating circumstances are not “elements” of the offense but rather “factors to be considered in determining whether the death penalty is appropriate.” *Hurst*, 18 So.3d at 995.

Thus, *Ring* does not require a unanimous jury verdict on aggravating circumstances. The Court in *Ring* did not address the unanimity requirement, and subsequent decisions have confirmed that the Sixth Amendment does not mandate unanimity in the penalty phase. See *Harris v. United States*, 536 U.S. 545, 550 (2002) (holding that the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to find every aggravating circumstance beyond a reasonable doubt, only those that are “elements” of the offense). The Florida Supreme Court correctly concluded that the jury’s advisory recommendation of death—though not unanimous—does not violate the Sixth Amendment because the jury is not required to find aggravating circumstances as elements of the crime.

Moreover, the Florida Supreme Court has long held that the jury’s role in the penalty phase is advisory, and that the trial court retains the ultimate authority to impose the death penalty. *Hurst*, 18 So.3d at 995. This structure is consistent with *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), and *Ring*, which do not require a unanimous jury verdict in the penalty phase. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the Constitution does not require a jury to agree unanimously on the existence of any particular aggravating factor.” *Harris*, 536 U.S. at 550. The Florida scheme, therefore, satisfies the Sixth Amendment.

### II. THE FLORIDA SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment, and the Court has held that “death is different” from all other punishments. *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994 (1991). But the Eighth Amendment does not require unanimity in jury recommendations or findings of aggravating circumstances. The Court has recognized that “the death penalty is a unique punishment that demands heightened reliability in its imposition.” *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). However, this “reliability” is achieved through procedural safeguards, not through a requirement of jury unanimity.

The Florida Supreme Court has emphasized that the jury’s recommendation is not binding, and that the trial court conducts a de novo review of the evidence and applies a balancing test under *Spencer v. State*, 407 So.2d 1147 (Fla. 1981). This process ensures that the death penalty is not imposed arbitrarily. The trial court considers all aggravating and mitigating factors, including those not found by the jury, and makes an independent determination. This independent review satisfies the Eighth Amendment’s demand for reliability.

Furthermore, Florida’s capital sentencing scheme is not an outlier in a constitutional sense. While Florida is one of the few states that does not require a unanimous jury verdict, the Court has never held that such a requirement is constitutionally mandated. The fact that other states have adopted different procedures does not render Florida’s scheme unconstitutional. *Woodward v. Alabama*, 134 S.Ct. 405 (2013) (Sotomayor, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari), cited by the Florida Supreme Court, involved a different constitutional issue—whether a non-unanimous jury could override a judge’s recommendation—and did not hold that non-unanimity violates the Constitution. The dissent in *Woodward* expressed concern, but the majority did not address the issue.

The Florida Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that the death penalty is not imposed unless the trial court finds aggravating circumstances outweigh mitigating ones. *Hurst*, 18 So.3d at 995. This independent judicial review ensures that the death penalty is not imposed based on a mere majority vote. The Court has upheld similar schemes in *Harris*, 536 U.S. 545, and *Hurst*, 18 So.3d 975. The Florida scheme, therefore, satisfies the Eighth Amendment.
