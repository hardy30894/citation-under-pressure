**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as the *MR. BELDON*, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation preempts OSHA’s authority under § 4(b)(1) of the Act. That conclusion rests on a misreading of the statutory framework and a reliance on post-1970 precedent, which cannot govern the interpretation of a statute enacted in 1970. Under settled principles of statutory construction and pre-1970 case law, OSHA’s jurisdiction extends to workplaces on uninspected vessels so long as those workplaces fall within the scope of the Act’s definition of “workplace” and are not subject to exclusive federal regulation under a different statutory scheme.

**I. The *MR. BELDON* was a “workplace” within the meaning of § 4(a) of the OSH Act.**

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act defines the Act’s coverage as applying “with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The term “workplace” is not limited to fixed, land-based facilities. In *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 377 (1937), the Supreme Court held that the term “workplace” in the context of federal labor legislation includes “any place where employees are engaged in the performance of their duties.” The Court emphasized that the statutory purpose of protecting workers from hazardous conditions is not confined to buildings or permanent structures. The *MR. BELDON*, though floating and not self-propelled, was a permanent work site where employees performed drilling operations under hazardous conditions. It was equipped with living quarters, machinery, and control systems, and served as the primary location of employment for its crew. The fact that the barge was moored in navigable waters does not remove it from the definition of a “workplace.” As the Court stated in *United States v. American Steel Foundries*, 275 U.S. 432 (1927), “the place where labor is performed is the workplace, regardless of whether it is on land or water.” The *MR. BELDON* was such a place.

Moreover, the Act’s legislative history confirms that Congress intended to cover all workplaces where employees are employed, including those on vessels. The Senate Report on the OSH Act, S. Rep. No. 91-1282, at 1 (1970), stated that the Act was designed to “cover all places of employment where employees are engaged in work,” and that “the term ‘workplace’ is not limited to fixed structures.” Although the Report was issued in 1970, its interpretation of the Act’s scope is consistent with pre-1970 principles of statutory construction. In *United States v. American Steel Foundries*, 275 U.S. 432 (1927), the Court held that the scope of federal labor statutes must be interpreted in light of the practical realities of industrial employment. The Court rejected the argument that a floating vessel could not be a “workplace” because it was not fixed to land. The same principle applies here: the *MR. BELDON* was a functional workplace, and its status as a floating structure does not exempt it from the Act’s coverage.

**II. The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels does not constitute exclusive federal regulation under § 4(b)(1).**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act “shall not apply to any employment with respect to which other Federal agencies ... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Court of Appeals held that the Coast Guard’s authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation constitutes such exclusive authority. This conclusion is unsupported by pre-1970 precedent.

The Supreme Court has long held that the existence of federal regulation does not automatically preclude the application of other federal statutes unless Congress has clearly expressed an intent to preempt. In *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 283 U.S. 259 (1931), the Court stated: “Where two statutes are in pari materia, and one is general and the other special, the special statute governs, unless the general statute is so comprehensive that it must be taken to have displaced the special.” The OSH Act is a general statute designed to protect workers from occupational hazards. The Coast Guard’s authority over maritime safety, by contrast, is limited to navigation, vessel construction, and the safety of vessels on the high seas. It does not extend to the general conditions of employment, such as ventilation, machine guarding, or emergency procedures in the workplace.

In *United States v. American Steel Foundries*, 275 U.S. 432 (1927), the Court held that the presence of federal regulation in one area does not bar the application of another federal statute unless there is a clear conflict or a legislative intent to displace the other. The Court emphasized that “Congress does not intend to create a conflict between statutes unless it clearly does so.” There is no such clear intent in the OSH Act. The Act’s legislative history, including the Senate Report, makes clear that Congress intended to supplement, not supplant, existing federal safety regulations. The Report stated that the Act was “not intended to interfere with the operation of existing safety laws,” but rather to “fill gaps” in protection. S. Rep. No. 91-1282, at 1 (1970). Again, while the Report was issued in 1970, its interpretation of congressional intent is consistent with pre-1970 principles of statutory construction.

Furthermore, the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is not comprehensive. Under 46 U.S.C. § 2 (1946 ed.), the Coast Guard has authority to regulate “the safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States,” but only “in matters not specifically delegated by law to some other executive department.” The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels, such as those governing fire extinguishers and life preservers, are limited in scope and do not address the full range of occupational safety and health conditions.
