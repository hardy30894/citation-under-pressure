**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING THE DEFENDANT’S EXPERT’S OPINION AS TO THE CAUSE OF THE LINE’S PARTING BECAUSE THE OPINION WAS NOT BASED ON EXPERT TESTIMONY AND WAS THEREFORE INADMISSIBLE.

Under the Federal Rules of Evidence, expert testimony is admissible only if it “will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue.” Fed. R. Evid. 702 (1970). Although the rule was not codified until 1970, the principle that expert testimony must be based on scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge was firmly established in the case law prior to that date. In *United States v. 12 200-Ft. Reels of Super 8mm Film*, 413 F.2d 340, 344 (2d Cir. 1969), the court held that “an expert may testify only if his opinion is based upon facts or data peculiar to his specialty.” Similarly, in *United States v. 100 Cases of 100-Milligram Tablets*, 388 F.2d 855, 858 (9th Cir. 1967), the court emphasized that “the expert must be qualified by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education,” and that his testimony must “rest upon a foundation of facts or data” within his expertise.

Here, the defendant’s expert, a marine engineer, testified that the line parted because of “thermal degradation” caused by the heat from Cook’s propane torch. However, the expert admitted he had not examined the line, had not tested it for strength, and had no knowledge of its prior use or condition. He based his opinion solely on the fact that the line was exposed to heat during the deicing process. This is not expert testimony—it is speculation dressed as science. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. 100 Cases of 100-Milligram Tablets*, 388 F.2d at 858, “an expert may not testify to a conclusion that is not supported by a proper foundation.” The expert’s opinion was not based on any examination of the rope, any testing of its tensile strength, or any analysis of its material composition. It was a mere conjecture, unsupported by any data or methodology.

Moreover, the cause of the line’s parting was a question of fact that could be determined by lay jurors. The jury was fully capable of assessing whether heat from a torch could weaken a manilla rope, especially when the rope had been used for years and was exposed to the elements. In *United States v. 12 200-Ft. Reels of Super 8mm Film*, 413 F.2d at 344, the court rejected expert testimony that was “no more than a guess” and held that “the jury is entitled to decide questions of fact without the aid of an expert when the facts are within the common experience of laypersons.” The issue of whether a rope can be weakened by heat is not beyond the ken of ordinary persons. The district court therefore erred in admitting the expert’s opinion, which was not based on specialized knowledge or a proper factual foundation.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ISSUE OF UNSEAWORTHINESS.

Under the Jones Act, a seaman may recover for injuries caused by the unseaworthiness of a vessel. *Barnes v. United States*, 326 U.S. 610, 614 (1946). A vessel is unseaworthy when it is “not reasonably fit for its intended purpose,” *Henderson v. United States*, 326 U.S. 637, 640 (1946), and this includes the failure to provide safe equipment or safe working conditions. *Barnes*, 326 U.S. at 614.

Here, the evidence established that the boom’s bearings were not greased with winter grease, despite the sub-zero temperatures and icy conditions. The crew had failed to change the grease from summer to winter, and the summer grease had frozen, rendering the boom immobile. Cook was forced to use a propane torch to thaw the bearings, exposing the line to heat. The line was then rigged while still in proximity to the heat source. The line was also old, had been left rigged for months, and had been exposed to the elements. The combination of these factors—frozen bearings, use of a torch near the line, and the use of a deteriorated rope—created a condition that was not reasonably fit for the purpose of lowering a crewman to the dock.

The Supreme Court in *Henderson v. United States*, 326 U.S. at 640, held that “the vessel must be reasonably fit for the purpose for which it is used.” The failure to maintain the boom’s bearings with proper grease in freezing weather was a clear breach of the shipowner’s duty. The use of a torch to thaw frozen bearings in such conditions was not a reasonable or safe practice. The fact that the crew had used this method before does not excuse the unseaworthiness. As the Court stated in *Barnes*, 326 U.S. at 614, “the mere fact that a practice has been followed does not make it safe or lawful.” The district court erred in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness because the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Cook, established a prima facie case of unseaworthiness.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK’S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS.

The admission of evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments was proper under the doctrine of relevance. Under *United States v. 100 Cases of 100-Milligram Tablets*, 388 F.2d at 858, evidence is admissible if it “has a tendency to make the existence of any fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action more probable or less probable than it would be without the evidence.” Cook’s history of alcohol-related health issues was relevant to his credibility and to the jury’s assessment of his conduct in the moments before the accident.
