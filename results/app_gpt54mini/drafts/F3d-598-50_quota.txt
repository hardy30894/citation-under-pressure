### ARGUMENT

#### I. The district court abused its discretion by admitting improper opinion testimony from prosecutors and a police lieutenant.

A trial court abuses its discretion when it admits testimony that invades the jury’s role on credibility, offers legal conclusions, or amounts to improper vouching. *See* **United States v. Scop**, 846 F.2d 135, 142–43 (2d Cir. 1988); **United States v. Garcia**, 413 F.3d 201, 210–11 (2d Cir. 2005); **United States v. Rea**, 958 F.2d 1206, 1215–16 (2d Cir. 1992). That is exactly what happened here.

The jury heard two ADAs and a police lieutenant opine that Ramos and Rivera were credible, that probable cause existed, and that evidence strengthened or weakened Appellants’ claims. Those opinions were not helpful explanations of specialized police or prosecutorial practices; they were direct assurances about the truth of the officers’ account and the legal merits of the arrests and prosecution. The Second Circuit has repeatedly held that witnesses may not “usurp the role of the jury” by telling jurors what result to reach. *Scop*, 846 F.2d at 142; *see also* **Hygh v. Jacobs**, 961 F.2d 359, 363–64 (2d Cir. 1992) (excluding testimony that was “patently designed to invade the province of the jury”). Nor may a witness testify that probable cause existed or that conduct was “unlawful” when those conclusions are for the jury and court, not a fact witness. *Hygh*, 961 F.2d at 363–64; *Garcia*, 413 F.3d at 210–11.

The problem was especially acute because the witnesses were government actors. This Court has warned that testimony from law enforcement or prosecutors can carry an “aura of special reliability and trustworthiness” that makes improper vouching particularly prejudicial. *Scop*, 846 F.2d at 142; *Rea*, 958 F.2d at 1216. Here, the ADAs’ testimony effectively told the jury that neutral authorities had already concluded Appellants’ claims were unfounded. That is precisely the kind of credibility bolstering that the rules of evidence forbid. *See* **United States v. Cruz**, 981 F.2d 659, 663–64 (2d Cir. 1992).

The error was not harmless. An evidentiary error warrants a new trial when it is likely to have affected the verdict. *See* **Crigger v. Fahnestock & Co.**, 443 F.3d 230, 235 (2d Cir. 2006). Credibility and probable cause were the central disputed issues. Because the admitted testimony went directly to both, there is at least a substantial likelihood that it tipped the balance. *See* **Tesser v. Board of Education of City School District of New York**, 370 F.3d 314, 319–20 (2d Cir. 2004). A new trial is required.

#### II. The district court properly denied judgment as a matter of law because the photographs did not so blatantly contradict Appellees’ version as to permit only one reasonable conclusion.

A court may disregard testimony only where objective evidence “blatantly contradict[s]” it so that no reasonable jury could believe it. *See* **Scott v. Harris**, 550 U.S. 372, 380–81 (2007). That demanding standard is not met merely because photographs or video support one side’s case better than the other’s. *See* **Higgins v. Metro-North Railroad Co.**, 318 F.3d 422, 425–26 (2d Cir. 2003). Rather, judgment as a matter of law is appropriate only if the record permits but one rational outcome. *See* **Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.**, 477 U.S. 242, 250–52 (1986).

Here, the security-camera photographs did not conclusively establish that Cameron never reached into the police car or that Higgenbottom never interfered with the officers. At most, they created an evidentiary conflict for the jury. Under *Scott*, courts may not replace a jury’s role unless the visual evidence utterly discredits the challenged account. 550 U.S. at 380–81. The district court therefore correctly denied Appellants’ motion.

#### III. The jury should have been instructed that obstructing governmental administration requires an underlying lawful act.

New York Penal Law § 195.05 requires interference with an officer engaged in the performance of an “official function,” and the New York Court of Appeals has held that the police action obstructed must itself be lawful. *See* **People v. Case**, 42 N.Y.2d 98, 101–02 (1977); **People v. Lupinacci**, 191 A.D.2d 589, 590, 595 N.Y.S.2d 76 (2d Dep’t 1993). Where a proposed instruction accurately states the law and is supported by the evidence, refusal to give it is reversible error. *See* **Anderson v. Branen**, 17 F.3d 552, 556–57 (2d Cir. 1994).

Because Higgenbottom’s arrest was predicated on obstructing an allegedly unlawful arrest of Cameron, the jury needed to know that if the officers lacked lawful authority to arrest Cameron, Higgenbottom could not be convicted of obstruction for her conduct in resisting that arrest. *Case*, 42 N.Y.2d at 101–02. The requested instruction was therefore necessary and should be given on remand.

#### IV. The district court should have instructed the jury on punitive damages.

Punitive damages are available under § 1983 when the defendant acted “motivated by evil motive or intent” or with “reckless or callous indifference” to federally protected rights. **Smith v. Wade**, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). A punitive-damages charge is warranted where the evidence would permit a reasonable jury to make that finding. *See* **King v. Macri**, 993 F.2d 294, 298–99 (2d Cir. 1993); **DiSorbo v. Hoy**, 343 F.3d 172, 186–87 (2d Cir. 2003).

Appellants’ evidence—that Ramos and Rivera fabricated the arrest narrative and pursued charges despite the absence of lawful grounds—would allow a finding of malicious or reckless misconduct. *See* **Zeno v. Pine Plains Central School District**, 702 F.3d 655, 671–72 (2d Cir. 2012). Accordingly, if this issue recurs, the jury should be instructed on punitive damages.

For these reasons, Appellants respectfully request vacatur and a new trial.
