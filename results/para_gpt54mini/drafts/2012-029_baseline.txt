**Argument**

The Texas Supreme Court erred in holding that federal jurisdiction is exclusive whenever a state-law malpractice claim requires the application of patent law. That rule is broader than Congress authorized and broader than the Supreme Court’s “arising under” cases permit. Federal jurisdiction over patent matters extends to state claims only when the embedded patent issue is “actually disputed and substantial” and when federal adjudication will not “disturb[] any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” **Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).** Under that standard, this case belongs in state court.

Section 1338(a) gives federal district courts exclusive jurisdiction only over civil actions “arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents.” **28 U.S.C. § 1338(a).** The Supreme Court has long held that a case does not arise under federal law merely because it “involves” a federal issue. **Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912).** Rather, the federal issue must be “really and substantially” in dispute, and “the result depends” on its resolution. **Id.** Likewise, in **Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936),** the Court warned that courts must not define arising-under jurisdiction so broadly that federal issues are pulled from “the web” of state claims whenever they appear incidentally.

In **Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800 (1988),** the Court explained that § 1338 reaches a state-law claim only if “a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” **Id. at 808-09.** That test is demanding. It focuses on the plaintiff’s claim, not on every federal issue that may arise in proving causation or damages. **Id. at 809.** And in **Grable**, the Court clarified that even where a federal issue is necessary and disputed, jurisdiction exists only if it is substantial and federal adjudication will not upset the state-federal balance. **545 U.S. at 314.**

Minton’s malpractice claim does not satisfy that standard. Texas malpractice law turns on duty, breach, causation, and damages. **Alexander v. Turtur & Associates, Inc., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004).** As the Texas courts recognize, the claim is a “case within a case.” **Schaeffer v. O’Brien, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied).** The patent issue here is only one piece of the causation inquiry: whether a timely experimental-use argument would have changed the result in the underlying infringement case. That sort of embedded federal question is not enough to create exclusive federal jurisdiction. See **Christianson, 486 U.S. at 809; Grable, 545 U.S. at 314.**

Nor is the patent issue “substantial” in the jurisdictional sense. The Supreme Court has made clear that substantiality asks whether the issue is important to the federal system as a whole, not merely to the parties. **Gunn v. Minton, 568 U.S. 251, 260 (2013).** A malpractice court’s resolution of whether an experimental-use defense would have succeeded would have no precedential effect on the validity of the patent, would not bind the PTO or future infringement suits, and would not alter the body of patent law. **Id. at 261-62.** At most, the case requires a backward-looking determination about how patent law would have been applied in one prior dispute. That is not the kind of substantial federal issue that justifies federal jurisdiction. **Id.; Grable, 545 U.S. at 313-14.**

Finally, exclusive federal jurisdiction would upset the congressionally approved division of labor between state and federal courts. Legal malpractice has traditionally been the province of the states. **Gunn, 568 U.S. at 264.** Extending exclusive patent jurisdiction to every malpractice case that requires a patent-law analysis would sweep countless state claims into federal court, including disputes over drafting, litigation strategy, settlement, and causation. The Supreme Court has repeatedly refused to read § 1338 so broadly. **Christianson, 486 U.S. at 819; Grable, 545 U.S. at 314.**

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach in **Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007),** cannot expand § 1338 beyond what the Supreme Court has approved. Federal jurisdiction cannot turn on whether patent law is “necessary” to one element of a state claim if the issue is not substantial in the federal sense and federal adjudication would distort the state-federal balance. **Gunn, 568 U.S. at 264.**

This Court should hold that federal courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction over a state malpractice action merely because it requires application of patent law. Under **Christianson**, **Grable**, and **Gunn**, this case belongs in Texas court.
