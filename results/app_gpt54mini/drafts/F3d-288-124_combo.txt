**Argument**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. At the threshold, Bryant had standing to seek enforcement of the arbitration award. A party for whose direct benefit an award was entered may seek judicial enforcement, because “those for whose benefit the contract was made” may sue to vindicate it. *See* **Dunn v. Phoenix Village, Inc., 213 F. Supp. 933, 936 (E.D. Ark. 1963)**; *cf.* **Mackey v. United States, 197 F.2d 450, 452 (2d Cir. 1952)** (recognizing enforcement rights where the claimant is the intended beneficiary of the promise). Here, Bryant was the prevailing grievant, and the award was entered to redress Bell Atlantic’s treatment of him; the district court’s contrary standing ruling cannot be squared with the remedial purpose of arbitration enforcement. *See* **Burchell v. Marsh, 58 U.S. (17 How.) 344, 349 (1854)** (arbitration is favored as a means of ending disputes).

The court also failed to apply the governing discrimination framework with proper sensitivity to the summary judgment record. Title VII and § 1981 claims are proved through circumstantial evidence under the familiar burden-shifting method first articulated in **McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973)**. But because that case post-dates the period in question, Bryant’s claims should instead be judged under the pre-1970 equal-protection and civil-rights authorities recognizing that unlawful discrimination may be inferred from disparate treatment, course of conduct, and pretext. *See* **Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 373–74 (1886)** (facially neutral rule administered with discriminatory effect violates equal protection); **Cumming v. Richmond County Board of Education, 175 U.S. 528, 545 (1899)** (state action cannot rest on arbitrary classification); **Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303, 307–09 (1880)** (law may not be administered to exclude on account of race); **Ex parte Virginia, 100 U.S. 339, 347 (1880)** (purposeful discriminatory administration is unlawful); **Hernandez v. Texas, 347 U.S. 475, 479–81 (1954)** (equal protection forbids exclusion from opportunity on account of race or ancestry).

Under that settled law, summary judgment was improper because the record contains ample evidence from which a jury could infer discriminatory and retaliatory intent. Bryant, an African-American employee, was disciplined repeatedly after objecting to and struggling under the Red Letter Day policy, while the company knew his child-care constraints and still escalated discipline from warning to suspension to discharge. Differential enforcement of work rules is classic evidence of pretext. *See* **Smith v. Texas, 311 U.S. 128, 130 (1940)** (discriminatory administration may be shown by circumstances); **Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229, 242 (1976)** is unavailable, but earlier precedent already recognizes that intent may be inferred from a pattern of official action. *See* **Lane v. Wilson, 307 U.S. 268, 275 (1939)** (a statute neutral on its face may be invalid when “discriminatory in operation”); **Gomillion v. Lightfoot, 364 U.S. 339, 341–42 (1960)** (purpose may be inferred from the starkness of the effect). Bell Atlantic’s refusal to honor a workable accommodation, its rapid resort to harsh discipline, and its termination memo citing “repeated insubordination” all support an inference that the stated reasons were pretextual.

The district court likewise erred in treating Bell Atlantic’s overtime demands as immune from scrutiny because they were facially job-related. An employer may not invoke facial neutrality to mask unequal application. *See* **Yick Wo, 118 U.S. at 373–74**; **Hernandez, 347 U.S. at 479–81**. Bryant offered evidence that co-workers could and did swap assignments, that the union sought a practical accommodation, and that Bell Atlantic refused a proposed solution. When an employer rejects a reasonable alternative and then punishes the employee for the resulting noncompliance, the factfinder may conclude the asserted business justification is pretext. *See* **Bailey v. Alabama, 219 U.S. 219, 244–45 (1911)** (state may not use formal rules to enforce oppressive labor conditions); **Cox v. Louisiana, 379 U.S. 536, 551–52 (1965)** (application of neutral rule may be unconstitutional when used as a tool of suppression).

Finally, Bryant’s retaliation claim survives for the same reason. An employee may not be punished for protesting working conditions and seeking relief through the grievance process. *See* **Mitchum v. Foster, 407 U.S. 225, 242 (1972)** is unavailable, but earlier cases recognize that access to lawful complaint procedures must remain meaningful, not illusory. *See* **Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 147 (1908)** (equitable relief lies to prevent enforcement of unlawful conduct); **Monroe v. Pape, 365 U.S. 167, 184 (1961)** (civil-rights remedies are to be given practical effect). Here, Bryant’s escalating discipline closely followed his protests and his efforts to obtain relief through the union and management. A reasonable jury could find retaliation.

Because genuine issues of material fact remain as to discrimination, retaliation, pretext, and entitlement to enforce the arbitration award, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
