# ARGUMENT

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This guaranty binds the States with the same force it binds the federal government, for it is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice." Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). Among its central protections, the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal" and against multiple punishments for the same offense. North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The California Supreme Court held that neither protection applies to a proceeding to determine a prior-conviction sentencing allegation in a noncapital case. That holding cannot be squared with this Court's double jeopardy precedents, which have never confined the Clause to the adjudication of guilt on the underlying charge.

## I. A Determination That the Evidence Is Insufficient to Prove an Enhancement Allegation Is the Equivalent of an Acquittal, and the Government May Not Relitigate It.

This Court has long held that a factfinder's resolution of a charge against the government is final and unreviewable through a second proceeding, whatever the merits of the ruling that produced it. In United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), the Court held that a verdict of acquittal was "final, and could not be reviewed, on error or otherwise, without putting the defendant twice in jeopardy, and thereby violating the Constitution." The Court applied that same principle decades later in Fong Foo v. United States, 369 U.S. 141 (1962), holding that a judgment of acquittal, once entered, bars any further proceeding on the same charge regardless of whether the trial court's ruling was legally correct. And in Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100 (1904), the Court held that the government may not obtain appellate correction of a finding that the evidence was insufficient to convict, because permitting such review would necessarily subject the defendant to a second jeopardy for the same charge.

The reason for this rule was explained in Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187-188 (1957): "the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity, as well as enhancing the possibility that even though innocent he may be found guilty." That rationale applies with undiminished force here. The Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Monge personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury. That finding is functionally indistinguishable from an acquittal, and under Ball, Fong Foo, and Kepner, the State is not entitled to a second opportunity to prove what it failed to prove the first time—merely because the allegation it failed to prove determined the length of Monge's sentence rather than his guilt on the substantive charge.

## II. The Prohibition on Multiple Punishments Independently Forecloses a Second Attempt to Enhance Monge's Sentence.

The Clause's protection against multiple punishments for the same offense traces to Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874), where the Court held that once a defendant has been subjected to a lawful punishment, the Constitution forbids a second, additional punishment for the same conduct. Nothing in that principle confines its protection to the substantive offense while leaving the enhancement finding unprotected. The enhancement determination here doubled Monge's sentence and added a further one-year term; it is not incidental to punishment—it is punishment. A rule allowing the State a second attempt to prove that finding, after a factfinder resolved it in Monge's favor, exposes him to precisely the successive jeopardy Ex parte Lange condemns.

This conclusion finds further support in Sealfon v. United States, 332 U.S. 575 (1948), where the Court held that the government may not relitigate an issue of fact already resolved against it in an earlier proceeding between the same parties. The prior-conviction allegation here was fully and fairly tried, with the State afforded a complete opportunity to prove it. Having lost, the State should not be permitted to try again.

## III. Because California Voluntarily Adopted Trial-Like Procedures for Prior-Conviction Proceedings, Those Proceedings Bear the Hallmarks That Trigger Double Jeopardy Protection.

While a State is not constitutionally compelled to provide a trial-type sentencing proceeding, Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 251 (1949), a State that elects to do so cannot disclaim the safeguards that accompany a trial once it has chosen to hold one. California requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt, application of the ordinary rules of evidence, and the right "to be confronted with witnesses against him [and] to cross-examine" them. Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967). Having structured its enhancement proceeding as an adversarial, evidence-based trial in every meaningful respect, California cannot now insist the proceeding is something less than a trial for double jeopardy purposes. This Court's admonition in Williams—that "[t]he due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure," 337 U.S. at 251—cuts against the State here: having voluntarily adopted trial procedure, California must accept the constitutional consequences of that choice.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
