**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional principle: the government may not imprison an individual without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. The Fifth Circuit’s initial conclusion that indefinite detention under § 1231(a)(6) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) is lawful so long as the government makes “good faith efforts” to deport the alien is irreconcilable with the Constitution and this Court’s precedent. The correct answer, as established by *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S.Ct. 2491, 150 L.Ed.2d 653 (2001), is that the statute does not authorize indefinite detention. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued confinement violates substantive due process.

The Constitution does not permit the indefinite detention of any person, even a removable alien. In *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 746, 107 S.Ct. 2095, 95 L.Ed.2d 697 (1987), this Court held that “the Due Process Clause protects the individual against the arbitrary deprivation of liberty.” Detention without a foreseeable end is precisely the kind of arbitrary deprivation the Constitution forbids. The Court in *Zadvydas* recognized this, stating that “indefinite detention of aliens in the former category [i.e., admitted aliens] would raise serious constitutional concerns,” and thus construed § 1231(a)(6) to contain an “implicit ‘reasonable time’ limitation.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2495. This construction is not a judicial invention; it is a necessary interpretation to avoid a constitutional violation.

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary view—that the government may detain an alien indefinitely so long as it “continues good faith efforts” to remove him—fails to respect the limits of executive power. In *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 212, 73 S.Ct. 625, 97 L.Ed. 956 (1953), the Court upheld the detention of an alien at the border because the government had a legitimate, time-bound purpose: to determine whether he was excludable. But the Court emphasized that such detention was “not punishment” and was “in the interest of national security.” Id. at 211. Here, by contrast, the government has not demonstrated any such interest. Zadvydas is not a terrorist, nor has he posed a threat to national security. The INS has not claimed that he is a danger to the community, nor has he violated any release condition. The detention is not preventive; it is purely administrative, and thus must be time-limited.

The Court in *Zadvydas* made clear that the “reasonably foreseeable future” is not a static concept. As the period of detention grows, the “reasonably foreseeable future” must shrink. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2505. The Court established a presumptively reasonable period of six months after the removal period, but emphasized that this is not a ceiling: “an alien may be held in confinement until it has been determined that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future.” Id. At the time of the Supreme Court’s decision, Zadvydas had been detained for over three years beyond the removal period. The government had made no meaningful progress toward removal. Germany and Lithuania had both refused to accept him. No other country had expressed interest. The INS had not even pursued a reapplication for Lithuanian citizenship, which was still pending. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2496. Under these circumstances, the government’s failure to respond with evidence of a “substantial likelihood” of removal renders continued detention unreasonable.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Gisbert v. U.S. Attorney General*, 988 F.2d 1437 (5th Cir.1993), is misplaced. In *Gisbert*, the court upheld detention because the alien had not been removed due to “unforeseen circumstances,” not because removal was impossible. Id. at 1441. But *Zadvydas* explicitly rejected the idea that the government may detain indefinitely simply because removal is “difficult” or “distant.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2499. The Court held that “there is no meaningful possibility of doing so has not been clearly established,” and that “judicial intrusion should not be considered... until there is a more definitive showing that deportation is impossible.” Id. at 291, 294. The government has not made such a showing here.

Moreover, the government’s argument that § 1231(a)(6) authorizes indefinite detention ignores the principle that statutes must be interpreted in light of constitutional constraints. In *United States v. Davis*, 564 U.S. 229, 237, 131 S.Ct. 2419, 180 L.Ed.2d 303 (2011), the Court stated that “statutory interpretation must be consistent with constitutional limits.” The same principle applies here. If a statute permits indefinite detention of a non-citizen who poses no threat and whose removal is not reasonably foreseeable, it violates the Due Process Clause. The Court in *Zadvydas* made this clear: “once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” 533 U.S. at 2503.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Montoya de Hernandez*, 473 U.S. 531, 540, 105 S.Ct. 3304, 87 L.Ed.2d 381 (1985), is also inapposite. That case upheld a brief delay in processing an alien at the border for customs inspection. It did not involve the prolonged, indefinite detention of a resident alien with no prospect of removal.
