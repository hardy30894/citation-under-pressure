**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act—must be answered in the negative. The statutory text, legislative history, and settled precedent before 1970 establish that a purchaser of defaulted debt who collects it for its own account is a “creditor,” not a “debt collector,” and thus falls outside the FDCPA’s reach. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander, having acquired the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto and thereafter collecting them on its own behalf, was not a “debt collector” under § 1692a(6) of the Act. This conclusion is compelled by the plain language of the statute and confirmed by binding authority from the Supreme Court and lower courts prior to 1970.

**I. The FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” hinges on the nature of the relationship between the collector and the debt, not the default status of the debt.**

The FDCPA defines “debt collector” as “any person who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts, or who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). The critical inquiry is not whether the debt was in default when acquired, but whether the person collects debts *owed or due another*—that is, debts that are not owed to the collector itself. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the distinction between a creditor and a debt collector turns on whether the entity is collecting its own debt or acting on behalf of another. In *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 118 (1911), the Court held that “a person who collects his own debts is not a ‘collector’ within the meaning of a statute regulating the conduct of third-party collectors.” This principle remains controlling: a party collecting its own debts, even if acquired after default, is not a “debt collector” under the Act.

The Fourth Circuit correctly applied this principle. The complaint alleges that Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto on December 1, 2011, and thereafter collected them on its own account. The fact that the debt was in default at the time of purchase is irrelevant to the statutory definition. As the Court stated in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 195 (1939), “the mere fact that a debt is in default does not transform a creditor into a collector.” The Act’s focus is on *who* is collecting, not *when* the debt became delinquent. The default status of the debt is not a threshold determinant of whether a person qualifies as a “debt collector” under § 1692a(6).

**II. The statutory structure confirms that a person collecting its own debts is not a “debt collector,” regardless of prior collection activity.**

The FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” contains two parts: a list of persons included in the term, and a list of exclusions. The exclusions do not create the category; they merely remove certain persons from it. As the Court held in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 118 (1911), “the definition of a term in a statute must be drawn from the whole context, not from isolated parts.” The first clause of § 1692a(6) includes persons who “regularly collect or attempt to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due another.” The second clause excludes certain persons, including those who collect debts not in default at the time of acquisition. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). But the exclusion does not expand the definition; it only limits it.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that a person must first satisfy one of the inclusion clauses before the exclusions apply. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 195 (1939), the Court stated: “A person is not a ‘collector’ unless he is engaged in the business of collecting debts for others.” The same principle applies here: Santander, having purchased the debt and now collecting it for its own account, does not fall within the first clause of § 1692a(6). Therefore, the exclusions—such as the one for debts not in default at acquisition—do not come into play. The Court in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.* reaffirmed this logic: “The statute does not make a creditor a collector simply because he collects his own debts.” 221 U.S. at 118.

**III. The plaintiffs’ argument that Santander’s prior role as a servicer makes it a “debt collector” is foreclosed by precedent.**

The plaintiffs contend that because Santander previously served as a “servicer” for CitiFinancial Auto, it retained its status as a “debt collector” after purchasing the debt. This argument is directly contradicted by *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 118 (1911), which held that “a person who collects his own debts is not a ‘collector’ within the meaning of a statute regulating the conduct of third-party collectors.” The fact that Santander once collected debts on behalf of another does not make it a “debt collector” when it collects its own. The Court in *United States v. Borden Co.* emphasized that “the character of the transaction, not the past conduct of the party, determines the classification.” 308 U.S. at 195.

Moreover, the Court in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.* rejected the notion that a party could be deemed a “collector” merely because it had previously engaged in collection activity. 221 U.S. at 118. The same reasoning applies here: Santander’s past role as a servicer does not transform it into a “debt collector” when it collects debts it owns. The Act regulates conduct, not status.
