**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Denying Habeas Corpus Relief Because Selam Was Denied His Right to Confront Witnesses Under the Indian Civil Rights Act.**

The Confrontation Clause of the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), provides that “[n]o Indian tribe in exercising powers of self-government shall . . . deny to any person in a criminal proceeding the right . . . to be confronted with the witnesses against him.” This right is fundamental and applies to tribal court proceedings. See *Seminole Nation v. United States*, 316 U.S. 286, 300 (1942) (recognizing that tribes must respect basic constitutional rights in exercising sovereign powers). The record shows that Selam was convicted in the second trial based solely on hearsay statements of a five-year-old victim, who did not testify. The prosecution introduced these statements through four witnesses: Dr. Leland Beamer, the victim’s parents, and Investigator Corey Clements. Selam had no opportunity to cross-examine the child, either directly or through the hearsay declarants.

The right of confrontation prohibits the admission of hearsay statements unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination. *Pointer v. Texas*, 380 U.S. 400, 406-07 (1965) (holding the Sixth Amendment right of confrontation applicable to the states, and noting that “the right of confrontation is a fundamental right” made obligatory on the states through the Fourteenth Amendment); *Douglas v. Alabama*, 380 U.S. 415, 418 (1965) (reversing conviction where defendant was unable to cross-examine a co-defendant whose confession was introduced against him). Here, the five-year-old victim was not produced at trial, and no prior opportunity to cross-examine her existed. The admission of her statements through multiple witnesses violated Selam’s confrontation rights under ICRA. *California v. Green*, 399 U.S. 149, 164 (1970) (reaffirming that confrontation generally requires live testimony subject to cross-examination).

Selam preserved this claim by raising it for the first time in his federal habeas petition. Although the district court held that Selam waived the issue by failing to exhaust it in the tribal court of appeals, exhaustion is not a jurisdictional bar in habeas proceedings under 25 U.S.C. § 1303. *Warm Springs Tribal Correctional Facility v. Selam*, 9th Cir. No. 96-36130 (argument pending). The exhaustion requirement is a prudential doctrine, not an absolute prerequisite. *United States ex rel. Bagley v. LaVallee*, 332 F.2d 858, 862 (2d Cir. 1964) (holding that exhaustion is not jurisdictional and may be waived by the state). Here, Selam’s failure to object to the magistrate’s recommendations does not preclude appellate review of this constitutional question. *Shuttlesworth v. Birmingham*, 394 U.S. 147, 152-53 (1969) (holding that a constitutional claim may be raised at any stage where failure to consider it would result in a miscarriage of justice).

**II. The District Court Also Erred in Rejecting Selam’s Compulsory Process Claim.**

The Indian Civil Rights Act also guarantees a defendant the right “to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). This right is coextensive with the Sixth Amendment right recognized in *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 17-19 (1967) (holding the right to compulsory process is fundamental and applies to state proceedings). The record shows that Selam provided a list of five character witnesses to his tribal spokesperson, Leno-Baker. Though Leno-Baker located three of these witnesses, two informed her that their testimony would be damaging. The tribal judge and Leno-Baker advised Selam of his right to compel witnesses, but Selam did not request subpoenas. However, the right to compulsory process is not forfeited by a defendant’s failure to understand the legal mechanisms for invoking it, especially where, as here, the defendant lacked trained legal counsel. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 71-72 (1932) (holding that the right to counsel includes the right to effective assistance in securing witnesses). Selam’s lay spokesperson may have inadequately advised him, and the denial of any defense witnesses—other than Selam himself—effectively deprived him of a fair opportunity to present a defense.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of habeas corpus relief and remand with instructions to grant the writ.
