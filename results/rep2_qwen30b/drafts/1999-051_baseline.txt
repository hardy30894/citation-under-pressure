**ARGUMENT**

The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, which held that a state prisoner’s claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may serve as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of a separate habeas claim challenging the sufficiency of the evidence—provided that the ineffective-assistance claim itself is not procedurally defaulted and is meritorious. The Court has long recognized that “cause” for a procedural default must be “something external to the petitioner” that “prevents him from complying with the State’s procedural rule.” Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). The Sixth Circuit correctly concluded that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel satisfies this standard when it is both timely and substantively valid.

Here, Robert Carpenter’s claim that his appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective for failing to raise a sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge on direct appeal is not itself procedurally defaulted. The claim was raised in a timely application to reopen his direct appeal in the Ohio Court of Appeals, and the state courts’ dismissal of that application on procedural grounds—based on timeliness—does not bar federal habeas review of the underlying constitutional claim. See Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977) (“[T]he procedural default of a claim does not bar federal habeas review if the petitioner can show cause for the default and actual prejudice therefrom.”). The Sixth Circuit properly applied this framework, recognizing that the ineffective-assistance claim was not defaulted and thus could serve as cause.

The Court has repeatedly held that ineffective assistance of counsel constitutes “cause” under the habeas corpus statute. In Murray v. Carrier, the Court stated that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may constitute cause for a procedural default.” 477 U.S. at 488. This principle applies with particular force when the defaulted claim is itself a constitutional violation—here, the denial of a fair trial due to the failure to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence. The Sixth Circuit correctly found that Carpenter’s appellate counsel failed to raise a claim that was both legally sound and potentially dispositive. The evidence at trial, including Carpenter’s own admissions and the presence of drugs and firearms in his residence, supported a conviction, but the sufficiency of that evidence was not beyond reasonable dispute. See Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 318 (1979) (“The relevant question is whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.”). A rational jury could have found Carpenter guilty, but the failure to challenge this issue on appeal deprived him of a full and fair opportunity to contest the conviction.

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit properly found prejudice under Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984). The Court in Strickland held that a defendant must show both that counsel’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced the defense. 466 U.S. at 687. Here, the record shows that the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was strong: Carpenter had no prior criminal record, the drugs were found in his home, and he admitted ownership of the firearm but denied knowledge of the cocaine. The failure to raise this claim on appeal deprived him of a meaningful appellate review. The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that prejudice was established is supported by the record and consistent with precedent. See United States v. Cronic, 466 U.S. 648, 659 (1984) (where counsel fails to raise a meritorious claim, prejudice is presumed when the claim is “obvious” or “strong”).

The respondent does not dispute that the ineffective-assistance claim is meritorious. Rather, the argument centers on whether such a claim can excuse a procedural default. The Court has never held that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot serve as cause simply because it is raised in a habeas petition. On the contrary, the Court has emphasized that “the cause requirement is not a mere technicality” but a “safeguard against the harsh consequences of procedural defaults.” Murray, 477 U.S. at 488. To hold otherwise would allow procedural rules to insulate constitutional violations from federal review—precisely what the habeas corpus statute was designed to prevent.

Finally, the Court has consistently rejected the notion that a petitioner must exhaust every possible state remedy before seeking federal relief. See Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722, 752 (1991) (“[T]he exhaustion requirement is not absolute.”). The Sixth Circuit did not err in finding that Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was not procedurally defaulted and thus could serve as cause. The state courts’ dismissal of the application to reopen the direct appeal was based on a procedural bar, not on the merits of the claim. That the claim was raised in state court—albeit unsuccessfully—demonstrates that it was not abandoned or forfeited.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The Sixth Circuit correctly applied the law of this Court, and its conclusion that ineffective assistance of appellate counsel constitutes “cause” to excuse a procedural default is sound and consistent with precedent.

**WHEREFORE**, the respondent respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit.
