## ARGUMENT

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (“FDCPA”) regulates “debt collectors,” not creditors collecting their own debts. 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). The Act defines a “debt collector” as any person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due *another*.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). A “creditor,” by contrast, is “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). The critical distinction is whether the entity collects debts for itself or for others. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (quoting § 1692a(6)). When Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto, it became the owner of those debts. It then collected on its own behalf, not for another. Santander was therefore acting as a creditor, not a debt collector, and the FDCPA does not apply.

### I. The Plain Language of the FDCPA Confirms That a Debt Purchaser Collecting Its Own Debts Is Not a “Debt Collector”

The FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” has two parts: an inclusionary clause and a series of exclusions. The inclusionary clause defines a debt collector as a person whose “principal purpose” is debt collection or who “regularly collects . . . debts owed or due *another*.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). The phrase “owed or due another” unambiguously refers to debts that are owed to a third party at the time of collection. *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1318 (11th Cir. 2015) (“[O]ur inquiry under § 1692a(6) is . . . whether [the defendant] regularly collects on debts owed or due another *at the time of collection*.”); *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (“The statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs’] interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another.’”).

Petitioners argue that the default status of a debt at acquisition determines whether the purchaser is a debt collector. But the statute says nothing of the sort. The default status appears only in an *exclusion* from the definition of debt collector—subsection (F)(iii)—which provides that a person collecting debts “owed or due another” is *not* a debt collector if the debt “was not in default at the time it was obtained.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). That exclusion applies only to persons who already satisfy the inclusionary clause—i.e., those who collect debts for another. *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1314 (“[W]here a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a ‘debt collector’ as a matter of course.”). Petitioners invert the statute by treating the exclusion as a definitional element. The default status of a debt is irrelevant unless the entity first qualifies as a debt collector by collecting for another.

### II. Santander Collected Its Own Debts, Not Debts Owed to Another

The complaint alleges that on December 1, 2011, CitiFinancial Auto sold the petitioners’ defaulted loans to Santander. After that date, Santander owned the debts. It then attempted to collect on its own behalf. The complaint does not allege that Santander collected these debts for CitiFinancial Auto or any other third party. To the contrary, it alleges that Santander purchased the loans “for a few cents on the dollar” and thereafter sought to recover more than it paid. That is the classic activity of a creditor collecting its own debt.

Petitioners’ reliance on the definition of “creditor” is misplaced. Section 1692a(4) excludes from the definition of creditor “any person to the extent that he receives an assignment or transfer of a debt in default solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) (emphasis added). That exclusion applies only when the debt is acquired “solely for the purpose of facilitating collection *for another*.” Santander did not acquire the debts to collect for CitiFinancial Auto; it acquired them for its own account. The exclusion therefore does not apply, and Santander remains a creditor.

### III. Petitioners’ Interpretation Would Create an Unworkable Loophole

Under petitioners’ theory, any entity that purchases defaulted debt becomes a debt collector subject to the FDCPA, regardless of whether it collects for itself or for others. That interpretation would sweep in every debt purchaser, including banks, finance companies, and other creditors that buy portfolios of defaulted loans. Congress did not intend such a result. The FDCPA’s purpose is to “eliminate abusive debt collection practices by *debt collectors*,” not by creditors. 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e) (emphasis added). As the Fourth Circuit correctly observed, “a creditor, when it collects its own debt for its own account, is not generally acting as a debt collector.” *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA, Inc.*, 817 F.3d 131, 136 (4th Cir. 2016). Petitioners’ interpretation would also create an anomaly: a company that both collects for others and collects its own debts would be regulated by the FDCPA even when acting as a creditor. Congress did not intend that result. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (defining debt collector by the nature of the collection activity, not the entity’s overall business).

### IV. The Fourth Circuit’s Decision Is Consistent with the Weight of Authority

The Fourth Circuit’s holding aligns with the plain text and with decisions of other circuits. *See Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1315-16; *Schlegel*, 720 F.3d at 1209. Petitioners rely on cases such as *Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB*, 681 F.3d 355 (6th Cir. 2012), and *Ruth v. Triumph P’ships*, 577 F.3d 790 (7th Cir. 2009), but those decisions conflate the inclusionary and exclusionary provisions of § 1692a(6). The Fourth Circuit correctly rejected that approach. The default status of a debt is relevant only to the exclusion in subsection (F)(iii), which presupposes that the entity is already a debt collector because it collects for another. Because Santander collected its own debts, it never qualified as a debt collector in the first instance.

### CONCLUSION

The FDCPA regulates third-party debt collectors, not creditors collecting their own debts. Santander purchased the petitioners’ loans and collected on its own behalf. It was therefore a creditor, not a debt collector. The judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
