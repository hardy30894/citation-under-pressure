**ARGUMENT**

The government bears the unshifting burden of proving every element of a charged conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the statute-of-limitations period. This bedrock principle, rooted in the presumption of innocence, compels reversal of petitioners' convictions.

**I. The Government Must Prove Membership Within the Limitations Period Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

A conspiracy is a continuing offense. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 607–08 (1910). To convict, the government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and that the defendant was a party to it during that time. *United States v. Cohen*, 145 F.2d 82, 89 (2d Cir. 1944); *United States v. Liss*, 105 F.2d 144, 146 (2d Cir. 1939). This requirement flows from the fundamental principle that no person may be deprived of liberty except upon proof of every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895); *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895); *United States v. Gooding*, 25 U.S. 460, 475 (1827).

The government's burden is not satisfied merely by showing that a conspiracy once existed. Rather, the government must prove—by evidence that permits a rational inference—that the defendant's membership continued into the limitations period. *Kissel*, 218 U.S. at 607–08. When the government's own evidence shows that the conspiracy's leaders split up before the limitations period began, that evidence undermines any inference of continuity and creates a reasonable doubt as to petitioners' membership during the relevant period.

**II. Withdrawal Before the Limitations Period Does Not Shift the Burden to the Defendant**

This Court has recognized that a defendant may withdraw from a conspiracy by affirmative acts. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 370–71 (1912). Critically, *Hyde* placed the burden of proving withdrawal on the defendant *only* after the government had already established membership within the limitations period. *Id.* at 369. *Hyde* does not—and cannot—relieve the government of its initial burden to prove membership during the limitations period when the defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal before that period.

To hold otherwise would impermissibly shift the burden of persuasion on an essential element of the offense. This Court has consistently rejected such burden-shifting. *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943) (a criminal statute must not "undercut the requirement that the prosecution must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt"); *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 274–75 (1952) (the government must prove "an essential ingredient of the offense" and "no presumption can infect that proof").

When a defendant introduces evidence that he withdrew from a conspiracy before the limitations period, that evidence is relevant to whether the government has carried its burden on the element of membership during the limitations period. The jury must be instructed that the government bears the burden of proving membership within that period beyond a reasonable doubt, and that evidence of withdrawal—even if insufficient to establish an affirmative defense—may be considered in determining whether the government has met that burden. *United States v. Mennuti*, 239 F.2d 747, 750 (2d Cir. 1957) ("The burden is on the Government to prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy within the period of the statute of limitations.").

**III. The Lower Court's Error Was Not Harmless**

The lower court denied petitioners' motions for a new trial, effectively requiring petitioners to disprove their membership during the limitations period. This error cannot be harmless. The government's evidence showed that the alleged leaders of the conspiracy split up before the limitations period began. A properly instructed jury could have credited that evidence and found that the government failed to prove petitioners' membership within the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt. The erroneous instruction—or the absence of any instruction placing the burden on the government—deprived petitioners of their right to have every element of the offense proved beyond a reasonable doubt. *Davis*, 160 U.S. at 488; *Coffin*, 156 U.S. at 453.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the cause remanded for a new trial with proper instructions on the burden of persuasion.
