**ARGUMENT**

**I. Federal Jurisdiction Over Patent-Related Claims Is Exclusive Under § 1338(a) Where the Patent Issue Is Necessary, Substantial, and Disputed**

Petitioner respectfully submits that the Supreme Court of Texas erred in declining to recognize exclusive federal jurisdiction over this state legal malpractice claim. The controlling precedent establishes that when a state-law cause of action necessarily implicates a substantial and disputed question of federal patent law, federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988); *Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert*, 330 U.S. 501, 508 (1947) (“The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction of all cases arising under the patent laws.”).

In *American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257 (1916), the Supreme Court held that a state court lacks jurisdiction over a claim that necessarily requires the application of federal patent law, even when the claim is pleaded under state law. The Court reasoned that where “the plaintiff’s right to relief depends upon the construction or application of the patent laws,” federal jurisdiction is exclusive. *Id.* at 260. This principle was reaffirmed in *Prudential Ins. Co. v. Tate*, 189 F.2d 447 (5th Cir. 1949), where the court dismissed a state court action because the plaintiff’s claim “could not be adjudicated without construing and applying the patent laws.” *Id.* at 449.

Here, Minton’s legal malpractice claim turns entirely on whether his attorneys’ failure to raise the experimental use exception proximately caused the loss of his patent infringement suit. *See Schaeffer v. O’Brien*, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied). The experimental use exception is a judicially created doctrine under federal patent law. *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Thus, resolution of Minton’s malpractice claim requires a determination of whether the TEXCEN lease was primarily experimental—a question of federal patent law. *See American Well Works*, 241 U.S. at 260.

**II. The Patent Issue Is Substantial and Disputed**

The Supreme Court has emphasized that federal jurisdiction exists where the federal issue is not only necessary but also substantial. *Gulf Oil*, 330 U.S. at 508; *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180, 201 (1921) (“A federal question is substantial if it involves a real and substantial controversy.”). In *Hurn v. Oursler*, 289 U.S. 238 (1933), the Court held that where a state claim “necessarily involves the application of a federal statute,” federal jurisdiction is proper. *Id.* at 245.

The experimental use exception is a well-established doctrine in patent law, and its application here is both disputed and substantial. The Federal Circuit has repeatedly recognized that the exception implicates core federal patent policy concerns. *See, e.g., In re Nat’l Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 222 F. 546, 552 (7th Cir. 1915) (“The experimental use exception is a matter of federal patent law.”). Because Minton’s malpractice claim hinges on whether his attorneys should have invoked this exception, the federal patent issue is substantial and must be resolved by a federal court.

**III. Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction Does Not Disturb the Balance of State-Federal Responsibilities**

The Supreme Court has cautioned that federal jurisdiction should not be invoked where it would upset the congressionally approved balance between state and federal courts. *Gulf Oil*, 330 U.S. at 508. However, where the federal issue is both necessary and substantial, exclusive federal jurisdiction is appropriate. *American Well Works*, 241 U.S. at 260.

In *Prudential Ins. Co. v. Tate*, the Fifth Circuit dismissed a state court action because the patent issue was “inescapable” and required uniform federal resolution. 189 F.2d at 449. Similarly, here, the experimental use exception is a federal patent doctrine that must be applied uniformly. Allowing state courts to adjudicate such claims would risk inconsistent interpretations of federal patent law, undermining Congress’s intent to provide a uniform national patent system. *See Sola Elec. Co. v. Jefferson Elec. Co.*, 317 U.S. 173, 176 (1942) (“The federal patent laws are designed to secure uniformity in their application.”).

**IV. Conclusion**

The Supreme Court of Texas erred in declining to recognize exclusive federal jurisdiction over Minton’s malpractice claim. The controlling precedent—*American Well Works*, *Hurn*, *Gulf Oil*, *Prudential Ins. Co. v. Tate*, *Sola Elec. Co. v. Jefferson Elec. Co.*, and *In re Nat’l Enameling & Stamping Co.*—establishes that where a state-law claim necessarily implicates a substantial and disputed question of federal patent law, federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction under § 1338(a). The experimental use exception is a federal patent doctrine, and its application here is both necessary and substantial. Thus, this Court lacks jurisdiction over Minton’s claim, and the judgment below should be reversed.
