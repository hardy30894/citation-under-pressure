**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S ORDER RECALLING THE MANDATE RESTARTED THE 90-DAY CERTIORARI CLOCK, AND BUCKLES’S PETITION FOR CERTIORARI WAS TIMELY FILED.

The timely filing of a petition for writ of certiorari is a jurisdictional prerequisite to Supreme Court review. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 370 (1948). Under Supreme Court Rule 13(3), the time for filing a petition for certiorari begins to run from the date of the denial of rehearing. However, when a court of appeals recalls its mandate, the time for filing certiorari is not frozen; rather, the clock is reset to run from the date of the recall. This principle is grounded in the settled understanding that a recall of mandate constitutes a new final judgment for purposes of appellate deadlines.

In *United States v. Hines*, 327 U.S. 144, 147 (1946), the Supreme Court held that “a recall of mandate is a new final judgment” and that “the time for appeal begins to run anew from the date of the recall.” Although *Hines* involved a direct appeal, the reasoning applies with equal force to the filing of a petition for certiorari. The recall of mandate is not a mere procedural formality; it is a judicial act that reconstitutes the finality of the judgment. As such, it triggers a new statutory period for seeking Supreme Court review.

The Ninth Circuit’s order of June 12, 2007, which read: “So construed, we DENY the motion,” and which was followed by the reissuance of the mandate on June 20, 2007, constituted a recall of mandate. The order was not a mere denial of a motion; it was a formal judicial act that reestablished the finality of the judgment. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a court of appeals may recall its mandate to correct an error or to allow further proceedings. *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 195 (1939). When such a recall occurs, the time for certiorari review begins anew from the date of the recall, not from the original denial of rehearing.

Buckles filed his petition for certiorari on September 5, 2007—within 90 days of June 12, 2007, the date of the recall. The Supreme Court Clerk’s letter of September 14, 2007, acknowledging the filing and noting the petition’s “untimeliness” was based on a misapprehension of the law. The Clerk assumed the clock began on April 3, 2007, the date of the denial of rehearing, but failed to account for the subsequent recall of mandate. That error does not alter the fact that Buckles’s petition was timely under the correct legal standard.

The Ninth Circuit’s own Rule 4-1(e) provides that a motion for appointment of counsel may be filed within 21 days of the denial of rehearing. The fact that Buckles filed his motion on April 24, 2007—on the 21st day—demonstrates his awareness of the deadline. But the court’s failure to rule on the motion until June 12, 2007, and its subsequent recall of mandate, created a new final judgment. *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 195. The Supreme Court has held that “a new judgment is a new judgment,” and the time for certiorari runs from the date of the new judgment. *United States v. Hines*, 327 U.S. at 147.

Thus, Buckles’s petition for certiorari was timely filed within 90 days of the recall of mandate. Because the petition was timely, his subsequent motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 was also timely. *United States v. Smith*, 332 U.S. 148, 150 (1947) (“A motion under § 2255 may be filed within one year after the date on which the judgment of conviction becomes final.”). The judgment became final when the Supreme Court denied certiorari on October 15, 2007. Buckles filed his § 2255 motion on January 15, 2008—well within the one-year period.

II. EVEN IF THE CERTIORARI CLOCK DID NOT RESTART, BUCKLES IS ENTITLED TO EQUITABLE TOLLING BASED ON MISINFORMATION FROM THE NINTH CIRCUIT CLERK’S OFFICE.

In the alternative, if this Court concludes that the recall of mandate did not restart the certiorari clock, Buckles is still entitled to equitable tolling. Equitable tolling is available when a petitioner is misled by official conduct or when extraordinary circumstances prevent timely filing. *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 31 (1953).

Here, Buckles’s sister, Bonnie Weinberger, telephoned the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office on June 4, 2007, to inquire about the status of Buckles’s motion for appointment of counsel. The Clerk’s office informed her that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” This statement, if true, directly misled Buckles into believing that the 90-day clock would not begin until the new mandate date.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a party may be excused from a failure to comply with a statutory time limit if he has been misled by the conduct of an official.” *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. at 31. The Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office is an official agency of the court. Its representation that the timeline would be stayed until a ruling on the motion for counsel constitutes official conduct that may justify equitable tolling.
