The Ninth Circuit correctly held that an uncounseled tribal court conviction cannot be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) because it would violate the Sixth Amendment's guarantee of a right to counsel if it occurred in state or federal court. The use of such convictions as an essential element of a federal felony prosecution undermines the reliability concerns that underpin the Sixth Amendment.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a right to counsel in state and federal prosecutions where a sentence of imprisonment is imposed, Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367, 373-74 (1979). Although the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, see Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008), Congress has recognized the importance of ensuring due process in tribal court proceedings. The Indian Civil Rights Act ("ICRA") provides some, but not all, of the protections afforded by the Bill of Rights, including a right to counsel when due process so dictates. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)-(c).

The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir.1989), established that prior tribal court misdemeanor convictions that do not provide an equivalent right to counsel cannot be used as evidence of guilt in a subsequent federal prosecution. The court in Bryant reaffirmed this holding, concluding that Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), does not permit the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a).

Nichols is distinguishable from the present case because it involved the use of a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction for sentencing purposes, not as an essential element of a federal offense. The Court in Nichols emphasized the reliability concerns that inhere in the Sixth Amendment right to counsel and noted that the sentencing scheme at issue "accommodated" these concerns. Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747-48. In contrast, using an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) raises significant reliability concerns because it is being used to establish an essential element of a federal felony prosecution.

Furthermore, the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) creates a circuit split. The Eighth and Tenth Circuits have held that such convictions can be used under § 117(a), see United States v. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d 592 (8th Cir.2011); United States v. Shavanaux, 647 F.3d 993 (10th Cir.2011), while the Ninth Circuit has held that they cannot.

The Ninth Circuit's decision is consistent with the principles of comity and respect for tribal sovereignty. By conditioning the use of prior tribal court misdemeanor convictions on the provision of counsel, the court is not disparaging tribal courts or undermining their authority. Rather, it is ensuring that the use of such convictions in federal prosecutions comports with the reliability concerns that underpin the Sixth Amendment.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit correctly held that an uncounseled tribal court conviction cannot be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The use of such convictions as an essential element of a federal felony prosecution raises significant reliability concerns and creates a circuit split. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
