**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT’S TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL.**

The Constitution vests “[a]ll legislative Powers herein granted . . . in a Congress of the United States.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. While this “non-delegation doctrine” bars Congress from transferring its core legislative authority, it does not prohibit Congress from “obtain[ing] assistance from coordinate branches” so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegatee’s exercise of authority. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 371–72 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)).

Here, § 811(h) of the Controlled Substances Act (CSA) satisfies this standard. It authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance as a Schedule I drug only if he finds that doing so is necessary to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). The statute further requires the Attorney General to consider specific factors—including the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of such abuse, and the risk to public health—before exercising this authority. *Id.* § 811(h)(3). These provisions supply the “intelligible principle” demanded by the Court. *See Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372–73 (upholding delegation where Congress specified goals, factors, and limits).

The Toubys’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved statutes that granted “unfettered” discretion without any statutory standards. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415; *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 542. By contrast, § 811(h) imposes meaningful constraints on the Attorney General’s discretion. *See Touby v. United States*, 909 F.2d 712, 717 (3d Cir. 1990) (upholding delegation under § 811(h) as consistent with *Mistretta*).

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL LAWFULLY DELEGATED HIS TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA ADMINISTRATOR.**

The CSA explicitly authorizes the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions” under the Act. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). Pursuant to this authority, the Attorney General delegated his scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, a delegation upheld in *United States v. Moore*, 486 F.2d 1139, 1144 (D.C. Cir. 1973). The DEA Administrator’s subsequent exercise of this delegated authority was therefore valid.

The Toubys argue that the Attorney General’s subdelegation was improper because § 811(h) does not expressly mention subdelegation. But the Supreme Court has long recognized that “delegation includes subdelegation unless expressly prohibited.” *See United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911); *see also Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372 (upholding subdelegation where Congress authorized the Attorney General to “establish” the Sentencing Commission). Section 871(a) contains no such prohibition, and the Attorney General’s subdelegation was consistent with the CSA’s structure.

**III. THE STATUTORY BAR ON JUDICIAL REVIEW OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING ORDERS DOES NOT PRECLUDE CONSTITUTIONAL REVIEW.**

Section 811(h)(6) provides that a temporary scheduling order “is not subject to judicial review.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6). However, the Supreme Court has held that “when constitutional questions are at issue, the availability of judicial review is presumed,” and courts will not read a statute to foreclose review unless Congress’s intent is “manifested by clear and convincing evidence.” *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). The legislative history of § 811(h) contains no such evidence, and every court to consider the issue has concluded that constitutional challenges to the delegation are not barred. *See, e.g., United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1429 (10th Cir. 1987). The Third Circuit correctly followed this precedent in affirming the Toubys’ convictions. *Touby*, 909 F.2d at 718.

**CONCLUSION**

The CSA’s temporary scheduling provision does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power, and the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator was lawful. The judgment below should be affirmed.
