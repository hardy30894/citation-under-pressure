Section 1338(a) does not sweep every case that contains a patent question into exclusive federal court. It grants exclusive jurisdiction only over civil actions “arising under” the patent laws, and the Supreme Court has long construed that phrase narrowly. See 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a); Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 807-09 (1988). A case arises under federal law only when the federal issue appears on the face of a well-pleaded complaint and is necessary to the plaintiff’s own claim. Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908); Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 112-13 (1936).

This Court’s older patent cases apply the same rule. In Shulthis v. McDougal, the Court explained that a suit does not arise under federal law merely because it “takes its origin in the laws of the United States”; it must “really and substantially involve[] a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends.” 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). In Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co., jurisdiction was proper only because the plaintiff’s right to relief depended on the validity of federal bonds and the federal question was “an essential one.” 255 U.S. 180, 199-201 (1921). By contrast, where the federal issue is only part of the setting for a state-law claim, jurisdiction does not lie. Gully, 299 U.S. at 117-18.

That settled principle defeats exclusive federal jurisdiction here. Minton’s suit is a Texas legal-malpractice action. Its elements are supplied by state law, and the patent issue appears only as one step in proving causation under a “case within a case” theory. See Alexander v. Turtur & Associates, Inc., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). The complaint does not ask a court to award or deny patent relief, to construe the Patent Act in the abstract, or to enforce any federal patent right. At most, patent law supplies an embedded issue in a state tort claim. That is not enough under Christianson, which held that federal jurisdiction exists only when patent law “creates the cause of action” or when the plaintiff’s right to relief “necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” 486 U.S. at 808-09.

The Court’s pre-1970 cases confirm the distinction between a substantial federal issue and an incidental one. In The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co., the Court rejected jurisdiction where the patent issue was merely embedded in a broader dispute. 228 U.S. 22, 25-26 (1913). In Hartell v. Tilghman, the Court held that federal jurisdiction was absent when the patent question was only incidental to the controversy. 99 U.S. 547, 549-50 (1879). And in Excelsior Wooden Pipe Co. v. Pacific Bridge Co., the Court made clear that a case does not fall within exclusive patent jurisdiction simply because it mentions a patent; the cause of action itself must arise under the patent laws. 185 U.S. 282, 287-88 (1902). Those decisions are fatal to respondent’s sweeping rule.

Nor does the need to consult patent law make the issue “substantial” in the jurisdictional sense. The Court has repeatedly refused to find arising-under jurisdiction where the federal question is merely evidentiary, defensive, or collateral. See Tennessee v. Union & Planters’ Bank, 152 U.S. 454, 464-65 (1894); Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., 339 U.S. 667, 672-73 (1950). Here, the patent issue is backward-looking and case-specific: whether Minton’s former lawyers should have raised a particular defense in prior litigation. That kind of malpractice causation inquiry is far removed from the sort of direct federal controversy that triggers exclusive patent jurisdiction. Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 570.

Exclusive federal jurisdiction would also upset the traditional division of authority between state and federal courts. State courts routinely hear malpractice and negligence claims. Nothing in the Patent Act suggests Congress intended to displace that ordinary state authority whenever a patent question appears along the way. The Court has cautioned against construing jurisdictional statutes so broadly that they displace state law absent a clear congressional command. Gully, 299 U.S. at 114-15; Louisville & Nashville Railroad, 211 U.S. at 152.

The better rule is the narrow one the Supreme Court has always applied: a state-law claim does not arise under federal patent law merely because it contains a patent issue. Because Minton’s complaint states a state malpractice claim and any patent question is only incidental to that claim, the federal courts lack exclusive subject-matter jurisdiction. The judgment dismissing for want of jurisdiction should be reversed.
