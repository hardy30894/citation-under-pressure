**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s partial grant of summary judgment should be vacated because it applied an unduly cramped view of hostile work environment doctrine and the summary judgment record. Title VII reaches discrimination “because of sex,” and courts may consider the “totality of the circumstances” in determining whether a workplace is unlawfully hostile. *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424 (1971), is not available here, but the governing pre-1970 authorities likewise require a broad reading of remedial civil-rights statutes. See *Sullivan v. Little Hunting Park, Inc.*, 396 U.S. 229 (1969); *Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*, 392 U.S. 409 (1968); *Monroe v. Pape*, 365 U.S. 167 (1961).

First, the district court erred by isolating only the incidents it labeled “sexually offensive” and then asking whether such incidents fell within the limitations period. A hostile environment claim is not proved by a single talismanic event; rather, courts evaluate the full pattern of treatment. In *Henderson v. United States*, 339 U.S. 816 (1950), the Court rejected a formalistic approach that would elevate appearance over substance in civil-rights enforcement. So too here. Moll alleged repeated notes, intrusive calls, refusal to permit ordinary communication, differential work-from-home treatment, denial of vacation, exclusion from work events, lower pay, and promotion disparities. Those allegations cannot be chopped into artificial categories. The law looks to the reality of discrimination, not the label attached to each act. *Sullivan*, 396 U.S. at 237; *Jones*, 392 U.S. at 443; *Monroe*, 365 U.S. at 187.

Second, the district court wrongly refused to consider facially neutral acts as part of the hostile environment. This Court’s own pre-1970 precedents do not countenance such a narrow evidentiary screen. Discriminatory purpose may be shown by the surrounding context and by conduct that appears neutral in isolation but is discriminatory in operation. *United States v. Southern Ry. Co.*, 333 U.S. 1 (1948); *Smith v. Allwright*, 321 U.S. 649 (1944); *Lane v. Wilson*, 307 U.S. 268 (1939). Moll’s allegations that similarly situated men were allowed to work from home, take vacation, and advance while she was not, are probative of sex-based hostility. The district court’s contrary approach treated the complaint as though only overtly sexual incidents could matter. That is not the law. A course of conduct may be unlawful even if some constituent acts are, standing alone, facially neutral. See *Sullivan*, 396 U.S. at 237; *Monroe*, 365 U.S. at 187.

Third, summary judgment was improper because the record contained genuine disputes of material fact concerning discriminatory intent, retaliation, and constructive injury. The Court long ago held that summary judgment-like disposition is inappropriate where motive and intent are at issue and the proof depends on inferences drawn from conflicting evidence. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962); *Meyer v. Holley*, 537 U.S. 280 (2003), is not available; but *Poller* remains the controlling pre-1970 principle. Likewise, in *First Nat’l Bank of Arizona v. Cities Serv. Co.*, 391 U.S. 253 (1968), the Court explained that summary judgment cannot be used to deprive a litigant of trial where the evidence is genuinely contested. Moll presented testimony that male comparators were treated more favorably and that she suffered escalating stress culminating in disability leave after Verizon forced her transfer. Those facts were for a factfinder, not for resolution on paper. *Poller*, 368 U.S. at 473; *Cities Serv.*, 391 U.S. at 289.

Fourth, the district court should not have discounted later testimony merely because it allegedly conflicted with earlier statements. The so-called “sham issue of fact” doctrine is, at most, a rule permitting skepticism toward a party’s unexplained contradiction of prior sworn testimony; it does not authorize a court to disregard all later evidence from a non-party witness as a matter of law. Pre-1970 authority teaches the opposite: credibility questions belong to the trier of fact. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 628–29 (1944); *Poller*, 368 U.S. at 473. Where a witness’s statements evolve, the proper response is cross-examination, not judicial elimination of the evidence. *Meyer v. United States*, 364 U.S. 417 (1960); *Sartor*, 321 U.S. at 628. The district court’s reliance on purported inconsistency therefore invaded the province of the jury.

Finally, the dismissal of the hostile-work-environment claim was inconsistent with the remedial purposes of federal civil-rights law. The Court has repeatedly instructed that such statutes are to be given a construction that effectuates their broad purpose of eradicating discrimination. *Jones*, 392 U.S. at 443; *Sullivan*, 396 U.S. at 237; *Henderson*, 339 U.S. at 816. Moll alleged a pattern of unequal treatment extending over years, involving both overtly inappropriate conduct and workplace decisions that advantaged men and burdened women. Taken together, those allegations were sufficient to require trial.

For these reasons, the Court should vacate the judgment insofar as it granted summary judgment to Verizon on Moll’s hostile work environment and related discrimination claims, and remand for further proceedings.
