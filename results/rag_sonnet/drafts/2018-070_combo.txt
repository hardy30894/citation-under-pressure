**ARGUMENT**

**I. OCSLA borrows state law only as a gap-filler for a federal enclave, not as a freestanding source of concurrent regulation.**

Congress did not annex the Outer Continental Shelf to the States; it declared that federal law governs there "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (quoting 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1)). Only "[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws" are the "civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State" borrowed as "the law of the United States" for fixed structures on the Shelf. *Id.* This is the classic technique for filling interstices in a body of exclusively federal law governing a federal enclave—not a general savings clause preserving concurrent state regulation.

This Court's application of that provision in *Rodrigue* confirms the point. The Court adopted Louisiana's wrongful-death remedy as surrogate federal law only after concluding that no federal remedy—maritime or otherwise—covered deaths occurring on fixed platforms, since the accidents "involved no collision with a vessel, and the structures were not navigational aids." *Id.* at 360. Once the Death on the High Seas Act was found inapplicable, "any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]" was removed. *Id.* at 366. The necessary premise of that holding is that state law is incorporated *because* federal law is silent, not merely because state law happens to touch the same subject matter. Similarly, in *Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co.*, 375 U.S. 34 (1963), the Court recognized that OCSLA carefully extends specific federal remedial schemes—there, the Longshoremen's and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act—while leaving that scheme "explicitly inapplicable" to categories of workers already covered by other federal law, *id.* at 34, illustrating that OCSLA's design is to allocate coverage among federal schemes first, resorting to state law only where federal coverage runs out.

**II. The Fair Labor Standards Act comprehensively occupies the field of wage-and-hour regulation for drilling employees, leaving no gap for California law to fill.**

The FLSA is not a skeletal statute awaiting supplementation; it is, and was from its inception, a detailed, comprehensive regulatory regime specifically addressing the wage practices of drilling employees like respondent. In *Warren-Bradshaw Drilling Co. v. Hall*, 317 U.S. 88 (1942), this Court held that rotary drilling employees "engaged in the production of goods for commerce" fall squarely within the FLSA's coverage and overtime requirements. *Id.* at 88. The FLSA's overtime and minimum-wage mandates were built for exactly the kind of irregular, multi-shift, on-call employment at issue here. This Court has repeatedly worked through the statute's application to fluctuating and guaranteed-wage arrangements, demonstrating the Act's exhaustive reach. In *Overnight Motor Transportation Co. v. Missel*, 316 U.S. 572 (1942), the Court construed the Act's liquidated-damages and overtime provisions as establishing a complete remedial mechanism for miscalculated wages. In *Walling v. A. H. Belo Corp.*, 316 U.S. 624 (1942), the Court parsed a guaranteed-wage contract to determine the "regular rate" for overtime purposes under the Act's formula, and in *Walling v. Halliburton Oil Well Cementing Co.*, 331 U.S. 17 (1947)—a case involving oil-field employees whose "usual workweek" fluctuated much like respondent's—the Court applied that same comprehensive Belo framework to irregular-hours drilling-adjacent work. *Id.* at 17.

The Court's decisions in *149 Madison Avenue Corp. v. Asselta*, 331 U.S. 199 (1947), and *United States v. Townsley*, 323 U.S. 557 (1945), further confirm that Congress and this Court have worked out, in painstaking detail, the methods for calculating regular and overtime rates, scheduled-hour formulas, and compensation for irregular schedules—precisely the subject matter of respondent's meal-period and overtime claims. And in *Walling v. Helmerich & Payne, Inc.*, 323 U.S. 37 (1944), and *Walling v. Youngerman-Reynolds Hardwood Co.*, 325 U.S. 419 (1945), the Court policed employer efforts to evade the Act's wage-computation rules through split-day and piece-rate plans, underscoring that the FLSA was designed as a self-contained, nationally uniform code governing exactly these wage-and-hour questions, not a floor to be freely supplemented by fifty different state regimes.

**III. Because the FLSA leaves no void, OCSLA affords no room for California law here.**

Given this comprehensive federal scheme, there is no "significant void or gap" of the kind that justified borrowing state law in *Rodrigue*. The Ninth Circuit's rule—that state law applies whenever it is not affirmatively inconsistent with federal law—would convert OCSLA's narrow enclave-based incorporation mechanism into a broad concurrent-regulation regime, contrary to the enclave rationale this Court applied in *Rodrigue* and the comprehensive federal wage code Congress enacted in the FLSA. The judgment below should be reversed.
