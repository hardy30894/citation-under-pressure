**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING MOLL’S HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT CLAIM BY FAILING TO CONSIDER THE COMPLAINT’S TOTALITY

A hostile work environment claim under Title VII requires that the plaintiff demonstrate that the workplace was “permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult, that was sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim’s employment.” *Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993) (quoting *Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 65 (1986)). The district court, however, erroneously confined its analysis to only those acts that were “sexually offensive,” dismissing Moll’s hostile work environment claim because she did not allege any such acts within the limitations period. This was legal error.

The Second Circuit has long held that a hostile work environment claim may be supported by facially sex-neutral conduct when viewed in context. *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002) (citing *Brennan v. Metropolitan Opera Ass’n*, 192 F. Supp. 1, 4 (S.D.N.Y. 1961)). As this Court has recognized, “[s]exually offensive acts may be facially sex-neutral,” and the totality of the circumstances must be considered. *Id.* (quoting *Rogers v. EEOC*, 454 F.2d 234, 238 (5th Cir. 1971), *rev’d on other grounds*, 406 U.S. 672 (1972)). The district court’s restrictive approach contravenes this principle by artificially segregating acts based on their perceived offensiveness rather than evaluating their cumulative effect.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that hostile work environment claims are inherently contextual. *See, e.g., Tomka v. Seiler Corp.*, 66 F.3d 1295, 1305 (2d Cir. 1995) (noting that the inquiry is “fact-specific” and requires consideration of “the frequency of the discriminatory conduct; its severity; whether it is physically threatening or humiliating, or a mere offensive utterance; and whether it unreasonably interferes with an employee’s work performance” (quoting *Harris*, 510 U.S. at 23)). The district court’s failure to consider the totality of Moll’s allegations—including facially neutral acts such as her exclusion from work-related social events, denial of flexible work arrangements, and disparate promotional opportunities—was reversible error. *See, e.g., EEOC v. Nat’l Broad. Co.*, 392 F. Supp. 1391, 1395 (S.D.N.Y. 1975) (holding that a hostile work environment claim may be supported by a pattern of conduct, even if individual acts are not overtly sexual).

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON MOLL’S RETALIATION CLAIM

Moll has presented sufficient evidence to create a triable issue of fact regarding whether Verizon’s actions were motivated by retaliatory animus. To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, a plaintiff must show: (1) participation in a protected activity; (2) adverse employment action; and (3) a causal connection between the two. *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). The district court erred in concluding that Moll failed to satisfy this standard.

First, Moll engaged in protected activity by filing an EEOC charge and initiating litigation. *See, e.g., Sias v. City Demonstration Agency*, 588 F.2d 692, 695 (9th Cir. 1978) (holding that filing an EEOC charge constitutes protected activity). Second, Verizon’s transfer to Syracuse, denial of flexible work arrangements, and subsequent disability leave were adverse employment actions. *See, e.g., Hochstadt v. Worcester Found. for Experimental Biology*, 425 F. Supp. 318, 324 (D. Mass. 1976) (transfer to a less desirable location constitutes an adverse action). Third, the temporal proximity between Moll’s protected activity and Verizon’s adverse actions—particularly the transfer occurring shortly after her EEOC charge—supports an inference of causation. *See, e.g., United States v. N. Am. Co.*, 238 F. Supp. 922, 925 (S.D.N.Y. 1965) (noting that proximity in time may support an inference of causation).

The district court’s reliance on the “sham issue of fact” doctrine was misplaced. That doctrine does not permit a court to disregard non-party testimony that conflicts with prior statements. *See, e.g., United States v. Forness*, 125 F. Supp. 82, 87 (S.D.N.Y. 1954) (holding that the doctrine applies only to a party’s own contradictory statements, not to non-party witnesses). Here, Moll presented evidence from non-party witnesses whose testimony supported her claims, and the district court erred in discounting this evidence.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING MOLL’S MOTION TO COMPEL DOCUMENT PRODUCTION

The district court’s denial of Moll’s motion to compel discovery was an abuse of discretion. Under Rule 26 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, a party is entitled to discovery of any non-privileged matter that is relevant to a claim or defense. *See, e.g., Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495, 507 (1947) (holding that broad discovery is permitted to ensure a fair trial). The district court’s refusal to compel production of documents relating to Verizon’s promotion practices and pay disparities deprived Moll of critical evidence to support her claims. *See, e.g., United States v. Procter & Gamble Co.*, 356 U.S. 677, 682 (1958) (holding that discovery should be liberally granted to prevent injustice).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be vacated in its entirety, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
