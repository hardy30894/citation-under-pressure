# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of § 523(a)(2)(A) Excepts From Discharge the Entire Debt Traceable to Fraud, Not Merely the Value Originally Obtained

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." The phrase "to the extent obtained by" modifies the character of the transaction giving rise to the debt—identifying fraud as the source of the obligation—not the quantum of recovery once liability is established. Congress chose the word "debt," which the Code defines as "liability on a claim," 11 U.S.C. § 101(12), and a "claim" includes any "right to payment," 11 U.S.C. § 101(5). Where Congress uses different terms in related provisions, this Court presumes the choice is deliberate. Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983) ("[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another ... it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely"). Congress could have limited the exception to "the money, property, or services obtained by fraud"; instead it excepted the "debt," a term of art embracing the full liability—including treble damages, fees, and costs—that flows from the fraudulent transaction.

This Court's construction of the neighboring "actual fraud" language confirms that Congress intended § 523(a)(2)(A) to be given its ordinary common-law meaning. Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. 59, 69 (1995) (looking to "the general common law of torts" and the Restatement to construe the elements of actual fraud under this very subsection). At common law, a defrauded creditor recovers not merely the value handed over, but all damages proximately caused by the fraud, including consequential and punitive damages. There is no textual basis for truncating that recovery once it reaches the threshold of nondischargeability.

## II. Pre-Code Practice, Which Congress Is Presumed to Have Retained, Excepted the Entire Fraud-Based Judgment From Discharge

This Court has repeatedly cautioned that when Congress reenacts bankruptcy provisions, it "does not write on a 'clean slate'"; established pre-Code interpretations survive unless Congress manifests a clear intent to depart from them. Dewsnup v. Timm, 502 U.S. 410, 419 (1992) (citing Congress's practice of "adopt[ing] the interpretation placed on the ... Act" before recodification). Under the 1867 Act, this Court held that a debt founded on "fraud" was not dischargeable in its entirety once the debtor's conduct met the requisite standard of moral culpability. Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877) (construing the term "fraud" in the discharge-exception provision to require conduct involving "moral turpitude or intentional wrong," and treating the resulting debt, not merely a fraction of it, as nondischargeable). Nothing in the 1978 recodification or subsequent amendments to § 523(a)(2)(A) signals any intent to abandon that understanding and instead discharge the enhanced portion of a judgment attributable to the debtor's own fraud.

## III. The Fresh-Start Policy Protects Only the Honest Debtor, Not One Who Has Defrauded His Creditors

The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions serve the salutary purpose of giving debtors "a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). But this Court has made clear that this "opportunity for a completely unencumbered new beginning" is reserved for the "honest but unfortunate debtor," Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286–87 (1991), and that Congress intended § 523(a) to ensure that "the interests of ... victims of fraud" outweigh "a defrauding debtor's interest in a complete discharge." Id. at 287. Likewise, in Brown v. Felsen, 442 U.S. 127, 138 (1979), the Court recognized that Congress designed the discharge exceptions to prevent debtors from using the bankruptcy forum to escape "the consequences of ... fraud," and permitted courts to look behind prior judgments to vindicate that purpose. Allowing Cohen to discharge the treble-damages judgment—an amount made possible only because he defrauded tenants who were particularly vulnerable to exploitation—would reward exactly the kind of debtor Grogan and Local Loan excluded from the fresh-start policy.

## IV. The Eleventh Circuit's Reasoning in St. Laurent, Not the Ninth Circuit's Contrary View in Levy, Reflects the Correct Understanding of "Debt"

The courts of appeals have divided on this question. The Ninth Circuit held that only the compensatory portion of a fraud judgment is nondischargeable, reasoning that punitive damages are not "obtained by" fraud. In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196, 199 (9th Cir. 1991). The Eleventh Circuit rejected that approach, holding that the statutory term "debt" is broad enough to encompass the entire judgment—compensatory and punitive—because the whole liability originates in the debtor's fraud and because permitting discharge of the punitive component would reward the dishonest debtor with exactly the "fresh start" reserved for the honest one. In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672, 679–80 (11th Cir. 1993) (citing Grogan, 498 U.S. at 286–87). St. Laurent's reading harmonizes the text, the pre-Code history recognized in Neal v. Clark, and the equitable purposes identified in Local Loan and Grogan. Levy's atomized approach, by contrast, would fracture a single fraud-based judgment into dischargeable and nondischargeable pieces with no textual warrant.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
