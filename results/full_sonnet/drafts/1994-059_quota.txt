**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Qualifications Clauses Are Exclusive and Foreclose Any State-Imposed Term Limit on Members of Congress.**

Article I prescribes, with specificity, the age, citizenship, and residency qualifications for Representatives and Senators. U.S. Const. art. I, §2, cl. 2; §3, cl. 3. This Court has already held that those enumerated qualifications are the only ones a member of Congress must meet, because the Framers deliberately rejected proposals allowing future alteration of the qualifications standard. In Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court traced the Convention debates and, quoting Alexander Hamilton's assurance in The Federalist, explained that the qualifications for Congress "are defined and fixed in the Constitution, and are unalterable by the legislature." Powell, 395 U.S. at 539. If Congress itself may not supplement the constitutional qualifications by ordinary legislation, a single State surely may not do so by amending its own constitution. Section 3 of Amendment 73 does precisely what Powell forbids: it imposes a durational-service disqualification found nowhere in Article I.

**II. Denominating the Disqualification "Ballot Access" Does Not Rescue It.**

Petitioners characterize Section 3 as a permissible exercise of the States' Article I, Section 4 power over the "Times, Places and Manner" of elections rather than an added qualification. This Court's ballot-access cases foreclose that characterization. In Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724 (1974), the Court recognized that States may adopt "reasonable regulations of parties, elections, and ballots" to prevent electoral disorder, but that authority has never extended to excluding candidates who satisfy every constitutional prerequisite for office. A permanent, absolute bar to appearing on the ballot is not a regulation of election mechanics; it is a qualification in substance, regardless of the label chosen by its drafters. This Court has likewise treated restrictions that categorically exclude candidates as substantive burdens subject to heightened scrutiny, not routine time-place-manner rules. Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983). Amendment 73's Section 3 permanently forecloses ballot access to any Senator or Representative who has served the specified number of terms; it does not regulate *how* an election is conducted, it dictates *who* may run.

**III. Permitting Arkansas to Add a Qualification Would Undermine the National Character of the Federal Legislature.**

The Framers created a national legislature answerable to a single constitutional standard so that no individual State could dilute or distort its composition. In Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1 (1964), this Court emphasized that the House of Representatives was designed to be chosen "by the People" of the whole nation under one constitutional standard, not fifty divergent ones. Similarly, in United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), the Court recognized that the right to choose members of Congress is a right "established and protected by the Constitution," not one that varies by state enactment. Allowing Arkansas to append a disqualification for congressional service would recreate the very patchwork of state-specific qualifications the Framers sought to avoid, and would offend the principle of McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), that a State may not "retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control" the operations of the constitutional processes that structure the federal government. A state-created barrier to congressional service is precisely such an impediment.

**IV. If Section 3 Falls, the Remainder of Amendment 73 Falls With It.**

Even apart from the qualifications question, the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Sections 1 and 2 cannot be severed from the invalid Section 3. Under Arkansas law, "the test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion." Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). Here, the ballot title and preamble presented state and federal term limits as a single, undifferentiated package applicable to "elected officials," denying voters any "straightforward, up-or-down vote" on state term limits alone. As in Hoban v. Hall, 229 Ark. 416, 316 S.W.2d 185 (1958), where this Court declined to let a severability clause salvage a measure that coupled a legally infirm provision with a popular one, a severability clause cannot rescue a measure the voters were never permitted to consider piecemeal.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court invalidating Section 3 of Amendment 73—and, derivatively, Amendment 73 in its entirety—should be affirmed.
