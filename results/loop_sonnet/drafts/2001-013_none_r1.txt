# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 4(b)(1) Displaces OSHA Only Where Another Agency Has Actually Exercised Regulatory Authority Over the Working Conditions at Issue

Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA's jurisdiction only for "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies … exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards." Congress's use of the active verb "exercise" demands more than the theoretical existence of regulatory power; it requires that the power actually have been brought to bear on the specific working conditions in question. This Court has consistently measured federal displacement of an otherwise applicable regulatory scheme by what an agency has actually done, not by what it might someday do. See Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (displacement of a coextensive regulatory scheme requires "the clear and manifest purpose of Congress").

The Coast Guard's regulations governing uninspected vessels—covering fire extinguishers, life preservers, backfire arrestors, and signaling equipment, 46 U.S.C. § 4102—address navigational and life-saving equipment. They say nothing about the occupational hazards of oil and gas exploration, including the well-control and blowout-prevention procedures whose absence caused the explosion here. Because the Coast Guard has never exercised authority over these particular working conditions, Section 4(b)(1) does not apply, and OSHA's citation rests on solid statutory footing.

## II. The Comprehensive Field-Occupation Doctrine of Napier Does Not Extend to Minimal, Unexercised Coast Guard Authority

Petitioner's reliance on a field-occupation theory analogous to Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co., 272 U.S. 605 (1926), is misplaced. In Napier, the Court found state regulation of locomotive equipment displaced because the Interstate Commerce Commission had promulgated a pervasive, detailed set of rules addressing the very equipment at issue, leaving no room for state supplementation. No comparable comprehensive exercise of authority exists here. The Coast Guard's uninspected-vessel regulations are narrow and equipment-specific; they leave untouched the drilling and well-control practices that caused the deaths and injuries in this case. Absent the kind of pervasive regulatory occupation present in Napier, there is no basis to infer that Congress intended silent, largely unexercised Coast Guard authority to oust OSHA from an entire field of occupational hazards the Coast Guard has never addressed.

## III. Concurrent Regulation of Vessel Safety Is Permissible Absent Actual Conflict

This Court has repeatedly tolerated overlapping regulation of vessel-related safety by different regulators where no genuine conflict exists between the schemes. In Kelly v. Washington ex rel. Foss Co., 302 U.S. 1 (1937), the Court upheld a state's regulation of tug-and-barge towing operations notwithstanding federal inspection laws, holding that Congress had not manifested an intent to exclude such regulation merely because federal authority touched the same general subject. Similarly, in Huron Portland Cement Co. v. City of Detroit, 362 U.S. 440 (1960), the Court sustained a municipal smoke-abatement ordinance as applied to federally licensed vessels, explaining that regulation addressing a "legitimate local concern," id. at 445, survives absent an actual, operative federal rule addressing the identical conduct. The same principle governs here: the Coast Guard's failure to regulate drilling-related occupational hazards leaves ample room for OSHA's complementary regulation without producing the kind of conflict Congress sought to avoid in Section 4(b)(1).

## IV. OSHA's Remedial Purpose and the Secretary's Reasoned Interpretation Support Coverage of the MR. BELDON

The Act is remedial legislation, and this Court has long construed such statutes to serve their protective purposes rather than to create artificial gaps in coverage. See NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 129 (1944) (construing "employee" broadly where a narrow construction would frustrate the statute's remedial design). Where statutory language is plain, moreover, courts need not resort to policy considerations to narrow its reach. United States v. American Trucking Assns., 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940). Section 4(a)'s broad reference to "employment performed in a workplace" plainly reaches a barge actively engaged in drilling operations on state waters and staffed by employees performing industrial tasks. The Secretary's interpretation of her own statute, reached through reasoned application of these principles to the facts, warrants substantial weight. See Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944) (an agency's interpretation merits deference commensurate with "the thoroughness evident in its consideration").

## V. The Workers' Actual Function, Not the Vessel's Documentation Status, Determines Coverage

To the extent petitioner suggests that its employees' mere presence aboard a vessel automatically triggers exclusive maritime regulation, this Court's seaman-status precedents establish that coverage turns on the function actually performed, not on the vessel's Coast Guard classification. See Desper v. Starved Rock Ferry Co., 342 U.S. 187, 190 (1952) (seaman status requires a substantial employment-related connection to a vessel "in navigation," not mere presence aboard); Norton v. Warner Co., 321 U.S. 565, 571–72 (1944) (assessing status by reference to the employee's actual duties). Employees engaged in oil and gas extraction from a stationary drilling barge perform industrial, not seafaring, functions. This confirms that the barge was a "workplace" under Section 4(a) and that the Coast Guard's unexercised authority over uninspected vessels does not divest OSHA of jurisdiction under Section 4(b)(1).

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
