**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed because the voluntary revision of Georgia Gwinnett College’s policies rendered this case moot, and a bare request for nominal damages cannot revive an otherwise moot constitutional challenge.

**I. The Rescission of the Challenged Policies Mooted All Claims for Prospective Relief**

A case becomes moot when the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. Here, GGC unambiguously terminated its prior campus speech policies—including a policy that had confined student expressive activity to two designated speech zones and a related student conduct provision—and replaced them with policies that permit expressive activity anywhere on campus without a permit. The district court concluded there was no reasonable basis to expect that GGC would return to its prior policies, and appellants do not contest that finding. In *Monroe v. Board of Commissioners*, 391 U.S. 450, 456 (1968), this Court held that a case was not moot because the challenged plan remained in effect. The corollary is that when the challenged policy is rescinded, the case becomes moot. See *Millsaps College v. City of Jackson*, 275 U.S. 129, 133 (1927) (dismissing as moot when the contested tax assessment was no longer at issue). Because appellants no longer face any threat of enforcement, their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot.

**II. Nominal Damages Cannot Sustain a Live Controversy**

Appellants’ sole remaining claim is for nominal damages. But nominal damages do not redress any ongoing injury; they merely symbolize a past constitutional violation. This Court has consistently required actual injury to support a damages award under § 1983. In *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254, 279 (1964), the Court held that a public official must prove “actual malice” to recover damages, emphasizing that the First Amendment requires safeguards for freedom of speech and of the press. Similarly, in *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130, 155 (1967), the Court applied the same standard, underscoring that damages must be based on concrete harm. Appellants alleged no actual injury—no monetary loss, no physical harm, no emotional distress. Their complaint mentions injury only twice without specification. A claim for nominal damages alone does not present a live case or controversy.

**III. The Absence of Ongoing Harm Distinguishes This Case**

In *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503, 514 (1969), this Court recognized that students have First Amendment rights, but the case was not moot because the students were still in school and faced ongoing restrictions. Here, Uzuegbunam has graduated, and Bradford no longer faces the challenged policies. The school’s good-faith revision eliminates any reasonable expectation of recurrence. See *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 108 (1943) (holding that “spreading one’s religious beliefs” is protected, but not addressing mootness). In *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 309 (1952), the Court dismissed a challenge to a released-time program that was no longer in effect, noting that the case was unlike the prior precedent. Here, too, the challenged policies are gone.

**IV. Courts Should Not Decide Abstract Questions**

This Court has long cautioned against deciding abstract questions. In *Board of Ed. of Central School Dist. v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236, 241 (1968), the Court addressed a textbook loan program that was still in effect; here, the policies are not. In *Jones v. Opelika*, 316 U.S. 584, 597 (1942), the Court upheld a licensing ordinance, but only after finding a live controversy. And in *Zwicker v. Boll*, 391 U.S. 353, 354 (1968), the Court affirmed dismissal of a challenge to a disorderly conduct statute where the underlying prosecution had ended. These cases confirm that federal courts lack jurisdiction to issue advisory opinions. Allowing nominal damages to salvage a moot case would invite precisely that.

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
