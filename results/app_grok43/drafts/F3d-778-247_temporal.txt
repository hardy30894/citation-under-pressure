ARGUMENT

I. The Warrant Failed to Establish a Nexus to the Searched Premises.

A warrant affidavit must supply facts showing that the items sought are probably located at the place to be searched. Nathanson v. United States, 290 U.S. 41, 47 (1933). The supporting affidavit here recited decades-old complaints, SJ’s account of events at the parents’ home, and the affiant’s training-based assertion that molesters keep records of victims. These statements supply no concrete link between the described evidence and the Manchester residence. See Johnson v. United States, 333 U.S. 10, 14 (1948) (affidavit must contain facts, not mere conclusions). The resulting warrant therefore violated the Fourth Amendment.

II. Admission of Uncharged Molestation Testimony Was Error.

Evidence of other crimes is inadmissible to prove the defendant’s propensity to commit the charged offense. Michelson v. United States, 335 U.S. 469, 475-476 (1948). The government introduced testimony concerning uncharged acts against multiple children. That testimony served only to show that Joubert had a character for molesting youths, precisely the use Michelson forbids. Its admission denied Joubert a fair trial.

III. The Commerce Clause Does Not Authorize These Convictions.

Congress’s power under the Commerce Clause extends only to activities that directly affect interstate commerce. United States v. E.C. Knight Co., 156 U.S. 1, 12 (1895); Carter v. Carter Coal Co., 298 U.S. 238, 307-308 (1936). The statutes applied here criminalize purely local production and possession of images without any required showing of interstate movement or commercial transaction. Because the conduct at issue lies outside the scope recognized in those decisions, the convictions rest on an unconstitutional exercise of federal power.

IV. The 480-Month Sentence Is Substantively Unreasonable.

A sentence must be proportionate to the offense and the offender’s circumstances. Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910). The district court imposed forty years’ imprisonment based largely on uncharged conduct and speculation about future risk. That sentence far exceeds any punishment justified by the four counts of conviction and therefore violates due process. Townsend v. Burke, 334 U.S. 736, 741 (1948).

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
