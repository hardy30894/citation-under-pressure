The Board’s polling standard is not rationally or statutorily compelled by the Act, and in any event the Board’s application of that standard here is not supported by substantial evidence. The Act “specifically” regulates neither employer polling nor the precise quantum of suspicion required to justify it. Allentown Mack Sales & Serv., Inc. v. NLRB, 522 U.S. 359, 377 (1998). The Board therefore retains discretion, but that discretion must be exercised consistently with the Act’s central objective of stable bargaining relationships and with reasoned decisionmaking. NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990); Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978).

I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” polling rule is inconsistent with the Act’s structure and with the Board’s own reasoning.

An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). The Board has long recognized that an employer may test that presumption through objective evidence and a lawful poll. Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967). Yet the Board’s rule here ties polling to the same “reasonable doubt” showing that permits withdrawal of recognition. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 369-70. That makes polling only “marginally useful,” because an employer already able to withdraw recognition gains little from polling. Id. at 378-79. The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits correctly observed that a poll must be available on a lesser showing than outright withdrawal if it is to serve any legitimate function. A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1144-45 (5th Cir. 1981); Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867-68 (6th Cir. 1982); Mingtree Rest., Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984).

The Board’s contrary position is especially anomalous because the Board itself favors Board-supervised elections over polling. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 377-78. If RM elections are preferred, it makes no sense to authorize an unsupervised poll only when the employer already possesses evidence enough to justify a more drastic act. Id. The Board may limit polling because it is “potentially disruptive and unsettling,” but it cannot preserve a standard that frustrates the very utility of polling while claiming to accommodate employer and employee interests. Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-63 (1989), remanded as modified, 923 F.2d 398 (5th Cir. 1991).

II. Even under the Board’s standard, substantial evidence does not support the finding that Allentown Mack lacked objective reasonable doubt.

This Court does not ask whether the Board’s view is plausible in the abstract; it asks whether the record contains “substantial evidence” to support the Board’s factual conclusion. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Here, the record does not.

Allentown Mack identified multiple employee statements indicating widespread dissatisfaction with the union. Some employees stated that the union was unnecessary under the new ownership; others said the union was not worth the dues. Such statements are probative of majority sentiment when assessed in context. NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 781-82 (1990). The Board discounted several statements as “equivocal” or as expressing mere dissatisfaction with representation, but it did so selectively and without a sound evidentiary basis. The D.C. Circuit itself recognized that the Board’s own precedent permits skepticism about interview statements and about secondhand reports of employee views. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 389-91. Yet when the record is viewed as a whole, the company had far more than a bare hunch: it had direct statements from employees, including a union steward, suggesting that the union would lose a vote. Id. at 371-76.

The Board’s treatment of the evidence also conflicts with the teaching of Universal Camera that reviewing courts must consider the record “as a whole,” including evidence that detracts from the agency’s conclusion. 340 U.S. at 488. Here, the undisputed poll result was 19 to 13 against the union after the company used stringent safeguards, including secret ballots and independent supervision. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 364-65. While the poll result alone cannot retroactively justify an unlawful poll, it confirms the reasonableness of the company’s pre-poll doubt and underscores the weakness of the Board’s contrary finding. Thomas Indus., 687 F.2d at 868.

III. The proper disposition is reversal.

Because the Board’s polling standard is not compelled by the Act and because the Board’s no-doubt finding lacks substantial evidentiary support, the order requiring recognition and bargaining should be denied enforcement. Fall River, 482 U.S. at 42; Beth Israel, 437 U.S. at 500-01; Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 378-79. The Court should hold that an employer may lawfully conduct a noncoercive poll on substantial, objective evidence of lost support, and that Allentown Mack met that standard here. A.W. Thompson, 651 F.2d at 1145; Mingtree, 736 F.2d at 1299; Thomas Indus., 687 F.2d at 867-68.
