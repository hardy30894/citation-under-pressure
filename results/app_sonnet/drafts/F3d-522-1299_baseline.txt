**ARGUMENT**

**I. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, drawing all reasonable inferences in favor of the non-movant. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) (summary judgment is improper where "the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party"). Whether a claim is entitled to the filing date of an earlier application under 35 U.S.C. § 120 turns on compliance with the written description requirement of § 112, which is a question of fact reviewed for clear error when tried, but which, on summary judgment, must be resolved in PowerOasis's favor if any genuine dispute exists. *Vas-Cath Inc. v. Mahurkar*, 935 F.2d 1555, 1563 (Fed. Cir. 1991) ("compliance with the 'written description' requirement is essentially a factual inquiry").

**II. The District Court Erred in Holding the Asserted Claims Not Entitled to the Original Application's 1997 Filing Date**

**A. The governing legal standard does not require the earlier application to use the identical claim language.**

To satisfy § 112 for priority purposes, "the applicant does not have to describe exactly the subject matter claimed," but the earlier disclosure "must clearly allow persons of ordinary skill in the art to recognize that [the applicant] invented what is claimed." *In re Gosteli*, 872 F.2d 1008, 1012 (Fed. Cir. 1989). The touchstone is whether the applicant "convey[ed] with reasonable clarity to those skilled in the art that, as of the filing date sought, he or she was in possession of the invention." *Vas-Cath*, 935 F.2d at 1563-64. A description is adequate "if it reasonably conveys to the artisan that the inventor had possession of the claimed subject matter as of the filing date." *Union Oil Co. of Cal. v. Atl. Richfield Co.*, 208 F.3d 989, 997 (Fed. Cir. 2000). Critically, the written description inquiry proceeds "on a limitation by limitation basis," so that new matter added elsewhere in a continuation-in-part does not automatically strip priority from every claim; a claim is entitled to the earlier date so long as *its own* limitations are supported in the original disclosure. *In re Chu*, 66 F.3d 292, 297 (Fed. Cir. 1995).

**B. The Original Application reasonably conveyed possession of a "customer interface" located on the customer's own device.**

The district court's error was one of framing: it treated the addition of expanded language in the 2000 CIP Application as dispositive of *every* limitation, rather than asking whether the specific "customer interface" limitation was independently supported in the 1997 Original Application. It was. The stated purpose of the invention from its inception was to allow "a customer to connect a laptop to a telecommunications channel" through a vending machine. A person of ordinary skill reading that disclosure would necessarily understand that the customer's own laptop screen and software—not merely a display bolted to the vending machine—served as the means by which "information [is] passed between a human user and hardware or software components of the system," the very definition the parties stipulated for "customer interface." Because the original specification disclosed a system whose entire operative function depended on the customer's laptop communicating with the vending machine, the Original Application "reasonably convey[ed] to the artisan that the inventor had possession" of a customer interface residing on that laptop. *Union Oil*, 208 F.3d at 997. As in *Vas-Cath*, the fact that the earlier application does not use the precise phrase later claimed is not fatal, because "the invention is, for purposes of the 'written description' inquiry, whatever is now claimed," and here what is claimed—a customer-side interface—was inherent in the 1997 disclosure of laptop-based access. 935 F.2d at 1565.

**C. At the very least, genuine issues of material fact precluded summary judgment.**

Even if this Court concludes the question is close, that is precisely why summary judgment was improper. Written description compliance is a factual question, *Vas-Cath*, 935 F.2d at 1563, and PowerOasis presented evidence—including the specification's consistent focus on laptop connectivity from the Original Application forward—sufficient to create a genuine dispute as to whether a skilled artisan would have understood the 1997 filing to disclose a laptop-resident customer interface. Under *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255, that dispute should have gone to trial rather than being resolved against PowerOasis as a matter of law. The district court's characterization of the 2000 CIP Application as containing "substantial new matter," *PowerOasis, Inc. v. T-Mobile USA, Inc.*, No. 05-cv-42-PB, 2007 WL 962937, at *2 (D.N.H. Mar. 30, 2007), addressed the specification as a whole, not the discrete "customer interface" limitation actually at issue, and thus does not resolve the limitation-specific inquiry *Chu* requires.

**III. Because the Asserted Claims Are Entitled to the 1997 Priority Date, the MobileStar Network Cannot Anticipate Them Under § 102(b)**

Anticipation under § 102(b) requires that the prior use, sale, or offer for sale occur more than one year before the *effective* filing date of the claims. Once the asserted claims are properly accorded the benefit of the Original Application's February 6, 1997 filing date under § 120, the MobileStar Network—admittedly developed and deployed no earlier than "prior to June 15, 1999"—cannot constitute prior art at all, because it postdates the 1997 priority date by more than two years. T-Mobile's entire anticipation defense collapses once the correct priority date is applied, and the grant of summary judgment cannot stand.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, PowerOasis respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment of invalidity and remand for further proceedings.
