The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding.

The Clause by its terms forbids a person from being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” for the “same offence.” U.S. Const. amend. V. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that sentencing differs fundamentally from the adjudication of guilt. In Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court upheld a sentence based on information received outside the trial record and explained that “the Due Process Clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” Id. at 251. That principle has never been limited to capital cases as a matter of ordinary sentencing law. See Witte v. United States, 515 U.S. 389, 398-99 (1995); United States v. Watts, 519 U.S. 148, 151-52 (1997) (per curiam).

Monge asks the Court to extend a narrow line of capital-sentencing cases into the noncapital recidivist context. But the Court has never held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies generally to noncapital sentencing enhancements. In Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), the Court allowed a harsher sentence on retrial after reversal of a first conviction, even though the earlier jury had returned a verdict without capital punishment. The Court treated sentencing consequences as distinct from the offense itself. Id. at 17-18. Likewise, in North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711 (1969), the Court addressed due process limits on vindictive resentencing, not double jeopardy limits on the ability to retry sentencing facts. Id. at 717-19. Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784 (1969), incorporated the Double Jeopardy Clause against the States, but did not suggest that every sentencing determination becomes an “offense” for double jeopardy purposes. Id. at 794-96.

The capital cases on which petitioner relies do not control here. In Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that a capital sentencing proceeding could, in a very limited setting, resemble a trial “on guilt or innocence” because Missouri required the State to prove aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt to a jury in a separate, bifurcated proceeding. Id. at 438-46. Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), followed Bullington for the same reason: the sentencer had made a factual determination akin to an acquittal of the death penalty. Id. at 209-11. But the Court later emphasized the narrowness of those decisions. In Poland v. Arizona, 476 U.S. 147 (1986), the Court stated that Bullington applies only when a sentencer has made findings that “amount to an acquittal” of the greater punishment. Id. at 154-57. And in Sattazahn v. Pennsylvania, 537 U.S. 101 (2003), the Court reiterated that double jeopardy concerns arise in capital sentencing only when the proceeding produces the functional equivalent of an acquittal. Id. at 109-10.

Those cases do not carry over to a noncapital prior-conviction allegation. The Court has never held that a recidivist enhancement hearing—especially one concerning a prior conviction already established in another proceeding—places the defendant in jeopardy for a new “offence.” See Monge v. California, 524 U.S. 721, 728-34 (1998). Rather, prior convictions are sentencing facts, not elements of the current offense. Almendarez-Torres v. United States, 523 U.S. 224, 243-47 (1998). And because the State may constitutionally forgo a trial on sentencing facts altogether, it follows that the Constitution does not require the additional double-jeopardy protection petitioner seeks. See Williams, 337 U.S. at 245-51; Witte, 515 U.S. at 398-99.

Nor does Burks v. United States, 437 U.S. 1 (1978), help petitioner. Burks forbids retrial after reversal for evidentiary insufficiency of a conviction on the merits. Id. at 16-18. But a prior-conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding is not a conviction of a separate offense. The Court has refused to extend Burks beyond the guilt phase in analogous contexts. See Tibbs v. Florida, 457 U.S. 31, 45-47 (1982). Here, the Court should preserve the settled distinction between trial and sentencing.

Because the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against repeated prosecutions for the same offense, not against a renewed effort to prove a noncapital sentencing enhancement, the judgment below should be affirmed. See Monge, 524 U.S. at 728-34; Bullington, 451 U.S. at 438-46; Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 209-11; Poland, 476 U.S. at 154-57; Williams, 337 U.S. at 245-51; Witte, 515 U.S. at 398-99; Watts, 519 U.S. at 151-52; Almendarez-Torres, 523 U.S. at 243-47.
