The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones, rather than limiting damages to the portion of profits attributable to the infringing design elements. The Patent Act requires that damages for design patent infringement be awarded based on the "total profits" gained by the infringer, but this provision must be interpreted in light of the statutory text and relevant precedent.

In this case, the infringed design patents applied only to specific components of Samsung's smartphones, such as the iPhone's graphical user interface. The district court allowed the jury to award damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones, rather than limiting damages to the portion of profits attributable to the infringing design elements. This was error.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that damages for patent infringement should be limited to the harm caused by the infringement. In Aro Manufacturing Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co., 365 U.S. 336 (1961), the Court held that damages for patent infringement should be calculated based on the "difference between [the infringer's] pecuniary condition after and before the infringement." Id. at 341 (quoting Yale Lock Mfg. Co. v. Sargent, 117 U.S. 536, 552 (1886)). This principle is consistent with the requirement that damages be awarded based on the "total profits" gained by the infringer, but only to the extent that those profits are attributable to the infringement.

In the context of design patents, this principle requires that damages be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits that are attributable to the infringing design elements. This is consistent with the holding in Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Co. v. Wagner Electric & Manufacturing Co., 225 U.S. 604 (1912), where the Court held that damages for patent infringement should be calculated based on the "profits on the patented device, or ... the entire profits if it is not practicable to ascertain the profits on the patented device." Id. at 614-15.

The Federal Circuit's decision to affirm the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones is inconsistent with these principles. By failing to limit damages to the portion of profits attributable to the infringing design elements, the Federal Circuit has effectively allowed Apple to recover damages for non-infringing features of Samsung's smartphones. This is contrary to the statutory text and relevant precedent, and will have significant implications for the calculation of damages in design patent cases.

The judgment of the Federal Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for a recalculation of damages that is limited to the portion of Samsung's profits attributable to the infringing design elements.
