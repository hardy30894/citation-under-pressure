**Argument**

The District violated the First Amendment when it denied Lamb’s Chapel access to school premises because the proposed film series was religiously oriented. Even before this Court’s modern forum cases, the settled principle was that the government may not exclude speech from otherwise open public property because of disfavored subject matter or viewpoint. *Police Dep’t of Chicago v. Mosley*, 408 U.S. 92, 96 (1972), is the later articulation of that rule; but the older foundational cases already recognized that the State cannot condition access to public facilities on the suppression of ideas. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939); *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501, 506-09 (1946). Where the government opens its property for expressive use, it may not discriminate against religious speech merely because it is religious. *Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 15-16 (1947); *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 430 (1962).

Here, the School District allowed after-hours community use of its premises under a policy of regulated public access. Once the District chose to open school facilities for expressive activities, it was bound by the First Amendment’s core command of neutrality. *Hague*, 307 U.S. at 515-16; *Marsh*, 326 U.S. at 506-09. It could impose reasonable time, place, and manner controls to protect school operations, but it could not single out a particular religious perspective for exclusion. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940); *Prince v. Massachusetts*, 321 U.S. 158, 166-67 (1944). The District’s own explanation—repeatedly refusing Lamb’s Chapel because the films were “church related” or “for religious purposes”—confirms that the exclusion was based on the religious character of the proposed speech, not on any neutral scheduling concern. The First Amendment forbids that kind of discrimination. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-04; *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 69-70 (1953).

The District’s position also collapses under this Court’s precedents rejecting special disabilities imposed on religious speech in public and quasi-public settings. In *Everson*, the Court held that the Constitution requires government neutrality between religion and nonreligion, explaining that the State may not prefer one over the other. 330 U.S. at 15-16. In *Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495, 505 (1952), the Court held that “sacrilegious” expression could not be suppressed simply because of its religious character. And in *Fowler*, the Court reversed a conviction where officials permitted other speakers to use a public park but excluded Jehovah’s Witness preaching; the Court held that the government may not discriminate among religious viewpoints in the use of a public forum. 345 U.S. at 69-70. The same principle controls here. The District did not merely enforce a neutral rule of limited access; it denied access because Lamb’s Chapel proposed to address family values and child rearing from a Christian perspective. That is content discrimination at the least, and viewpoint discrimination at the most. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-04; *Fowler*, 345 U.S. at 69-70.

The District cannot justify its discrimination by invoking the Establishment Clause. This Court has long held that neutrality, not exclusion, is the constitutional answer to religion’s presence in public life. *Everson*, 330 U.S. at 15-16. The Constitution “prohibits government from making adherence to a religion relevant in any way to a person’s standing in the political community.” *Torcaso v. Watkins*, 367 U.S. 488, 495 (1961). Allowing Lamb’s Chapel to show its films on equal terms with other community groups would not establish religion; it would simply treat religious speech like other speech. *Everson*, 330 U.S. at 15-16; *Engel*, 370 U.S. at 430. What the First Amendment forbids is the District’s opposite choice: granting access to the public forum in general, while denying access because the speaker is religious. *Fowler*, 345 U.S. at 69-70.

The Second Circuit’s reliance on the absence of prior religious use cannot save the District. Once a public authority permits community expression on its property, it may not reserve the expressive privilege for secular viewpoints alone. *Hague*, 307 U.S. at 515-16; *Marsh*, 326 U.S. at 506-09. The relevant question is not whether other religious groups have previously used the premises, but whether the District opened the facility for expressive activity and then excluded Lamb’s Chapel because of its religious message. The record answers that question yes. A policy that forbids only “religious purposes” is facially discriminatory. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-04; *Fowler*, 345 U.S. at 69-70.

Because the District denied access based on the religious viewpoint of Lamb’s Chapel’s speech, the judgment should be reversed. The First Amendment demands that when government opens a forum for expression, it may not close the door to religious speech alone. *Hague*, 307 U.S. at 515-16; *Everson*, 330 U.S. at 15-16; *Fowler*, 345 U.S. at 69-70.
