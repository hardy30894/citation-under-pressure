The releases fail to bar appellants' WARN Act claims. Statutory rights of this character may be relinquished only through a knowing and voluntary waiver supported by consideration independent of preexisting entitlements. Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O'Neil, 324 U.S. 697, 704 (1945); D.A. Schulte, Inc. v. Gangi, 328 U.S. 108, 114 (1946). The agreements here supplied no such consideration; the severance payments merely substituted for wages already due under the collective-bargaining contracts. See J.I. Case Co. v. National Labor Relations Board, 321 U.S. 332, 337 (1944).

Even had consideration existed, the abbreviated decision periods—two days for the ten-week package and at most six weeks for the remainder—rendered any assent involuntary. A release executed under economic coercion or without adequate opportunity for reflection is voidable. Callen v. Pennsylvania R. Co., 332 U.S. 625, 630 (1948); Garrett v. Moore-McCormack Co., 317 U.S. 239, 248 (1942). The simultaneous requirement that drivers complete rebid forms compounded the pressure, leaving no meaningful choice. Boyd v. Grand Trunk Western R. Co., 338 U.S. 263, 266 (1949).

Public policy precludes waiver of rights designed to protect workers from sudden dislocation. Wilko v. Swan, 346 U.S. 427, 437 (1953). The union's negotiation of the releases cannot supply the missing voluntariness, for a bargaining representative may not surrender individual statutory protections without explicit, uncoerced consent. Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R. Co., 323 U.S. 192, 198-199 (1944); Tunstall v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen & Enginemen, 323 U.S. 210, 213 (1944). Because the releases were neither knowing nor voluntary, they do not foreclose the WARN claims.
