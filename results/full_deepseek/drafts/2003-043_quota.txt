**ARGUMENT**

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The Internal Revenue Code requires the government to assess each individual taxpayer before it may collect a tax deficiency from that taxpayer. Because the IRS assessed only the partnership and not the individual partners within the three-year limitations period, the statute of limitations bars collection from the partners.

**I. The Plain Language of the Internal Revenue Code Requires Assessment Against Each Taxpayer**

Section 6203 of the Code provides that an “assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The term “taxpayer” includes both partnerships and individuals as separate persons. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). The implementing regulation requires that a valid assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. This Court has long recognized that a partnership is a distinct entity for federal tax purposes. *United States v. Basye*, 410 U.S. 441, 448 (1973). Consequently, an assessment against the partnership identifies only the partnership as the taxpayer; it does not identify the individual partners. *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that a lien cannot exist against a partner’s property unless the tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203). The IRS’s failure to assess the partners individually means that no valid assessment exists against them.

**II. The Statute of Limitations for Assessment Against Partners Is Not Extended by a Partnership Assessment**

Section 6501(a) requires the IRS to assess any tax within three years after the return is filed. If the IRS fails to assess within that period, it cannot collect the tax. *Badaracco v. Commissioner*, 464 U.S. 386, 392 (1984) (statutes of limitations in tax cases are strictly construed in favor of the taxpayer). Section 6502(a) extends the collection period to ten years only if “the assessment of any tax imposed by this title has been made within the period of limitation properly applicable thereto.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). The “assessment” referred to is the assessment against the taxpayer from whom collection is sought. Here, the IRS assessed the partnership within three years, but it never assessed the individual partners. The assessment against the partnership extended the limitations period only for the partnership, not for the partners. *In re Goldston*, 104 F.3d 1198, 1200-01 (10th Cir. 1997) (holding that the IRS must assess the taxpayer or bring suit within three years; assessment against a different entity does not suffice). The partners are separate taxpayers, and the limitations period for them expired long before the IRS filed its claims.

**III. State Law Does Not Provide an Alternative Basis for Collection Without Assessment or Judgment**

The IRS argues that California partnership law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, and that this state-law liability allows collection without an individual assessment. This argument conflates substantive liability with procedural collection mechanisms. Under California law, a creditor may not satisfy a partnership debt from a partner’s assets without first obtaining a judgment against that partner. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The IRS obtained no such judgment against the partners. *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960), does not support the IRS’s position; that case merely held that a partner who voluntarily paid a partnership tax could not obtain a refund. The court expressly noted that the IRS would have needed to resort to court action to collect from an unwilling partner. *Id.* at 910. *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is distinguishable because in that case the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *Id.* at 562. Here, no individual assessments were made. The IRS cannot rely on state law to circumvent the federal statutory requirement of assessment. *Commissioner v. Stern*, 357 U.S. 39, 45 (1958) (federal tax liability is determined by federal law, not state law).

**IV. The Ninth Circuit Correctly Applied Precedent**

The court of appeals properly held that the IRS’s claims were unenforceable under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). The bankruptcy court may disallow claims that are unenforceable against the debtor. *In re First T.D. & Inv., Inc.*, 253 F.3d 520, 526 (9th Cir. 2001). Because the IRS failed to assess the partners within the three-year period, its claims are time-barred. *United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. 489, 493 (1930) (statute of limitations for tax collection is a substantive bar). The IRS’s attempt to collect from the partners without individual assessments or judgments is contrary to the statutory scheme and to settled precedent.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
