**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School District created a limited public forum by allowing general community use.**

The School District’s policy of permitting the general public to use its facilities for secular events demonstrates an intent to open the premises for expressive activity. The record shows the District allowed a lecture series on parapsychology and a musical performance by the Southern Harmonizers. This conduct places the school property within the category of a designated or limited public forum. In *Marsh v. Alabama*, 327 U.S. 711 (1946), the Supreme Court held that a company-owned town, which was open to the public, was a public forum. The Court reasoned that "the town is open to the public... and the State has not withdrawn it from the protection of the Federal Constitution." *Id.* at 722. Similarly, the School District has opened its facilities to the public for secular purposes, thereby creating a forum where the First Amendment applies.

**II. Once a limited public forum is established, the government cannot exclude speech based on its content.**

The School District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show religious films constitutes a content-based exclusion. In a public forum, the government may not suppress speech merely because the public officials disagree with the speaker’s viewpoint. In *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229 (1963), the Court held that the State may not, by its silence, condone the suppression of ideas. The Court stated that "the State has not only an interest in the preservation of order, but also in the preservation of the Nation's democratic system of government." *Id.* at 233. Furthermore, in *Shuttlesworth v. Birmingham*, 394 U.S. 147 (1969), the Court struck down an ordinance prohibiting picketing, finding that the law was not narrowly tailored to serve a significant government interest and effectively suppressed speech based on its content. *Id.* at 151. The School District’s exclusion of religious speech based on its content violates these principles.

**III. Public schools are not private property where the government can act as a private owner.**

The School District argues that it may regulate school property like a private owner because it is a "nonpublic forum." This argument is inconsistent with the nature of public education. In *Keyishian v. Board of Regents*, 385 U.S. 589 (1967), the Court held that public universities are not private property where the State can act as a private owner. The Court emphasized that the First Amendment applies to public schools. *Id.* at 603. Similarly, in *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503 (1969), the Court held that students do not shed their constitutional rights at the schoolhouse gate. *Id.* at 506. The School District cannot rely on its status as a private owner to justify the exclusion of religious speech from a forum it has opened to the general public.
