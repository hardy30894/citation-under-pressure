**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Case Is Moot Because the School’s Policies Have Been Permanently Revised.**

The district court correctly concluded that the controversy is moot because Georgia Gwinnett College (GGC) has permanently abandoned the challenged policies. While the case was pending, GGC revised its “Freedom of Expression Policy” to permit students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances. GGC also removed the challenged portion of its “Student Code of Conduct.” These changes superseded the prior policies and have been in full force and effect since February 28, 2017. Because the policies are no longer in effect, the plaintiffs have no live controversy to litigate.

The Supreme Court has held that a voluntary cessation of an illegal practice is not a defense if the practice is permanently abandoned. *United States v. Utah Power & Light Co.*, 243 U.S. 389, 410 (1917). Here, the policy change was not a temporary stopgap but a permanent institutional revision. The Eleventh Circuit has consistently applied this principle, holding that a case is moot when the controversy has been resolved. *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc). Because the unconstitutional policies have been permanently replaced, the plaintiffs’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot.

**II. The Case Is Moot Because the Plaintiff Has Graduated.**

Even assuming the policies remained in effect, the case is moot because the named plaintiff, Chike Uzuegbunam, has graduated from GGC. A plaintiff must suffer an “injury in fact” to have standing. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992). Once a plaintiff is no longer subject to the challenged policy, they lack the concrete injury necessary to maintain a lawsuit. Uzuegbunam is no longer a student at GGC and is therefore no longer subject to the school’s speech zone regulations. Consequently, he has no concrete stake in the outcome of this litigation.

**III. Nominal Damages Cannot Save a Moot Constitutional Challenge.**

The district court properly applied Eleventh Circuit precedent in dismissing the case. The Eleventh Circuit has held that a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law. *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia*, 868 F.3d at 1255. Nominal damages are awarded to vindicate a right that has been violated, but they cannot create a live controversy where one does not exist. Because the policies have been changed and the plaintiff has graduated, no live controversy remains. Therefore, the claims for nominal damages cannot resurrect the case.
