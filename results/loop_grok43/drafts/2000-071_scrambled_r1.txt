The school district's denial of access rested on a content distinction that the First Amendment permits. The proposed meetings consisted of prayer, Bible memorization, and instruction in Christian doctrine. Such activities fall outside the secular purposes for which the district opened its facilities. See Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947) (noting the prohibition of laws "which aid one religion, aid all religions, or prefer one religion over another").

The Establishment Clause forbids the district from furnishing its premises for these purposes. The First Amendment "has erected a wall between church and state" that the state may not breach by placing its facilities at the disposal of sectarian teaching. Everson, 330 U.S. at 16. That wall is breached when public property is used to aid religion. The district therefore acted within constitutional bounds by declining to furnish a venue for the Club's devotional program. See Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 431 (1962) (holding that government may not "compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government").

The district's action likewise accords with the principle that government may not "prefer one religion over another" or "aid all religions." Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952). Allowing the Club's meetings would have required the district to extend the same privilege to every other religious group seeking after-hours use, thereby placing the state's facilities in the service of religious instruction. See Reynolds v. United States, 98 U.S. 145, 164 (1879) (recognizing that government may not "interfere with the free exercise of religion" while simultaneously refraining from "aid[ing] one religion").

The district's policy does not abridge free speech. Public property remains under the control of the state, which may limit its use to the purposes for which it was acquired. Davis v. Massachusetts, 167 U.S. 43, 47 (1897). When the state opens property for limited, secular ends, it need not open the same property for religious worship or instruction. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 304 (1940) (noting that the state may regulate "the times, places and manner of" expression "in the interest of all"). The district's refusal to convert its schoolhouse into a site for sectarian activity therefore violates no constitutional command.

The same result follows from the recognition that the state may protect children from religious indoctrination conducted under the apparent sanction of public authority. Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158, 165 (1944); West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 631 (1943). Because the Club's program was "quintessentially religious," the district could exclude it without offending the speech clause while remaining faithful to the Establishment Clause.
