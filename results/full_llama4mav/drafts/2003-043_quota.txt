The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing those partners is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code (IRC) requires the IRS to assess a tax against a "taxpayer" within three years of the filing of the taxpayer's return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The IRC defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, an individual partner is a separate "taxpayer" from the partnership. United States v. Galletti, 298 F.3d 1107 (9th Cir. 2002).

The government's reliance on Young v. Riddell is misplaced. In Young, the district court held that a partner was not entitled to a refund of taxes paid because the partner was liable for the partnership's tax debt under state law. 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,049 (S.D.Cal.1959), aff'd 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir.1960). However, the district court's holding was limited to the context of a refund action, and it acknowledged that the IRS would have had to assess the partner individually or obtain a judgment against the partner to collect the tax. Id. at 76,050, 76,054. The Ninth Circuit's affirmance of the district court's decision in Young did not reject this interpretation. Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir.1960).

In United States v. Coson, the Ninth Circuit further supported the proposition that an individual assessment is required to collect a tax debt from a partner. 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir.1961). Although the court ultimately affirmed the district court's decision on other grounds, the district court's holding that an assessment against a partnership does not create a lien against an individual partner's property without an individual assessment is instructive. Coson v. United States, 169 F.Supp. 671, 675-76 (S.D.Cal.1958).

Other circuits have also held that an individual assessment is necessary to collect a tax debt from a partner. See, e.g., United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir.1995) (implying that an individual assessment is necessary, although the issue was not directly presented); Stallard v. United States, 12 F.3d 489, 493 (5th Cir.1994) (holding that the government must assess a tax against a taxpayer to create a valid tax lien).

The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners under state law is also unavailing. While state law may make partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, it does not permit a creditor to collect a debt from a partner without first obtaining a judgment against the partner. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c); 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp.2001). The IRS has not obtained a judgment against the individual partners, and the statute of limitations for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

The government's position is also inconsistent with the Bankruptcy Code, which permits a creditor to make a claim against a debtor's estate only if the creditor has a "right to payment, whether or not such right is reduced to judgment, liquidated, unliquidated, fixed, contingent, matured, unmatured, disputed, undisputed, legal, equitable, secured, or unsecured." 11 U.S.C. § 101(5)(A). A claim may be disallowed if it "is unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor, under ... applicable law for a reason other than because such claim is contingent or unmatured." 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). The IRS's claim against the individual partners is unenforceable because it has not obtained a judgment against them, and the statute of limitations has expired.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS must assess individual partners to collect a partnership's tax debt from those partners. The government's arguments to the contrary are unpersuasive, and the decision below should be affirmed. See also Goldston v. United States (In re Goldston), 104 F.3d 1198, 1200-01 (10th Cir.1997); LeBeau v. United States, 951 F. Supp. 1369, 1374 (D. Neb. 1996); United States v. W. Prods., Ltd., 168 F.Supp.2d 84, 91 (S.D.N.Y.2001).
