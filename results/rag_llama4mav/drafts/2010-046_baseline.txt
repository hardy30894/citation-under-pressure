The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) does not prohibit a court order requiring a state to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions. 18 U.S.C. § 3626. The PLRA permits prospective relief, including population reduction, if it is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of a federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation. Id. § 3626(a)(1)(A).

The district court's order in this case meets these requirements. The court found that overcrowding was the primary cause of the unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons, and that reducing the population was necessary to remedy those conditions. This finding is supported by the record and is consistent with Supreme Court precedent. See Rhodes v. Chapman, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981) (holding that "deprivations denying the minimal civilized measure of life's necessities" violate the Eighth Amendment); Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 685 (1978) (affirming a district court's order requiring a state to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions).

The court's order is also narrowly drawn and extends no further than necessary to correct the violation. The order requires the state to reduce its prison population to 137.5% of design capacity, which is a specific and measurable target. See Wilkinson v. Austin, 545 U.S. 209, 224 (2005) (noting that a court's order must be "narrowly tailored to address the specific constitutional violation").

Furthermore, the order is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation. The court considered alternative remedies, such as increasing prison capacity or improving healthcare services, but determined that reducing the population was the most effective and efficient way to remedy the unconstitutional conditions. See Turner v. Safley, 482 U.S. 78, 89 (1987) (holding that a court must consider alternative means of achieving a legitimate penological interest).

The state's argument that the order violates the Eleventh Amendment is also without merit. The Eleventh Amendment does not bar a court from ordering a state to comply with federal law, including the Eighth Amendment. See Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908). The district court's order is a permissible exercise of its remedial authority to enforce the Eighth Amendment.

In conclusion, the district court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population is a permissible exercise of its remedial authority under the PLRA and the Eighth Amendment. The order is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation. The court should affirm the district court's order.

The state's petition for a writ of mandamus is also without merit. The Bauman factors do not support the issuance of a writ in this case. See Bauman v. United States Dist. Court, 557 F.2d 650, 654-55 (9th Cir. 1977). The state has an adequate remedy on appeal, and the district court's order is not clearly erroneous as a matter of law. Id.

Therefore, the court should deny the state's petition for a writ of mandamus and affirm the district court's order.
