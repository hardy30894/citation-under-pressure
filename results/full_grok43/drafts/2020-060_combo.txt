The question presented is whether Guam may pursue cost recovery under CERCLA § 107 or is confined to a contribution action under § 113(f)(3)(B). The 2004 consent decree, entered in an action brought solely under the Clean Water Act, did not resolve liability for any CERCLA response action. Guam therefore retains its § 107 claim.

CERCLA imposes liability only for “hazardous substances” released at a facility. The decree addressed untreated leachate discharged into waters of the United States, a matter governed exclusively by the Clean Water Act. A consent decree binds the parties only to the extent of the claims actually asserted. Swift & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 311, 327 (1928). Because the decree contains no adjudication or admission of CERCLA liability, it cannot trigger the contribution right created by § 113(f)(3)(B).

Even if the decree were read to touch CERCLA matters, contribution among joint tortfeasors is an equitable remedy available only after one party has paid more than its share. Union Stock Yards Co. v. Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co., 196 U.S. 217, 224 (1905). The decree imposed obligations on Guam alone and contained an express disclaimer of liability. Such language precludes any inference that Guam’s liability was “resolved” within the meaning of the contribution statute.

Statutes of limitations are strictly applied according to their terms. Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99, 110 (1945). The three-year period for contribution actions runs from the date a qualifying settlement is approved. Because the 2004 decree is not such a settlement, the six-year period governing cost-recovery actions controls. Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392, 395 (1946). Guam’s 2017 complaint is therefore timely.

Guam is an unincorporated territory whose relations with the United States are governed by congressional legislation rather than by any general common-law rule of contribution. Downes v. Bidwell, 182 U.S. 244, 287 (1901); Hawaii v. Mankichi, 190 U.S. 197, 209 (1903); Dorr v. United States, 195 U.S. 138, 149 (1904). Federal courts sitting in such territories apply federal law as declared by Congress, not judge-made doctrines of contribution. Erie R.R. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938); Clearfield Trust Co. v. United States, 318 U.S. 363, 366 (1943). No pre-1980 statute created a contribution right against the United States for environmental costs incurred under a Clean Water Act decree. The judgment below must be reversed.
