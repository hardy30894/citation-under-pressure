**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 (“OSH Act”) applies to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The drilling barge Rig 52 was located in Louisiana’s territorial waters, which are within a “State” for purposes of the Act. *See Skiriotes v. Florida*, 313 U.S. 69, 75 (1941) (state sovereignty extends to its territorial waters). The barge was a fixed workplace where employees performed industrial drilling operations—not a vessel in navigation. *Cf. The Robert W. Parsons*, 191 U.S. 17, 30 (1903) (defining “vessel” as a watercraft used for transportation). The OSH Act therefore covers Rig 52 unless Section 4(b)(1) preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction.

Section 4(b)(1) provides that the OSH Act does not apply to “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard does not exercise such authority over uninspected vessels like Rig 52. The Coast Guard’s pre-1970 statutory authority over uninspected vessels was limited to specific equipment and navigation safety. The Motorboat Act of 1940, 46 U.S.C. § 526 (1940), authorized the Coast Guard to regulate only fire extinguishers, life preservers, backfire flame arrestors, and ventilation on uninspected vessels. This narrow grant does not constitute “statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health” as required by Section 4(b)(1). The Coast Guard has never claimed comprehensive authority over working conditions on uninspected vessels. *See United States v. Moran*, 113 U.S. 100, 103 (1885) (Coast Guard’s predecessor’s authority limited to navigation and maritime safety).

Pre-1970 precedent confirms that the Coast Guard’s limited regulations do not occupy the field of occupational safety. In *The Steamer Eclipse*, 135 U.S. 599, 608 (1890), this Court held that federal maritime jurisdiction does not automatically preempt state safety laws. Similarly, the Coast Guard’s equipment-specific rules do not displace OSHA’s comprehensive safety regime. Section 4(b)(1) preempts only where another agency “exercise[s] statutory authority” over the same “working conditions.” The Coast Guard has not exercised authority over blowout prevention, well control, or explosion hazards—the very conditions at issue here. Its regulations under 46 C.F.R. Part 25 (pre-1970) address only life preservers, fire extinguishers, and ventilation. These do not cover the hazards that caused the explosion on Rig 52.

Moreover, the workers on Rig 52 are not “seamen” under traditional maritime law. Pre-1970 cases define seamen as those who contribute to the navigation or operation of a vessel. *Senko v. LaCrosse Dredging Corp.*, 352 U.S. 370, 374 (1957). Drilling barge workers perform industrial tasks—operating drilling equipment, maintaining well control—unrelated to navigation. They are more akin to longshoremen or harbor workers, whose safety is regulated by OSHA. *See Swanson v. Marra Brothers, Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 7 (1946) (Longshoremen’s Act covers workers engaged in maritime employment but not seamen). Even if some workers could be classified as seamen, the Coast Guard does not regulate their occupational safety on uninspected vessels. The OSH Act fills the gap where no other agency provides protection.

Preemption under Section 4(b)(1) requires a “clear and manifest purpose” to supersede other federal authority. *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). No such purpose exists here. Congress enacted the OSH Act to ensure “safe and healthful working conditions” for all employees. 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). The Act’s legislative history reveals an intent to cover workplaces not already regulated by other agencies. The Coast Guard’s limited authority over uninspected vessels does not satisfy that condition.

The Fifth Circuit erred in extending preemption to uninspected vessels based on post-1970 cases that misread pre-1970 law. The Coast Guard’s statutory authority over uninspected vessels is minimal and does not encompass occupational safety and health. OSHA’s citation was therefore proper. This Court should reverse the judgment below.
