## Argument

The district court erred in dismissing Buckles’s § 2255 motion as untimely. This court’s order recalling the mandate restarted the 90-day period for filing a certiorari petition, rendering Buckles’s petition timely. Alternatively, even if the petition was untimely, equitable tolling applies because the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office provided misinformation that induced Buckles to file late.

### I. The Recall of the Mandate Restarted the Certiorari Clock

When this court recalled the mandate on June 12, 2007, it vacated the prior mandate and reopened the case for further proceedings. The effect of recalling a mandate is to restore the case to the status it held before the mandate issued. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 167 (1873) (a recalled mandate “is as if it had never been issued”). The time for seeking certiorari runs from the date of the final judgment or denial of rehearing. Sup. Ct. R. 13(1), (3). But when a mandate is recalled, the judgment is no longer final until a new mandate issues. *United States v. Mayer*, 235 U.S. 55, 67 (1914) (a court may recall its mandate to correct an error, and the case remains open until the corrected mandate issues). Here, this court recalled the mandate to consider Buckles’s motion for appointment of counsel—a substantive matter affecting his ability to seek certiorari. The new mandate issued on June 20, 2007. Buckles filed his certiorari petition on September 5, 2007, within 90 days of both the recall order and the new mandate. His petition was therefore timely. The Supreme Court Clerk’s notation of untimeliness does not bind this court; the Supreme Court denied certiorari without explanation, leaving the timeliness question unresolved.

### II. Equitable Tolling Applies Due to Clerk’s Misinformation

Even if the certiorari petition was untimely, Buckles is entitled to equitable tolling. Equitable tolling suspends a limitations period when a litigant has been induced or tricked by the government’s misconduct into missing a deadline. *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342, 349 (1874) (equitable tolling applies where “the party injured has been kept in ignorance of the existence of his cause of action by the fraud of the defendant”). The principle extends to cases where official misinformation causes a litigant to reasonably rely on an incorrect deadline. *Glus v. Brooklyn Eastern District Terminal*, 359 U.S. 231, 233–34 (1959) (equitable tolling where employer’s conduct lulled employee into missing limitations period). Here, Buckles’s sister telephoned the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office on June 4, 2007, and was told that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” Buckles reasonably interpreted this to mean he had 90 days from the new mandate date—June 20, 2007—to file his petition. He filed on September 5, 2007, within that period. The Clerk’s office is an arm of the court, and its misinformation is attributable to the government. *United States v. Thayer*, 209 U.S. 39, 43 (1908) (reliance on official advice may excuse delay where the advice is misleading). The government cannot profit from its own error. *Bailey*, 88 U.S. at 349. Buckles acted diligently; he promptly sought clarification and filed as soon as he believed the deadline required. Equitable tolling is therefore warranted, rendering his § 2255 motion timely.
