Ohio’s clemency process does not create a protected life or liberty interest, and its optional interview does not compel self-incrimination.

I. Ohio’s clemency process does not create a protected liberty interest.

The Constitution does not guarantee clemency. From the Founding forward, clemency has been understood as an act of grace vested in the Executive, not as a right enforceable by courts. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380–81 (1866); *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486–87 (1927). The Court has likewise recognized that pardon power is committed to executive discretion and is not a judicially manageable entitlement. *United States v. Klein*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128, 147–48 (1872); *Schick v. Reed*, 419 U.S. 256, 266 (1974).

That principle defeats any claim that a state clemency procedure, standing alone, creates a protected liberty interest. The Due Process Clause protects interests of “life, liberty, or property,” but it does not create them by itself; rather, it protects against deprivations of interests otherwise recognized by law. *Board of Regents v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564, 569–71 (1972); *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 599–603 (1972). Long before those decisions, the Court held that where the government retains discretion, there is no enforceable entitlement. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369–70 (1886); *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union, Local 473 v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895–99 (1961). Here, Ohio’s clemency scheme leaves the Governor free to grant or deny mercy as he sees fit. A discretionary procedure of that sort does not create a protected liberty interest in either the process or its outcome.

The Court’s modern clemency cases confirm that point. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 463–67 (1981), the Court held that there is no constitutional liberty interest in commutation. That holding follows directly from the older authorities: clemency is “the prerogative of the executive,” *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. at 380–81, and judicial review of such decisions is generally inappropriate. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. Ohio’s procedure is no different. If the Governor remains free to say yes or no without substantive constraint, there is no life or liberty interest cognizable under the Fourteenth Amendment.

II. The optional interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.

The Fifth Amendment forbids compulsion; it does not forbid a prisoner from choosing whether to speak in hopes of obtaining mercy. *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43, 67–68 (1906); *McCarthy v. Arndstein*, 266 U.S. 34, 40–41 (1924). The privilege protects against being “compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” U.S. Const. amend. V. A voluntary interview, by definition, lacks that compulsion.

That is especially true here because the interview is optional. Ohio does not require Woodard to attend, and it does not require him to answer any question. A choice between speaking and remaining silent is not unconstitutional merely because silence may make clemency less likely. *Mann v. People of State of Illinois*, 292 U.S. 602, 604–05 (1934); *United States v. Monia*, 317 U.S. 424, 427 (1943). The Court has repeatedly distinguished compulsion from the mere presentation of a strategic choice. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 598–605 (1896); *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562–64 (1892).

Nor does the fact that a prisoner might voluntarily say something incriminating transform the process into unconstitutional compulsion. The Self-Incrimination Clause is not violated simply because a person’s own statements may later prove harmful; it is violated when the State forces those statements. *United States ex rel. Vajtauer v. Commissioner of Immigration*, 273 U.S. 103, 106–07 (1927); *Maggio v. Zeitz*, 333 U.S. 56, 63–64 (1948). Ohio’s regulation does not force Woodard to say anything. It merely offers him an opportunity to speak in support of clemency, while leaving him free to say nothing at all.

The opposing theory would turn every discretionary government interview into a constitutional trap. That is not the law. The Court has cautioned against transforming discretionary procedures into judicially enforceable entitlements absent clear legal limits. *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers*, 367 U.S. at 895–99; *McAndrews v. State of North Carolina*, 154 U.S. 391, 399–400 (1894). Ohio’s clemency interview is a voluntary avenue for seeking mercy, not an unconstitutional condition or compelled confession.

For those reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
