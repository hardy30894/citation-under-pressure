**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges.**

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office in any State or political subdivision of any State by the qualified voters thereof, or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri judges appointed under the Non-Partisan Court Plan and retained by election exercise the core judicial function of “lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988) (quoting 680 F. Supp. at 462). Their decisions establish binding rules of decision, supervise court administration, and resolve disputes of constitutional dimension. See, e.g., Cruzan v. Harmon, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988) (en banc), cert. granted, 492 U.S. 917 (1989).

Congress did not manifest a clear intention to intrude upon this traditional state domain. See United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). The statute’s structure and the legislative history of the 1972 amendments confirm that the policymaking exception encompasses appointed judges. EEOC v. Wyoming, 460 U.S. 226, 240 (1983), recognized that the ADEA tests state mandatory-retirement rules “against a reasonable federal standard,” but that standard does not extend to officials whose functions place them outside the Act’s definition of employee. Because the judges fall within the statutory exception, the ADEA supplies no basis for invalidating Article V, § 26(1).

**II. The Mandatory-Retirement Provision Satisfies Equal Protection.**

Age is not a suspect classification. Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979). Missouri’s rule therefore need only be rationally related to a legitimate state interest. The provision eliminates the “unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges,” ensures that “important decisions . . . will be made by judges who command respect because they share experiences and understandings similar to those of the majority of adult citizens,” and facilitates “increase[d] . . . proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary.” Id. at 96-97; see also Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). These objectives are legitimate, and the age-based line is a rational means of achieving them. Western Air Lines, Inc. v. Criswell, 472 U.S. 400, 422 (1985), confirms that Congress itself has treated chronological age as a permissible proxy when the demands of a position make it so. Missouri’s judgment that judicial service imposes “unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode” is therefore constitutional. Vance, 440 U.S. at 97.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
