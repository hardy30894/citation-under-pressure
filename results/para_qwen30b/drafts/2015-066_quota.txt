**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not prohibit the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), and the Ninth Circuit’s decision to the contrary is irreconcilable with Supreme Court precedent, undermines tribal sovereignty, and creates an unjustified circuit split. The government may properly rely on prior tribal court convictions—valid under the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA)—to establish the essential elements of a federal felony offense, so long as those convictions are not constitutionally infirm under the *Sixth Amendment*. The Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s holding that such convictions are inherently unreliable and thus inadmissible in a guilt-phase determination.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings, and Thus No Sixth Amendment Violation Occurs When Counsel Is Not Provided**

The foundational error in the Ninth Circuit’s analysis is its assumption that tribal court proceedings are subject to the Sixth Amendment. That is not the law. The Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes. *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337, 128 S.Ct. 2709, 171 L.Ed.2d 457 (2008) (“It is beyond dispute that the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.”). The Sixth Amendment, like the rest of the Bill of Rights, is a limitation on federal and state power, not tribal authority. *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71, 98 S.Ct. 1670, 56 L.Ed.2d 106 (1978) (holding that “the Constitution does not require tribes to provide the same procedural protections as the federal government”).

The Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. § 1302, provides a parallel but distinct framework for due process in tribal courts. It guarantees certain rights, including the right to counsel in cases where the defendant faces imprisonment, but only to the extent that such rights are “coextensive with” those guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment. *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389, 1394 (9th Cir.1989). The ICRA does not mandate the same right to counsel as the Sixth Amendment, nor does it require that tribal courts adopt federal procedural rules. *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir.1997) (recognizing that “comity does not require that a tribe utilize judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts”).

Thus, a tribal court conviction is not rendered invalid by the absence of counsel under the Sixth Amendment because the Sixth Amendment does not govern tribal proceedings. The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that such convictions are “constitutionally infirm” because they lack Sixth Amendment protections is legally erroneous. *See also* *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595 (8th Cir.2011) (holding that tribal court convictions may be used as predicate offenses even without Sixth Amendment counsel); *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997–98 (10th Cir.2011) (same).

**II. The Supreme Court Has Permitted the Use of Uncounseled Misdemeanor Convictions in Recidivist Statutes, and § 117(a) Is a Recidivist Statute**

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 114 S.Ct. 1921, 128 L.Ed.2d 745 (1994), is misplaced. *Nichols* did not hold that uncounseled convictions are inadmissible in guilt-phase determinations. Rather, it held that such convictions may be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, so long as they are not constitutionally invalid. *Id.* at 746–47, 114 S.Ct. 1921. The Court emphasized that the sentencing phase requires only a preponderance of the evidence, not proof beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 748, 114 S.Ct. 1921.

But *Nichols* did not address whether such convictions may be used to establish an essential element of a crime. That distinction is critical. In *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386, 128 S.Ct. 1783, 170 L.Ed.2d 719 (2008), the Court reaffirmed that recidivist statutes “penalize only the last offense committed by the defendant.” The prior convictions are not the basis of punishment; they are elements of the offense. *Id.* at 386, 128 S.Ct. 1783. The Court has never held that the *guilt* phase of a criminal trial requires a higher standard of reliability for prior convictions than the sentencing phase—indeed, the opposite is true. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364, 90 S.Ct. 1068, 25 L.Ed.2d 368 (1970) (requiring proof beyond a reasonable doubt for all elements of a crime).

The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Nichols* on the ground that it was a sentencing case is legally unsound. *Nichols* did not create a bright-line rule that uncounseled convictions are inadmissible in guilt-phase determinations. It simply held that such convictions may be used in sentencing. The Court has never extended that holding to the guilt phase. *See also* *United States v. Acuna-Reyna*, 677 F.3d 1282, 1284–85 (11th Cir.2012) (upholding use of uncounseled tribal conviction in federal prosecution under § 117(a)).
