The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial here.

The Clause protects against repeated prosecution “for the same offense,” U.S. Const. amend. V, and this Court has repeatedly tied that protection to the trial of guilt or innocence. See North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969); Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). But the Court has never held that a sentencing enhancement allegation is itself an “offense” for double-jeopardy purposes. To the contrary, the longstanding rule is that when a conviction is set aside at the defendant’s behest, retrial is ordinarily permitted. United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662, 671-72 (1896); United States v. Ewell, 383 U.S. 116, 121-22 (1966).

That principle applies with particular force in noncapital sentencing. In Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 246, 251 (1949), the Court held that due process does not freeze sentencing procedures into the mold of a trial on guilt. The Court reaffirmed that the Constitution does not require adversarial hearings at sentencing in the ordinary case. Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 606 (1967). And the Court has long recognized that habitual-criminal proceedings are sentencing devices, not new prosecutions. See McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311, 312-13 (1901); Murphy v. Massachusetts, 177 U.S. 155, 159-60 (1900); Sigler, Warden v. Losieau, 396 U.S. 988 (1969) (memorandum decision).

Those cases matter because the Double Jeopardy Clause is offense-specific. In Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957), the Court barred a second prosecution for first-degree murder after the first jury had necessarily rejected that charge. But Green rested on the fact that the State was trying the defendant again for a substantive offense on which he had already been placed in jeopardy. Id. at 190-91. Likewise, Sapir v. United States, 348 U.S. 373, 373-74 (1955) (Douglas, J., concurring in result), reflects the settled rule that an acquittal for insufficient evidence bars another attempt to convict of the same crime. Here, by contrast, the State sought not to relitigate Monge’s drug convictions, but to establish a fact relevant to punishment: the existence and character of a prior conviction allegation. That is sentencing, not a second prosecution.

Petitioner cannot convert a sentence enhancement into a separate jeopardy merely because California chose to provide trial procedures for the allegation. A state may afford more process than the Constitution requires, but it does not follow that every extra safeguard becomes constitutionally mandatory. See Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. at 251; Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. at 606. The defendant’s reliance on retrial principles from the guilt phase also fails because this Court has distinguished between a reversal that wipes out a conviction and one that ends the litigation entirely. Ball, 163 U.S. at 671-72; Ewell, 383 U.S. at 121-22. A sentencing allegation, especially one tied to recidivism, falls on the retrial-permitted side of that line.

Nor do the capital-sentencing cases compel a different result. Whatever force they may have where the sentencer is deciding life or death, those decisions rested on the “uniqueness” of capital punishment and do not extend to ordinary enhancements. See North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. at 719-21; Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. at 796; cf. Cichos v. Indiana, 385 U.S. 76, 79-81 (1966) (declining to resolve broader double-jeopardy incorporation question where it was unnecessary). The sentence here was not capital, and the allegation did not expose Monge to a new punishment for a new offense.

Finally, allowing retrial serves the very policies that underlie the Double Jeopardy Clause as construed in this Court’s cases. The State’s interest in enforcing valid sentencing laws should not be defeated by a reversible evidentiary error at the first hearing. See United States v. Ewell, 383 U.S. at 121-22. The Clause protects against harassment and successive prosecutions for the same offense; it does not immunize a defendant from retrial on a recidivist sentencing allegation after a reversal for insufficiency in a noncapital proceeding. See Green v. United States, 355 U.S. at 187-88; McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. at 313-14; Murphy v. Massachusetts, 177 U.S. at 159-60.

Because the prior conviction allegation is part of sentencing, not a separate offense, the Double Jeopardy Clause does not preclude retrial.
