ARGUMENT

I. The Bankruptcy Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Determine Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs

The bankruptcy court's determination that it had jurisdiction over the matter pursuant to 28 U.S.C. §§ 1334(a) and 157(a) was erroneous. Although the court correctly noted that the proceeding was a "core proceeding" under § 157(b)(2)(A), it failed to consider the implications of the bankruptcy estate's closure on its jurisdiction.

As established in Gardner v. New Jersey, 329 U.S. 565 (1947), a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is limited to matters that are closely related to the bankruptcy proceeding. In Gardner, the Supreme Court held that a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction "is not limited to the period until the case is closed, but extends to the determination of the amount due on a claim." Id. at 573. However, this jurisdiction is not without bounds, and the court must carefully consider whether the matter at hand is sufficiently related to the bankruptcy proceeding.

In this case, the bankruptcy court erred in exercising jurisdiction over the determination of post-petition interest and collection costs. The loan at issue was not paid in full during the bankruptcy proceeding, and the court's determination of the amount due on the loan, including post-petition interest and collection costs, is not closely related to the bankruptcy proceeding.

Furthermore, as held in Thompson v. Magnolia Petroleum Co., 309 U.S. 478 (1940), a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is limited to matters that are "ancillary to the bankruptcy proceeding." Id. at 483. The determination of post-petition interest and collection costs is not ancillary to the bankruptcy proceeding, as it involves issues that arose after the closure of the bankruptcy estate.

The bankruptcy court's award of post-petition interest and denial of collection costs were therefore improper exercises of jurisdiction. As the Supreme Court held in United States v. Marxen, 307 U.S. 200 (1939), a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is not a "roving commission" to adjudicate all matters related to the debtor. Id. at 205. Rather, the court's jurisdiction is limited to matters that are closely tied to the bankruptcy proceeding.

In this case, the bankruptcy court's determination of post-petition interest and collection costs was not closely tied to the bankruptcy proceeding, and the court therefore lacked jurisdiction to make such determinations.

II. The Bankruptcy Court Erred in Failing to Award Collection Costs

ECMC was entitled to collection costs, and the bankruptcy court erred in failing to award them. As established in Reconstruction Finance Corp. v. Lee, 125 F.2d 505 (5th Cir. 1942), a creditor is entitled to recover collection costs incurred in connection with a debt. The court in Lee held that "the right to recover costs and expenses incurred in collecting a debt is a matter of contract or statute." Id. at 508.

In this case, ECMC was entitled to collection costs pursuant to the terms of the loan agreement. The bankruptcy court erred in failing to award such costs, as ECMC had provided documentation of the costs incurred.

The judgment of the bankruptcy court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings to determine the amount of post-petition interest and collection costs to which ECMC is entitled.
