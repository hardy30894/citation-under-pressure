The Court should answer the question presented no. A fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may not be enhanced solely because counsel performed well and achieved good results. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the lodestar—the product of reasonable hours and a reasonable hourly rate—is the “reasonable fee,” and that quality and results generally do not justify an additional multiplier. See Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 434-37 (1983); Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 897-901 (1984); Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546, 565-68 (1986) (“Delaware Valley I”).

In Hensley, the Court held that a prevailing party “should recover a fully compensatory fee,” but it explained that the starting point is the lodestar, and that the fee should reflect “the degree of success obtained.” 461 U.S. at 435-36. That formulation does not authorize a second, duplicative premium for quality or success already captured by the reasonable hourly rate and hours reasonably expended. Id. at 434-37. The Court later made that point explicit in Blum, where it rejected an enhancement based on “the quality of representation” and “the results obtained,” explaining that those factors are ordinarily subsumed within the lodestar. 465 U.S. at 898-900. The Court held that “[n]either complexity nor novelty of the issues, the special skill and experience of counsel, nor the quality of representation, are appropriate factors” for increasing the lodestar where they are already reflected in the reasonable rate. Id. at 898-99.

Blum left only a narrow possibility for an enhancement in an “exceptional” case supported by “specific evidence” that the lodestar was not adequate. 465 U.S. at 899. Delaware Valley I confirmed that principle and emphasized that “the lodestar figure includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” making enhancement unnecessary in the ordinary case. 478 U.S. at 566. The plurality further explained that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” though it acknowledged that a rare case might warrant adjustment upon a proper evidentiary showing. Id. at 566-68. Thus, the governing rule is not that quality and results can independently support enhancement whenever they are impressive; rather, they may matter only if they are not already reflected in the lodestar and the record contains specific evidence warranting an adjustment. Blum, 465 U.S. at 899; Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 565-68.

That framework forecloses the fee award below. The district court enhanced the lodestar because counsel’s “superb quality” supposedly “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee.” That reasoning conflicts with Blum’s command that quality of representation is ordinarily subsumed within the lodestar, 465 U.S. at 898-900, and with Delaware Valley I’s instruction that enhancement is reserved for rare cases supported by specific evidence that the lodestar is inadequate, 478 U.S. at 565-68. Where, as here, the court has already approved a market-rate lodestar for the work performed, a further premium based solely on performance and outcome is impermissible double counting. Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-99.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary approach cannot be squared with this Court’s cases. Although courts may consider an enhancement in extraordinary circumstances, the Court has never held that superior representation and exceptional results, standing alone, are a proper independent basis for increasing a fee already calculated under the lodestar method. See Blum, 465 U.S. at 899-900; Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 566-68. To the contrary, the Court has warned that the lodestar already captures “the most useful starting point” for determining a reasonable fee. Hensley, 461 U.S. at 433. Allowing a premium solely because counsel did a very good job would convert the exception into the rule and undermine the lodestar’s function as an objective, administrable measure of reasonable compensation. Blum, 465 U.S. at 897-900; Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 566.

This Court should therefore reverse and hold that a fee enhancement under a federal fee-shifting statute is not available based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. At minimum, any enhancement must rest on specific evidence showing that the lodestar does not already adequately compensate counsel and that the case is truly exceptional. Blum, 465 U.S. at 899-901; Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 565-68. Here, the record shows only a disagreement over how much extra counsel deserved for doing excellent work. That is not enough.
