**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Admitting Expert Testimony on the Cause of the Line’s Parting**

The opinion of American Steamship’s expert, Captain Richard Block, that the line parted due to “heat damage” from Cook’s torch was not based on specialized knowledge and therefore did not constitute expert testimony under Federal Rule of Evidence 702. “The touchstone of admissibility under Rule 702 is helpfulness to the trier of fact—an opinion that is within the ken of a layperson is not expert testimony.” *United States v. Simmons*, 470 F.2d 1335, 1338 (5th Cir. 1972). Captain Block merely offered a lay inference drawn from common experience—that a torch flame could damage rope. This is no different from what any juror could deduce. In *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 590 (1993), the Supreme Court emphasized that Rule 702 requires “scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge” that “will assist the trier of fact.” Because Captain Block’s opinion lacked any scientific methodology, was not based on testing or analysis, and was simply his personal speculation, it was inadmissible. *See United States v. Starzecpyzel*, 880 F. Supp. 1027, 1041 (S.D.N.Y. 1995) (rejecting expert opinion lacking “reliable principles and methods”). The district court abused its discretion by admitting this testimony, which directly prejudiced Cook by suggesting he caused his own injury. *See General Electric Co. v. Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136, 146 (1997) (review for abuse of discretion).

**II. The District Court Erred in Denying Cook’s Motion for Directed Verdict on Unseaworthiness**

A shipowner’s duty to provide a seaworthy vessel is absolute and nondelegable. *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 549 (1960). The vessel’s boom was rendered unseaworthy because the crew used summer grease that froze in sub-zero temperatures, preventing the boom from pivoting. This condition constituted a “defective condition of the vessel” that proximately caused Cook’s injury. *See Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85, 94 (1946). The evidence was undisputed that the frozen grease made the boom inoperable and forced Cook to use a torch near the line. No reasonable jury could find the vessel seaworthy. *See Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96, 100 (1944) (failure to provide proper equipment renders vessel unseaworthy). Moreover, Cook’s contributory negligence does not bar his unseaworthiness claim; comparative fault only reduces damages. *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424, 429 (1939). Because the shipowner’s duty is absolute, Cook was entitled to judgment as a matter of law on this issue. *See Hill v. Texaco, Inc.*, 674 F.2d 447, 451 (5th Cir. 1982) (directed verdict proper where “no reasonable juror could find the vessel seaworthy”).

**III. The District Court Erred in Admitting Evidence of Cook’s Alcohol-Related Ailments**

The district court permitted American Steamship to introduce evidence that Cook suffered from alcohol-related health problems, including cirrhosis. This evidence was irrelevant to any issue in the case and, even if marginally relevant, its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. Federal Rule of Evidence 403 forbids admission of evidence that “confuses the issues, misleads the jury, or causes unfair prejudice.” *Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 180 (1997). Cook’s alcohol use had no bearing on whether the line parted due to the torch or caused his fall. The only purpose was to portray Cook as an irresponsible person, inviting the jury to punish him for his lifestyle. *See United States v. Mendez*, 514 F.3d 1035, 1045 (10th Cir. 2008) (alcohol evidence inadmissible when used solely to impugn character). This improper character evidence violated Federal Rule of Evidence 404(a) and denied Cook a fair trial. *See Huddleston v. United States*, 485 U.S. 681, 685 (1988). The prejudicial effect is clear: the jury reduced Cook’s damages by 50% for comparative negligence, likely influenced by this irrelevant prejudice. The error was not harmless. *See Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946) (error requires reversal if “substantial rights” affected).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should vacate the judgment and remand for a new trial.
