# ARGUMENT

## I. THE ACT IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION THAT SURVIVES INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY

The Massachusetts Reproductive Health Care Facilities Act regulates a fixed location—a thirty-five-foot zone around clinic entrances—without regard to what is said there. This Court has long held that "[t]he principal inquiry in determining content neutrality... is whether the government has adopted a regulation of speech because of disagreement with the message it conveys." *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). The Act's text confirms neutrality: it restricts entry into a defined zone by "any person," Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½(b)-(c), without singling out abortion-related speech, pro-life advocacy, or any viewpoint.

This Court's decision in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), controls. There, the Court upheld a Colorado statute creating a floating eight-foot no-approach zone within one hundred feet of healthcare facility entrances, holding it was "not a regulation of speech" in the constitutionally problematic sense but rather "a regulation of the places where some speech may occur." *Id.* at 715. The Court reasoned that the statute was content-neutral because it applied to all speakers approaching for purposes of "protest, education, or counseling," regardless of viewpoint. *Id.* at 723. The Massachusetts Act is, if anything, more clearly content-neutral than the Colorado statute, since it does not distinguish among categories of speech-related conduct at all—it simply establishes a buffer zone that all members of the public must observe, subject to narrow, content-neutral exemptions for clinic employees, patients, and passersby. *See McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 176-81 (1st Cir. 2009) (*McCullen I*).

The exemptions themselves do not convert the statute into a content-based scheme. In *Hill*, the Court rejected a similar argument, explaining that a regulation is not content-based merely because "an easily identifiable class of speech is more likely to violate the statute than another class." 530 U.S. at 723 n.25. The four exemptions here—for patients, employees acting within the scope of employment, public-safety officials, and those merely passing through—are defined by function and status, not by the content of any message. *See McGuire v. Reilly*, 386 F.3d 45, 61 (1st Cir. 2004) (*McGuire II*) (rejecting identical challenge to predecessor statute).

Because the Act is content-neutral, it is subject to intermediate scrutiny under *Ward*: it must be "narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest" and must "leave open ample alternative channels for communication." 491 U.S. at 791. The Act easily satisfies this standard.

**A. The Commonwealth's interests are significant.** This Court has repeatedly recognized the state's substantial interest in "protecting the health and safety of clinic patients and staff," in ensuring "public safety," and in preserving "unobstructed access" to healthcare facilities. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715; *see also Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of W.N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 375-76 (1997). The trial record documented years of congestion, obstruction, and confrontation at clinic entrances that necessitated legislative action. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 844 F.Supp.2d 206, 213-25 (D. Mass. 2012) (*McCullen III*).

**B. The Act is narrowly tailored.** Narrow tailoring "does not require that the least restrictive means be used." *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798. It requires only that the regulation not "burden substantially more speech than is necessary." *Id.* A fixed thirty-five-foot zone—smaller in scope than the buffer sustained in *Hill*—is a modest and proportionate response to documented safety concerns. The First Circuit correctly found that petitioners retain access to "oral speech of varying degrees of volume and amplification, distribution of literature, displays of signage and symbols, wearing of evocative garments and costumes, and prayer alone and in groups." *McCullen v. Coakley*, 708 F.3d 1, 12 (1st Cir. 2013).

**C. Ample alternative channels remain.** The undisputed trial evidence showed that petitioners have successfully counseled patients—McCullen estimated persuading approximately eighty women—notwithstanding the buffer zones. *Id.* at 6. That petitioners prefer closer, unmediated contact does not render the Act unconstitutional; the First Amendment guarantees "ample" alternatives, not optimal ones. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 802.

## II. *HILL* WAS CORRECTLY DECIDED AND SHOULD NOT BE OVERRULED

Petitioners' invitation to overrule *Hill* disregards this Court's stringent standard for abandoning precedent. Stare decisis demands "special justification," not merely the conviction that a prior decision was wrong. *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443 (2000). No such justification exists here.

*Hill*'s core holding—that a facially neutral buffer zone regulating conduct near healthcare facilities is a valid time-place-manner restriction—rests on sound doctrinal footing consistent with this Court's broader jurisprudence on content neutrality, from *Ward* through *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288 (1984). Petitioners' reliance on *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), and *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), is misplaced; those cases addressed speaker-based and content-based restrictions in wholly different contexts and articulated no new rule inconsistent with *Hill*. As the First Circuit observed, these decisions announce "conventional First Amendment principles" applied to "factual scenarios far different" from buffer-zone regulation of healthcare-facility sidewalks. *McCullen*, 708 F.3d at 9.

Overruling *Hill* would also destabilize reliance interests. Massachusetts and numerous other jurisdictions have structured healthcare-facility safety regulations around *Hill*'s framework for over a decade. *See McGuire v. Reilly*, 260 F.3d 36 (1st Cir. 2001) (*McGuire I*). This settled reliance counsels strongly against disturbing precedent absent a showing—absent here—that *Hill* has proven unworkable or that its factual premises have been undermined. *See Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827 (1991).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
