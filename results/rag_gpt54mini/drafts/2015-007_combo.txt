The judgment below should be affirmed. Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not offend the Sixth or Eighth Amendments as those provisions were understood in the pre-1970 cases that define the relevant constitutional floor.

The Sixth Amendment claim fails because the jury’s historic role has always been to determine guilt, while sentencing has long been committed to judicial or statutory discretion. In Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court explained that “[a] sentencing judge . . . is not confined to the narrow issue of guilt,” and that “Highly relevant — if not essential — to his selection of an appropriate sentence is the possession of the fullest information possible concerning the defendant’s life and characteristics.” Id. at 247. That principle is consistent with earlier capital cases recognizing that punishment may be fixed by the court, even after a jury’s verdict. See Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 135-36 (1879); McElvaine v. Brush, 142 U.S. 155, 159-60 (1891). Florida’s procedure, which permits the jury to hear aggravation and mitigation and then recommend a sentence, fits comfortably within that tradition. See Williams v. Oklahoma, 358 U.S. 576, 585-86 (1959).

Nothing in the pre-1970 cases requires a unanimous jury recommendation of death. To the contrary, the Court repeatedly upheld sentencing arrangements in which the jury’s role was advisory or limited. In Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), the Court described older capital practice in which the jury was given “a discretion in making that choice.” Id. at 748. And in Winston v. United States, 172 U.S. 303 (1899), the Court emphasized the “full and free exercise by the jury of powers” under the statute, but did so in a system where the jury’s function was defined by state law, not by a freestanding constitutional unanimity rule. Id. at 310. Florida’s seven-to-five recommendation, therefore, does not violate the Sixth Amendment merely because it is not unanimous. The Constitution has never been construed, in the pre-1970 cases, to require unanimity as a condition of a death sentence where the state assigns the jury a recommendation rather than final sentencing authority.

The Eighth Amendment claim fails for similar reasons. The Court’s pre-1970 decisions make clear that death as a punishment is not per se cruel and unusual. Wilkerson, 99 U.S. at 135-36; McElvaine, 142 U.S. at 159-60. The Court likewise distinguished between punishment and the procedures used to impose it. See Witherspoon v. Illinois, 391 U.S. 510, 518 (1968) (recognizing that the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments’ jury guarantees are “separate and distinct from the question whether the death penalty offends the Eighth Amendment”). Florida’s procedure does not authorize arbitrary punishment; it channels sentencing through a guilt-phase conviction, an evidentiary penalty phase, jury recommendation, and judicial sentencing findings. That is the kind of individualized sentencing process the Court approved in Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. at 247-50.

Nor does Atkins-type mental-retardation litigation transform this case into an Eighth Amendment violation. The pre-1970 authorities hold only that a defendant’s mental condition may be considered in sentencing, not that the jury must first decide it as an absolute bar to execution. In United States Ex Rel. Smith v. Baldi, 344 U.S. 561 (1953), the Court explained that evidence of mental condition could go “to the question of the appropriate penalty.” Id. at 570. Likewise, Williams v. New York approved the sentencing court’s reliance on broad information about the defendant’s background and character. 337 U.S. at 247. Florida allowed Hurst to present mental-health evidence in mitigation, which is all the Constitution required under these earlier cases.

The Florida Supreme Court’s judgment should therefore be affirmed because Hurst asks this Court to impose a constitutional rule not found in the pre-1970 decisions: that a capital jury must unanimously determine aggravation and unanimously recommend death, and must itself resolve mental-retardation claims as an absolute bar. The Court’s older authorities point the other way. They show that sentencing in capital cases has long been a matter of legislative design, guided by individualized consideration and substantial judicial discretion. See Williams, 337 U.S. 241; Williams, 358 U.S. 576; Andres, 333 U.S. 740; Baldi, 344 U.S. 561; Wilkerson, 99 U.S. 130; McElvaine, 142 U.S. 155; Winston, 172 U.S. 303; Witherspoon, 391 U.S. 510. Under that settled law, Florida’s scheme is constitutional.
