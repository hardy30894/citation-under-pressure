The plain-error doctrine authorizes an appellate court to notice forfeited errors only in exceptional circumstances where the error affects substantial rights and seriously impugns the fairness or integrity of judicial proceedings. United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 36, 38 (1936). That inquiry necessarily extends beyond the evidence formally admitted before the jury. An appellate court examines the entire record made in the cause, including matters of which it may take judicial notice, to ascertain whether the error produced prejudice. Ballew v. United States, 160 U.S. 187, 193 (1895); see also Clyatt v. United States, 197 U.S. 207, 221-22 (1905).

Prejudice is measured by the effect of the error upon the verdict in light of the whole case. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946). The reviewing court therefore consults all proceedings had in the district court, not merely the portion presented to the trier of fact. This practice accords with the historic function of appellate review, which reaches every part of the record properly before the court. Espinoza v. United States, 160 U.S. 167, 169 (1895). Prior convictions appearing in the court files fall within that compass; their existence is not “outside” the record of the cause but constitutes part of the proceedings whose regularity the court is bound to examine. See Crain v. United States, 162 U.S. 625, 632-33 (1896).

The same principle governs the assessment whether an error affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. That determination rests on the entire course of the litigation, not on an artificially truncated view limited to the trial transcript. Judicial notice of undisputed facts of record has long been available to prevent miscarriage of justice. See De La Rama v. De La Rama, 201 U.S. 303, 309 (1906). Consequently, an appellate court may consider a defendant’s prior felony convictions, though not introduced at trial, when deciding whether a Rehaif-type omission warrants relief under the plain-error standard.

Because the court of appeals acted within these settled bounds, its judgment should be affirmed.
