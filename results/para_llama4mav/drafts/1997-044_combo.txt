The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are intended to provide a fresh start for honest debtors, but they do not shield those who have committed fraud. The question presented here is whether individuals who commit fraud can discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded, under the Bankruptcy Code. The answer is no.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." The plain language of this provision indicates that debts incurred through fraud are nondischargeable. As the Supreme Court held in Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), "the frauds included in the portion of [the Bankruptcy Act] under consideration are those which in fact involve moral turpitude or intentional wrong." Id. at 562. The Court further noted that the statute is intended to prevent the discharge of debts obtained by "fraudulent means." Id.

In Brown v. Garey, 267 F.2d 298 (9th Cir. 1959), the Ninth Circuit held that a debt incurred through false representations is nondischargeable under the Bankruptcy Act. The court noted that the Act's purpose is to "relieve the honest debtor" but not to "protect the dishonest debtor who has incurred debts through fraudulent means." Id. at 300. Similarly, in In re Barbato, 93 F.2d 918 (2d Cir. 1938), the Second Circuit held that a debt obtained by false pretenses is not dischargeable in bankruptcy.

The debt at issue here was incurred through Cohen's fraudulent conduct, specifically his failure to comply with the rent control ordinance and his implicit representation to tenants that the rents charged were lawful. As the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit noted in Cohn v. Katzenstein, 111 F. 511 (3d Cir. 1901), "a false representation, made with intent to deceive, and relied upon by the creditor, is sufficient to render the debt non-dischargeable." Id. at 513.

Furthermore, the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are also nondischargeable. As the Supreme Court held in Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181 (1902), "the national Bankruptcy Act ... supersedes the laws of the several States, so far as they affect the administration of bankrupt estates." Id. at 188. However, the Court also noted that state laws may be relevant in determining the nature and extent of a debt. In this case, the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act provides for treble damages, which are intended to punish and deter fraudulent conduct.

The award of treble damages in this case is consistent with the principles enunciated in cases such as Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704 (1877), where the Supreme Court held that a debt incurred through "fraud, actual or constructive" is not dischargeable in bankruptcy. Id. at 709. Similarly, in Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176 (1904), the Court held that a debt obtained through "false pretenses or false representations" is not dischargeable. Id. at 193.

In addition, the Court has long recognized that the Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are not intended to protect debtors who have engaged in egregious conduct. As the Court noted in Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234 (1934), "the bankruptcy law is intended to relieve the honest debtor, not to protect the dishonest one." Id. at 244.

In Tinker v. Colwell, 193 U.S. 473 (1904), the Court held that a debt incurred through willful and malicious conduct is not dischargeable in bankruptcy. The Court's reasoning in Tinker is applicable here, as Cohen's conduct was willful and malicious, and the debt incurred through his fraud is therefore nondischargeable.

Finally, as the Ninth Circuit noted in Brown v. Garey, a debtor who has incurred debts through fraudulent means is not entitled to a discharge. 267 F.2d at 300. Cohen's debt to the tenants, including the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, is therefore nondischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code.

In conclusion, the debt incurred by Cohen through his fraudulent conduct is nondischargeable under Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. The treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are also nondischargeable, as they are intended to punish and deter fraudulent conduct. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
