**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, to sustain punitive damages under Title VII. The answer is yes. Section 1981a(b)(1) authorizes punitive damages only where the plaintiff demonstrates “malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” That standard demands more than simple intentional discrimination. It requires conduct that is independently egregious or outrageous, reflecting a culpable state of mind beyond the intent necessary for liability.

**A. The Plain Language of § 1981a Requires More Than Intentional Discrimination**

The text of § 1981a(b)(1) conditions punitive damages on proof of “malice or reckless indifference.” This language, as the House Report explained, imposes “an even higher standard” than the “intentional discrimination” needed for compensatory relief. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 29 (1991). Malice requires “evil motive or intent,” and reckless indifference demands “callous disregard” for federal rights. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (interpreting analogous standard under § 1983). Mere proof of discriminatory motive—the standard for liability—does not alone establish this heightened mental state.

Every circuit to confront the issue under § 1981, which provides the template for § 1981a, has required egregious conduct. *See, e.g., Beauford v. Sisters of Mercy*, 816 F.2d 1104, 1109 (6th Cir. 1987) (requiring “egregious” discrimination); *Rowlett v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc.*, 832 F.2d 194, 205 (1st Cir. 1987) (same); *Stephens v. South Atlantic Canners, Inc.*, 848 F.2d 484, 488 (4th Cir. 1988) (same). In *Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co.*, 817 F.2d 1290, 1296 (7th Cir. 1987), the court observed that punitive damages under § 1981 require that “the application of the law to the facts at hand was so clear at the time of the act that reasonably competent people would have agreed on its application”—a standard that presupposes egregious disregard. And in *Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co.*, 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996), the same circuit recognized that § 1981a itself imposes a “higher hurdle” than simple intent. These uniform interpretations confirm that Congress, by adopting similar language, intended to reserve punitive damages for the most blameworthy conduct.

**B. Legislative History Confirms an “Extraordinarily Egregious” Standard**

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 underscores this meaning. Senator Dole, a key sponsor, stated that punitive damages are available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Interpretive Memorandum). This statement—uncontradicted by any competing interpretation—reflects the consensus that § 1981a demands independent egregiousness. The House Report likewise emphasized that punitive damages require “clear and convincing evidence” of “malice or reckless indifference,” a heavier burden than preponderance of the evidence. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 29. These pronouncements are consistent with the common law tradition that punitive damages “are not compensation for injury” but “private fines” to punish “reprehensible conduct.” *Browning-Ferris Industries v. Kelco Disposal*, 492 U.S. 257, 275 (1989) (quotation omitted). Absent egregiousness, such punishment is unwarranted.

**C. The Common Law and Precedent Support an Independent Egregiousness Requirement**

At common law, punitive damages require “something more than the mere commission of a tort.” *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. at 58-59 (Rehnquist, J., dissenting) (describing traditional “egregious” standard). This Court has consistently held that punitive damages are reserved for “outrageous” conduct. *Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.*, 418 U.S. 323, 350 (1974) (First Amendment context). The same principle applies in civil rights cases. In *Patterson v. McLean Credit Union*, 491 U.S. 164, 189 (1989), the Court noted that punitive damages under § 1981 are available only when the defendant acts with “malicious or reckless” intent—a standard that “focus[es] on the nature of the defendant’s conduct.” Conduct that is merely discriminatory, without more, lacks the independent outrage necessary to justify punitive awards.

**D. The Lower Court Correctly Applied the Egregiousness Standard**

The D.C. Circuit, sitting en banc, properly held that punitive damages under § 1981a require a showing of egregiousness beyond intentional discrimination. The court’s decision aligns with the statute’s text, legislative history, and the uniform weight of circuit authority. *Cf. Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 254 (1981) (deference to factfinder’s assessment of pretext). The district court’s refusal to submit punitive damages to the jury was thus correct because Kolstad failed to demonstrate that the ADA’s conduct was “extraordinarily egregious.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473. At most, she proved a disputed promotion decision—hardly the outrageous conduct Congress intended to punish.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. An employer’s conduct must be independently egregious or outrageous, not merely intentional, to sustain punitive damages under Title VII.
