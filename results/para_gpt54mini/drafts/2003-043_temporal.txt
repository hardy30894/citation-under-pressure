The government did not need to assess each individual partner to obtain the ten-year collection period. Under the Internal Revenue Code, assessment is the recording of “the liability of the taxpayer,” 26 U.S.C. § 6203, and the term “taxpayer” includes any “person subject to any internal revenue tax,” including a partnership and an individual. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). Where the liability assessed is the partnership’s tax debt, a timely assessment against the partnership is a timely assessment of that debt; it is not defeated simply because the government did not repeat the same administrative act against every partner. See Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960) (“[Appellant] is personally liable for the debts and liabilities of the partnership, including its tax liability”); United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458, 462-63 (9th Cir. 1961) (recognizing that the government’s collection remedies may proceed from the assessed partnership debt).  

The better reading of the pre-1970 authorities is that assessment fixes the debt and permits collection from those legally liable for it. In Young v. Riddell, the district court explained that where taxes are assessed against a partnership and state law makes the partners jointly and severally liable, “it is unnecessary and superfluous to name the individual partners in the assessment in order to create liability; their liability arises as a matter of state law.” Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959), aff’d, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). The court further observed that naming individual partners is required only “to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action.” Id. at 76,050. That is exactly this case: the partnership debt was timely assessed, and the government now seeks to collect from partners who are substantively liable under state law. The assessment need not be duplicated to preserve that right.  

Coson does not hold otherwise. There, the district court noted that the taxpayer had “never was assessed” individually and reasoned that a lien could not attach to a particular individual’s property absent an underlying assessment against him. Coson v. United States, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). But this Court affirmed on different grounds and did not adopt a rule that separate assessments are always required before the government may reach a partner’s assets. Coson, 286 F.2d at 458, 462-63. At most, Coson reflects the familiar principle that a lien or forced collection remedy must rest on a valid underlying tax obligation; it does not convert a partnership assessment into a nullity as to the partners who are liable for the partnership’s debt.  

Nor do the cases cited by the court below compel a contrary result. The government’s position follows from the partnership-law premise that partners are liable for partnership debts. As this Court recognized in Young, once that substantive liability exists, the absence of the partner’s name on the assessment does not erase liability; it affects only the government’s mode of collection. Young, 283 F.2d at 910. The government does not contend that a partnership assessment automatically becomes a personal assessment of each partner for all purposes. It contends only that a timely assessment of the partnership debt starts the collection period for that debt, including collection from those who are already liable for it under state law. That view is consistent with Young’s treatment of liability and with Coson’s focus on the validity of the underlying assessment.  

At bottom, respondents seek to add a requirement Congress did not impose: that the Service must restate the same partnership debt separately against every partner in order to preserve the ten-year period. The pre-1970 authorities do not support such a rule. Because the partnership debt was timely assessed, the government’s collection period was extended, and the government may proceed against the partners liable for that debt. Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960); Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831 (S.D. Cal. 1959); Coson v. United States, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961); Coson v. United States, 169 F. Supp. 671 (S.D. Cal. 1958).
