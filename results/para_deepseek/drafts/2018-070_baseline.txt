**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), adopts state law as surrogate federal law only to fill a significant void or gap in federal law. The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, regardless of whether federal law already covers the subject. That interpretation contradicts the statute’s text, this Court’s precedents, and the uniform approach of the Fifth Circuit.

**I. The Text and Structure of OCSLA Require a Gap-Filling Role for State Law**

Section 1333(a)(2)(A) provides that state law applies “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” The phrase “to the extent that” is a limiting qualifier. It directs courts to adopt state law only insofar as federal law does not already supply a rule of decision. As the Fifth Circuit explained, state law is incorporated “only where federal law is not ‘applicable’ or where there is a ‘gap’ in federal law.” *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). This reading gives effect to the word “extent” and avoids rendering the federal-law clause superfluous.

The OCSLA’s structure confirms this. Section 1333(a)(1) extends “the Constitution and laws of the United States” to the OCS “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” That language mirrors the federal-enclave model, under which state law supplies only where Congress has not occupied the field. *See* *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355–56 (1969) (treating OCS platforms as “federal enclaves”). In enclaves, state law applies only to fill gaps in federal law. *See* *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 293 (1958) (Assimilative Crimes Act adopts state law “to fill gaps in the Federal criminal code”).

Congress enacted OCSLA because federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). The purpose was to supply a “body of law” for the OCS, not to displace federal law where it already governs. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355. The gap-filling interpretation is thus faithful to Congress’s intent.

**II. This Court’s Precedent Endorses the Gap-Filling Approach**

In *Rodrigue*, this Court applied Louisiana wrongful-death law under OCSLA only after concluding that the Death on the High Seas Act did not apply to fixed platforms. 395 U.S. at 366. The Court held that “any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law” was removed because federal maritime law did not reach the accidents. *Id.* The Court did not apply state law simply because it was “not inconsistent” with federal law; it applied state law because federal law left a gap.

Similarly, in *Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19 (1988), this Court addressed state taxation under OCSLA. The Court noted that OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision “adopts state law as federal law” only “to the extent that it is not inconsistent with federal law.” *Id.* at 27. The Court did not suggest that state law could apply concurrently with federal law on the same subject. Instead, it recognized that OCSLA “borrows” state law to fill “gaps” in federal law. *Id.* at 28 n.9.

The Fifth Circuit has consistently applied the gap test. *See* *Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990) (state law applies only if “there is a gap in federal law”); *Breton Energy, L.L.C. v. Mariner Energy Res., Inc.*, 764 F.3d 394, 398 (5th Cir. 2014) (same). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule would create a circuit split and undermine the uniformity that OCSLA was designed to achieve.

**III. The FLSA Is a Comprehensive Federal Scheme That Leaves No Gaps**

The Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 201–219, establishes a comprehensive regime for minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping. It covers employees “engaged in commerce or in the production of goods for commerce,” 29 U.S.C. § 206(a), and its regulations address meal periods, rest breaks, and pay frequency. *See* 29 C.F.R. §§ 785.19, 785.22. The FLSA leaves no “significant void or gap” for state wage-and-hour law to fill on the OCS. *Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036.

The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), preserves more protective state laws “in any State.” But that clause operates as a preemption rule for states within their territorial jurisdiction. On the OCS, there is no state territory; state law applies only as surrogate federal law under OCSLA. The savings clause does not authorize states to regulate conduct on federal enclaves. *See* *Parker Drilling Mgmt. Servs., Ltd. v. Newton*, 139 S. Ct. 1881, 1888 (2019) (Thomas, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (noting that the savings clause “does not speak to the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision”). Applying California’s wage-and-hour laws on the OCS would displace the FLSA’s uniform standards and create inconsistency, not fill a gap.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Test Would Undermine Federal Uniformity**

The Ninth Circuit’s “applicable and not inconsistent” standard would allow each adjacent state to impose its own wage-and-hour rules on OCS platforms. That would defeat OCSLA’s purpose of providing “a stable and predictable body of law” for offshore operations. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355. The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision is designed to ensure that federal law governs, with state law serving only as a backup. The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation turns that structure on its head.

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and hold that state law applies under OCSLA only when there is a gap in federal law.
