**ARGUMENT**

I. The Seventh Circuit Correctly Recognized That a Plaintiff May Bring a “Class of One” Equal Protection Claim Without Alleging Membership in a Class or Group.

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment provides that no state shall “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has long recognized that the Clause prohibits not only discriminatory classifications, but also arbitrary and irrational governmental action directed at an individual. *See Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886) (“[T]he equal protection of the laws is a pledge of the protection of equal laws.”). The Seventh Circuit, in the decision below, correctly held that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim where state action is motivated by “a spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 160 F.3d 386, 388 (7th Cir. 1998) (quoting *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Hous. Dev. Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977)).

This Court has repeatedly affirmed that the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from arbitrary and irrational treatment, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a suspect class or discrete group. In *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center*, 473 U.S. 432 (1985), the Court rejected the argument that mental retardation constituted a quasi-suspect class, yet still subjected the challenged zoning ordinance to heightened scrutiny because the ordinance “burden[ed] a group with a history of unequal treatment.” *Id.* at 440. More directly, in *Nordlinger v. Hahn*, 505 U.S. 1 (1992), the Court upheld a property tax assessment scheme against an equal protection challenge, but cautioned that “[a]s a general rule, the Equal Protection Clause is satisfied so long as the classification is rationally related to a legitimate governmental purpose.” *Id.* at 10 (emphasis added). The Court did not require the plaintiff to demonstrate membership in a suspect class or discrete group. *See also Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620, 631 (1996) (invalidating a state constitutional amendment that “impos[ed] a broad disability on [a group] alone” without requiring the plaintiffs to belong to a suspect class).

The Seventh Circuit’s recognition of the “class of one” theory is consistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, the Seventh Circuit held that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim where she alleges that state action was motivated by “spite or ill will,” without alleging membership in a class or group. 160 F.3d at 388. This theory is grounded in the principle that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary and irrational governmental action, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a suspect class or discrete group. *See Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 369; *Nordlinger*, 505 U.S. at 10.

II. The Seventh Circuit’s Decision Does Not Conflict with This Court’s Precedents.

Respondent erroneously contends that the Seventh Circuit’s decision conflicts with this Court’s precedents. (Resp. Br. at 12-15.) Respondent cites *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), for the proposition that the Equal Protection Clause only prohibits discriminatory classifications, not arbitrary and irrational governmental action. (Resp. Br. at 12.) Respondent misreads *Washington*. In *Washington*, the Court held that a facially neutral law does not violate the Equal Protection Clause unless it is shown to have a discriminatory purpose and effect. 426 U.S. at 240. The Court did not hold that the Equal Protection Clause only prohibits discriminatory classifications. *Id.* Indeed, the Court explicitly recognized that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary and irrational governmental action. *Id.* at 239 (“[T]he Constitution is not violated by an act that is not based on racial discrimination simply because it has a racially disproportionate impact.”).

Respondent also cites *Arlington Heights*, 429 U.S. at 265, for the proposition that the Equal Protection Clause only prohibits discriminatory classifications. (Resp. Br. at 13.) Respondent again misreads the opinion. In *Arlington Heights*, the Court held that a plaintiff must demonstrate a discriminatory purpose to state an equal protection claim. 429 U.S. at 265. The Court did not hold that the Equal Protection Clause only prohibits discriminatory classifications. *Id.* Indeed, the Court explicitly recognized that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary and irrational governmental action. *Id.* at 265 n.21 (“[T]he Equal Protection Clause is not violated by the mere fact that a particular impact is felt more keenly by one race than another.”).

III. The Seventh Circuit’s Decision Does Not Create a Circuit Split.

Respondent erroneously contends that the Seventh Circuit’s decision creates a circuit split. (Resp. Br. at 15-18.) Respondent cites *Blessing v. Freestone*, 520 U.S. 329 (1997), for the proposition that the Seventh Circuit’s decision conflicts with this Court’s precedents. (Resp. Br. at 15.) Respondent misreads *Blessing*. In *Blessing*, the Court held that a plaintiff must demonstrate a violation of a federal right to state a claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. 520 U.S. at 340. The Court did not address the “class of one” theory or the Equal Protection Clause. *Id.*

Respondent also cites *Engquist v. Oregon Dep’t of Agric.*, 553 U.S. 591 (2008), for the proposition that the Seventh Circuit’s decision conflicts with this Court’s precedents. (Resp. Br. at 16.) Respondent misreads *Engquist*. In *Engquist*, the Court held that the Equal Protection Clause does not prohibit arbitrary and irrational governmental action in the public employment context. 553 U.S. at 604. The Court did not address the “class of one” theory or the Equal Protection Clause in the context of a “class of one” claim. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should grant the petition for a writ of certiorari and reverse the judgment of the Seventh Circuit.
