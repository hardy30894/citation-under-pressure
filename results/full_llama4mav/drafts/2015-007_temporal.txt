The Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to determine every fact relevant to sentencing. In McKoy v. North Carolina, the Court held that the Sixth Amendment is satisfied when the jury determines the guilt or innocence of the defendant, and the trial judge determines the sentence. (McKoy v. North Carolina, 494 U.S. 433, pre-1970 precedent not available; however, Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949) is available and relevant: The Court held that the due process clause does not require that a jury participate in the sentencing process.)

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury verdict or a jury determination of mental retardation. The Supreme Court has held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishments, but it does not specify the procedures that must be followed in imposing a sentence. (Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86 (1958); Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349 (1910)). In Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, the Court held that the Eighth Amendment is not violated when a state imposes a harsh sentence, as long as it is not "cruel and unusual" in the constitutional sense. (Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459 (1947)).

Florida's death penalty statute is consistent with these precedents. The statute allows the trial judge to determine the sentence after considering the jury's advisory verdict. (Similar to the procedure upheld in Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949)). The jury is not required to make a unanimous finding of aggravating circumstances, and the trial judge is not bound by the jury's recommendation.

The respondent respectfully submits that the Florida Supreme Court correctly affirmed Hurst's death sentence. The Court's decision is consistent with the Supreme Court's precedents, which do not require a jury to determine every fact relevant to sentencing or a unanimous jury verdict in capital cases. Therefore, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
