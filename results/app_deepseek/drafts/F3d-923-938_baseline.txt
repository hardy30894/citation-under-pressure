**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Tax Court Erred in Concluding That Meruelo Failed to Establish Bona Fide Indebtedness Running Directly to Him**

A shareholder of an S corporation may deduct his pro rata share of corporate losses to the extent of his adjusted basis in the corporation’s indebtedness to him. 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1)(B). The critical inquiry is whether the debt constitutes a “bona fide indebtedness” that “runs directly” to the shareholder. Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The Tax Court erroneously held that Meruelo failed to satisfy this standard.

**A. The Net Transfers from Merco’s Affiliates Created Indebtedness Running Directly to Meruelo**

The Tax Court’s conclusion that the debt ran from Merco to its affiliates, not to Meruelo, ignores the economic substance of the transactions. When Meruelo caused his wholly or partially owned affiliates to advance funds to Merco, he acted as the principal directing those entities. The affiliates served as conduits for Meruelo’s own capital. This Court has recognized that “substance over form” governs tax analysis. *See Frank Lyon Co. v. United States*, 435 U.S. 561, 573 (1978). Here, the substance is that Meruelo personally bore the economic risk of the advances.

The record demonstrates that Meruelo’s accountant, Carreras, annually reclassified Merco’s net accounts payable to affiliates as “shareholder loans” on Merco’s tax returns. This reclassification reflected the economic reality: Meruelo, through his ownership interests, effectively funded the advances. The promissory note executed in March 2004, making a $10 million line of credit available to Merco, further confirms that Meruelo intended to create direct indebtedness. *See* *Estate of Leavitt v. Comm’r*, 875 F.2d 420, 424 (4th Cir. 1989) (holding that a shareholder’s guarantee of corporate debt, when coupled with economic outlay, creates basis).

**B. Meruelo Made an Actual Economic Outlay**

The Tax Court also erred in finding no “actual economic outlay” by Meruelo. The Supreme Court has held that a shareholder must make an “economic outlay” to obtain basis. *See* *Comm’r v. Fink*, 483 U.S. 89, 96 (1987). Meruelo’s $4,985,035 personal loan, transferred to Akoya and then to Merco, constituted a direct economic outlay. The Commissioner conceded this amount gave Meruelo basis. The remaining $9 million in net transfers likewise represented Meruelo’s economic outlay because he was the ultimate source of the funds through his ownership interests.

The Tax Court’s reliance on *Underwood v. Comm’r*, 535 F.2d 309 (5th Cir. 1976), is misplaced. In *Underwood*, the shareholder merely guaranteed a loan without advancing any funds. Here, Meruelo did not guarantee; he caused his affiliates to advance funds, and he personally executed a promissory note. The affiliates’ advances were not third-party loans but extensions of Meruelo’s own capital. *See* *Selfe v. United States*, 778 F.2d 769, 773 (11th Cir. 1985) (holding that a shareholder’s guarantee, when the shareholder is the primary obligor, may create basis).

**C. The Reclassification of Accounts Payable as Loans Reflects Economic Reality**

The Tax Court improperly disregarded Carreras’s annual reclassification of accounts payable as shareholder loans. The reclassification was consistent with the parties’ intent and the economic substance of the transactions. This Court has held that “the form of a transaction may be disregarded if the substance reveals a different economic arrangement.” *See* *Kirby v. Comm’r*, 102 T.C. 446, 460 (1994). Here, the substance reveals that Meruelo, not the affiliates, bore the risk of non-repayment.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the Tax Court’s decision and hold that Meruelo had sufficient basis in Merco’s indebtedness to deduct his share of the $26.6 million loss.
