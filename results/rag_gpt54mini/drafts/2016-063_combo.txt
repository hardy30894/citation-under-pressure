The decision below should be reversed because a Board order dismissing a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court, not the Federal Circuit. The Civil Service Reform Act channels “cases of discrimination” to district court, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2), and the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that jurisdictional and merits questions are analytically distinct. See Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596 (2012); Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998).

The statutory scheme begins with the premise that review of MSPB decisions ordinarily lies in the Federal Circuit, but Congress created a specific exception for mixed cases. Under 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), a mixed case is one in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [he] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board” and alleges discrimination. Kloeckner held that when the Board rejects a mixed case, judicial review belongs in district court. 568 U.S. at 50–52, 56. The Court’s reasoning was not limited to the particular procedural defect there; rather, it focused on the statutory text and Congress’s choice to route discrimination claims to the district courts. Id. at 50–51, 55–56.

That reading is confirmed by the structure of the CSRA. Section 7703(b)(1)(A) supplies the default forum: the Federal Circuit. Section 7703(b)(2) creates the mixed-case exception. In Kloeckner, the Court rejected a forum rule that would force employees into the Federal Circuit whenever the Board did not reach the merits. 568 U.S. at 50, 55–57. The Court explained that “mixed cases—those appealable to the MSPB and alleging discrimination—shall be filed in district court.” Id. at 55. Nothing in the text suggests that a discrimination claimant must split review between district court and the Federal Circuit depending on whether the Board labels its threshold ruling “procedural” or “jurisdictional.”

Perry’s case is a mixed case. He alleged that adverse actions affecting his employment were motivated by race, age, disability, and retaliation. The Board’s dismissal rested on its conclusion that the underlying settlement and retirement were voluntary, and therefore outside its jurisdiction. That threshold ruling does not alter the nature of the case. Jurisdictional label or not, Perry “allege[d] that a basis for the action was discrimination.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(B). As Kloeckner teaches, the relevant question is whether the employee brought a mixed case to the Board, not whether the Board later reached the merits. 568 U.S. at 54–57.

The government’s contrary position relies on a false distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals. But the Court has long treated jurisdictional determinations as distinct from merits adjudications. See Levers v. Anderson, 326 U.S. 219 (1945); Elgin, Joliet & Eastern Railway Co. v. Burley, 325 U.S. 711 (1945). In the administrative setting, when a tribunal dismisses for lack of jurisdiction, it is not deciding the underlying discrimination claim; it is deciding only whether it may hear the case at all. See National Labor Relations Board v. MacKay Radio & Telegraph Co., 304 U.S. 333 (1938). That is precisely why a uniform district-court forum makes sense for mixed cases: the district court can review the discrimination allegations and the threshold question together, avoiding fragmented review.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on Powell cannot stand in light of Kloeckner. Although Powell, 158 F.3d 597, held that jurisdictional dismissals go to the Federal Circuit, that rule is incompatible with Kloeckner’s broad understanding of mixed-case review. Kloeckner did not turn on the Board’s reason for dismissal; it turned on the fact that the employee brought a mixed case. 568 U.S. at 53–57. The Supreme Court has also recognized that when an employee is “affected by an action” and raises discrimination, district-court review is the statutory default for that class of cases. Id. at 54–55. Powell’s contrary rule creates the very fragmentation Kloeckner rejected.

The jurisdictional nature of the MSPB’s ruling here does not justify a different outcome. This Court has long recognized that appellate jurisdiction should not depend on hypertechnical distinctions that defeat the statutory scheme. See Beadles v. Smyser, 209 U.S. 393 (1908); Glaspell v. Northern Pacific Railroad, 144 U.S. 211 (1892). And where Congress has created a special review channel for a category of cases, courts should honor that channel. See Hussey v. United States, 222 U.S. 88 (1911); Douglas v. Green, 363 U.S. 192 (1960). Under the CSRA, the channel for mixed cases is district court.

Finally, nothing in the Board’s ability to characterize its ruling as “jurisdictional” can defeat Congress’s forum choice. Agencies cannot by nomenclature alter judicial review prescribed by statute. See Local No. 207, International Ass’n of Bridge, Structural & Ornamental Iron Workers Union v. Perko, 373 U.S. 701 (1963); Lerner v. Casey, 357 U.S. 468 (1958). Because Perry brought a mixed case and the Board dismissed it without reaching the discrimination merits, review lies in district court.

The judgment should be reversed.
