ARGUMENT

The district court erred in certifying the class because plaintiffs failed to adduce adequate admissible evidence that damages are susceptible of measurement on a class-wide basis. Rule 23(b)(3) demands that common questions predominate. This requirement cannot be satisfied when each class member must separately establish the fact and extent of injury.

The Sherman Act confers a right of action only upon a person who has been injured in his business or property. Each plaintiff therefore bears the burden of proving actual damages. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931) (“The plaintiff is required to prove his damages.”). Where the proof of damages turns on individualized facts—such as the particular services purchased, the duration of subscription, and the effect of local competition—those questions necessarily predominate over any common issues. See Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32, 44 (1940) (class treatment is proper only when the interests of absent members are “fairly represented” through issues common to the whole group).

Early decisions confirm that class certification is inappropriate when damages cannot be shown by evidence common to the class. In Supreme Tribe of Ben Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 366 (1921), the Court emphasized that a class suit may proceed only when the rights asserted are “joint, or common, or secondary.” Separate damage claims lack that character. Similarly, in Smith v. Swormstedt, 57 U.S. 288, 302 (1853), the Court required that the controversy involve “a common interest” shared by all parties; divergent damage calculations destroy that unity.

Because plaintiffs offered no competent proof that damages could be calculated for the entire class through common evidence, the predominance requirement was not met. The certification order therefore rests on an erroneous legal standard and must be reversed.
