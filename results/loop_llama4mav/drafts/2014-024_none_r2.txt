ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit's affirmance of Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) without requiring proof of his subjective intent to threaten is inconsistent with established First Amendment jurisprudence. The "true threats" exception, which § 875(c) embodies, has long been understood to require a showing of the speaker's intent to convey a threat.

The Supreme Court has consistently emphasized the importance of intent in determining the constitutionality of speech regulations. In Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 309-310 (1940), the Court held that the speaker's intent is a crucial factor in evaluating the constitutionality of a statute regulating speech. Similarly, in Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 573-574 (1942), the Court upheld a statute prohibiting "fighting words" because such words, by their very utterance, inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace, implying that the speaker's intent is essential.

Lower courts have also recognized the significance of intent in determining whether a statement constitutes a threat. The Seventh Circuit, in United States v. Prochaska, 222 F.2d 1, 2 (7th Cir. 1955), and the Second Circuit, in United States v. Carrier, 168 F.2d 881, 882 (2d Cir. 1948), have held that a threat requires an intent to cause fear or apprehension. These decisions demonstrate that the speaker's intent is a critical factor in assessing the nature of a statement.

The Third Circuit's reliance on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), which adopted an objective standard for determining true threats, is misplaced. Kosma's holding is inconsistent with the long line of cases emphasizing the importance of the speaker's intent. In Pierce v. United States, 314 U.S. 306, 311 (1941), and Gorin v. United States, 312 U.S. 19, 27-28 (1941), the Court held that statutes regulating speech require proof of the defendant's intent to convey a prohibited message.

Furthermore, the Court has consistently required a showing of intent to incite violence or lawlessness when regulating speech. In Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949), and Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242, 259-260 (1937), the Court held that convictions for disorderly conduct and insurrection, respectively, require a showing of intent. These decisions reinforce the necessity of a subjective intent standard in determining the constitutionality of speech regulations.

The requirement of subjective intent is also consistent with the Court's decisions in cases such as Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), which highlight the importance of intent in determining the constitutionality of speech regulations. In light of these precedents, it is clear that a conviction under § 875(c) necessitates proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten.

The Third Circuit's decision to the contrary should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with a subjective intent standard. By requiring proof of Elonis' subjective intent to threaten, the Court can ensure that the "true threats" exception is applied in a manner consistent with the First Amendment's protections.
