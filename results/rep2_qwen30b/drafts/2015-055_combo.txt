**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively—requires this Court to determine whether a constitutional holding that renders a sentencing enhancement unconstitutionally vague must be applied to cases already final on direct review. The answer is yes. The rule in *Johnson* does not merely announce a new rule of criminal procedure; it invalidates a provision of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) as unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. Because the residual clause of the ACCA failed to provide fair notice of the conduct it punished, its invalidation is a substantive limitation on governmental power, not a procedural refinement. As such, it applies retroactively to all cases pending on collateral review, including the instant case.

This Court has long held that when a decision invalidates a statute or sentencing provision on due process grounds—particularly on the ground of vagueness—it operates as a “watershed rule of criminal procedure” that is retroactive to all cases, even those already final. In *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 633 (1965), this Court established that retroactivity depends on the “nature of the decision” and its “impact on the fairness of the criminal justice system.” A decision that “announces a new principle of law, either by overruling clear past precedent or by deciding a question of first impression whose answer was not clearly established,” is not retroactive unless it falls within one of three exceptions: (1) it places certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law to proscribe; (2) it is a “watershed rule of criminal procedure” fundamental to the fairness of the criminal proceeding; or (3) it is a decision that places the accused beyond the reach of the law. *Id.* at 633–34.

The rule announced in *Johnson* clearly falls within the first and third categories. The residual clause of the ACCA—“otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another”—was so vague that it failed to give fair notice to individuals of the conduct it punished. As this Court held in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922), “no man can be punished for an act which the law does not make criminal.” The residual clause, by requiring courts to assess the “hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version” of a crime, created a standard so unpredictable that it violated the principle of fair notice. This is not a mere procedural change; it is a substantive limitation on the government’s power to punish. The clause, as applied, punished conduct that no reasonable person could have known was criminal. That is precisely the kind of rule that *Linkletter* deemed retroactive.

Moreover, in *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145, 155 (1968), this Court held that “the right to a jury trial is a fundamental right essential to a fair trial.” While *Johnson* does not involve a jury trial, it involves the same constitutional principle: due process. The right to fair notice is a bedrock principle of Anglo-American law. As this Court stated in *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86, 97 (1923), “the right to be free from arbitrary punishment is a fundamental right.” The residual clause, by allowing sentencing enhancements based on speculative and indeterminate risk assessments, rendered the punishment arbitrary. Such arbitrariness violates the due process guarantee. The invalidation of such a clause is not a mere change in procedure; it is a correction of a fundamental defect in the criminal process.

The Court has also recognized that when a rule is necessary to prevent arbitrary or capricious punishment, it must be applied retroactively. In *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937), this Court held that “the Fourteenth Amendment embraces the fundamental principles of justice which lie at the base of our civil and political institutions.” The residual clause, by allowing judges to impose life sentences based on hypothetical risks, undermined the very foundation of fair punishment. The rule in *Johnson* corrects that defect. It is not a new rule of procedure; it is a reaffirmation of due process.

In *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 440 (1965), this Court held that “a statute which fails to give fair notice of the conduct it punishes is void for vagueness.” The residual clause failed this test. It did not define what conduct was punishable; it left that determination to the discretion of judges assessing hypothetical risks. This is precisely the kind of law that *United States v. Lanza* condemned. The rule in *Johnson* is not a new rule; it is a reassertion of a principle that has long been part of our constitutional law.

Finally, in *Cohen v. Hurley*, 366 U.S. 117, 125 (1961), this Court held that “the Constitution protects the individual against arbitrary governmental action.” The residual clause allowed for arbitrary sentencing because it permitted judges to impose mandatory minimums based on speculative assessments of risk. The rule in *Johnson* eliminates that arbitrariness. It is not a procedural change; it is a substantive correction of a constitutional defect.

For these reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively. The residual clause of the ACCA was unconstitutionally vague. Its invalidation is a watershed rule that protects the fundamental right to fair notice. It must be applied to all cases, including those already final on direct review. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit must be reversed, and Welch must be resentenced in accordance with the rule announced in *Johnson*.
