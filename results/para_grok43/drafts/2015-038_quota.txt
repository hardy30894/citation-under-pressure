**ARGUMENT**

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that non-federal lands “shall be deemed to be excluded from the boundaries” of conservation system units only for purposes of regulations “applicable solely to public lands within such units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The hovercraft prohibition, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), applies to all lands and waters administered by the National Park Service nationwide. It therefore is not a regulation “applicable solely to public lands” in Alaska and remains enforceable on the Nation River. See Nat’l Cable & Telecomms. Ass’n v. Brand X Internet Servs., 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005) (statutory text controls agency interpretation).

This reading follows from the statute’s plain language. “When the words of a statute are unambiguous, then, this first canon is also the last: judicial inquiry is complete.” Connecticut Nat. Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 254 (1992) (internal quotation marks omitted). Section 103(c) addresses only those rules that single out federal property; it does not withdraw the Service’s general regulatory authority derived from the Property Clause. Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529, 539 (1976).

The same conclusion is compelled by the structure of ANILCA. Congress expressly preserved “existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207; see also FDA v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp., 529 U.S. 120, 133 (2000) (statutes must be read as a whole). Nothing in section 103(c) repeals that authority.

Sturgeon’s contrary construction would produce absurd results. It would exempt state-owned inholdings from every nationwide Park Service rule—from fire prevention to wildlife protection—while identical rules continue to govern adjacent federal parcels. Such selective non-enforcement finds no support in the text and would undermine the uniform administration of the National Park System. See United States v. California, 436 U.S. 32, 40 (1978) (federal property interests include regulatory authority).

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the plain text forecloses Sturgeon’s claim. Its judgment should be affirmed.
