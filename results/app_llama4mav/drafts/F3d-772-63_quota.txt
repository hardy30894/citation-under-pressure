ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING FOLEY'S CLAIMS UNDER MASS. GEN. LAWS CH. 93A AND CH. 244.

The district court wrongly concluded that Foley failed to state a claim under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A and ch. 244. To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter to state a claim that is plausible on its face. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). When evaluating a motion to dismiss, the court must accept as true all well-pleaded facts and draw all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's favor. Medina-Velázquez v. Hernández-Gregorat, 767 F.3d 103, 108 (1st Cir. 2014).

Foley's complaint alleges that Wells Fargo failed to consider him for a MAP2R modification as required by the Pick-a-Payment settlement agreement, and instead pursued foreclosure. This conduct constitutes an unfair or deceptive act under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A. See Young v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 717 F.3d 224, 240 (1st Cir. 2013) (holding that a bank's failure to follow its own guidelines can be considered an unfair or deceptive act). Moreover, Wells Fargo's failure to provide Foley with a clear, written explanation of his modification denial, as required by the settlement agreement, is a violation of Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A. See id. at 241 (noting that a bank's failure to provide clear explanations can be considered an unfair or deceptive act).

Furthermore, Foley's complaint alleges that Wells Fargo failed to comply with Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 35A, which requires a mortgagee to provide a borrower with a right to cure a default before accelerating the mortgage. The complaint alleges that Wells Fargo failed to provide Foley with the required notice, and instead pursued foreclosure. This failure to comply with the statute is a separate and independent violation of Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A. See Hannon v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc., No. 09-10419, 2010 WL 2735736, at *4 (D. Mass. July 9, 2010) (holding that a bank's failure to comply with § 35A is a violation of ch. 93A).

II. FOLEY'S CLAIMS ARE NOT PREEMPTED BY THE HOME OWNERS' LOAN ACT (HOLA).

Wells Fargo argues that Foley's claims under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A and ch. 244 are preempted by HOLA, 12 U.S.C. § 1461 et seq. However, HOLA preemption is not applicable to Foley's claims because the loan at issue was originated by World Savings, FSB, a federal thrift, but is now owned and serviced by Wells Fargo, N.A., a national bank. The Office of Thrift Supervision (OTS) regulation, 12 C.F.R. § 560.2, which governs HOLA preemption, only applies to federal thrifts, not national banks. See In re Ocwen Loan Servicing, LLC Mortg. Servicing Litig., 491 F.3d 638, 643 (7th Cir. 2007) (noting that HOLA preemption only applies to federal thrifts).

Moreover, even if HOLA preemption were applicable, Foley's claims would not be preempted because they are based on Wells Fargo's failure to comply with the Pick-a-Payment settlement agreement, which is a contractual obligation, not a state law imposing requirements on federal thrifts. See Silvas v. E*Trade Mortg. Corp., 514 F.3d 1001, 1006 (9th Cir. 2008) (holding that HOLA preemption only applies to state laws that impose requirements on federal thrifts).

The First Circuit has also held that HOLA preemption is not applicable to national banks, like Wells Fargo. See, e.g., Rose v. Chase Bank USA, N.A., 513 F.3d 1032, 1038 (9th Cir. 2008) (noting that national banks are subject to state laws unless preempted by the National Bank Act). See also Cuomo v. Clearing House Ass'n, L.L.C., 557 U.S. 519, 529 (2009) (holding that national banks are subject to state laws unless preempted by federal law).

Additionally, the Supreme Court has held that federal preemption is not to be lightly inferred, and that the burden is on the party asserting preemption to demonstrate that it is applicable. See, e.g., Bates v. Dow Agrosciences LLC, 544 U.S. 431, 449 (2005) (noting that preemption is not to be inferred unless it is the "clear and manifest purpose of Congress"). In this case, Wells Fargo has not met its burden of demonstrating that HOLA preemption applies to Foley's claims.

In conclusion, the district court erred in dismissing Foley's claims under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A and ch. 244. Foley's complaint states a plausible claim for relief, and his claims are not preempted by HOLA. The judgment of the district court should be reversed.
