ARGUMENT

**I. The text of §875(c) and settled canons of construction require proof of subjective intent.**

Every federal criminal statute that imposes liability without proof of a culpable mental state is disfavored. In *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952), this Court observed: "The contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion." When Congress uses a word like "threat," it invokes the familiar meaning of that term: a declaration of the speaker's intention to injure. A communication that an objective listener regards as threatening, but that the defendant never intended as a declaration of his own resolve, is not a threat "contained in" the communication within the meaning of the statute. The Third Circuit's objective standard reads the speaker out of the statute.

**II. The First Amendment's "true threat" exception turns on the speaker's subjective purpose.**

In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707 (1969), this Court reversed a conviction under a similar threat statute, stating: "The statute initially requires the Government to prove a true 'threat.'"
