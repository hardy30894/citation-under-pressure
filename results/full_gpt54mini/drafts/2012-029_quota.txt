**Argument**

Federal courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction merely because a state-law claim requires application of patent law at some stage. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), exclusivity turns on whether the case “arises under” federal patent law, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “arising under” jurisdiction is narrow. *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988); *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912); *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936). This Court correctly rejected the broad federal-court rule the Federal Circuit announced in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and the Supreme Court should do the same.

Under *Christianson*, a patent case arises under § 1338(a) only if “federal patent law creates the cause of action” or if “the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” 486 U.S. at 808–09. That test does not convert every malpractice case with a patent issue into an exclusive federal case. The Court in *Christianson* emphasized that jurisdiction depends on what appears on the face of the well-pleaded complaint, not on defenses or collateral issues. Id. at 809. Here, the cause of action is legal malpractice, a classic state-law claim. See *Minton v. Gunn*, 355 S.W.3d 634, 640–41 (Tex. 2011). Patent law appears only as an element of the “case within a case” showing on causation. Id. at 641; *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., Inc.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). That is not enough to create exclusive federal jurisdiction.

The Supreme Court’s modern embedded-federal-question cases confirm the point. In *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, the Court held that federal jurisdiction exists only when a state claim necessarily raises a federal issue that is “actually disputed and substantial,” and only if federal jurisdiction will not upset the congressionally approved balance between federal and state courts. 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). That framework is restrictive, not expansive. See *Merrell Dow Pharm. Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 810–12 (1986). The Court later reiterated that the “substantial” inquiry concerns the importance of the issue to the federal system as a whole, not merely to the parties. *Gunn v. Minton*, 568 U.S. 251, 260 (2013). A malpractice case concerning a missed patent defense may require a patent-law determination, but that determination is only hypothetical and backward-looking; it does not affect the validity of any patent or control future patent rights. Id. at 261–64.

That is fatal to exclusivity. In *Gunn*, the Supreme Court held that although the patent issue was “necessary,” “actually disputed,” and “substantial” to the parties, it was not substantial in the jurisdictional sense because its resolution would have no broader effect on the federal system. 568 U.S. at 258–64. The Court explained that deciding the patent issue in a state malpractice suit would not undermine uniform patent law because “state court resolution of a hypothetical patent issue” would not control “federal patent law in the future.” Id. at 261. Nor would it disturb the federal-state balance, because states have a strong interest in regulating the conduct of attorneys licensed in their courts. Id. at 264. Those reasons foreclose the rule urged by Gunn here.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary decisions should not govern. *Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1272–73, and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281, 1285–87 (Fed. Cir. 2007), treated the presence of patent issues in malpractice causation as sufficient for exclusive jurisdiction. But that approach collapsed *Grable*’s separate substantiality and federalism inquiries into a general preference for federal adjudication. The Supreme Court rejected that approach in *Gunn*. 568 U.S. at 264.

Nor does exclusivity follow from cases like *Empire HealthChoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh*, 547 U.S. 677, 699–701 (2006), which likewise refused to extend federal-question jurisdiction to a state-law reimbursement claim with a federal overlay. The Court there held that the federal issue was too fact-bound and situation-specific to warrant federal jurisdiction. Id. at 700–01. The same is true here: whether a lawyer should have raised the experimental-use defense is a case-specific malpractice question, not a federal patent issue of the kind Congress meant to reserve to the federal courts.

In short, § 1338(a) does not make federal jurisdiction exclusive whenever patent law must be consulted. The question is whether patent law creates the claim or supplies a substantial federal issue in the *Grable* sense. *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808–09; *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314; *Gunn*, 568 U.S. at 258–64. Because Minton’s claim is state legal malpractice and any patent question is merely incidental to the damages-and-causation inquiry, the Texas courts retained jurisdiction. The judgment of dismissal should be affirmed.
