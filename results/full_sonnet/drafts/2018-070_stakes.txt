**ARGUMENT**

**The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act Adopts State Law as Surrogate Federal Law Only to Fill Gaps Left by Federal Law**

**A. The Text and Structure of OCSLA Confirm That State Law Applies Only as a Gap-Filler**

Congress enacted OCSLA to answer a jurisdictional question the Constitution does not: what law governs an artificial island fixed to the seabed many miles from any State. Its answer was categorical. The Act declares that "the Constitution and laws of the United States" extend to installations on the Shelf "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). Only after establishing federal law as the governing sovereign authority does the Act provide, "[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws," that "the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State … are declared to be the law of the United States." § 1333(a)(2)(A).

This is the vocabulary of federal enclave law, not concurrent-jurisdiction law. Congress modeled OCSLA on the regime governing federal enclaves, where state law is assimilated into federal law only to supply rules the federal sovereign has not itself supplied. The closest analogue is the Assimilative Crimes Act, which borrows state criminal law for federal enclaves only for "any act or omission which, although not made punishable by any enactment of Congress, would be punishable if committed" under state law. 18 U.S.C. § 13(a). That statute does not import state law that merely "pertains to the subject matter"; it imports state law only to fill a void Congress left unaddressed. OCSLA's declaration that the Shelf is to be treated "as if" it were "an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State," 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1), signals that Congress intended the same gap-filling function here.

The Fifth Circuit—the court with the longest experience administering OCSLA, given its geographic concentration of Shelf platforms—reached the same conclusion, holding that state law is adopted only out of "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" in existing federal law. Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). That test respects the statute's structure: federal law governs of its own force wherever it speaks; state law enters only where federal law is silent.

**B. This Court's Decisions Confirm the Gap-Filling Function of § 1333(a)(2)(A)**

This Court's leading OCSLA decision proves the point. In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court considered whether Louisiana wrongful-death law could be borrowed as federal law for deaths on fixed Shelf platforms, when the Death on the High Seas Act supplied a federal admiralty remedy for deaths "on the high seas." The Court explained that "[t]he purpose of the [OCSLA] was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf." Id. at 355. Because the platforms are treated as islands rather than vessels, the Court held that "the [federal] admiralty action under the [High] Seas Act no more applies to these accidents actually occurring on the islands than it would to accidents occurring in an upland federal enclave." Id. at 366. Only because the federal maritime remedy did not reach these facts—only because there was a gap—did the Court hold that incorporation of state law was permissible: the absence of an applicable federal remedy "remove[d]" the "obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law." Id. Rodrigue's analysis was not academic; it was dispositive. The Court asked first whether federal law occupied the field, and only upon finding it did not, turned to state law. That sequence—federal law first, state law only to fill what remains—is the gap-filling approach Parker urges here, and it forecloses the Ninth Circuit's contrary holding that "the absence of federal law is not … a prerequisite to adopting state law as surrogate federal law."

**C. The FLSA Leaves No Gap for California's Wage and Hour Law to Fill**

Respondent's claims arise entirely from a comprehensive federal wage-and-hour scheme, the Fair Labor Standards Act, which Congress enacted with painstaking specificity regarding minimum wages, overtime, and remedial enforcement. See 29 U.S.C. §§ 201–219. The FLSA's savings clause, § 218(a), permits States to enact more protective minimum-wage and overtime laws for employment within their own borders, but that provision is not itself a source of authority to project state substantive law onto federal enclaves like the Shelf. Where, as here, federal law—the FLSA—directly and comprehensively addresses the wage-and-hour subject matter at issue, there is no "significant void or gap," Continental Oil, 417 F.2d at 1036, for state law to fill under § 1333(a)(2)(A). The FLSA governs Newton's claims to the exclusion of California law, exactly as the district court held.

The Ninth Circuit's "applicable and not inconsistent" test, detached from any gap requirement, would permit wholesale importation of fifty different bodies of state employment law onto federally controlled installations whenever a State's law is not technically preempted—precisely the balkanized regime that led Congress to declare the Shelf subject to federal law "as if" it were "an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). This Court should reverse and hold that OCSLA borrows state law only to fill gaps in existing federal law.
