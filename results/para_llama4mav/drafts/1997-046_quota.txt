The Ohio Adult Parole Authority's clemency process does not implicate a protected life or liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment, and the voluntary interview option does not violate an inmate's Fifth Amendment rights.

First, it is well-established that clemency proceedings do not create a constitutionally protected life or liberty interest. As the Supreme Court held in Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), a state's clemency procedure does not give rise to a federally protected liberty interest. The Court reasoned that "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." Id. at 464. Similarly, in Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard, the Sixth Circuit correctly concluded that Woodard has no constitutionally protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency procedure itself.

Moreover, the Due Process Clause does not apply to clemency proceedings in the same way it applies to other stages of the adjudicatory process. As the Supreme Court noted in Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), the right to effective assistance of counsel on appeal is grounded in the Due Process Clause, but this does not extend to clemency proceedings. The Court has also recognized that the Constitution does not require a state to provide clemency or pardon procedures. See Herrera v. Collins, 506 U.S. 390 (1993). Therefore, the minimal due process protections suggested by the Sixth Circuit are not supported by Supreme Court precedent.

Regarding the Fifth Amendment claim, the voluntary interview option in Ohio's clemency process does not compel an inmate to waive their right against self-incrimination. As the Supreme Court held in Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), a state's disciplinary procedures did not impermissibly burden an inmate's Fifth Amendment rights where the inmate was not compelled to testify. Similarly, Ohio's clemency regulation allows an inmate to participate in a personal interview voluntarily and to remain silent if they choose to do so. The regulation does not require a waiver of the Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination.

Furthermore, the Sixth Circuit's concern about the potential for an unconstitutional condition is speculative and premature. As the Supreme Court noted in Baxter, it is premature to instruct a state on how to deal with hypothetical situations that may never materialize. See Baxter, 425 U.S. at 324. The risk of self-incrimination in this case is purely theoretical, and the district court's task on remand would be to engage in speculative analysis.

The Supreme Court's decisions in Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539 (1974), and Sandin v. Conner, 515 U.S. 472 (1995), also support the conclusion that clemency proceedings are not subject to the same level of due process scrutiny as other stages of the adjudicatory process. In Wolff, the Court held that certain prison disciplinary procedures implicated a liberty interest, but the Court has since limited the application of Wolff in Sandin. Similarly, the Court's decision in Meachum v. Fano, 427 U.S. 215 (1976), emphasizes that the Due Process Clause is not implicated by a state's decision to grant or deny clemency.

In conclusion, Ohio's clemency process does not implicate a protected life or liberty interest, and the voluntary interview option does not violate an inmate's Fifth Amendment rights. The Sixth Circuit's decision to remand the case for further proceedings is not supported by Supreme Court precedent, and the district court's judgment should be affirmed.
