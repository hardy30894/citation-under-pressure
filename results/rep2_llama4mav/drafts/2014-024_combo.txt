ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in affirming Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) without requiring proof of his subjective intent to threaten. The "true threats" exception to First Amendment protection, which § 875(c) embodies, has long been understood to require a showing of the speaker's intent to convey a threat.

The Supreme Court has not directly addressed the issue, but relevant precedents suggest that a subjective intent standard is necessary. In Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), the Court emphasized the importance of considering the speaker's intent when evaluating the constitutionality of a statute regulating speech. The Court held that a statute that "forbids a communication" must be "interpreted to apply only when a reasonable person would have realized that his utterance would have the natural and probable effect of causing annoyance to another" (Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 311). This reasoning implies that the speaker's intent is a crucial factor in determining whether a statement constitutes a true threat.

In Terminiello v. City of Chicago, 337 U.S. 1 (1949), the Court further underscored the significance of the speaker's intent, holding that a conviction for disorderly conduct based on speech could not stand where the speaker's words, although provocative, were not intended to incite violence. The Court noted that "the test is not whether the speech was actually intended to provoke violence, but whether it is likely to do so" (Terminiello, 337 U.S. at 4-5, citing Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 573 (1942)). However, Chaplinsky itself highlights the importance of the speaker's intent, as it involved a face-to-face insult where the speaker's words were "inherently likely to provoke violent reaction" (Chaplinsky, 315 U.S. at 573).

Other cases have also emphasized the role of intent in determining the constitutionality of speech restrictions. In Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359 (1931), the Court invalidated a statute that prohibited the display of a red flag as a symbol of opposition to organized government, noting that the statute was overly broad and failed to distinguish between peaceful and violent opposition (Stromberg, 283 U.S. at 369). Similarly, in Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242 (1937), the Court reversed a conviction under a statute that prohibited attempting to incite insurrection, holding that the statute was unconstitutionally vague and failed to provide adequate notice of what conduct was prohibited (Herndon, 301 U.S. at 259-60).

In Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47 (1919), the Court established the "clear and present danger" test, which requires consideration of the speaker's intent and the circumstances in which the speech is made (Schenck, 249 U.S. at 52). The Court has also recognized that the intent behind a statement is crucial in determining its constitutionality, as seen in Frohwerk v. United States, 249 U.S. 204 (1919), where the Court upheld convictions for conspiracy to obstruct the recruiting service, emphasizing the importance of the defendants' intent in making their statements (Frohwerk, 249 U.S. at 213).

Furthermore, in Gitlow v. New York, 268 U.S. 652 (1925), the Court upheld a statute that prohibited advocating the overthrow of the government by force or violence, noting that the statute was constitutional because it only prohibited speech that was intended to incite imminent lawless action (Gitlow, 268 U.S. at 669). In Whitney v. California, 274 U.S. 357 (1927), the Court reaffirmed the importance of intent, holding that a statute that prohibited joining an organization that advocated the overthrow of the government by force or violence was constitutional because it required a showing of specific intent to achieve that end (Whitney, 274 U.S. at 371-72).

De Jonge v. Oregon, 299 U.S. 353 (1937), also supports the requirement of subjective intent. The Court held that a statute that prohibited participating in a meeting called by an organization that advocated the overthrow of the government by force or violence was unconstitutional as applied, because it did not require a showing of the individual's intent to participate in or advocate such action (De Jonge, 299 U.S. at 365).

These precedents collectively suggest that a subjective intent standard is necessary to ensure that § 875(c) is applied constitutionally. The Third Circuit's objective intent standard, which focuses solely on whether a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat, is insufficient to protect Elonis' First Amendment rights. By affirming Elonis' conviction without requiring proof of his subjective intent to threaten, the Third Circuit erred and its judgment should be reversed.
