**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Violation of Rule 32(a)(2) Does Not Entitle a Defendant to Collateral Relief Where the Defendant Already Knew of His Right to Appeal.**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) requires the sentencing court to "advise the defendant of any right to appeal." The purpose of this requirement is purely informational: it exists to ensure that a defendant is not deprived of appellate review through ignorance of his rights. Where, as here, the defendant already possessed that knowledge, the purpose of the Rule has been fully served, and the court's omission works no injury warranting collateral relief.

This Court has long distinguished between violations of the Federal Rules that cause genuine prejudice and technical omissions that do not. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), the Court held that a sentencing court's failure to comply with a procedural requirement of Rule 32—there, the failure to ask the defendant personally whether he had anything to say before sentence was imposed—did not present "an omission inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure" sufficient to support relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255. *Id.* at 428. The Court explained that a "formal violation" of the Rules, unaccompanied by any showing that the defendant was prejudiced, does not rise to the level of "a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice." *Id.* Peguero's claim is functionally identical: he asks this Court to grant relief for a formal violation of Rule 32(a)(2) even though the District Court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that he knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing.

This Court reinforced that principle in *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780 (1979), holding that a "technical" violation of Rule 11—there, the failure to mention a special parole term—did not justify collateral relief absent a showing that the violation affected the defendant's decision to plead guilty or otherwise caused prejudice. The Court reasoned that collateral relief exists to correct "fundamental defects," not to police "every asserted error of law." *Id.* at 783-84 (quoting *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428). The same logic controls here. Rule 32(a)(2)'s advisement requirement, like Rule 11's colloquy requirements, is a procedural safeguard designed to protect a substantive right—the right to appeal. Where the defendant already enjoys the benefit that the procedure was designed to secure, namely, actual knowledge of his appellate rights, the required showing of prejudice cannot be made.

**II. Harmless-Error Principles Govern Rule 32(a)(2) Claims, and Peguero Cannot Demonstrate That the District Court's Omission Affected His Substantial Rights.**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) provides that "[a]ny error, defect, irregularity, or variance which does not affect substantial rights shall be disregarded." This Court has consistently applied harmless-error analysis to procedural violations that do not implicate constitutional rights of the first order. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court articulated the now-familiar standard: an error is harmless unless it had a "substantial influence" on the outcome, or leaves the reviewing court in "grave doubt" as to whether it did. *Id.* at 765. Applying that standard to a Rule 32(a)(2) violation, the question is not whether the district judge complied with the letter of the Rule, but whether the omission had any effect on the defendant's ability to exercise his appellate rights.

Here it plainly did not. The District Court, after an evidentiary hearing, made the factual finding that Peguero knew of his right to appeal notwithstanding the court's failure to advise him. That finding is not clearly erroneous and is entitled to deference. Given that finding, the Rule's protective purpose—ensuring the defendant is not deprived of appellate review through lack of information—was fully achieved through means other than the court's compliance with Rule 32(a)(2). The Third Circuit therefore correctly concluded that the Rule's "purpose had been served" notwithstanding the technical violation, and that no relief was warranted.

Nor does this case implicate the sort of structural or constitutional defect that this Court has exempted from harmless-error review. Compare *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279 (1991) (identifying limited category of "structural" errors not subject to harmless-error analysis). The right at issue—the right to be informed of the right to appeal—is not itself a constitutional guarantee; it is a rule-based procedural protection ancillary to the right to appeal. Where the defendant independently possessed the knowledge the Rule was designed to supply, no structural harm of the kind identified in *Fulminante* can be said to have occurred.

**III. Collateral Relief Requires a Showing of Actual Prejudice.**

Finally, this Court's collateral-review jurisprudence demands more than the bare showing of a procedural misstep. In *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152 (1982), the Court required a movant seeking collateral relief to demonstrate "actual prejudice" resulting from the claimed error. *Id.* at 168. Peguero cannot make that showing. He does not contend that, had the court advised him of his appellate rights, he would have appealed; indeed, the District Court found he already knew of those rights and chose not to exercise them. Under *Hill*, *Timmreck*, and *Frady*, that failure of proof is fatal to his claim.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
