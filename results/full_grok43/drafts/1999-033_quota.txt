The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment secures equal treatment under law by prohibiting arbitrary classifications that single out disfavored groups. *Strauder v. West Virginia*, 100 U.S. 303, 307-08 (1880). Its text and history presuppose differential treatment of identifiable classes, not isolated disputes between an individual and the state. *The Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36, 81 (1873).

This Court has consistently required plaintiffs asserting equal-protection violations to demonstrate membership in a class or group that has been treated differently from similarly situated persons. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373-74 (1886) (invalidating enforcement “directed so exclusively against a particular class”). Absent such a showing, claims reduce to ordinary tort or due-process grievances outside the Clause’s scope. *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) (“[A] showing of discriminatory intent is not sufficient; there must be a showing of intentional or purposeful discrimination” against a defined class).

The Seventh Circuit’s “class of one” theory, resting solely on allegations of “spiteful” motivation “wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective,” finds no support in precedent. *Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448, 456 (1962) (selective-enforcement claims require proof that the decision “was based upon an unjustifiable standard such as race, religion, or other arbitrary classification”). Mere allegations of ill will, without more, do not transform garden-variety regulatory disagreements into constitutional violations. *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976).

Requiring class membership also preserves the Clause’s focus on systemic inequality rather than every instance of uneven treatment. *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985) (“The Equal Protection Clause . . . is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.”). Allowing class-of-one suits would federalize countless local land-use and permitting disputes, contrary to the principle that the Clause “does not require [government] to treat all persons alike.” *Personnel Administrator v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 271-72 (1979).

Even under rational-basis review, the Clause demands only that the classification bear a rational relationship to a legitimate governmental purpose; it does not authorize judicial second-guessing of every discretionary decision affecting a single property owner. *Nordlinger v. Hahn*, 505 U.S. 1, 11-12 (1992); *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Commission*, 488 U.S. 336, 343 (1989). Because respondent pleaded no class-based discrimination, the complaint fails to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause.
