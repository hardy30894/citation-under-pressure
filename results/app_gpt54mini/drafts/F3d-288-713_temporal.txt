**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Due Process Clause required a fair trial before an impartial tribunal, and the State’s use of its chief prosecutor as a witness destroyed that fairness.**

“A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process.” *In re Murchison*, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955). That guarantee is violated not only by direct judicial bias, but whenever the State’s own conduct creates a proceeding that cannot be trusted. *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510, 523 (1927). Here, the Potter County District Attorney, Danny Hill, did more than supervise the prosecution: he testified as a rebuttal witness against Riddle and then continued to prosecute the case. The result was a prosecution backed by the prestige of the State’s chief prosecutor’s sworn testimony. That practice offended the “rudimentary demands of fair procedure.” *In re Murchison*, 349 U.S. at 136.

A prosecutor is not an ordinary advocate. His duty is not simply to win, but to see that justice is done. *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935). Because of that special role, courts have long recognized the danger when a prosecutor injects himself into the evidentiary dispute. *Berger* condemned prosecutorial conduct that “overstepp[ed] the bounds of propriety and fairness.” *Id.* at 84. Hill’s testimony did precisely that. He became a witness on the very issue that the State later argued to the jury in closing: Riddle’s credibility. Once Hill took the stand, he ceased to be merely an advocate and became a source of factual proof. Allowing him to continue prosecuting the case after testifying impermissibly fused the roles of witness and advocate and invited the jury to accept his official stature as evidence of guilt.

The prejudice was acute. The prosecutor’s office was the source of the evidence, and the office then argued that Hill’s testimony showed Riddle was a liar. That is fundamentally unlike the ordinary case in which counsel merely comments on testimony in the record. The Supreme Court has warned that courts must guard against procedures that “offend those canons of decency and fairness” embedded in due process. *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941). Here, the State’s chief prosecutor became a partisan witness whose testimony was used to impeach the accused while he simultaneously retained the authority and prestige of his office before the jury. Due process does not tolerate such a proceeding.

II. **Riddle was entitled to an evidentiary hearing because, on this record, the material facts surrounding the prosecutor-witness issue were not fully and fairly resolved.**

Federal habeas courts have long recognized that where a petitioner alleges facts which, if true, would entitle him to relief, the court must afford an opportunity to prove them. *Walker v. Johnston*, 312 U.S. 275, 284 (1941). The district court’s summary disposition was improper because the prosecutor’s dual role and its effect on the verdict present factual questions that cannot be resolved against Riddle without a hearing.

The record shows that Hill called witnesses to rebut Riddle’s testimony, then took the stand himself, and afterward remained the prosecutor. But the constitutional injury does not turn only on the bare fact of testimony; it also depends on the extent to which Hill’s participation influenced the jury, whether the State exploited his status in argument, and whether the defense could effectively challenge the prejudice created by his dual role. Those matters warranted a hearing. As the Court explained in *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312-19 (1963), a federal habeas court must hold a hearing where the facts are not adequately developed or the state process did not reliably resolve the federal claim. The petition here raised a substantial due process violation arising from the State’s own trial conduct. Denying a hearing foreclosed the only meaningful opportunity to establish the full extent of that constitutional harm.

III. **The conviction cannot stand because the State’s misconduct undermined confidence in the verdict in a capital case.**

Capital cases demand the most exacting fidelity to due process. *Tumey*, 273 U.S. at 535; *Murchison*, 349 U.S. at 136. When the State’s chief prosecutor testifies and then resumes his role as advocate, the danger is not merely technical. It strikes at the integrity of the verdict. The jury was asked to decide whether Riddle acted with the requisite criminal intent and whether his account of events was believable. Hill’s testimony went directly to that issue. By placing the office of the prosecutor behind factual assertions about Riddle’s truthfulness, the State increased the risk that the verdict rested on official authority rather than an impartial assessment of the evidence.

This Court should therefore reverse the denial of habeas relief, or at minimum remand for an evidentiary hearing on the due process violation created by the prosecutor’s dual role. The Constitution guarantees more than a conviction obtained by an interested and participating accuser; it guarantees a fair trial. *Murchison*, 349 U.S. at 136; *Berger*, 295 U.S. at 88. Riddle did not receive one.
