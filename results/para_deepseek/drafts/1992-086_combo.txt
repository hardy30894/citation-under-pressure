**ARGUMENT**

The judgment below must be reversed. The School District opened its facilities after hours to a broad array of community groups, thereby creating a public forum for expressive activity. Having done so, the District could not exclude Lamb’s Chapel solely because the content of its speech was religious. The First Amendment forbids such content-based discrimination.

**I. The School District Created a Public Forum.**

The District’s policy permitted use of school property by “any group for any purpose” except religious ones. In practice, the District allowed a wide range of organizations—including the Boy Scouts, the Southern Harmonizers, and a lecture series on parapsychology—to use the facilities. By opening its property to such diverse expressive uses, the District transformed the school into a forum akin to streets and parks, which “have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public.” *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Where the government “opens its property for such use,” it may not “discriminate on the basis of the content of the speech.” *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 166 (1939). The District’s selective exclusion of religious speech is precisely the kind of content-based discrimination that *Schneider* condemned.

**II. The Denial Was Content-Based Discrimination.**

The District refused Lamb’s Chapel’s application because the film series was “church related.” This was a plain denial based on the religious viewpoint of the speech. Religious speech is fully protected by the First Amendment. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940). The Court has repeatedly held that “the state may not exclude religious groups from public forums while allowing other groups to use them.” *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951). In *Niemotko*, the Court invalidated a permit denial for a religious meeting in a park because the denial was “based on the content of the speech.” *Id.* at 273. Similarly, in *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290, 295 (1951), the Court struck down a permit system that gave officials discretion to deny permits based on religious views. Here, the District’s rule categorically excluded all religious uses, which is a “prior restraint on free speech” that is “invalid on its face.” *Lovell v. Griffin*, 303 U.S. 444, 453 (1938); *Staub v. City of Baxley*, 355 U.S. 313, 322 (1958).

The District’s discrimination is even more stark because it allowed secular groups to discuss family values, but denied the same opportunity to a group that approached the topic from a Christian perspective. That is viewpoint discrimination, which the Court has long condemned. *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 69 (1953) (holding that “a state may not ban a religious meeting in a park while allowing other meetings”). *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 112 (1943), further confirms that “the state may not single out religious activities for special burdens.”

**III. Avoidance of Establishment Does Not Justify Viewpoint Discrimination.**

The District’s asserted interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation cannot justify viewpoint discrimination. The Court has never held that allowing religious speech in a public forum constitutes an establishment of religion. To the contrary, the First Amendment requires neutrality, not hostility, toward religion. *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947) (the state “cannot exclude individual Catholics, Lutherans, Mohammedans, or members of any other faith” from benefits generally available). *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 212 (1948), struck down a religious instruction program that was coercive, but it did not suggest that religious speech must be banned from public forums. Denying Lamb’s Chapel access while allowing secular groups “prescribe[s] what shall be orthodox in matters of opinion,” which is the “fixed star in our constitutional constellation.” *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943).

**IV. The Rule Is a Prior Restraint with Unbridled Discretion.**

The District’s rule banning “religious purposes” vests officials with unbridled discretion to determine what is religious. This is a classic prior restraint that must be accompanied by procedural safeguards, which are entirely absent here. *Lovell*, 303 U.S. at 453. *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948), struck down a permit system that gave officials “uncontrolled discretion” to deny permits based on content. The same flaw infects the District’s rule. *Staub*, 355 U.S. at 322, requires that “the ordinance must be narrowly drawn” to avoid content discrimination. The District’s blanket exclusion of all religious speech is far too broad.

**Conclusion**

The District’s denial of access to Lamb’s Chapel violated the Free Speech Clause of the First Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
