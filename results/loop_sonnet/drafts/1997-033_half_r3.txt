# ARGUMENT

## I. The plain, mandatory text of Section 1407(a) forecloses any transferee court's use of Section 1404(a) to retain a case for trial.

Section 1407(a) provides that the Panel "shall" remand each transferred action at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings. That word is not a suggestion. As this Court has recognized, when a statute fixes a duty by using the word "shall," it speaks in "the language of command." *Escoe v. Zerbst*, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935). Congress's choice of mandatory phrasing must be given effect according to its terms; where the words Congress used are clear, a court's task is to give those words their natural effect rather than to search for reasons to depart from them. *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940). Nothing in Section 1407(a) qualifies the remand duty for cases in which a transferee judge concludes, under the wholly separate discretionary standard of Section 1404(a), that retention would be convenient.

Respondent's countermotion asked the District of Arizona to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent one by invoking Section 1404(a)'s general convenience standard. But Congress did not leave the duration of a Panel transfer to the discretion of the transferee judge; it fixed that duration by statute, and courts have no license to nullify a mandatory command because they judge that departure serves efficiency. This Court has long insisted that where the words of a statute are plain, courts must give them effect rather than search for reasons to depart from that plain meaning. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

## II. Because Congress specifically restricted multidistrict transfer to pretrial proceedings, the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a) cannot be used to override that specific limitation.

Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) serve different purposes and were drafted with different scope. Section 1404(a) is a "federal judicial housekeeping measure," generally applicable to ordinary civil litigation and "intended, subject to the transferee district court's discretion, to protect litigants, witnesses, and the public against unnecessary inconvenience and expense." *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 636–37 (1964). It operates only where the transferee forum is one in which the action "might have been brought" by the plaintiff in the first instance—a limitation this Court has strictly enforced even against arguments of convenience or party consent. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343–44 (1960). Section 1407(a), by contrast, is a specialized mechanism enacted for the narrow purpose of coordinating pretrial proceedings among related actions pending in different districts, with the Panel's authority expressly bounded by the remand command. That mechanism operates against the backdrop of the discretionary standard Congress separately established for ordinary venue transfer under Section 1404(a). *Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960).

Where a specific statutory provision addresses a particular subject, it is not displaced by a general provision addressing the same general subject matter. This Court has held that a specific venue provision governing a particular class of actions controls over a more general venue statute addressing the same subject, and is not to be supplemented or enlarged by that general provision. *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957). This Court applied the same principle decades earlier, holding that the general language of a statutory provision will not be extended to override a matter specifically addressed in another part of the same enactment. *Ginsburg & Sons, Inc. v. Popkin*, 285 U.S. 204, 208 (1932). Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the temporal scope of a Panel-ordered transfer; it cannot be controlled or nullified by resort to the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a), a statute of general applicability that says nothing about multidistrict litigation at all.

Moreover, Section 1404(a)'s general purpose has never been understood to authorize a court to alter the plaintiff's chosen forum for trial absent satisfaction of the statute's own criteria. This Court has treated Section 1404(a) as a statute "dealing with the placement of litigation in the federal courts," designed for ordinary venue disputes, not as a vehicle for a transferee court to entrench itself in a case that Congress committed to it for the limited purpose of pretrial coordination. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 61 (1949). Lexecon selected the Northern District of Illinois as its forum. Nothing in Section 1404(a)'s convenience calculus permits a court sitting by virtue of the Panel's pretrial assignment to substitute its own trial-venue preference for the plaintiff's chosen forum, particularly when doing so directly contradicts the specific remand command of Section 1407(a).

## III. The Ninth Circuit erred in treating the conflict as unresolved.

The court below acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate" yet declined to enforce it, resting instead on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and non-binding circuit precedent. But an agency rule cannot rewrite the words Congress chose, and no court may relax a mandatory statutory command because compliance is inconvenient. Under *American Trucking*, 310 U.S. at 543–44, and *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485, a reviewing court's task when statutory language is plain is to enforce it—not to weigh policy trade-offs Congress itself resolved. Because Section 1407(a) required remand of this action to the Northern District of Illinois at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings, and because Section 1404(a) supplies no authority to override that specific command, the judgment below should be reversed.
