**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court may certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis—requires this Court to reaffirm a foundational principle of class action practice: *class certification is not a license to conduct a mini-trial on the merits, nor is it a vehicle for speculative or hypothetical damage models that lack empirical foundation.* The Third Circuit’s decision in this case, which affirmed certification based on a damages methodology that fails to satisfy even the most basic requirements of Rule 23(b)(3), directly contravenes this Court’s precedent and undermines the integrity of the class action device. We respectfully submit that the lower court’s certification order must be reversed.

### I. The District Court’s Certification Was Based on a Flawed and Unsupportable Damage Model

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011). This predominance requirement is not satisfied by a theory of damages that relies on speculative assumptions and lacks a factual basis for class-wide measurement. Here, the District Court certified a class of over 1.5 million subscribers based on the testimony of Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. James McClave, who proposed a damages model that purports to estimate the “but-for” price of non-basic cable programming in a competitive market. Yet, the model rests on a series of unproven and unverifiable assumptions—most notably, that the absence of competition in the Philadelphia DMA led to a uniform, class-wide overcharge, and that this overcharge can be measured without individualized inquiry into each subscriber’s actual behavior, contract terms, or willingness to pay.

The Court has made clear that “a damages model that cannot be applied on a class-wide basis is fatal to certification.” *Amgen Inc. v. Conn. Ret. Plans & Trust Funds*, 568 U.S. 455, 467 (2013). In *Dukes*, the Court emphasized that “the predominance requirement is not satisfied if individualized issues—such as whether each employee was actually subjected to the alleged discriminatory practice—will overwhelm the common issues.” 564 U.S. at 350. Similarly, here, the damages model fails because it cannot account for the vast variation in subscriber behavior, contract terms, and pricing across the Philadelphia cluster. The model assumes a single, uniform overcharge across all class members, but the record contains no admissible evidence that such a uniformity exists. Indeed, the expert’s own testimony acknowledged that individual subscribers may have negotiated different rates, received different promotions, or subscribed to different tiers of service—factors that would render a class-wide damages calculation impossible.

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that the model was “common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis” is not only erroneous but also contradicts *Dukes* and *Amgen*. In *Amgen*, the Court held that “a damages model that requires individualized proof of damages for each class member cannot satisfy Rule 23(b)(3).” 568 U.S. at 467. The model here, which relies on hypothetical market conditions and untested assumptions about consumer behavior, is precisely the kind of speculative framework that *Amgen* condemned. The Third Circuit’s failure to recognize this distinction renders its decision legally unsound.

### II. The District Court’s Evidentiary Hearing Did Not Cure the Deficiency

The Third Circuit held that the evidentiary hearing “rigorously analyzed” the expert reports and testimony, and thus the certification was not an abuse of discretion. But the mere fact that a court holds a hearing does not transform a flawed damages model into a valid one. *Dukes* made clear that “a district court may not certify a class based on a damages model that cannot be applied on a class-wide basis, even if the court has conducted a thorough evidentiary inquiry.” 564 U.S. at 350. The Court in *Dukes* rejected the argument that a “rigorous analysis” of the merits at the certification stage was permissible, stating that “the Rule 23 inquiry may not become a merits trial.” Id. at 351.

Here, the District Court’s analysis crossed that line. It did not merely assess whether the damages model was *plausible*—it effectively adopted it as the basis for certification. The court concluded that “the class has met its burden to demonstrate that the element of antitrust impact is capable of proof at trial through evidence that is common to the class.” *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). But this conclusion was based on a model that the court itself acknowledged was “highly speculative” and “dependent on assumptions that cannot be verified.” Id. at 168. The court’s acceptance of this model as “common evidence” was not a permissible exercise of discretion—it was a legal error.

### III. The Third Circuit’s Decision Creates a Dangerous Precedent

The Third Circuit’s decision allows class certification based on a damages model that lacks any empirical foundation. This opens the door to class actions in which plaintiffs can certify massive classes based on hypothetical damages, without ever proving that those damages can be measured on a class-wide basis. Such a result is inconsistent with the *Dukes* and *Amgen* line of cases, which require that class certification be grounded in *admissible* evidence of common impact and common damages.

The Court has repeatedly warned against “certification decisions that are effectively mini-trials on the merits.” *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 351. The Third Circuit’s decision does precisely that. It certifies a class based on a damages model that cannot be applied without individualized inquiries, thereby violating Rule 23(b)(3) and undermining the very purpose of the class action device.

### Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit must be reversed. Class certification cannot be based on a damages model that lacks admissible evidence of class-wide measurement. The District Court’s certification order, affirmed by the Third Circuit, fails to meet this basic requirement. The judgment below should be vacated, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s precedent.
