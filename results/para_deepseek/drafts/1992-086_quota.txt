**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School District Violated the First Amendment by Excluding Lamb’s Chapel Based on the Religious Viewpoint of Its Speech.**

The Court of Appeals erred in affirming summary judgment for the School District. The District opened its facilities as a limited public forum for after-hours community use, and having done so, it could not discriminate against speech based on its religious viewpoint. *See Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983) (once government opens a forum for expressive activity, content-based exclusions must be narrowly drawn to serve a compelling state interest). The District’s policy—barring all “religious purposes”—is a classic viewpoint-based restriction, not a permissible content-neutral limitation.

In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267 (1981), this Court held that a public university that created a forum generally open to student groups could not exclude religious groups. The Court reasoned that “the University’s exclusionary policy violates the fundamental principle that a state regulation of speech should be content-neutral.” *Id.* at 269. The same principle applies here. The District permitted a wide array of community groups—including the Boy Scouts, the Kiwanis Club, the Southern Harmonizers, and a lecture series on parapsychology—to use school facilities. Having opened its doors to such groups, the District could not deny access to Lamb’s Chapel solely because its film series addressed family values from a religious perspective.

This Court’s decision in *Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), reinforces this conclusion. Although *Mergens* arose under the Equal Access Act, the Court emphasized that “the logic of *Widmar*” applies to secondary schools, and that “there is no constitutional violation” when a school “denies equal access to a religious group” only if the forum is not open to other groups. *Id.* at 247-48. Here, the District’s forum was open to many secular groups, making the exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel impermissible viewpoint discrimination.

**II. The District’s Exclusion of Religious Speech Is Not Required by the Establishment Clause.**

The District cannot justify its exclusion by invoking the Establishment Clause. In *Widmar*, this Court rejected the argument that allowing religious groups equal access would violate the Establishment Clause, holding that “an open-forum policy does not confer any imprimatur of state approval on religious sects or practices.” 454 U.S. at 274. Similarly, in *Mergens*, the Court stated that “secondary school students are mature enough and are likely to understand that a school does not endorse or promote student speech that it merely permits on a nondiscriminatory basis.” 496 U.S. at 250. The same reasoning applies to community groups using school facilities after hours. The District’s fear of an Establishment Clause violation is “unfounded.” *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993) (reversing the decision below).

**III. The District’s Policy Is Not Viewpoint-Neutral.**

The Court of Appeals concluded that the District’s exclusion was “viewpoint-neutral” because it barred all religious uses. But a policy that excludes all religious speech is not viewpoint-neutral; it discriminates against an entire category of speech based on its religious character. In *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 828-29 (1995), this Court held that discrimination against religious speech is “viewpoint discrimination” because “the University’s regulation is not a content-neutral exclusion of secular and religious alike, but a viewpoint-specific exclusion of religious speech.” The same is true here. The District’s policy singles out religious viewpoints for disfavored treatment.

**IV. The District’s Prior Practices Confirm That the Forum Was Open to a Broad Range of Speech.**

The record shows that the District permitted use of its facilities by groups such as the Boy Scouts, the Kiwanis Club, and a lecture series on parapsychology. These groups engaged in expressive activities that were not religious in nature, but the District’s forum was nonetheless open to them. As this Court stated in *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985), the “government does not create a public forum by inaction or by permitting limited discourse, but only by intentionally opening a nontraditional forum for public discourse.” Here, the District intentionally opened its facilities to a wide range of community groups, thereby creating a limited public forum. Once it did so, it could not exclude Lamb’s Chapel based on the religious viewpoint of its speech. *See also Good News Club v. Milford Central School*, 533 U.S. 98, 106-07 (2001) (holding that a school district violated the First Amendment by excluding a religious club from a limited public forum).

**V. The District’s Denial of Access Violates the Free Speech Clause.**

The First Amendment’s Free Speech Clause prohibits the government from “suppressing speech because the government opposes the speaker’s view.” *Police Department of Chicago v. Mosley*, 408 U.S. 92, 95 (1972). The District’s policy, which bars all religious speech, is a content-based restriction that cannot survive strict scrutiny. *See Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45. The District has no compelling interest in excluding religious speech, and its policy is not narrowly tailored to serve any legitimate interest. *See Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 270.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
