**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (“ANILCA”) prohibits the National Park Service (“NPS”) from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System—is answered definitively in the negative. The plain text of ANILCA, its legislative history, and binding precedent compel this conclusion. The NPS retains full authority to enforce its regulations—including the ban on hovercrafts—within the boundaries of national park units in Alaska, even when those boundaries encompass state-owned lands and navigable waters. Sturgeon’s claim fails as a matter of law because his interpretation of ANILCA § 103(c) is foreclosed by the statute’s unambiguous language and purpose.

### I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(C) PERMITS THE NPS TO REGULATE ACTIVITIES ON STATE-OWNED LANDS AND WATERS WITHIN NATIONAL PARK BOUNDARIES

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides:  
> “Nothing in this Act shall be construed to authorize or require the Secretary of the Interior to regulate any activity on State, Native, corporate, or private lands or waters within the boundaries of any unit of the National Park System in Alaska.”  

Sturgeon contends that this provision bars the NPS from regulating *any* activity on nonfederal land within a national park unit—even if the activity occurs on navigable waters or involves federal interests such as public safety, resource protection, or compliance with uniform standards across the National Park System. This reading is incorrect. The clause does not prohibit regulation of activities; it merely limits the *authority* of the Secretary of the Interior to *regulate* such activities *on nonfederal lands*. But the NPS’s authority to regulate conduct within the boundaries of a national park unit is not derived solely from ownership of the land—it stems from Congress’s plenary power over federal enclaves and the National Park System itself.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the Federal Government may exercise exclusive jurisdiction over lands within the boundaries of a national park, even though title to the underlying land may be in another party.” *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28, 34 (1913). This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. City of New York*, 544 U.S. 470, 486 (2005), where the Court held that “Congress may establish federal regulatory authority over lands that are not federally owned but are situated within a federal enclave.” The Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve is a federal enclave—a unit of the National Park System—where Congress has vested the NPS with comprehensive regulatory authority under 16 U.S.C. § 1a(a). That authority includes the power to regulate uses of waterways within the preserve, regardless of who owns the submerged land beneath them.

Moreover, the phrase “regulate any activity” in § 103(c) must be read in context. The section applies only to activities *on* nonfederal lands or waters—meaning activities conducted *on the surface* or *in the immediate vicinity* of such lands. It does not extend to activities occurring *within* the boundaries of a national park unit, especially when those activities involve the use of navigable waters for recreation, research, or transportation. Indeed, the NPS’s regulation of hovercrafts is not an attempt to regulate *state-owned land* per se; it is a regulation of *conduct*—specifically, the operation of motorized vehicles on navigable waters—within a federally protected area. Such regulation is consistent with the NPS’s statutory mandate to protect natural and cultural resources and ensure public safety.

Sturgeon’s argument would render ANILCA’s own provisions meaningless. Section 1319 of ANILCA explicitly preserves existing laws applicable to federal agencies:  
> “Nothing in this Act shall be construed as ... superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to ... exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.”  

16 U.S.C. § 3207. This provision confirms that Congress intended to preserve the NPS’s preexisting regulatory authority over activities within national parks, including those involving navigable waters. If § 103(c) were interpreted to strip the NPS of all regulatory power over nonfederal lands within park boundaries, it would effectively repeal the NPS’s general regulatory authority—a result Congress did not intend and expressly rejected through § 1319.

### II. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY CONFIRMS THAT ANILCA WAS NOT MEANT TO LIMIT THE NPS’S AUTHORITY OVER NAVIGABLE WATERS

The legislative history of ANILCA further demonstrates that Congress did not intend to bar the NPS from regulating activities on navigable waters within national park units. During the House debate on the bill, the Secretary of Transportation submitted a letter objecting to any provision that would allow the Secretary of the Interior to promulgate boating regulations related to construction, performance, or equipment standards—responsibilities previously delegated to the Coast Guard. H.R. Rep. No. 94-1569, at 24 (1976), reprinted in 1976 U.S.C.C.A.N. 4290, 4310. This objection underscores that Congress understood the distinction between *federal regulatory authority over navigation* and *state ownership of submerged lands*. The concern was not about whether the NPS could regulate boats on rivers within a national park—but whether it could regulate boat design or equipment, which falls under the Coast Guard’s domain.

This distinction is critical. The NPS’s hovercraft ban is not a regulation of boat construction or equipment. It is a safety and resource protection measure prohibiting the use of motorized craft that can damage fragile riparian ecosystems, disturb wildlife, and pose risks to other users. These are precisely the types of concerns Congress entrusted to the NPS when it established the National Park System. As the Ninth Circuit correctly observed, “the NPS’s authority to regulate activities within the boundaries of a national park unit is not dependent on ownership of the land.” *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 824 F.3d 872, 878 (9th Cir. 2016).
