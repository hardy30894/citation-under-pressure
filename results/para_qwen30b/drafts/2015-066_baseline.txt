**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an uncounseled tribal court conviction may be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)—requires this Court to interpret the scope of the Sixth Amendment in the context of tribal sovereignty and federal criminal law. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that such a conviction, even if constitutionally valid under the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), cannot serve as a predicate offense because it did not afford the defendant the right to counsel under the Sixth Amendment. That holding is contrary to Supreme Court precedent, undermines congressional intent, and threatens the integrity of tribal self-governance. This Court should reverse.

### I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT MISAPPLIED NICHOLS AND IGNORED THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN SENTENCING AND GUILT PHASES

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 114 S.Ct. 1921, 128 L.Ed.2d 745 (1994), is misplaced. *Nichols* held that a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction may be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, so long as the conviction does not violate the Sixth Amendment. Id. at 746–47, 114 S.Ct. 1921. But *Nichols* was a sentencing case, not a guilt-phase case. The Court explicitly recognized that the burden of proof at sentencing is lower—preponderance of the evidence—than at trial, where the government must prove every element of an offense beyond a reasonable doubt. See *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364, 90 S.Ct. 1068, 25 L.Ed.2d 368 (1970). The Court emphasized that the sentencing scheme in *Nichols* “accommodated” reliability concerns by allowing defendants to challenge the validity of prior convictions and by applying a less stringent burden of proof. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747–48, 114 S.Ct. 1921.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision, however, applies *Nichols* to the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution—where the government must prove the existence of two prior domestic violence convictions beyond a reasonable doubt. That is precisely what *Nichols* did not sanction. As the Court has repeatedly held, “the Constitution requires that the government prove every element of a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364, 90 S.Ct. 1068. To permit the use of a conviction that is constitutionally valid under ICRA but deemed “unreliable” under the Sixth Amendment for the purpose of establishing an essential element of a felony offense would violate *Winship* and the fundamental principle that “the government must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Middleton v. McNeil*, 541 U.S. 433, 437, 124 S.Ct. 1830, 158 L.Ed.2d 701 (2004).

### II. TRIBAL COURT CONVICTIONS ARE VALID UNDER THE INDIAN CIVIL RIGHTS ACT, NOT THE SIXTH AMENDMENT

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that tribal court convictions are “constitutionally infirm” because they do not afford the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is legally erroneous. The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts. *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337, 128 S.Ct. 2709, 171 L.Ed.2d 457 (2008) (“It is beyond dispute that the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.”). Instead, tribal court proceedings are governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. § 1302, which provides a right to counsel in certain cases, including those involving imprisonment. See id. § 1302(a)(1)–(2). The ICRA’s protections are not coextensive with the Sixth Amendment, but they are constitutionally sufficient under federal law.

The Ninth Circuit’s insistence that tribal court convictions must meet the Sixth Amendment standard—“at minimum, coextensive with the Sixth Amendment right”—is judicial overreach. It imposes a federal constitutional standard on tribal courts that Congress never required. This is not comity; it is judicial paternalism. As this Court has warned, “federal courts must be careful to respect tribal jurisprudence along with the special customs and practical limitations of tribal court systems.” *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 811 (9th Cir.1997) (quoting *Hilton v. Guyot*, 159 U.S. 113, 205, 16 S.Ct. 139, 40 L.Ed. 95 (1895)). The Ninth Circuit’s decision undermines tribal sovereignty by treating tribal court judgments as inherently suspect when they comply with ICRA.

### III. CONGRESS INTENDED § 117(A) TO INCLUDE TRIBAL COURT CONVICTIONS

Congress enacted § 117(a) to combat the epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country. The Violence Against Women and Department of Justice Reauthorization Act of 2005, Pub.L. No. 109-162, § 909, 119 Stat. 2960, 3084, was designed to empower tribes and the federal government to hold repeat offenders accountable. The statute explicitly includes “Indian tribal court proceedings” as a source of predicate convictions. 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). This inclusion reflects Congress’s recognition that tribal courts are legitimate and effective institutions for addressing domestic violence.

To hold that tribal court convictions cannot be used as predicate offenses because they do not afford the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is to frustrate Congress’s intent. It creates a perverse incentive: a defendant who commits domestic violence in tribal court may avoid federal prosecution under § 117(a) simply by being denied counsel—despite the fact that the conviction was valid under ICRA and the defendant was not deprived of due process.
