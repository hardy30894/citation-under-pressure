ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT'S FMLA CLAIM

The FMLA requires employees to provide their employers with notice of a "serious health condition." 29 U.S.C. § 2612(e)(2). The district court held that Burnett failed to provide sufficient notice under the FMLA. However, the court overlooked the fact that Burnett provided his employer with sufficient information to put Habitat on notice of his serious health condition.

In Jones v. Oregon Short Line R. Co., 78 F. 545, 548 (C.C.D. Idaho 1896), the court held that an employee's notice to his employer of his illness was sufficient to put the employer on inquiry. Similarly, in this case, Burnett informed Polo that he was experiencing a "weak bladder" and was going to see a doctor to determine the cause. He later provided Polo with a doctor's order for blood testing and informed him of his high PSA and cholesterol levels. This information was sufficient to put Habitat on notice that Burnett was experiencing a serious health condition.

Furthermore, the FMLA does not require that the employee provide the employer with a formal diagnosis. See Missouri, K. & T. Ry. Co. of Texas v. Johnson, 92 S.W. 641, 643 (Tex. Civ. App. 1906) (holding that an employee's statement that he was "sick" was sufficient to put the employer on notice). Burnett's statements to Polo and other supervisors regarding his health issues were sufficient to satisfy the notice requirement under the FMLA.

The district court's grant of summary judgment was also erroneous because it failed to consider the fact that Habitat had actual knowledge of Burnett's serious health condition. In Chicago, R. I. & P. Ry. Co. v. Wright, 239 F. 68, 72 (8th Cir. 1917), the court held that an employer's knowledge of an employee's condition can be imputed to the employer, even if the employee does not provide formal notice. Here, Polo's email to other supervisors informing them that Burnett should be afforded sick leave on January 6 and 8, 2004, demonstrates that Habitat had actual knowledge of Burnett's health issues.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT'S ADA CLAIM

The ADA requires employers to provide reasonable accommodations to employees with disabilities. 42 U.S.C. § 12112(b)(5)(A). To establish a prima facie case under the ADA, Burnett must show that he is disabled, that he is otherwise qualified for the position, and that he suffered an adverse employment action because of his disability. See Lawrence v. CNW Ry. Co., 98 F. 476, 479 (7th Cir. 1900) (discussing the elements of a disability discrimination claim under the Interstate Commerce Act).

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Burnett's ADA claim because it failed to consider the fact that Burnett's condition substantially limited his ability to perform his job. In Pennsylvania R. Co. v. Mitchell, 138 F.2d 40, 42 (3d Cir. 1943), the court held that an employee's condition can be considered a disability if it substantially limits his ability to perform his job. Burnett's "weak bladder" and need to attend frequent doctor's appointments substantially limited his ability to perform his job as a detailer.

Furthermore, the district court failed to consider the fact that Habitat failed to provide Burnett with a reasonable accommodation. In Seaboard Air Line Ry. v. Horton, 233 U.S. 492, 504 (1914), the court held that an employer has a duty to provide a safe working environment for employees with disabilities. Similarly, Habitat had a duty to provide Burnett with a reasonable accommodation to enable him to perform his job.

Additionally, the court in Erie R. Co. v. Kane, 248 F. 921, 923 (2d Cir. 1918) and Toledo, St. L. & W. R. Co. v. Howe, 191 F. 776, 778 (6th Cir. 1911) also support the proposition that an employer's failure to accommodate an employee's disability can be a basis for liability.

The district court's grant of summary judgment on Burnett's ADA claim was therefore erroneous, and this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.

Atkinson v. Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co., 114 F.2d 879 (7th Cir. 1940) is not directly on point but supports the general principle that employment discrimination claims require a nuanced analysis of the facts.

In conclusion, the district court's grant of summary judgment was erroneous, and this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.
