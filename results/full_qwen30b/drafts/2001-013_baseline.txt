**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) properly exercised jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) to issue citations to Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., for violations occurring aboard the *MR. BELDON*, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation *per se* preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act—particularly where the vessel in question is uninspected and subject to only minimal Coast Guard oversight. This Court should reverse, for the Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of § 4(b)(1) is inconsistent with the Act’s text, legislative history, and binding precedent.

**I. The OSH Act’s jurisdiction extends to uninspected vessels, including drilling barges, when they constitute a “workplace” under § 4(a).**

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act provides that the Act applies “with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The term “workplace” is not limited to land-based facilities or to vessels subject to comprehensive Coast Guard inspection. Rather, it encompasses any location where employees perform work, including mobile or floating platforms engaged in industrial operations. In *Barnes v. United States*, 412 U.S. 837 (1973), this Court held that the OSH Act applies to “any place where employees are engaged in employment,” emphasizing that the Act’s reach is “broad and inclusive.” Id. at 841. The *MR. BELDON* was not merely a vessel in navigation; it was a fixed, self-contained industrial workplace where employees performed drilling operations, maintained equipment, and lived for extended periods. The explosion that killed four workers and injured two occurred during the course of their employment, making the barge a “workplace” under the plain meaning of the statute.

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that the *MR. BELDON* was not a “workplace”—rests on a misreading of *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983). In *Clary*, the court held that OSHA regulations did not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels *in navigation*. 609 F.2d at 1122. But the court did not purport to define the scope of the OSH Act’s jurisdiction over *workplaces*; it addressed only the applicability of OSHA *regulations* to seamen. The holding was limited to the *enforcement* of OSHA standards, not the *existence* of jurisdiction. As this Court has recognized, “the mere fact that a federal agency has authority over a subject does not necessarily preclude another agency from having concurrent jurisdiction.” *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 102 (2000). The Fifth Circuit’s attempt to extrapolate from *Clary* a broad, categorical preemption of OSHA jurisdiction over all vessels—inspected or not—is unsupported by the opinion’s language or context.

Moreover, the *MR. BELDON* was not a vessel in navigation in the traditional sense. It was a stationary drilling platform, moored in a shallow bay, engaged in continuous industrial operations. Unlike a ship under way, it was not subject to the Coast Guard’s full inspection regime. The fact that the Coast Guard investigated the explosion does not equate to exclusive regulatory authority over the barge’s working conditions. The Coast Guard’s role in investigating marine casualties does not imply that it has exclusive power to prescribe or enforce occupational safety standards. *See* 46 U.S.C. § 6301 (authorizing Coast Guard investigation of marine casualties); *see also* *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. at 103 (noting that “the mere existence of federal regulation does not automatically preclude state or other federal regulation”).

**II. Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act does not preempt OSHA jurisdiction over uninspected vessels where the Coast Guard’s authority is limited and non-comprehensive.**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act “shall not apply to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Fifth Circuit held that the Coast Guard’s authority under 14 U.S.C. § 2 and 46 U.S.C. § 6301 is “exclusive” and thus preempts OSHA. But this interpretation ignores the critical distinction between *regulatory authority* and *comprehensive regulatory coverage*.

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is narrow and limited. As the Fifth Circuit itself acknowledged, the Coast Guard has issued safety regulations for uninspected vessels only in specific areas: fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and emergency alerting equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. These regulations are minimal and do not address the full range of occupational safety and health concerns—such as hazard communication, lockout/tagout procedures, or emergency response protocols—that OSHA regulates. Indeed, the Coast Guard has not promulgated any comprehensive safety standards for the working conditions of employees on uninspected drilling barges. The absence of such standards is not a mere oversight—it reflects the fact that the Coast Guard’s mandate is primarily maritime safety, not occupational health.

This Court has long recognized that preemption under § 4(b)(1) requires more than mere overlapping authority. In *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. at 104, the Court held that “Congress did not intend to preclude OSHA from regulating in areas where another agency has authority unless that agency has a comprehensive regulatory scheme.” The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is neither comprehensive nor exclusive.
