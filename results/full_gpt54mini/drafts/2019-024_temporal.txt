**ARGUMENT**

ERISA’s three-year limitations period begins only “from the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). That text requires more than the employer’s disclosure of information somewhere in a file, handbook, or website. It requires actual knowledge by the plaintiff. This Court has long distinguished actual knowledge from constructive notice. See *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342, 349-50 (1875) (limitations are not triggered by facts merely available to a claimant if the claimant lacks actual awareness); *Urie v. Thompson*, 337 U.S. 163, 169-70 (1949) (rejecting a limitations rule that would charge a plaintiff with knowledge he did not in fact possess); *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946) (equity does not “reward[]” a wrongdoer where a plaintiff has not discovered the basis for suit).

Intel’s rule would rewrite “actual knowledge” out of the statute and substitute constructive notice. But Congress chose a different phrase. Where Congress uses one term in contrast to another, courts must give the chosen term independent force. See *United States v. Alabama Great Southern R. Co.*, 142 U.S. 615, 621 (1892) (courts must give effect to the words Congress used); *Parker v. Bell*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 314, 317 (1853) (statutes are to be construed so that no word is rendered superfluous). “Actual knowledge” means knowledge that is actually possessed, not knowledge that could have been obtained had the plaintiff read every disclosure placed before him. See *The Wyoming*, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 713, 715-16 (1866) (knowledge in legal usage means awareness of the fact, not mere opportunity to know it).

That is especially true where, as here, Congress created a specific limitations rule in a fiduciary context. Equity has long required that fiduciaries act with candor and loyalty, and limitations rules have not historically protected fiduciaries who merely hide behind disclosed but unread documents. See *Prevost v. Gratz*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 481, 498-99 (1821) (fiduciary obligation is enforced with exacting regard to actual circumstances); *Twin-Lick Oil Co. v. Marbury*, 91 U.S. 587, 588-89 (1876) (limitations in trust-like settings depend on actual awareness of the wrong). To hold that a participant “had actual knowledge” because Intel made materials available would collapse actual knowledge into constructive notice, precisely the result Congress rejected. See *Davis v. Mills*, 194 U.S. 451, 454-55 (1904) (limitations provisions are applied according to their terms, not by judicially expanding them).

Intel’s position also conflicts with the traditional rule that ignorance of a wrong is not cured by the mere existence of documents in the public domain. In *Bailey v. Glover*, the Court explained that where a plaintiff is unaware of the fraud, limitations do not begin simply because the facts might have been discovered earlier. 88 U.S. at 349-50. Likewise, in *Urie v. Thompson*, the Court rejected a theory that would deem a plaintiff aware of a latent wrong before he in fact knew of it. 337 U.S. at 169-70. ERISA’s use of “actual knowledge” tracks that same principle: the clock starts when the participant actually knows the breach, not when the defendant has performed its disclosure obligations.

Intel’s contrary reading would create practical and doctrinal anomalies. Participants often receive dense plan documents, summaries, and periodic disclosures. If mere distribution sufficed, then a fiduciary could satisfy § 1113(2) by burying the breach in paperwork, even if no participant ever read or remembered it. Congress did not enact a paper-shuffling exception to fiduciary accountability. The better reading is the one grounded in the text: actual knowledge is a state of mind, not a mailing record. See *Holmberg*, 327 U.S. at 397; *Bailey*, 88 U.S. at 349-50.

Nor can Intel rely on the fact that disclosures were available on websites or in plan materials. Availability is not awareness. The law does not impute knowledge merely because a person had access to information. See *The Wyoming*, 70 U.S. at 715-16. If Congress had intended such a constructive-notice rule, it could have said so. Instead, it used the stricter phrase “actual knowledge,” which requires proof that Sulyma actually knew of the allegedly imprudent investments and their allegedly unlawful nature before the three-year period began. See *Parker*, 57 U.S. at 317; *Alabama Great Southern R. Co.*, 142 U.S. at 621.

Because Intel’s position would allow defendants to transform “actual knowledge” into “constructive availability,” it cannot be squared with the statute. The Court should reject that reading and hold that a plaintiff does not have “actual knowledge” merely because the defendant disclosed the relevant information, where the plaintiff did not actually read or remember it.
