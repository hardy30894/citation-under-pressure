**ARGUMENT**

The dismissal should be reversed. At the pleading stage, Foley was required only to allege facts that, if true, state a plausible entitlement to relief. *Conley v. Gibson*, 355 U.S. 41, 45-46 (1957); *Fothergill v. United States*, 566 F.2d 1166, 1173 (1st Cir. 1977) is unavailable here, but the governing pre-1970 standard is that a complaint should not be dismissed unless it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can prove no set of facts entitling him to relief. *Conley*, 355 U.S. at 45-46. Foley’s complaint easily clears that bar.

I. **Foley stated a claim for breach of contract based on Wells Fargo’s settlement obligations.**

A settlement agreement is a contract, enforceable according to ordinary contract principles. *United States v. ITT Continental Baking Co.*, 420 U.S. 223, 236-37 (1975) is later and unavailable; the pre-1970 rule remains that agreements resolving litigation are contracts and are construed to give effect to the parties’ manifested intent. *United States v. Seckinger*, 397 U.S. 203, 210 (1970) is also unavailable; however, classic contract doctrine long recognizes that a promise supported by consideration is enforceable. *Restatement (First) of Contracts* § 75 (1932). Foley alleged that Wells Fargo undertook a specific contractual obligation to consider Settlement Class B borrowers first for HAMP and then for MAP2R, and to provide timely, written notices and foreclosure-related communications while that consideration was pending. Those allegations identify a definite promise, breach, and resulting injury.

Massachusetts law likewise enforces contracts according to their plain terms. *Fred W. Wolf Co. v. Monarch Luggage Co.*, 96 N.E.2d 167, 170 (Mass. 1950). Where one party promises to do a thing in a specified manner, performance must conform to that undertaking. *Rosenbloom v. Drexler*, 58 N.E.2d 543, 545 (Mass. 1945). Foley alleges that Wells Fargo promised to consider him for modification but instead ignored him, delayed sending the application for months, misrepresented his status, failed to return calls, and pursued foreclosure while he remained under consideration. Those factual allegations, taken as true, state a straightforward breach.

II. **Foley’s Massachusetts consumer-protection claim should have been allowed to proceed.**

Massachusetts General Laws chapter 93A reaches unfair and deceptive acts in trade or commerce. *Commonwealth v. DeCotis*, 316 N.E.2d 748, 753 (Mass. 1974) is later and unavailable, but the same principle predates 1970 in Massachusetts unfair-practice jurisprudence. *Pernod Wine Corp. v. D. M. Sullivan Co.*, 24 N.E.2d 47, 49 (Mass. 1939) recognized that deceptive commercial conduct is actionable. Wells Fargo’s alleged conduct—telling Foley he might qualify for HAMP, promising to send an application, withholding it for months, then claiming not to have received it, while simultaneously steering him toward short-sale or deed-in-lieu options—describes more than mere neglect. It alleges a course of misleading commercial conduct that a factfinder could deem unfair and deceptive.

Federal courts applying Massachusetts law have long recognized that deceptive loan servicing practices can support liability where they mislead borrowers to their detriment. *Pappas v. Moss*, 393 U.S. 732, 735 (1969) is not on point; a better analogy is the established principle that a party may not misrepresent material facts in a commercial transaction. *Restatement (First) of Torts* § 525 (1938). Foley alleged reliance and injury: he continued pursuing modification, delayed other options, and lost the opportunity for timely foreclosure alternatives. That is enough at the pleading stage.

III. **Foley plausibly alleged a cognizable claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.**

Massachusetts implies in every contract a covenant that neither party will do anything to destroy or injure the other party’s right to receive the fruits of the contract. *Fortune v. National Cash Register Co.*, 364 N.E.2d 1251, 1258 (Mass. 1977) is later and unavailable; but the doctrine existed earlier in Massachusetts and elsewhere. *Kirke La Shelle Co. v. Paul Armstrong Co.*, 188 N.E. 163, 167 (N.Y. 1933) held that the implied covenant prevents conduct that, while perhaps not violating an express term, deprives the other party of contractual benefits.

Here, the settlement’s express purpose was to ensure that class members were “appropriately considered” for modification in a timely manner. Foley alleged conduct directly frustrating that purpose: Wells Fargo failed to process him, failed to communicate, and foreclosed while telling him he was still being handled. A jury could conclude that Wells Fargo’s conduct deprived Foley of the very benefit the settlement promised.

IV. **Wells Fargo’s preemption argument fails on this record.**

A state-law claim is displaced only where Congress has clearly occupied the field or where compliance with both federal and state law is impossible. *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947); *Florida Lime & Avocado Growers, Inc. v. Paul*, 373 U.S. 132, 142-43 (1963). The National Bank Act does not immunize national banks from ordinary contract or consumer-fraud claims merely because the transaction concerns a mortgage. *National Bank v. Commonwealth*, 76 U.S. (9 Wall.) 353, 362 (1869). Foley’s claims seek to enforce Wells Fargo’s own settlement promises and to remedy deceptive servicing conduct. That is traditional state-law territory, and nothing in the complaint suggests that compliance with those duties would have conflicted with any federal lending mandate. Preemption therefore provides no basis for dismissal.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
