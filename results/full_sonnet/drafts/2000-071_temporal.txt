**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford's Community Use Policy Draws a Permissible Line at the Boundary of a Limited Forum, Not an Unconstitutional Viewpoint Restriction.**

When a government entity opens its property to private speech, it need not open the property for every purpose. As this Court recognized in *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Id.* at 47. Milford's Community Use Policy dedicates its buildings after hours to "social, civic and recreational" activities and to instruction in "moral and character development." It excludes "religious purposes" altogether. That line is drawn at the level of subject matter—worship and religious instruction versus secular community activities—not at the level of viewpoint among competing speakers on a common topic.

This Court's early public-forum decisions confirm that government retains authority to define the boundaries of the access it grants, so long as it does not use that authority to suppress disfavored viewpoints on a subject it has chosen to admit. In *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), and *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court struck down licensing schemes because officials denied access based on hostility to the *particular views* of the speakers who wished to address permitted subjects in a forum already open to that category of speech. Milford's Policy does the opposite: it categorically excludes an entire subject—religious worship and instruction—for every organization, religious or secular. The Good News Club was not excluded because its views on morality differed from those of the Boy Scouts or 4-H; it was excluded because its meetings consist of proselytizing, prayer, and religious conversion, activities that no group, whatever its philosophy, may conduct under the Policy. That is a content-based limit on the forum's subject matter, not viewpoint discrimination among speakers on an admitted topic.

**II. Even If the Exclusion Implicated the Club's Speech Rights, Milford's Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation Independently Justified It.**

Milford's judgment that admitting the Club's activities would cross the Establishment Clause line finds firm support in this Court's precedents governing religious activity on public school property. The Establishment Clause commits the State to a position in which it may neither advance nor inhibit religion through the machinery of its public schools. *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), while upholding a neutral transportation subsidy, cautioned that "[t]he First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state. That wall must be kept high and impregnable. We could not approve the slightest breach." *Id.* at 18.

That caution has particular force where, as here, young children are involved and the religious activity occurs on the school's own premises in immediate connection with the school day. In *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), the Court held that a public school could not permit religious instructors to use its classrooms during the school day to teach religion to willing students, because doing so lent the prestige and machinery of the public school to religious instruction. Although *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), later approved a "released time" program, it did so only because the religious instruction there occurred *off* school premises, at churches or synagogues, with no religious activity conducted "in the public school buildings." *Id.* at 308, 315. The Court in *Zorach* expressly preserved *McCollum*'s condemnation of on-campus religious instruction, distinguishing the two cases precisely on the situs of the religious exercise.

Milford's elementary school occupies the same position *McCollum* addressed, not the position approved in *Zorach*. The Club's meetings take place inside the school building, immediately after the close of the school day, attended by the same young children who spent the preceding hours under the school's authority and supervision. The Club's own submissions establish that its purpose is not incidental religious content within a secular lesson but organized worship: opening and closing prayer, scripture memorization contests, a "challenge and invitation" segment inviting children to accept Jesus Christ as their personal savior, and private post-meeting prayer sessions with children as young as six. This is precisely the kind of religious exercise that *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421 (1962), and *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), forbid public schools from sponsoring, even indirectly, within their own walls. *Engel* held that it is "no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government." *Id.* at 425. *Schempp* likewise condemned school-connected religious exercises regardless of their voluntary character, because the school's imprimatur remains on activities conducted in its buildings under its sponsorship.

Milford reasonably concluded that opening its elementary school, immediately after hours, to a proselytizing religious club for six-to-twelve-year-old children would create the very appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *McCollum*, *Engel*, and *Schempp* forbid. That the exclusion also happens to track the boundary of Milford's limited forum confirms, rather than undermines, the legitimacy of the Policy. The judgment below should be affirmed.
