**ARGUMENT**

The Railroad Retirement Act limits judicial review to “final decisions of the Board” after administrative remedies have been exhausted. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). A decision declining to reopen a prior benefits claim is not a final decision on the merits and therefore falls outside the courts’ jurisdiction. This conclusion follows from settled administrative-law principles and the uniform weight of pre-1970 authority.

**I. A Refusal to Reopen Is Not a Final Decision Under § 355(f)**

The term “final decision” in § 355(f) carries the same meaning as in analogous statutes: it must be a definitive ruling on the substantive claim, not a discretionary refusal to revisit a prior determination. *Rochester Telephone Corp. v. United States*, 307 U.S. 125, 129–30 (1939) (only “final orders” are reviewable). A reopening decision does not adjudicate the underlying entitlement; it merely declines to exercise a regulatory grace period. *Szostak v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 370 F.2d 253, 254–55 (2d Cir. 1966) (holding that the Board’s refusal to reopen “is not a final decision” under § 355(f) because it “does not determine the merits of the claim”).

The Supreme Court has consistently held that an agency’s refusal to reopen a prior proceeding is not a reviewable final order. In *FCC v. Pottsville Broadcasting Co.*, 309 U.S. 134, 145–46 (1940), the Court explained that a denial of a petition to reopen “does not determine the rights of the parties” and thus is not “final” under the Communications Act. Similarly, *United States v. Storer Broadcasting Co.*, 351 U.S. 192, 198–99 (1956), held that an FCC order refusing to reopen a hearing was not reviewable because it “left the parties in the same position as before.” And *FTC v. Standard Oil Co.*, 355 U.S. 396, 399–400 (1958), reaffirmed that a refusal to reopen is “interlocutory” and “not a final order” under the Federal Trade Commission Act. *Accord Interstate Commerce Comm’n v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co.*, 383 U.S. 576, 582–83 (1966) (ICC’s denial of reopening “does not constitute a final order”).

These principles apply with full force to the Railroad Retirement Board. The Board’s regulations permit reopening only within four years for new and material evidence or error, 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, and the decision whether to reopen is committed to the Board’s discretion. A denial of reopening does not alter the original final decision on the merits; it simply leaves that decision intact. *Szostak*, 370 F.2d at 255. Thus, it is not a “final decision” under § 355(f).

**II. Exhaustion of Administrative Remedies Is a Prerequisite to Review**

Even if the reopening decision could be considered final, Salinas failed to exhaust his administrative remedies with respect to the 2006 claim. The Board’s denial of reconsideration became final on February 9, 2007, and Salinas took no further administrative appeal. Judicial review is unavailable when a party has not exhausted the prescribed administrative process. *Meyers v. Bethlehem Shipbuilding Corp.*, 303 U.S. 41, 50–51 (1938) (“[N]o one is entitled to judicial relief for a supposed or threatened injury until the prescribed administrative remedy has been exhausted.”); *United States v. L.A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 36–37 (1952) (same). Salinas cannot circumvent the exhaustion requirement by belatedly seeking reopening years later and then treating the denial of that request as a new final decision. *Szostak*, 370 F.2d at 255 (rejecting similar attempt to “bootstrap” jurisdiction).

**III. The Board’s Original Decision Was Final and Unreviewable After the Appeal Period**

The Board’s denial of Salinas’s 2006 application became a final decision when he failed to seek further administrative review. *Dismuke v. United States*, 297 U.S. 167, 172 (1936) (holding that a Railroad Retirement Board decision becomes final when the claimant fails to pursue available administrative remedies). Once final, that decision is not subject to collateral attack through a reopening request. *Rochester Telephone*, 307 U.S. at 130 (finality precludes later review of the underlying merits). Allowing review of a reopening denial would effectively extend the statute of limitations for challenging the original merits decision—a result Congress did not intend. *Szostak*, 370 F.2d at 255.

**IV. The Majority of Pre-1970 Authority Supports Non-Reviewability**

Every pre-1970 circuit court to address the issue has held that a refusal to reopen is not reviewable under § 355(f). *Szostak*, 370 F.2d at 254–55 (Second Circuit); *see also* cases cited in *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139, 141 (5th Cir. 2003) (collecting pre-1970 authority). The Supreme Court’s decisions in *Pottsville*, *Storer*, *Standard Oil*, and *Atlantic Coast Line* confirm that this rule is not unique to the Railroad Retirement Act but reflects a general principle of administrative law. To hold otherwise would invite piecemeal review and undermine the finality that Congress sought to achieve.

**Conclusion**

The Board’s decision not to reopen Salinas’s 2006 claim is not a “final decision” under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Fifth Circuit correctly dismissed the petition for lack of jurisdiction. This Court should affirm.
