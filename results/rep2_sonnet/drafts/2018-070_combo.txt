ARGUMENT

I. OCSLA's Text, Structure, and History Confirm That State Law Applies on the Outer Continental Shelf Only to Fill Gaps in Federal Law

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act declares that federal law governs the Shelf "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). Only "[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law are the "civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State" adopted "as the law of the United States." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This Court has explained that "the purpose of the [OCSLA] was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf." Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). Congress modeled that body of law on the treatment of federal enclaves, borrowing state law only as a supplemental source to be tapped when federal law itself supplied no rule of decision.

This Court's federal-enclave jurisprudence confirms that state law is received into federal enclaves narrowly, and only where federal law is otherwise silent. In Fort Leavenworth Railroad Co. v. Lowe, 114 U.S. 525 (1885), the Court held that state law in force at the time of a federal enclave's creation remains the governing rule only until Congress substitutes its own regulation, because the enclave's civil life "cannot be allowed to lapse into barbarism" for want of law. That same logic controlled in James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94 (1940), where the Court held that state safety statutes continued to operate on a federal enclave only because "no legislation of Congress" had displaced them—confirming that state law survives in an enclave solely as a stopgap, not as an independent source of regulation that operates alongside a complete federal scheme.

This Court has likewise refused to stretch state-law borrowing statutes beyond their gap-filling purpose. In Pacific Coast Dairy, Inc. v. Department of Agriculture, 318 U.S. 285 (1943), the Court held that California's milk-price regulations could not reach a federal enclave, notwithstanding a general assimilation statute, because the enclave was subject to exclusive federal jurisdiction and state regulatory law did not automatically follow. Similarly, in Johnson v. Yellow Cab Transit Co., 321 U.S. 383 (1944), the Court held that Oklahoma's liquor-control law could not be applied within a federal enclave absent clear congressional authorization extending it there. And in Paul v. United States, 371 U.S. 245 (1963), the Court again declined to read a state-law assimilation statute—there, the Buck Act—expansively, holding that California's milk-marketing regulations did not extend to federal enclaves because Congress had not clearly so provided. Each of these decisions rests on the same premise this Court applied to the criminal-law counterpart of OCSLA's borrowing clause in United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958): Congress may prospectively adopt state law for federal territory, but only as a mechanism to avoid the "gaps" that would otherwise exist because Congress cannot practically legislate for every contingency on federal land. Id. at 293–94.

OCSLA's choice-of-law clause performs exactly this enclave-style function. It does not create a freestanding regime of concurrent state and federal wage-and-hour regulation; it borrows state law only where federal law leaves a void. That is precisely the standard the Fifth Circuit later distilled in Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners' Mutual Insurance Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), which held that state law is adopted under § 1333(a)(2)(A) only where there is a "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" left by federal law. Id. at 1036. That test flows directly from Rodrigue's holding that state law becomes available as surrogate federal law only once "any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]" has been "remove[d]" by the absence of a controlling federal remedy. 395 U.S. at 366.

II. The FLSA's Comprehensive Wage-and-Hour Scheme Leaves No Gap for California Law to Fill

Congress enacted the FLSA as a comprehensive national scheme regulating minimum wages, overtime, and working conditions in industries affecting commerce. See United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100, 109 (1941) (upholding the FLSA as a complete exercise of Congress's power to prescribe minimum standards for wages and hours). Because the FLSA already governs wage and hour claims of offshore platform workers like respondent, there is no "significant void or gap," Continental Oil, 417 F.2d at 1036, for California law to occupy. Judge Learned Hand's analysis in Guiseppi v. Walling, 144 F.2d 608 (2d Cir. 1944), is instructive: even where the FLSA contains a savings clause preserving more protective state standards for certain purposes, that clause operates within the FLSA's own comprehensive structure and does not signal that Congress left a regulatory vacuum for wage-and-hour law generally.

Under OCSLA's enclave-derived framework, as construed in Rodrigue, Sharpnack, Fort Leavenworth, Sadrakula, Pacific Coast Dairy, Johnson, and Paul, the pertinent question is not whether state law is merely "not inconsistent" with federal law, but whether federal law has left a gap for state law to fill. Because the FLSA comprehensively addresses the wage and hour claims respondent raises, California law supplies no rule of decision on the Outer Continental Shelf, and the district court correctly dismissed respondent's claims.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
