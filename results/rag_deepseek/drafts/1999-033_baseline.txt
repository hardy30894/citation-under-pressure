**ARGUMENT**

**THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT GIVE RISE TO A CAUSE OF ACTION FOR A “CLASS OF ONE” WHERE THE PLAINTIFF ALLEGES ONLY THAT SHE WAS SINGLED OUT WITHOUT ANY CLAIM OF CLASS-BASED OR GROUP-BASED DISCRIMINATION.**

The text, history, and purpose of the Equal Protection Clause compel the conclusion that it protects individuals only from discrimination that is based on their membership in a class or group. This Court has never recognized a “class-of-one” theory untethered from any allegation of group-based animus, and the decision below—which permits a plaintiff to proceed solely on a claim that state action was motivated by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him”—cannot be squared with this Court’s precedents.

**I. The Equal Protection Clause, by Its Terms and Tradition, Addresses Group-Based Discrimination.**

The Equal Protection Clause commands that no State shall “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. From its inception, this provision was understood to prohibit discriminatory classifications that disadvantage a defined class of persons. *See Strauder v. West Virginia*, 100 U.S. 303, 307-08 (1880) (clause was designed to assure “the colored race” the “full and equal benefit of all laws”). This Court has consistently applied the Clause to claims that a plaintiff was treated differently because of membership in a protected class, such as race, *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954), or gender, *Reed v. Reed*, 404 U.S. 71, 76-77 (1971). The Clause does not create a general federal remedy for arbitrary government action; that is the province of the Due Process Clause. *See Engquist v. Oregon Dep’t of Agric.*, 553 U.S. 591, 598-99 (2008) (noting that the Equal Protection Clause is “not a guarantee against incorrect or ill-advised decisions”).

**II. This Court’s Precedents Require a Showing of Class-Based Discrimination.**

In *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976), this Court held that the Equal Protection Clause is violated only when a plaintiff proves “discriminatory purpose” against a “cognizable group.” Similarly, in *McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279, 292 (1987), the Court emphasized that a plaintiff must show that the State “selected or reaffirmed a particular course of action at least in part ‘because of,’ not merely ‘in spite of,’ its adverse effects upon an identifiable group.” The “class-of-one” theory advanced by respondent dispenses with this requirement entirely, allowing a plaintiff to prevail by showing only that she was treated differently from a single other person for reasons unrelated to any legitimate state objective. That approach would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a general tort of governmental unfairness, a result this Court has consistently rejected. *See Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) (the Equal Protection Clause does not “assure uniformity of judicial decisions or immunity from merely erroneous action”).

**III. The Decision Below Conflicts with This Court’s Holding in *Engquist*.**

In *Engquist v. Oregon Dep’t of Agric.*, 553 U.S. 591 (2008), this Court held that the “class-of-one” theory does not apply in the public employment context because “the government as employer” must have discretion to make individualized decisions. *Id.* at 605. The reasoning of *Engquist* applies with equal force here. The Village of Willowbrook’s decision to demand a 33-foot easement from respondent, rather than the 15-foot easement it required of others, was a discretionary, individualized determination about the terms of a municipal service connection. Such decisions are “by their nature subjective and individualized,” *id.* at 603, and subjecting them to federal equal protection scrutiny would invite federal courts to second-guess routine administrative judgments. This Court has never extended the “class-of-one” theory to the context of municipal service provision, and it should decline to do so now.

**IV. The “Spiteful Effort” Standard Is Unworkable and Unmoored from Equal Protection Doctrine.**

The court of appeals held that respondent could state an equal protection claim by alleging that the Village’s demand was motivated by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him.” That standard is both vague and inconsistent with this Court’s requirement of discriminatory purpose. *See Personnel Adm’r v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 279 (1979) (discriminatory purpose requires that the decisionmaker “selected or reaffirmed a particular course of action at least in part ‘because of’ ” its adverse effects on a group). A claim of “spite” or ill will, without more, does not implicate the Equal Protection Clause, which protects against discrimination based on group characteristics, not against arbitrary or malicious treatment of individuals. *See Village of Arlington Heights v. Metro. Hous. Dev. Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977) (requiring proof that “discriminatory purpose was a motivating factor”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
