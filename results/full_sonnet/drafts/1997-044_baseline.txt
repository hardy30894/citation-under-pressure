# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 523(a)(2)(A)'s Text Confines Nondischargeability to the Value "Obtained By" Fraud.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge any debt "to the extent obtained by" false pretenses, false representation, or actual fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). This limiting phrase is not surplusage; it defines the outer boundary of what Congress chose to except from the fresh start otherwise afforded debtors. The statute does not except "any debt arising from" fraud, nor "any debt related to" fraud — it excepts a debt only "to the extent obtained by" fraud. Congress's choice of this narrower formulation must be given effect. See *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983) ("[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another . . . it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.").

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this textual limitation in *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir. 1991), holding that a jury's punitive damages award was dischargeable because it did not constitute a debt "obtained by" the debtor's fraud — only the compensatory portion actually procured through the fraudulent conduct fell within the exception. *Id.* at 198–99. The same logic applies here. Cohen obtained $31,382.50 in excess rent from respondents. That sum, and only that sum, was "obtained by" the conduct the Bankruptcy Court characterized as fraudulent. The remaining $62,765 in treble damages, together with the additional award of attorney's fees and costs, was not money Cohen ever received from respondents; it is a statutory penalty imposed by the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, N.J. Stat. Ann. § 56:8-19, as a matter of state policy independent of what Cohen actually took. A penalty measured by a multiple of the underlying loss, or an award of fees incurred in litigating the claim, is conceptually and factually distinct from money "obtained by" the fraudulent act itself.

## II. The Bankruptcy Code's Fresh-Start Policy Requires That Exceptions to Discharge Be Construed Narrowly.

This Court has long recognized that the "central purpose" of the bankruptcy laws is to give debtors "a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). Consistent with that purpose, exceptions to discharge under § 523 must be construed narrowly against the creditor and in favor of the debtor's fresh start. *See Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286–87 (1991) (discussing the "fresh start" policy underlying the Code while confirming that exceptions exist only for the dishonest debtor, not to punish beyond the fraud itself).

Grogan's preponderance-of-the-evidence holding rested on the premise that § 523(a) exceptions are aimed narrowly at ensuring that "the wrongdoer, not the victim of the wrong, bears the burden" of the loss the fraud actually caused. 498 U.S. at 286–87. Nothing in Grogan suggests that Congress intended § 523(a)(2)(A) to operate as a vehicle for importing state-law punitive multipliers, fee-shifting statutes, or other derivative remedies into the nondischargeability exception. To read the statute otherwise — as the Third Circuit did below — transforms a narrow, carefully worded exception into an open-ended incorporation of whatever damages a state legislature chooses to attach to a fraud-based cause of action, regardless of whether those damages reflect anything "obtained by" the debtor.

## III. The Third Circuit's Reliance on *In re St. Laurent* Improperly Expands the Exception Beyond Its Text.

The court below rejected *Levy* in favor of the Eleventh Circuit's decision in *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), reasoning that the term "debt" in § 523(a) is broad enough to encompass an entire judgment, compensatory and punitive alike. Pet. App. at [ ] (citing *St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d at 679). But that reasoning reads the word "debt" in isolation, ignoring the immediately following restrictive clause — "to the extent obtained by." The Third Circuit compounded this error by treating the mandatory nature of the New Jersey treble-damages provision as a reason to treat the entire award as "foreseeable" and therefore part of the debt "incurred through his conduct." Pet. App. at [ ]. Foreseeability of a penalty is not the statutory test; the test is whether the debt was "obtained by" the fraud. A debtor's foresight that a state legislature might treble his liability does not transform the treble portion into money he obtained from his victim.

Nor does *Molzof v. United States*, 502 U.S. 301 (1992), support the court of appeals' analysis. *Molzof* held only that the meaning of "punitive damages" under a federal statute, the Federal Tort Claims Act, is a matter of federal common-law definition. *Id.* at 305. It says nothing about whether nominally "compensatory" statutory multipliers fall within the "obtained by" limitation of § 523(a)(2)(A); if anything, *Molzof*'s methodology confirms that this Court must look to the federal statutory text and the fresh-start purposes underlying it, not to state-law labels, in deciding what Congress excepted from discharge.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, only the $31,382.50 actually obtained by Cohen through the conduct found fraudulent should be excepted from discharge; the treble-damages award, attorney's fees, and costs — none of which constitute money "obtained by" the fraud — should be discharged. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
