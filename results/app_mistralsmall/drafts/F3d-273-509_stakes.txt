**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE RELEASE DOES NOT BAR APPELLANT’S CLAIMS BECAUSE IT DOES NOT EXTEND TO POST-INSOLVENCY INTEREST, FIDUCIARY BREACHES, OR CORPORATE WASTE.**

The release executed by Bancorp on June 6, 1988, is narrowly drafted and does not encompass the claims now asserted. Under New York contract law, the scope of a release is determined by its plain language, and any ambiguities are construed against the drafter. *See generally Metropolitan Life Ins. Co. v. RJR Nabisco, Inc.*, 906 F.2d 884, 890 (2d Cir. 1990). The release here releases the FDIC only from claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” This language is limited to the OCC’s closure decision and does not extend to the FDIC’s post-closure conduct, including its payment of post-insolvency interest, breach of fiduciary duty, or corporate waste.

The FDIC’s argument that the release bars all claims arising from the receivership is unsupported by the text. The release does not mention the FDIC’s actions as receiver, nor does it purport to waive claims unrelated to the OCC’s closure decision. *See Bank of N.Y. v. Resolution Trust Corp.*, 29 F.3d 30, 35 (2d Cir. 1994) (holding that a release must be construed narrowly and does not bar claims outside its express terms). The FDIC’s attempt to expand the release’s scope beyond its plain language is unavailing.

**II. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS DOES NOT BAR APPELLANT’S CLAIMS BECAUSE THE CAUSE OF ACTION DID NOT ACCRUE UNTIL THE TERMINATION OF THE RECEIVERSHIP.**

The district court erred in holding that New York’s six-year statute of limitations, N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7), bars Bancorp’s claims. Under New York law, a cause of action accrues when the plaintiff has a “complete present cause of action”—that is, when the plaintiff can succeed on the merits. *See Ackerman v. Price Waterhouse*, 84 N.Y.2d 535, 541 (1994). For claims of unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste, the injury is not complete until the FDIC’s actions are finalized or the harm is fully realized.

Here, the FDIC’s payment of post-insolvency interest, its breach of fiduciary duty, and its corporate waste were ongoing acts that were not finalized until the receivership terminated on November 1, 1995. *See generally In re Estate of Nevelson*, 127 A.D.3d 1006, 1008 (N.Y. App. Div. 2015) (holding that a cause of action accrues when the wrongful act is complete and the injury is suffered). Because the FDIC’s conduct was not finalized until the receivership ended, the statute of limitations did not begin to run until that date. *See also Doe v. City of New York*, 15 N.Y.3d 696, 700 (2010) (holding that a cause of action accrues when the plaintiff knows or should know of the injury).

The FDIC’s reliance on *CPLR* § 213(7) is misplaced. That statute applies to claims for “breach of fiduciary duty,” but only where the breach is a discrete act that causes immediate harm. *See Kaufman v. Cohen*, 307 A.D.2d 113, 117 (N.Y. App. Div. 2003). Here, the FDIC’s conduct was continuous and did not cause immediate harm; the injury was realized only upon the termination of the receivership. The district court’s contrary holding misapplies New York law.

**III. THE FDIC’S CONDUCT AS RECEIVER GIVES RISE TO INDEPENDENT CLAIMS NOT BARRED BY THE RELEASE OR THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS.**

Even if the release were construed to bar some claims, it does not bar claims arising from the FDIC’s conduct as receiver. The release pertains only to the OCC’s closure decision, not the FDIC’s post-closure actions. *See Resolution Trust Corp. v. Fleischer*, 913 F. Supp. 66, 70 (S.D.N.Y. 1996) (holding that a release does not bar claims arising from a receiver’s post-closure conduct).

Moreover, the FDIC’s conduct as receiver—including its payment of post-insolvency interest, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste—gives rise to independent claims that are not time-barred. The statute of limitations for these claims did not accrue until the receivership terminated, as the harm was not complete until that time. *See Nevelson*, 127 A.D.3d at 1008.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to the FDIC. The release does not bar Bancorp’s claims, and the statute of limitations did not begin to run until the termination of the receivership. The judgment below should be reversed.
