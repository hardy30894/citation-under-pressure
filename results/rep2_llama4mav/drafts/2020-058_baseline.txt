ARGUMENT

I. The Conferral of Temporary Protected Status Constitutes an "Admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255

The Third Circuit's decision in this case conflicts with the plain meaning of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) and ignores the legislative intent behind the Temporary Protected Status (TPS) program. The INA defines "admission" and "admitted" as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). However, the statute also provides that an alien granted TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4).

The conferral of TPS constitutes a form of admission, as it involves a formal determination by the government that the alien is entitled to remain in the United States. Flores v. USCIS, 718 F.3d 548, 554 (6th Cir. 2013) (holding that a grant of TPS is an "admission" for purposes of § 1255); Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017) (same). This interpretation is consistent with the legislative history of the TPS program, which was designed to provide relief to aliens who are unable to return to their home countries due to extraordinary conditions.

The Third Circuit's decision, on the other hand, relies on a narrow reading of the term "admission" that ignores the broader context of the INA. The court held that a grant of TPS does not constitute an "admission" because it does not involve a physical entry into the United States. Sanchez v. Mayorkas, No. 19-1311, slip op. at 7 (3d Cir. July 22, 2020). However, this interpretation is inconsistent with the statutory text, which defines "admission" in terms of "lawful entry" rather than physical presence. 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A).

Furthermore, the Third Circuit's decision undermines the purpose of the TPS program, which is to provide a safe haven for aliens who are unable to return to their home countries. By denying TPS recipients the opportunity to adjust their status to lawful permanent residents, the court's decision forces them to choose between remaining in the United States without authorization or returning to their home countries, where they may face danger or persecution.

II. The Third Circuit's Decision is Inconsistent with the Statutory Text and Legislative History

The Third Circuit's decision is also inconsistent with the statutory text and legislative history of the INA. The court held that the term "lawful status" in § 1254a(f)(4) is distinct from the term "admission" in § 1255. Sanchez, slip op. at 6. However, this interpretation ignores the fact that § 1254a(f)(4) specifically references § 1255, indicating that Congress intended for TPS recipients to be eligible for adjustment of status.

Moreover, the legislative history of the TPS program suggests that Congress intended to provide a pathway to permanent residence for TPS recipients. The program was created in response to the humanitarian crisis in El Salvador, and Congress explicitly stated that TPS recipients should be able to "make use" of the adjustment of status process. Ramirez, 852 F.3d at 961 (citing H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, pt. 1, at 11 (1990)).

In conclusion, the Third Circuit's decision is inconsistent with the plain meaning of the INA and the legislative intent behind the TPS program. The conferral of TPS constitutes an "admission" into the United States under § 1255, and TPS recipients should be eligible for adjustment of status. This Court should reverse the Third Circuit's decision and hold that a grant of TPS is an "admission" for purposes of § 1255.
