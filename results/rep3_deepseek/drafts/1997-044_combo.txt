ARGUMENT

I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF §523(a)(2)(A) LIMITS NONDISCHARGEABILITY TO THE VALUE OF PROPERTY ACTUALLY OBTAINED BY FRAUD.

The Bankruptcy Act of 1898, as amended, excepts from discharge “any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. §523(a)(2)(A). The critical phrase “to the extent obtained by” confines the exception to the precise value the debtor fraudulently acquired. This Court has long held that exceptions to discharge must be narrowly construed in favor of the debtor. *Wetmore v. Markoe*, 196 U.S. 68, 77 (1904) (“the policy of the bankrupt law is to relieve the honest debtor from the burden of his debts”). The statutory language does not sweep in punitive or statutory penalties that are not themselves “obtained” by the debtor’s fraud.

In *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877), this Court defined “actual fraud” under the prior Bankruptcy Act as “positive, intentional fraud,” not constructive fraud. The focus is on the debtor’s acquisition of property through deceit. The debt excepted is the debt “for” the money or property so obtained. Treble damages and attorney’s fees are not debts “for” property obtained; they are civil penalties imposed by state law for conduct that violates consumer protection statutes. They are not the fruits of the fraud but rather punishment for it.

II. TREBLE DAMAGES AND ATTORNEY’S FEES ARE NOT “OBTAINED BY” FRAUD AND ARE THEREFORE DISCHARGEABLE.

The lower court erred by treating the entire judgment as a single “debt” arising from fraud. This Court has consistently distinguished between compensatory damages that restore the victim’s loss and punitive damages that serve a different purpose. *Tinker v. Colwell*, 193 U.S. 473, 486 (1904) (discussing “willful and malicious injury” exception, noting that punitive damages may be dischargeable if not part of the injury). In *Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915), the Court held that a debt for fraud is nondischargeable only to the extent of the actual loss caused by the fraud. The “debt” that is excepted is the amount the debtor wrongfully obtained, not any additional penalty.

The phrase “to the extent obtained by” is a limitation of amount. *Davis v. Aetna Acceptance Co.*, 293 U.S. 328, 333 (1934) (interpreting similar language in §17a(2) of the Bankruptcy Act, holding that the exception applies only to the “property obtained” by false pretenses). The Court stated: “The debt is not excepted from discharge unless it was created by the fraud.” *Id.* at 334. Treble damages are not created by the fraud; they are created by the state statute. They are not “obtained” by the debtor; they are imposed by the court.

In *Bullock v. United States*, 265 U.S. 526, 530 (1924), the Court interpreted “obtained by” in a tax context to mean “procured through” the fraudulent act. The debtor does not “obtain” treble damages; the state imposes them as a penalty. The victim does not “obtain” treble damages from the debtor; the victim receives a windfall. The plain meaning of “obtained by” requires a direct causal link between the fraud and the specific property received. *Strang v. Bradner*, 114 U.S. 555, 561 (1885) (fraudulent conveyance case: “the debt must be one which the debtor incurred by means of the fraud”).

III. THE FRESH START POLICY OF THE BANKRUPTCY ACT REQUIRES DISCHARGE OF PUNITIVE AWARDS.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Bankruptcy Act is designed to give the honest debtor a fresh start. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (“the purpose of the Bankruptcy Act is to relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness and permit him to start afresh”). Punitive damages are not compensatory and do not represent a loss that the creditor suffered. Discharging them does not deprive the creditor of any property the debtor fraudulently obtained; it merely relieves the debtor of an additional penalty that serves no rehabilitative purpose in bankruptcy.

In *Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 236 U.S. 549, 554 (1915), the Court held that a debt for fraud is dischargeable if the creditor fails to prove the fraud. The burden is on the creditor to show that the debt falls within the exception. *Crawford v. Burke*, 195 U.S. 176, 193 (1904) (exception must be strictly construed). The creditor here has not shown that the treble damages were “obtained by” fraud; they are a statutory penalty.

IV. THE LOWER COURT’S INTERPRETATION IMPERMISSIBLY EXPANDS THE STATUTORY EXCEPTION.

The court below relied on a “foreseeability” rationale, but the statute does not ask what the debtor foresaw. It asks what the debtor obtained. *Hebert v. Crawford*, 228 U.S. 204, 212 (1913) (the exception is “confined to debts created by fraud”). The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act’s treble damages are not a debt created by fraud; they are a debt created by the statute. *Zavelo v. Reeves*, 227 U.S. 625, 630 (1913) (the Bankruptcy Act “should be construed liberally in favor of the debtor”). The lower court’s expansion of the exception to include all damages flowing from fraudulent conduct, regardless of whether they were obtained by the fraud, contradicts the narrow construction required by this Court. *Friend v. Talcott*, 228 U.S. 27, 33 (1913) (“the exceptions are to be strictly construed”).

The judgment below should be reversed, and the treble damages and attorney’s fees should be discharged.
