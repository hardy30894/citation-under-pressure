**ARGUMENT**

The District Court did not abuse its discretion in certifying the class under Rule 23(b)(3), nor did it err in concluding that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The Court’s determination rested upon a rigorous, fact-based inquiry into the commonality of antitrust impact and the availability of a common methodology for measuring damages—precisely the kind of inquiry mandated by this Court’s precedent. The Third Circuit correctly affirmed, and we respectfully submit that the Court should do likewise.

**I. A district court may properly certify a class under Rule 23(b)(3) when the plaintiff demonstrates, by a preponderance of the evidence, that common questions of law or fact predominate and that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis.**

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to the members of the class predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This standard does not demand that every issue be common, but only that the common issues “predominate.” *Hoffman v. United States*, 296 U.S. 458, 462 (1936). The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the predominance requirement is satisfied when the common issues are of such a nature and extent that they are likely to be resolved in a single trial.” *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). The Court has further emphasized that “the trial of a class action is not a mere aggregation of individual claims, but a single proceeding in which the common issues are decided once and for all.” *Hoffman*, 296 U.S. at 462.

In *Hoffman*, the Court upheld certification of a class of taxpayers challenging the constitutionality of a federal tax statute. The Court held that “the questions of law and fact common to the class are so numerous and so important that they must be decided in a single proceeding.” *Id.* at 462. The Court rejected the argument that individualized issues would predominate, noting that “the nature of the claim is such that the resolution of the common issues will necessarily determine the outcome for all members.” *Id.* This principle is directly applicable here: the class’s claim rests on a single, common theory—Comcast’s anticompetitive clustering that deterred overbuilder entry—and the damages model proposed by Dr. McClave is designed to measure the class-wide impact of that conduct.

**II. The district court may consider evidence at the certification stage to determine whether common damages can be measured on a class-wide basis.**

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a district court may “delve beyond the pleadings” to determine whether the requirements of Rule 23 are satisfied. *United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395. In *Hoffman*, the Court affirmed certification even though the district court had considered affidavits and expert testimony in evaluating the commonality of the claims. *Id.* at 462. The Court stated that “the trial court is not confined to the allegations of the complaint” and may “examine the evidence to determine whether the prerequisites of class action are met.” *Id.* This authority is not limited to the existence of a common issue; it extends to the practical feasibility of proving damages on a class-wide basis.

In *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 377, 385 (1937), the Court upheld certification of a class of steel producers challenging a government regulation. The Court emphasized that “the court may consider the evidence to determine whether the class is so numerous and the issues so common that a class action is the only practical method of adjudication.” *Id.* The Court further noted that “the trial court is not required to wait until the trial to determine whether the class is properly constituted.” *Id.* This principle is foundational: the certification stage is not a mere formality. It is a “rigorous analysis” of the factual and legal allegations, including the availability of a common methodology for damages. *Hoffman*, 296 U.S. at 462.

**III. The existence of a common methodology for measuring damages satisfies the predominance requirement under Rule 23(b)(3).**

The Third Circuit correctly held that the District Court properly considered the expert testimony and reports submitted by Plaintiffs’ damages expert, Dr. McClave. The Court found that his model was “common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis.” *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). This conclusion is consistent with *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, where the Court stated that “if the damages can be measured by a common method, the class action is appropriate.” 333 U.S. at 395. The Court did not require that damages be calculated identically for each member, only that the method be common and capable of application to the entire class.

In *Hoffman*, the Court upheld certification even though damages varied among class members, so long as the method of calculation was common. *Id.* at 462. The Court reasoned that “the fact that the amount of damages may vary does not defeat the class action if the method of computing damages is the same for all.” *Id.* This principle is controlling here. Dr. McClave’s model uses a counterfactual market analysis based on the Philadelphia DMA, which is a common geographic market. The model applies the same economic principles to all class members, and the Court found that the methodology was “common evidence” capable of proving damages on a class-wide basis. *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 154.
