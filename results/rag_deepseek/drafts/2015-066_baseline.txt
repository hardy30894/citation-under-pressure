## ARGUMENT

The decision below rests on a fundamental error: that an uncounseled tribal court conviction is “constitutionally infirm” for purposes of 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) because the Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply in tribal court. This reasoning conflicts with Supreme Court precedent, undermines tribal sovereignty, and creates an unwarranted circuit split.

### I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel governs only federal and state prosecutions. This Court has “consistently” held that the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes. *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). Tribal courts are “not constrained by the Sixth Amendment.” *United States v. Bryant*, 769 F.3d 671, 675 (9th Cir. 2014) (acknowledging this principle). Instead, tribal court proceedings are governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1303, which provides a right to counsel but does not require appointed counsel at tribal expense. *See Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) (ICRA “intruded needlessly on tribal self-government” when courts impose federal constitutional standards).

Because the Sixth Amendment does not apply, Bryant’s tribal court convictions are not “uncounseled” in any constitutional sense. They are valid under the law that governs them—ICRA—and are entitled to comity. *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997) (comity requires respect for tribal court judgments even when procedures differ from federal courts). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding in *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), which the panel below followed, erroneously imposed a Sixth Amendment requirement on tribal courts. That decision has been widely criticized as “odd” and “illogical.” *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 680–81 (Watford, J., concurring). It should be overruled.

### II. *Nichols v. United States* Permits Use of Valid Uncounseled Convictions as Predicate Offenses

In *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), this Court held that a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction—valid because no prison term was imposed—could be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines. The Court rejected the argument that such convictions are “void” for all purposes, explaining that they are “valid for the purposes of their own penalties” and may be used in later proceedings. *Id.* at 746–47 (quoting *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 372 (1979)). The Court distinguished *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), which barred use of uncounseled *felony* convictions, because misdemeanor convictions are treated differently. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 743–44.

The Ninth Circuit attempted to limit *Nichols* to the sentencing phase, arguing that the “reliability concerns” are greater when a conviction is used as an element of an offense. But *Nichols* itself rejected that distinction. The Court noted that recidivist statutes “penalize only the last offense committed by the defendant,” and the prior convictions are not punished anew. *Id.* at 747. The same is true under § 117(a): the predicate convictions are not elements of the crime in the sense of requiring proof of guilt; they simply establish the defendant’s status as a repeat offender. *See United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008) (“100% of the punishment is for the offense of conviction.”). Thus, the use of valid tribal court convictions as predicates does not violate the Sixth Amendment.

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with *Alabama v. Shelton* and Other Circuits

The panel below held that a tribal court conviction is invalid for predicate purposes if the defendant was imprisoned without counsel. But *Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654 (2002), makes clear that even when a suspended sentence is imposed without counsel, the underlying conviction remains valid. *Id.* at 661–62. The conviction is not “void”; only the sentence of imprisonment is invalid. *See also United States v. Acuna-Reyna*, 677 F.3d 1282, 1284–85 (11th Cir. 2012) (applying *Shelton*). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that the conviction itself is “constitutionally infirm”—directly conflicts with *Shelton*.

Moreover, the Eighth and Tenth Circuits have squarely held that uncounseled tribal court convictions may serve as predicates under § 117(a). *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997–98 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011). The Ninth Circuit’s decision creates a split that leaves tribal women in the Ninth Circuit less protected than those in other circuits—a result Congress could not have intended when it enacted § 117(a) to combat domestic violence in Indian country.

### IV. Congress Intended Tribal Court Convictions to Count Under § 117(a)

Section 117(a) explicitly includes “Indian tribal court” convictions as predicates. Congress enacted this statute to address the epidemic of domestic violence on tribal lands, where “American Indian and Alaska Native women are 2.5 times more likely to be raped or sexually assaulted.” *Ending Violence so Children Can Thrive* 38 (2014). The Ninth Circuit’s decision frustrates that purpose by requiring tribal courts to provide counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment—a requirement Congress has not imposed. Indeed, when Congress wanted to require appointed counsel in tribal court, it did so explicitly. *See* 25 U.S.C. § 1302(c) (requiring counsel for certain felony prosecutions under the Tribal Law and Order Act). The absence of such a requirement in § 117(a) demonstrates that Congress intended to allow use of tribal court convictions as predicates even without appointed counsel.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed. Tribal court convictions are valid under ICRA and may serve as predicate offenses under § 117(a) without violating the Sixth Amendment. This Court should clarify that *Nichols* applies to the guilt phase of recidivist prosecutions and that tribal court convictions are entitled to comity.
