**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals correctly held that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act does not impose a rigid situs-of-injury limitation on 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Congress extended Longshore Act coverage to disability or death “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf,” not merely to injuries physically suffered on the Shelf itself. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That text focuses on the relationship between the injury and shelf operations, not on geography alone. See *Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act*, 43 U.S.C. §§ 1331(a), 1333(b); *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 n.2 (1986) (noting that § 1333(b) is the only OCSLA provision that “superimposes a status requirement” and observing that it “has no bearing on this case”).

Petitioners’ situs-only reading cannot be reconciled with the statutory phrase “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” Congress did not say “injury occurring on the outer Continental Shelf.” Where Congress uses different words, courts must presume it meant something different. *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983). The Ninth Circuit properly gave effect to the broader causal language, holding that coverage exists where there is a substantial nexus between the injury and shelf operations. That construction also accords with the Supreme Court’s admonition that OCSLA’s coverage is determined “principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed,” *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219, while recognizing that § 1333(b) itself contains an express status component but no separate injury-situs requirement.

Petitioners rely on the Fifth Circuit’s contrary decision in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), but *Mills* should not be followed. *Mills* added a judicially created situs requirement that appears nowhere in the statute and narrows the coverage Congress wrote. See *id.* at 362. The better reading, adopted by the Third Circuit in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805, 809-11 (3d Cir. 1988), is that the statutory phrase “as the result of” imports causation, not geography alone. *Curtis* correctly allowed coverage for an employee injured off the Shelf because the injury was traceable to Shelf operations. *Id.* at 811.

That interpretation is reinforced by the remedial character of the LHWCA and OCSLA scheme. The Longshore Act is a workers’ compensation statute and is to be construed broadly to fulfill its humanitarian purpose. See *Northeast Marine Terminal Co. v. Caputo*, 432 U.S. 249, 268 (1977); *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69, 81-82 (1979). Congress incorporated that compensation regime into OCSLA to ensure coverage for workers whose injuries arise out of offshore resource operations, even when the injury manifests onshore. See *Pacific Operators Offshore, LLP v. Valladolid*, 565 U.S. 207, 214-15 (2012) (recognizing that § 1333(b) is not limited to injuries occurring on the Shelf itself and adopting a substantial-nexus standard). Although this Court need not rely on later precedent to resolve the issue, *Valladolid* confirms that the Ninth Circuit’s judgment was correct.

Petitioners also cannot derive support from *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985). That case addressed whether a worker on a platform in state waters satisfied the LHWCA’s “maritime employment” requirement; it did not construe § 1333(b)’s OCSLA compensation provision. *Id.* at 415-16. Likewise, *Tallentire* involved OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, not § 1333(b). *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 209, 219 n.2. Neither case compels the situs-of-injury limitation petitioners urge.

Finally, petitioners’ rule would produce arbitrary results inconsistent with the statutory purpose. Two workers performing identical offshore duties would receive different treatment depending on whether the final injury-causing event occurred a few feet offshore or a few feet inland. Congress enacted § 1333(b) to avoid such irrational gaps in coverage. The Ninth Circuit’s substantial-nexus test faithfully implements that purpose and the statutory text.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
