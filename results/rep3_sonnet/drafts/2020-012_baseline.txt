# ARGUMENT

## THE BOARD'S DENIAL OF SALINAS'S REQUEST TO REOPEN IS A "FINAL DECISION" SUBJECT TO JUDICIAL REVIEW UNDER 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)

Section 355(f) of the Railroad Unemployment Insurance Act, incorporated into the Railroad Retirement Act, provides that "[a]ny claimant... may... obtain a review of any final decision of the Board" in the courts of appeals. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Board's refusal to reopen Salinas's 2006 claim was such a final decision. The plain text of the statute, this Court's precedent in analogous contexts, and sound administrative practice all compel that conclusion.

### A. The Text of Section 355(f) Draws No Distinction Between Initial Decisions and Reopening Decisions

Section 355(f) speaks broadly of "any final decision of the Board." Nothing in that text excludes decisions denying reopening. A decision denying reopening is unquestionably a "decision"—it resolves a controversy between the claimant and the agency—and it is unquestionably "final," in that it terminates the administrative proceeding and leaves the claimant with no further recourse within the agency. See 20 C.F.R. § 261.5.

This Court has consistently held that when Congress uses broad, unqualified language like "any final decision," courts must give it full effect rather than read in silent exceptions. See *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 58 (1949) ("the word 'any' [means] all"). The Fifth Circuit's rule—and the rule of the four circuits it followed, see *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139, 141 (5th Cir. 2003)—reads into § 355(f) an exception for reopening decisions that Congress never wrote.

### B. This Court's Decision in Califano v. Sanders Does Not Support the Fifth Circuit's Categorical Bar

The Fifth Circuit in *Roberts* rested its holding on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), reasoning that Sanders foreclosed judicial review of refusals to reopen. *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 141. But *Sanders* addressed a fundamentally different statute and a fundamentally different question.

*Sanders* held that the Administrative Procedure Act does not itself confer subject-matter jurisdiction, and that 42 U.S.C. § 405(g)—the Social Security Act's judicial-review provision—does not authorize review of decisions refusing to reopen claims because such review would allow claimants to circumvent the Act's 60-day limitations period through "the simple expedient of terminating a prior action" and refiling. 430 U.S. at 108. The Court emphasized that § 405(g) authorizes review only of a "final decision of the Secretary made after a hearing," and concluded that a decision declining to reopen, which does not require a hearing, "is not a decision made after a hearing." *Id.* at 108.

Section 355(f) contains no comparable "after a hearing" limitation. It authorizes review of "any final decision," full stop. Moreover, unlike the Social Security Act's 60-day filing deadline at issue in *Sanders*, the Railroad Retirement Board's own regulations permit reopening for up to four years, 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, meaning judicial review of reopening denials poses none of the limitations-circumvention concerns that animated *Sanders*. The premise underlying *Roberts*—that *Sanders* controls here—does not withstand scrutiny.

### C. The D.C. Circuit's Reasoning in Stovic Is Correct and Should Guide This Court

The D.C. Circuit in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), correctly recognized that "the Railroad Retirement Act grants the Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations." *Id.* at 502. That court properly distinguished *Sanders* on the ground that § 355(f)'s text lacks the "after a hearing" qualifier central to the *Sanders* Court's reasoning, and that the Railroad Retirement Board's regulatory scheme—unlike the scheme in *Sanders*—expressly contemplates reopening as part of the ordinary administrative process. *Id.* at 504–05.

The Second Circuit reached the same conclusion decades earlier in *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), and the Eighth Circuit followed suit in *Sones v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991). These courts correctly recognized that a refusal to reopen, when it rests on substantive grounds—as here, where the Board considered and rejected Salinas's showing under its own four-year reopening regulation—constitutes a reviewable final decision no different in kind from any other agency action disposing of a claimant's rights.

### D. Practical and Equitable Considerations Confirm That Reopening Decisions Must Be Reviewable

Denying review of reopening decisions insulates from any judicial check the Board's application of its own regulations, including whether "new and material evidence" or "administrative error" was present under 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. A rule immunizing such determinations from review invites arbitrary or erroneous administrative action with no remedy. See *Bowen v. Michigan Academy of Family Physicians*, 476 U.S. 667, 670 (1986) (recognizing "the strong presumption that Congress intends judicial review of administrative action").

Nothing in the Railroad Retirement Act's structure or purpose suggests Congress intended such a result. To the contrary, Congress modeled § 355(f) on other broad judicial-review provisions precisely to ensure that claimants receive meaningful oversight of Board action. The Fifth Circuit's rule, adhered to only because of the "rule of orderliness," Pet. App. 3, cannot be reconciled with the statute's text or purpose.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for review of the Board's decision on the merits.
