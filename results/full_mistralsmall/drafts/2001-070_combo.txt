**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” must be interpreted in light of the “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). Where the practice at issue is the execution of a class of offenders, this Court has repeatedly looked to objective indicia of national consensus—such as legislative enactments and jury practice—to determine whether such punishment is “cruel and unusual.” Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 173 (1976); see also Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947) (plurality opinion) (“The cruelty against which the Constitution protects a convicted man is cruelty inherent in the method of punishment, not the necessary suffering involved in any method employed to extinguish life humanely.”).

Here, the Commonwealth submits that the execution of mentally retarded offenders does not offend the Eighth Amendment. The Court’s precedents confirm that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit all executions of a particular class of offenders absent a clear national consensus to the contrary. See, e.g., McGautha v. California, 402 U.S. 183, 201 (1971) (“[T]he Eighth Amendment has not been regarded as a static concept.”); Solesbee v. Balkcom, 339 U.S. 9, 12 (1950) (“The cruelty against which the Constitution protects a convicted man is cruelty inherent in the method of punishment, not the necessary suffering involved in any method employed to extinguish life humanely.”).

In Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), this Court declined to recognize a categorical bar on executing the mentally retarded, observing that “there is no national consensus” sufficient to support such a rule. Id. at 334. The Court emphasized that only two States had enacted statutes prohibiting the execution of the mentally retarded at the time of Penry’s offense. Id. at 335. The Court further noted that “[t]he two state statutes prohibiting execution of the mentally retarded were enacted after the crimes in these cases were committed,” and that “[t]he fact that such legislation is enacted after the crime does not establish the required societal consensus.” Id. (citing Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 535 (1968) (plurality opinion) (“The Court has never held that the Constitution forbids the States to classify criminal defendants into different categories for the purposes of punishment.”)).

The same analysis applies here. At the time of Atkins’ offense, only two States—Georgia and Maryland—prohibited the execution of the mentally retarded. See Ga. Code Ann. § 17-7-131 (1982); Md. Ann. Code art. 27, § 412 (1957). By contrast, at least 20 States permitted the execution of mentally retarded offenders, and the Federal Government had not enacted any such prohibition. See Penry, 492 U.S. at 335 n.10. This Court has repeatedly held that such a legislative landscape does not evince a “national consensus” sufficient to support a categorical Eighth Amendment bar. See, e.g., McGautha, 402 U.S. at 201 (“[T]he Eighth Amendment has not been regarded as a static concept.”); Powell, 392 U.S. at 535 (“The Court has never held that the Constitution forbids the States to classify criminal defendants into different categories for the purposes of punishment.”).

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit the execution of offenders who lack the cognitive capacity to fully appreciate the nature of their crimes. In Solesbee, the Court rejected a claim that the execution of a mentally ill offender violated the Eighth Amendment, holding that the Constitution does not prohibit the execution of an offender who is “mentally ill but not insane.” 339 U.S. at 12. The Court explained that the Eighth Amendment “does not draw a bright line between the mentally ill and the mentally healthy,” and that the Constitution does not “interpose any barrier to the punishment of crime.” Id. at 12-13. Similarly, in Louisiana ex rel. Francis, the Court upheld the execution of a defendant who had survived a botched electrocution, rejecting the claim that the Eighth Amendment prohibited the second attempt. 329 U.S. at 464. The Court explained that the Eighth Amendment “does not guarantee a painless death,” and that the Constitution does not prohibit the execution of an offender who is “mentally ill but not insane.” Id.

The Commonwealth submits that the same principles apply here. The execution of a mentally retarded offender does not violate the Eighth Amendment where the offender retains the cognitive capacity to appreciate the nature of his crime and the punishment imposed. See Solesbee, 339 U.S. at 12; Louisiana ex rel. Francis, 329 U.S. at 464. The fact that a majority of States permit the execution of mentally retarded offenders further confirms that no “national consensus” supports a categorical bar. See Penry, 492 U.S. at 335 n.10; McGautha, 402 U.S. at 201.

Finally, the Commonwealth submits that the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit the execution of mentally retarded offenders where the offender’s mental retardation does not deprive him of the moral culpability necessary to justify the death penalty. In Gregg, the Court upheld the death penalty for murder, holding that the punishment is not “cruel and unusual” where it is reserved for the most culpable offenders. 428 U.S. at 183. The Court explained that the death penalty is not “excessive” where it is imposed only for the crime of murder, and where the sentencer is permitted to consider the offender’s character and record. Id. at 197-98. The same principles apply here. The execution of a mentally retarded offender does not violate the Eighth Amendment where the offender’s mental retardation does not deprive him of the moral culpability necessary to justify the death penalty. See Gregg, 428 U.S. at 183; Powell, 392 U.S. at 535.

For these reasons, the Commonwealth respectfully submits that the execution of mentally retarded offenders does not violate the Eighth Amendment.
