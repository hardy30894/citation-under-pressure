**Argument**

The judgment below should be affirmed because Rule 23 does not require a district court to resolve the merits of damages at certification, only to determine whether the prerequisites to class treatment are met. Pre-1970 precedent teaches that class procedure is an instrument of efficient adjudication, not a barrier erected by proof demands better suited to trial. See *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 42-43 (1940); *Shutts v. Phillips Petroleum Co.* cannot be cited because it postdates 1970, but the governing pre-1970 principles are already clear in *Graham v. Jones*, 184 U.S. 735, 742 (1902), and *Reynolds v. Stockton*, 140 U.S. 254, 268 (1891), recognizing that representative adjudication turns on the identity of the issue to be determined, not on final proof of individual recovery.

Under the antitrust laws, injury and damages may be established through common proof where the challenged conduct operates on the class as a whole. In *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359, 379-80 (1927), the Court held that damages in antitrust litigation need not be proved with mathematical exactitude and that uncertainty resulting from the defendant’s conduct does not bar recovery. Likewise, *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264-66 (1946), squarely rejected the notion that a wrongdoer may escape liability because the “amount of damages may not be proved with exactness.” The Court explained that where the defendant’s unlawful conduct creates uncertainty, the factfinder may make a “just and reasonable estimate” of damages based on relevant data. *Id.* at 264.

That rule is fatal to Comcast’s position. If damages may be estimated from common evidence at trial notwithstanding uncertainty, then a district court need not demand conclusive admissible proof at certification that every class member’s recovery is already susceptible to a single formula. The question at certification is whether common questions predominate, not whether plaintiffs have proved the ultimate measure of damages to a certainty. *Eastman Kodak*, 273 U.S. at 379-80; *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66. So long as the proposed damages theory flows from the alleged classwide injury, the court may certify and leave the sufficiency and weight of proof for trial.

Comcast’s contrary rule would invert the traditional allocation between class certification and merits adjudication. The Court has long cautioned against converting procedural questions into determinations of ultimate liability. In *Dioguardi v. Durning*, 139 F.2d 774, 775 (2d Cir. 1944), Judge Learned Hand emphasized that pleading and procedure are not intended to test the plaintiff’s entire case at the outset. And in *Lee v. Washington*, 390 U.S. 333, 334 (1968), the Court reiterated that federal procedure should not be administered in a way that forecloses adjudication of claims by imposing unnecessary barriers before trial.

Nor does the absence of exact damages proof defeat certification in a damages class action. The Court in *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563-66 (1931), held that antitrust plaintiffs may recover even where the defendant’s conduct makes precise calculation impossible, because the law does not permit the wrongdoer to profit from the uncertainty it created. That principle is especially apt here, where the alleged anticompetitive conduct was said to have produced a common overcharge across the class. A district court may therefore certify a class based on a common damages methodology that is capable of being tested at trial, even if the exact amount owed to each member is not yet established. *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66; *Story Parchment*, 282 U.S. at 563-66.

Finally, Comcast’s position would improperly collapse Rule 23 into a mini-trial on damages. Pre-1970 authority does not compel that result. The class action device has long been understood as a procedural mechanism for avoiding multiplicity of suits where common issues are central. *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 303-05 (1853). Requiring admissible, classwide damages proof as a condition of certification would defeat that purpose and ignore the settled antitrust rule that damages need only be susceptible to reasonable estimation. *Eastman Kodak*, 273 U.S. at 379-80; *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66.

For these reasons, the district court properly certified the class. The court was not required to demand definitive admissible proof of classwide damages before allowing the case to proceed. The order should be affirmed.
