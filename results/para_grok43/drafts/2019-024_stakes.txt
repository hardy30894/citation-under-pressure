**ARGUMENT**

The three-year limitations period in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) bars respondent’s claims. That provision precludes any action for breach of fiduciary duty under 29 U.S.C. § 1104 more than “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” Respondent filed suit on October 29, 2015. The record establishes without genuine dispute that he possessed actual knowledge of the challenged investments before October 29, 2012.

Section 1113(2) requires knowledge of the facts constituting the breach, not knowledge that those facts violated ERISA. In Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that the statute “is triggered by the [plaintiff’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law.” Id. at 991-92. The same principle was applied in Meagher v. International Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), where receipt of reduced pension checks supplied the requisite knowledge even though the plaintiff “may not have known at the time that the reduction in benefits was unlawful under ERISA.” Id. at 1423. Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co., 916 F.2d 548 (9th Cir. 1990), likewise measured the limitations period from the date the plaintiff learned the material facts of the transaction. Id. at 552.

Petitioners’ disclosures supplied those facts. The 2010–2012 Fund Fact Sheets, the 2011 Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notice, the 2012 Summary Plan Description, and the contemporaneous website postings identified the precise asset allocations respondent now challenges and explained the strategy and risks associated with them. Respondent accessed the websites containing these documents. That access, coupled with the content of the documents, constitutes actual knowledge of the investments and the strategy that produced them.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—that a plaintiff must also have appreciated the imprudence of the disclosed facts—finds no support in the statutory text or in this Court’s ERISA jurisprudence. ERISA’s fiduciary standards are objective; a claim under § 1104 turns on whether the fiduciary’s conduct satisfied the prudent-man standard, not on the plaintiff’s subjective evaluation of that conduct. See Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996). Requiring plaintiffs to have formed a legal or prudential judgment would reintroduce the constructive-knowledge standard Congress eliminated in 1987 and would render the three-year period largely meaningless whenever a plaintiff simply declines to read plan disclosures.

Because respondent had actual knowledge of the investments more than three years before suit, § 1113(2) bars his claims. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
