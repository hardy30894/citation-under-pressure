# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Policy Reasonably Confines Its Limited Forum to Secular Community Uses, and Excluding Religious Worship Is a Permissible Definition of That Forum's Boundaries, Not Viewpoint Discrimination.

A school building is not a street or park traditionally devoted to public assembly and debate. Milford's building is a facility of limited and specific dedication—to the education of children during the day and, after hours, to "social, civic and recreational" activities benefiting the community. Because the building is not a traditional public forum, the school retains broad authority to define the boundaries of the access it grants. This Court has long recognized that the State, like any owner of property, may regulate and restrict the use of property under its control for the purposes to which that property is lawfully dedicated. *See Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). A government need not convert its property into a forum for every use citizens might wish to make of it merely because it has opened the property for some limited purposes. *Id.* Just as the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court concluded, in a decision this Court adopted, that a legislature's authority to forbid a particular use of public grounds is akin to the authority a private landowner would have to forbid the same use of his own property, *see Davis v. Massachusetts*, 167 U.S. 43, 47 (1897), Milford may exclude religious worship and instruction from a forum it opened only for secular civic and recreational purposes.

That line—between teaching morals and character generally, which Milford permits, and religious worship, instruction, and proselytizing, which it does not—is a reasonable, content-based limitation on the forum's subject matter, not an act of viewpoint suppression. This Court has long recognized that reasonable, evenhanded regulation of the use of public property is consistent with the First Amendment so long as it is not a subterfuge for suppressing particular ideas. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (upholding licensing scheme as a permissible "regulation of the times, the place, and the manner" of using public ways, "without unwarranted discrimination"). Milford's Community Use Policy operates the same way: it excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which faith or sect might wish to conduct it. The Good News Club's proposed meeting, which the record shows opens and closes in prayer, includes a "challenge and invitation" inviting children to accept Christ as their personal savior, and culminates in a recited sinner's prayer, is not the teaching of morals from a religious perspective; it is religious instruction and worship of the very kind the Policy reserves against. Excluding it from a limited forum dedicated to civic and recreational activity is a permissible definition of the forum's boundaries, not discrimination against a viewpoint on a subject otherwise open.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Involving School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the Policy's application to the Club implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that permitting the Club's activities in its facility would create Establishment Clause difficulties was reasonable and sufficient justification. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that public schools occupy a uniquely sensitive position in Establishment Clause doctrine because they involve compulsory, impressionable, school-age children. "[I]t is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government," *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962), and the state may not sponsor devotional religious exercises within the school setting, *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 223 (1963) ("the state may not establish a 'religion of secularism' " any more than it may sponsor religious observance—neutrality is the touchstone). The concern is heightened, not diminished, when the activity takes place in the school building immediately after the close of the school day, using the same classrooms, supervised implicitly by the school's continuing presence on its own property, and directed at the same young children who attend that school. This Court has recognized that even indirect governmental involvement with religious instruction in the school context demands close scrutiny, given the historical concern that public funds and public school facilities not be placed at the service of religious inculcation. *See Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 15–16 (1947) (discussing the Establishment Clause's design to prevent the state from aiding or supporting any religious activities or institutions).

To be sure, this Court has recognized that the Religion Clauses do not require the state to be actively hostile toward religion or to treat neutrality as a mandate to purge every religious reference from public life. *See Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313–14 (1952). But *Zorach* permitted released-time programs precisely because they occurred *off* school property, avoiding any appearance that the school itself sponsored religious exercise. *Id.* at 308–09. Milford's building, by contrast, is the very site of instruction, and the Establishment Clause commands that "government must be neutral" among religions and between religion and nonreligion, *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 104 (1968). Given the Club's own description of its activities as prayer, scripture memorization, and religious conversion directed at grade-school children on school premises, Milford could reasonably conclude that hosting the Club would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *Engel*, *Schempp*, and *Everson* counsel against. The exclusion was therefore justified on this independent ground as well.
