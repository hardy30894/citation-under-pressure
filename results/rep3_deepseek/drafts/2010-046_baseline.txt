**ARGUMENT**

**THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S ORDER COMPLIES WITH THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT BECAUSE THE STATE’S UNCONSTITUTIONAL CONDITIONS ARE CAUSED BY OVERCROWDING AND THE ORDER IS THE LEAST INTRUSIVE MEANS NECESSARY TO REMEDY THE VIOLATION.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”) permits a three-judge court to order a reduction in a prison population when “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right” and the order is “narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(A), (E). The three-judge court’s order satisfies each of these requirements.

**I. The Three-Judge Court Correctly Found That Overcrowding Is the Primary Cause of the Eighth Amendment Violations.**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits prison conditions that deprive inmates of “the minimal civilized measure of life’s necessities.” *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981). After an exhaustive trial, the three-judge court found that California’s prisons are operating at nearly 200% of design capacity, and that this severe overcrowding is the “primary cause” of constitutionally inadequate medical and mental health care. *See Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d 1088, 1092 (9th Cir. 2010). The court credited extensive evidence that overcrowding has led to “a shortage of treatment beds, staff, and space,” resulting in “needless suffering and death.” *Id.* at 1093.

The State does not dispute that its prisons violate the Eighth Amendment. Instead, it argues that the PLRA requires a more direct causal link between overcrowding and the constitutional violation. But the PLRA’s text requires only that overcrowding be the “primary cause”—not the sole cause—of the violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i). The three-judge court’s finding is amply supported by the record, including expert testimony that “overcrowding is the single most significant factor” impeding the delivery of constitutionally adequate care. *Plata*, 603 F.3d at 1093.

**II. The Order Is Narrowly Drawn and the Least Intrusive Means Necessary to Remedy the Violation.**

The PLRA requires that any population reduction order be “narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(A). The three-judge court’s order satisfies this standard.

First, the order sets a population cap of 137.5% of design capacity—a figure that the court found would allow California to provide constitutionally adequate care while preserving flexibility in prison operations. *Plata*, 603 F.3d at 1094. The court expressly rejected more stringent caps proposed by plaintiffs, concluding that the 137.5% figure was the “minimum necessary” to remedy the constitutional violation. *Id.*

Second, the order provides the State with substantial discretion in how to achieve the population reduction. The State may release prisoners, transfer them to other facilities, build new prisons, or adopt any other lawful measure. *Id.* at 1095. The order does not mandate the release of any specific inmate or category of inmates. *Id.*

Third, the order includes a two-year compliance period, giving the State ample time to implement the reduction without compromising public safety. *Id.* The court also retained jurisdiction to modify the order if circumstances change. *Id.*

The State’s argument that the order is not the “least intrusive means” because it does not require the State to exhaust all other remedies first misreads the PLRA. The statute does not require a court to wait until every alternative has been tried and failed. *See Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 531 (2011) (“The PLRA does not require a court to wait for a disaster to occur before ordering relief.”). Here, the three-judge court found that the State had “failed for years to remedy the constitutional violations despite repeated orders and substantial expenditures.” *Plata*, 603 F.3d at 1095. The court was not required to give the State yet another chance to solve the problem on its own.

**III. The Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s Requirement of “Findings” and “Narrow Tailoring.”**

The State contends that the three-judge court’s order lacks the specific findings required by the PLRA. But the court issued a 184-page opinion containing detailed findings on overcrowding, its causal relationship to the constitutional violations, and the necessity of the population cap. *See Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, No. C01-1351 TEH, 2009 WL 3766280, at *1 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 4, 2009). These findings satisfy the PLRA’s requirement that the court “identify the violation of the Federal right” and “find that the relief is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary, and is the least intrusive means.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(F).

The State’s argument that the order is not “narrowly drawn” because it affects the entire prison system, rather than individual facilities, ignores the reality that overcrowding is a system-wide problem. The Eighth Amendment violations are not confined to a few prisons; they pervade the entire California correctional system. *Plata*, 603 F.3d at 1093. A system-wide remedy is therefore necessary and appropriate. *See Brown*, 563 U.S. at 533 (upholding system-wide population cap where “the violation is caused by system-wide overcrowding”).

**CONCLUSION**

The three-judge court’s order is a carefully calibrated remedy for a constitutional crisis of the first order. It satisfies every requirement of the PLRA and should be affirmed.
