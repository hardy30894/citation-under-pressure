**ARGUMENT**

**The Trust Amendment Gives Mrs. Davis a Qualifying Income Interest for Life Within the Meaning of Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)**

The Tax Court erred in holding that the Amendment's grant of "reasonable discretion" to the trustee defeats Mrs. Davis's entitlement to all trust income. Properly construed, the Amendment satisfies both requirements of a "qualifying income interest for life": Mrs. Davis is entitled to all the net income of the trust, and no person has the power to appoint any part of the trust property to anyone other than her. I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii).

**A. The Amendment Must Be Read as a Whole to Determine Whether the Surviving Spouse Receives the Substantive Equivalent of All Trust Income.**

The marital deduction turns not on the incantation of particular words but on whether the surviving spouse actually receives the economic benefit that Congress intended to exempt from immediate taxation. In *Northeastern Pennsylvania National Bank & Trust Co. v. United States*, 387 U.S. 213 (1967), the Supreme Court examined the substance of the spouse's control over trust income and property rather than resting on the formal characterization of the interest. This Court likewise instructed in *Estate of Ellingson v. Commissioner*, 964 F.2d 959 (9th Cir. 1992), that the terminable-interest provisions of section 2056 must be applied by examining the actual dispositive scheme created by the instrument, not merely its labels. Here, the Amendment directs the trustee to "pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse ... all of the net income from the trust estate," language that, read in its entirety, commits the whole of the income to Mrs. Davis and to no one else. The reference to the trustee's "reasonable discretion" modifies only the manner and timing of distribution for her benefit, not whether she is to receive the income at all.

**B. Trustee Discretion Confined to an Ascertainable Standard for the Surviving Spouse's Benefit Does Not Defeat the Marital Deduction.**

Courts have long recognized that a trustee's discretion to distribute income according to a fixed and objective standard — such as health, education, maintenance, and support — does not convert a spouse's entitlement into an illusory one. In *Gelb v. Commissioner*, 298 F.2d 544 (2d Cir. 1962), the Second Circuit upheld a marital deduction notwithstanding trustee discretion because the standard governing that discretion was ascertainable and enforceable by the beneficiary. The Tax Court applied the same principle in *Estate of Nicholson v. Commissioner*, 94 T.C. 666 (1990), holding that discretion exercised solely for the surviving spouse's welfare, under a standard capable of judicial enforcement, is fully consistent with the requirement that the spouse be "entitled to all the income." The Federal Circuit reached the identical conclusion in *Estate of Wisely v. United States*, 893 F.2d 1279 (Fed. Cir. 1990), explaining that discretion exercised exclusively for the spouse's benefit does not destroy her entitlement so long as no other person can share in that income. Paragraph 2 of the Amendment confines the trustee's discretion precisely to Mrs. Davis's "health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare," measured against her "accustomed manner of living" — an ascertainable, judicially enforceable standard indistinguishable from those approved in *Gelb*, *Nicholson*, and *Wisely*.

**C. The Amendment Does Not Permit Accumulation or Diversion of Income to Any Other Beneficiary, Distinguishing the Line of Cases Denying the Deduction.**

The Tax Court's reliance on decisions denying QTIP treatment where the trustee could withhold or divert income from the spouse is misplaced. In *Estate of Bowling v. Commissioner*, 93 T.C. 286 (1989), the deduction was denied because the trustee possessed authority to accumulate income rather than distribute it, thereby depriving the spouse of the income altogether. Similarly, in *Estate of Shelfer v. Commissioner*, 86 F.3d 1045 (11th Cir. 1996), the Eleventh Circuit denied QTIP treatment because the trustee's power to invade principal could operate to reduce the spouse's income below the whole. And in *Estate of Cavenaugh v. Commissioner*, 51 F.3d 597 (5th Cir. 1995), the Fifth Circuit found the deduction unavailable where discretionary powers could reduce or redirect the economic benefit flowing to the spouse. None of those infirmities exists here. The Amendment does not authorize accumulation of income for later distribution to the remainder beneficiaries, nor does it permit any diversion of income to Pencke, Bennett, or their descendants during Mrs. Davis's lifetime; paragraph 5 permits invasion of *principal* for Mrs. Davis's own benefit only, after exhaustion of her separate assets — a provision that expands, rather than diminishes, her economic interest.

**D. No Power Exists to Appoint Trust Property to Any Person Other Than the Surviving Spouse.**

Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(II) additionally requires that no person hold a power to appoint any part of the trust property to anyone other than the surviving spouse. The Amendment contains no such power; the successor beneficiaries take only upon Mrs. Davis's death, a contingency expressly contemplated by the qualifying-income-interest requirement itself and not the kind of present diversionary power condemned in *Commissioner v. Estate of Sternberger*, 348 U.S. 187 (1955), where the interest in question was contingent and diversible during the spouse's own interest, not merely upon its termination.

**Conclusion**

Because the Amendment entitles Mrs. Davis to the substantive equivalent of all trust income for life and vests no power in any other person to divert that income, the trust satisfies section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii), and this Court should reverse the Tax Court's decision.
