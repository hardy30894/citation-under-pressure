**ARGUMENT**

**I. PETITIONER LACKS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO THE STATUTE**

The Third Circuit correctly held that petitioner Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to 18 U.S.C. § 229. This Court has long recognized that the Tenth Amendment "is but a truism" that "states the truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered." *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). It does not confer any substantive rights on individuals but instead "states a rule of construction" to limit federal power. *Id.* Consequently, private parties may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal laws unless they are asserting the rights of a state. *See Tennessee Elec. Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Auth.*, 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939) ("[T]he [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.").

Petitioner’s reliance on dicta in *Tennessee Electric Power* is misplaced. The Court in that case dismissed the Tenth Amendment claim after analyzing both the standing issue and the merits, making the standing holding an alternative ground for the decision rather than mere dicta. *See Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (citing *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978)). Moreover, the standing question in *Tennessee Electric Power* implicated the constitutional jurisdiction of a federal court, which predominates any discussion of the merits. *See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95 (1998).

The Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit correctly followed this precedent in denying petitioner standing. *See Bond v. United States*, 681 F.3d 149, 160-61 (3d Cir. 2012). As the court explained, petitioner’s argument that § 229 violates principles of federalism "is not a claim that she has been deprived of a right guaranteed by the Constitution." *Id.* at 161. Instead, it is a claim that the federal government has exceeded its enumerated powers—a challenge that may only be brought by a state. *See New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 155-56 (1992) (holding that a state may challenge federal legislation as beyond Congress’s enumerated powers).

Petitioner’s attempt to distinguish *Tennessee Electric Power* is unavailing. She argues that the holding in that case is dicta because it did not address the specific statutory context of treaty-implementing legislation. However, the Court’s reasoning was not limited to the facts of that case. The Court held broadly that private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment challenges, regardless of the statutory context. *See Tennessee Elec. Power*, 306 U.S. at 144.

**II. THE STATUTE IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER**

Even if petitioner had standing, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to implement treaties under the Necessary and Proper Clause. In *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), this Court held that Congress may enact legislation to enforce a valid treaty, even if that legislation would otherwise exceed its enumerated powers. *Id.* at 432 ("If the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government."). The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a necessary and proper means of implementing it. *See Bond*, 681 F.3d at 162-63.

Petitioner’s argument that *Holland* should be overruled is unpersuasive. The Court has repeatedly reaffirmed *Holland*’s holding that the treaty power is not limited by the enumerated powers of Congress. *See, e.g., United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004) (citing *Holland* with approval). Moreover, petitioner’s reliance on scholarly critiques of *Holland* is misplaced. This Court does not reconsider its precedents based on academic debate. *See, e.g., Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827 (1991) ("[W]e do not reconsider our precedents unless there is a special justification for doing so.").

**III. THE STATUTE IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE**

Petitioner’s void-for-vagueness challenge also fails. A statute is unconstitutionally vague if it "fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited" or if it "authorizes or encourages arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement." *United States v. Powell*, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975). Section 229 provides clear notice of the conduct it prohibits: the knowing possession or use of a chemical weapon. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 229(a)(1). The statute defines "chemical weapon" and "toxic chemical" with sufficient precision to apprise individuals of the prohibited conduct. *See id.* § 229F.

Petitioner’s argument that the statute is overbroad because it could theoretically apply to conduct not intended to be prohibited is unavailing. The statute includes exceptions for peaceful and lawful purposes, such as self-defense and industrial uses. *See id.* § 229C, § 229F. Petitioner’s conduct—repeatedly attempting to poison her husband’s mistress—falls squarely within the statute’s prohibition. *See Bond*, 681 F.3d at 164.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. Petitioner lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power, and the statute is not unconstitutionally vague.
