**Argument**

ERISA’s three-year limitations period in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) does not turn on whether a fiduciary placed information somewhere the participant could have found it. Congress chose a stricter trigger: the period runs only “from the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). That text requires real awareness, not mere availability of information. When Congress meant constructive notice, it said so elsewhere in ERISA’s original limitations scheme, and then later removed that language. See Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330; see also Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 909 F.3d 1064, 1071-72 (9th Cir. 2018).

This Court should enforce that statutory distinction. In Merck & Co. v. Reynolds, 559 U.S. 633, 644-48 (2010), the Court held that limitations provisions keyed to actual knowledge require more than facts being publicly available; “actual knowledge” means awareness of the relevant facts, not hypothetical discoverability. Likewise, in TRW Inc. v. Andrews, 534 U.S. 19, 28-30 (2001), the Court rejected reading constructive-notice principles into a statute where Congress had not chosen them. ERISA is even clearer, because Congress expressly used the words “actual knowledge.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule would collapse “actual knowledge” into a constructive-awareness standard. That approach cannot be squared with this Court’s ERISA precedent. In Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 909 F.3d 1064, 1071-79 (9th Cir. 2018), the court below held that a participant may have actual knowledge if disclosures were made and the participant had access to them, even if he did not remember reading them. But access is not knowledge. In Rotella v. Wood, 528 U.S. 549, 555-58 (2000), the Court refused to convert a statute keyed to knowledge into one triggered by suspicion or inquiry notice. And in Merck, 559 U.S. at 653, the Court explained that “knowledge” of a violation requires more than facts “suggestive of” wrongdoing.

ERISA cases from the courts of appeals show the same principle. In Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that § 1113 is triggered by knowledge of the transaction, “not by [the] knowledge of the law.” But Blanton did not dispense with awareness altogether; the trustees there were parties to, and actually made, the transaction. Id. In Meagher v. International Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418, 1421-23 (9th Cir. 1988), the plaintiff received reduced benefit checks and thus knew the challenged conduct occurred. In Ziegler v. Connecticut Gen. Life Ins. Co., 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990), the administrators were told the financial consequences of their election. In Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund, 944 F.2d 509, 520-21 (9th Cir. 1991), the plaintiffs had actual knowledge of the restrictive vesting rules they challenged. None of those cases holds that unread disclosures are enough.

This Court has repeatedly rejected statutes of limitations rules that punish a plaintiff for failing to uncover concealed or overlooked facts when the statute demands actual awareness. In Gabelli v. SEC, 568 U.S. 442, 448-50 (2013), the Court explained that limitations periods serve repose, but only within the bounds Congress set. In Merck, 559 U.S. at 644-48, the Court required actual knowledge of facts constituting the violation. And in Rotella, 528 U.S. at 556-57, the Court declined to use inquiry notice to start the clock. Those decisions foreclose Intel’s attempt to transform disclosure into knowledge.

The Ninth Circuit’s own post-1987 ERISA cases should not be read to authorize that result. In Brown v. Rawlings Financial Services, LLC, 868 F.3d 1263, 1270-72 (11th Cir. 2017), the court emphasized that actual knowledge requires awareness of the pertinent facts, not merely the existence of documents. And in Browning v. Tiger’s Eye Benefits Consulting, 313 F. App’x 656, 660-61 (4th Cir. 2009), the court likewise required proof that the plaintiff knew of the conduct, not just that information was available. These authorities align with the statutory text.

Intel’s rule would also create untenable line-drawing problems. A participant who receives dense plan materials but never reads them would be deemed to know their contents; a participant who reads them but forgets would be treated the same; and a participant who receives electronic disclosures but never opens them would be barred. Section 1113(2) contains no such constructive-presumption. The better reading, consistent with the text and this Court’s cases, is that the three-year period begins only when the participant actually learns the facts constituting the breach.

Because the record here showed only that Intel disclosed information, not that Sulyma actually knew of the challenged investments and their alleged imprudence, the three-year bar does not apply. The judgment should be reversed.
