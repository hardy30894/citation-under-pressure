# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 289's Text Limits Recovery To Profits On The "Article Of Manufacture" To Which The Patented Design Was Applied, Not Necessarily The Entire End Product.

Congress specified in unmistakable terms the object of a design-patent damages award. Section 289 makes an infringer "liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit," but only "on account of" his infringement, and only insofar as he "appl[ied]" the patented design "to any article of manufacture." 35 U.S.C. § 289. The statute nowhere states that the "article of manufacture" is always the finished consumer product as sold. To the contrary, an "article of manufacture" is simply "a thing made by hand or machine," and nothing in the statutory text confines that term to whole products rather than their components. Where, as here, a design patent claims only a component of a multicomponent product—here, a rounded-corner front face, a grid of colorful icons, and a flat glass screen, rather than any complete smartphone—the "article of manufacture to which [the] design . . . has been applied" is the component embodying the patented design, and the infringer's "total profit" recoverable under § 289 must be calculated with respect to that component.

The Federal Circuit's categorical rule—that a design-patent owner is always entitled to the infringer's entire profit on the end product, regardless of how small a role the patented design played in that product—cannot be squared with this text. That rule reads the phrase "article of manufacture to which the design . . . has been applied" out of the statute altogether, collapsing it into "the product as sold." Congress could have written such a rule; it did not.

## II. Historical And Analogous Patent-Damages Doctrine Confirm That Recovery Must Be Tied To The Value Attributable To The Patented Feature.

This reading accords with the bedrock principle, recognized throughout patent law, that a damages remedy must be causally tied to the value the patent owner's invention actually contributed. In the analogous context of utility-patent damages under § 284, this Court held in *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), that a patentee seeking an infringer's profits "must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features," and that "such evidence must be reliable and tangible, and not conjectural or speculative." *Id.* at 121. Although *Garretson* did not construe § 289 itself, its animating principle—that profits recoverable for infringement of a discrete feature must be apportioned to that feature—reflects the same causal logic embedded in § 289's "article of manufacture" language when a design patent covers only a component.

Courts construing the immediate predecessor to § 289 recognized this same limitation long before the current statute was codified. In *Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros.*, 234 F. 79 (2d Cir. 1916), the Second Circuit confronted a design patent covering the case of a piano, not the instrument as a whole. The court held that the "article of manufacture" to which the patented design had been applied was the piano case, explaining that the profit recoverable was the profit "made on the case," not the profit on the piano as an integrated musical instrument. *Id.* at 81. That decision—construing statutory language materially identical to § 289—demonstrates that Congress's chosen phrase has long been understood to permit an "article of manufacture" narrower than the complete commercial product, precisely because the patented design may cover only part of that product.

## III. A Categorical Entire-Product Rule Produces Results Untethered From The Statute's Purpose And Should Be Rejected.

The Federal Circuit's rule, exemplified by decisions such as *Nordock, Inc. v. Systems Inc.*, 803 F.3d 1344 (Fed. Cir. 2015), forecloses any inquiry into whether the "article of manufacture" is the whole product or a component, reasoning that consumers do not purchase separate parts of an infringing product. But that reasoning conflates consumer purchasing behavior with the distinct statutory question of what "article of manufacture" the design was "applied" to. A smartphone comprises hundreds of components, only a handful of which are covered by the design patents at issue; the same reasoning could award an entire automobile manufacturer's profits for infringement of a patented door handle, or an entire computer manufacturer's profits for infringement of a patented icon arrangement. Nothing in § 289's text or history compels—and the statute's structure forecloses—such a windfall untethered from the design's actual contribution to the product.

## Conclusion

Because § 289 permits an "article of manufacture" to be less than an entire end product, damages here should have been limited to the profits attributable to the smartphone components embodying Apple's patented designs, not Samsung's total profits on the accused smartphones. The judgment below should be reversed.
