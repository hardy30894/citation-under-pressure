The district court erred in concluding that the releases bar appellants' claims. Federal statutes creating employee rights against employers rest on considerations of public policy that preclude waiver by private agreement. Brooklyn Sav. Bank v. O'Neil, 324 U.S. 697, 704 (1945) ("no one can doubt that the statutory right in question is one which the employee is entitled to assert").

Individual releases cannot extinguish such rights even when supported by additional payments. D.A. Schulte, Inc. v. Gangi, 328 U.S. 108, 114 (1946) (FLSA rights "cannot be abridged by contract or otherwise"). The same principle governs rights arising from federal labor statutes. J.I. Case Co. v. NLRB, 321 U.S. 332, 337 (1944) ("individual contracts cannot subtract from collective ones").

The releases here purport to surrender claims under federal law in exchange for severance. Because the underlying rights are statutory and nonwaivable, the releases are ineffective. National Licorice Co. v. NLRB, 309 U.S. 350, 364 (1940) (agreements that "relinquish rights guaranteed by the Act" are invalid). Appellants therefore remain free to assert their claims notwithstanding the signed documents.

The short decisional periods and linkage to continued employment further demonstrate that the releases were not the product of free bargaining. Callen v. Pennsylvania R. Co., 332 U.S. 625, 630 (1948) (releases must reflect "a deliberate and informed choice"). Under these authorities the releases afford no defense.
