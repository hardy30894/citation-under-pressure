**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit must be reversed because the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act is unconstitutionally vague—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. This conclusion follows from settled due process principles that have long protected defendants from punishment under vague or unpredictable criminal statutes.

**I. The Due Process Clause Forbids Punishment Under a Vague Statute, and This Prohibition Applies Retroactively**

The Constitution requires that criminal statutes give “fair warning of the conduct which it prohibits.” *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 350 (1964). A statute that “fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that his contemplated conduct is forbidden” violates due process. *Id.* at 351. The residual clause of the ACCA, which criminalized conduct that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another,” is precisely such a statute. As this Court held in *Johnson*, the clause required courts to assess the hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version of a crime, making its application unconstitutionally arbitrary and unpredictable.

This Court has long recognized that a conviction under a void-for-vagueness statute is a fundamental constitutional error that cannot stand. In *Giaccio v. Pennsylvania*, 382 U.S. 399, 402-03 (1965), this Court struck down an 1860 Pennsylvania statute that authorized juries to impose costs on acquitted defendants without “prescribing definite standards,” holding that the Due Process Clause requires “standards sufficient to enable defendants to protect themselves against arbitrary and discriminatory impositions.” The same principle applies here: Welch was sentenced to a mandatory minimum of 15 years under a clause that provided no meaningful standard for determining which prior convictions qualified.

**II. A Conviction Under a Void Statute Is a Complete Nullity That Cannot Be Cured by Finality**

The retroactive application of *Johnson* is compelled by the fundamental principle that a conviction under an unconstitutional statute is void ab initio. In *Cole v. Arkansas*, 333 U.S. 196, 201 (1948), this Court held that defendants are “entitled to have the validity of their convictions appraised on consideration of the case as it was tried.” When the statute under which a defendant is convicted is itself unconstitutional, the conviction cannot stand—regardless of when the constitutional defect is recognized.

This Court has applied this principle in the context of other fundamental constitutional rights. In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344-45 (1963), this Court held that the right to counsel is fundamental and applied that rule retroactively to vacate convictions obtained without counsel. Similarly, in *Williams v. Kaiser*, 323 U.S. 471, 475-76 (1945), this Court held that a defendant who pleaded guilty without counsel and without waiving his right to counsel was deprived of due process. The right to be free from punishment under a vague statute is no less fundamental than the right to counsel.

**III. The Government Cannot Rely on Finality to Perpetuate an Unconstitutional Sentence**

The government may argue that Welch’s sentence became final before *Johnson* was decided, but finality cannot insulate a conviction that rests on an unconstitutional statute. In *Reynolds v. Cochran*, 365 U.S. 525, 531 (1961), this Court reviewed a Florida recidivist sentence even though the petitioner had completed his original sentence, holding that the collateral consequences of an unlawful conviction could be challenged. Here, Welch remains incarcerated under a sentence that was imposed pursuant to a void statutory provision.

Moreover, in *Lynumn v. Illinois*, 372 U.S. 528, 534 (1963), this Court reversed a conviction where the defendant’s confession was coerced, emphasizing that the voluntariness of a confession must be assessed by examining the totality of the circumstances to ensure fundamental fairness. The same principle applies here: Welch’s sentence was enhanced based on a prior conviction that the government now concedes may not qualify as a violent felony under the residual clause—a clause this Court has declared void.

**IV. The Eleventh Circuit’s Refusal to Apply *Johnson* Retroactively Violates Due Process**

The Eleventh Circuit denied Welch a certificate of appealability before *Johnson* was decided, effectively requiring him to serve an unconstitutional sentence. This Court has repeatedly held that a defendant cannot be punished for conduct that was not clearly proscribed at the time of the offense. *Bouie*, 378 U.S. at 353-54. The residual clause’s vagueness meant that Welch could not have known—and the government could not have proven—that his Florida robbery conviction qualified as a violent felony under the clause.

In *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242-43 (1969), this Court held that a guilty plea must be knowing and voluntary, and that the record must affirmatively show the defendant understood the consequences. Welch’s plea to being a felon in possession was entered without any understanding that his prior conviction would be used to trigger a 15-year mandatory minimum under a clause that this Court later found unconstitutionally vague. The plea was therefore not knowing in the constitutional sense.

**V. The Government Bears the Burden of Proving That Welch’s Sentence Was Lawful**

The government cannot meet its burden of showing that Welch’s sentence was lawful under the residual clause. In *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616, 629 (1912), this Court held that a recidivist sentencing proceeding must strictly comply with statutory requirements. Here, the government relied on a statutory provision that this Court has now declared void. The government cannot salvage Welch’s sentence by arguing that his prior conviction might qualify under a different clause of the ACCA, because the district court did not specify which clause it relied upon.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the rule in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for resentencing without application of the ACCA’s residual clause.
