**ARGUMENT**

**I. The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(5) (1994), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date.**

The reinstatement statute, enacted as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA), permits the Attorney General to reinstate a prior deportation order against an alien who illegally re-enters the United States after having been previously deported. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(5) (1994). However, this statute does not apply retroactively to aliens who re-entered the United States prior to its enactment. *See Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 274 (1994) (holding that statutes operate prospectively absent clear congressional intent to the contrary). The Supreme Court has long held that "[l]egislation is not retrospective merely because it draws upon antecedent facts for its operation." *United States v. Schooner Peggy*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103, 110 (1801). Thus, where Congress has not expressly stated that a statute applies to past conduct, courts presume that it does not. *See id.* at 110; *United States v. Burr*, 25 F. Cas. 187, 190 (C.C.D. Va. 1807) (Marshall, C.J.) (stating that laws are not to be construed as retrospective unless the language is "so clear as to leave no doubt").

In *United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co.*, 276 U.S. 160 (1928), the Supreme Court reiterated that "[r]etroactive application is not favored and statutes will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language is such as plainly to require it." *Id.* at 162-63. The Court further explained that "[t]he presumption against retroactivity is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence." *Id.* at 162. This presumption is particularly strong where, as here, the statute imposes a penalty or deprivation of a right. *See Shwab v. Doyle*, 258 U.S. 571, 582 (1922) (holding that statutes imposing penalties or forfeitures are not to be construed retroactively).

The reinstatement statute does not expressly state that it applies to aliens who re-entered the United States prior to its enactment. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(5) (1994). Therefore, under the well-established rule of construction articulated in *Schooner Peggy* and *Magnolia Petroleum*, the statute does not apply retroactively to Fernandez-Vargas, who re-entered the United States in 1982, well before the statute's effective date.

**II. The application of the reinstatement statute to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive.**

Even if the reinstatement statute could be construed to apply to Fernandez-Vargas, such an application would be impermissibly retroactive. The Supreme Court has held that a statute is retroactive if it "takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability in respect to transactions or considerations already past." *Sohn v. Waterson*, 17 Wall. 596, 603 (1873). In *Shwab*, the Court further explained that "[a] statute is not made retroactive merely because it draws upon antecedent facts for its operation, but it is retroactive if it changes the legal effect of past transactions." *Shwab*, 258 U.S. at 582.

Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States in 1982 and lived here continuously for nearly two decades. During this time, he established deep roots in the community, married a U.S. citizen, and sought to adjust his status to that of a lawful permanent resident. *See generally* 8 U.S.C. § 1255 (1994). The reinstatement statute, by denying Fernandez-Vargas any form of relief under the INA, effectively imposes a new disability based on his pre-enactment conduct. *See Sohn*, 17 Wall. at 603. This is precisely the type of retroactive application that the Supreme Court has condemned. *See Shwab*, 258 U.S. at 582.

In *Union Pacific R.R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190 (1913), the Supreme Court held that a statute is retroactive if it "changes the legal effect of past transactions." *Id.* at 201. The reinstatement statute does just that: it changes the legal effect of Fernandez-Vargas' pre-enactment re-entry by subjecting him to deportation without the possibility of relief. *See id.* This impermissible retroactivity violates the due process guarantees of the Fifth Amendment. *See Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269 (holding that retroactive legislation may violate due process).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should hold that the reinstatement statute does not apply to Fernandez-Vargas and that its application to him would be impermissibly retroactive. The petition for review should be granted, and Fernandez-Vargas should be permitted to pursue his application for adjustment of status.
