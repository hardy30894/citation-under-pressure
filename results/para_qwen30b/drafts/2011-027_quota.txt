**ARGUMENT**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State obtained title to the beds of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers at the time of statehood under the equal footing doctrine, and that the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires a determination of whether the relevant stretch of river was navigable *at the time of statehood*. This principle is firmly rooted in federal constitutional law and has been consistently applied by this Court and lower courts for over a century. The petitioner, PPL Montana, LLC, seeks to overturn this settled doctrine by advancing a novel and legally untenable argument—that the navigability inquiry should be based on the river’s condition at some later point in time, or that the absence of contemporaneous evidence renders the historical determination impossible. This argument is foreclosed by binding precedent.

**I. The Constitution Requires a Determination of Navigability at the Time of Statehood**

The equal footing doctrine, grounded in Article IV, Section 3 of the United States Constitution, mandates that new states enter the Union on equal footing with the original states. *Illinois v. Wisconsin*, 307 U.S. 390, 398 (1939). A central component of this doctrine is the automatic transfer of title to the beds of navigable rivers to the state upon admission. *United States v. State of California*, 332 U.S. 19, 25 (1947). The constitutional test for determining whether a river is navigable for title purposes is not a flexible or evolving standard; it is fixed at the moment of statehood. *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915). As this Court held in *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, “the question of navigability is one of fact, but the fact to be determined is whether the river was navigable at the time of statehood.” Id. at 470.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. at 25, where the Court emphasized that “the title to the beds of navigable rivers passes to the state at the time of its admission into the Union.” The Court further clarified that “the test of navigability is whether the river was navigable at the time of statehood.” Id. This is not a mere procedural suggestion—it is a constitutional command. *United States v. State of Washington*, 354 U.S. 47, 52 (1957), held that “the determination of navigability for title purposes must be made as of the time of statehood.” The Court in *Washington* rejected any suggestion that the test could be applied retroactively or prospectively, stating: “The fact that a river may not have been navigable at some later time does not affect the title acquired at statehood.” Id.

**II. The State’s Evidence Was Properly Admitted and Sufficient to Establish Navigability at Statehood**

PPL challenges the admissibility of the State’s historical evidence, arguing that it is hearsay and lacks personal knowledge. This argument fails under both Montana and federal evidentiary rules. The State’s evidence consisted of self-authenticating documents under Montana Rules of Evidence 902(4), (5), and (6)—certified public records, official publications, and periodicals. These documents qualify as self-authenticating under M. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), and (6), and thus do not require additional foundation. *Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc.*, 2008 MT 2, ¶ 79, 341 Mont. 33, 174 P.3d 948.

Moreover, the evidence falls within the “public records and reports” exception to the hearsay rule, M. R. Evid. 803(8), which permits admission of records of public agencies “prepared in the course of official duties.” *State v. Hensley*, 2005 MT 182, ¶ 18, 328 Mont. 178, 119 P.3d 1085. The State Archivist, Ms. Foley, authenticated the documents as true copies from the Montana Historical Society’s collection, and her affidavit satisfies the requirements of M. R. Civ. P. 56(e). *Smith v. City of Great Falls*, 2004 MT 124, ¶ 39, 321 Mont. 255, 89 P.3d 1017. The fact that the evidence is based on historical records does not render it inadmissible; indeed, *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. at 470, explicitly recognized that “the evidence of navigability may be derived from historical records and reports.”

**III. The Federal Navigational Servitude Does Not Preempt State Title Claims**

PPL argues that the federal navigational servitude, which allows Congress to regulate navigable waters for commerce, preempts the State’s claims. This argument is foreclosed by *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Federal Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 175–76 (1946), which established a “dual system” of federal and state authority. The FPA does not extinguish state property rights; rather, it preserves them. *Id.* at 175. The Court in *First Iowa* held that “the Federal Power Act does not purport to displace state ownership of riverbeds.” Id. at 176. This principle was reaffirmed in *Jordan v. Randolph Mills, Inc.*, 716 F.2d 1053, 1055 (4th Cir. 1983), where the court stated that a FERC license “neither transfers nor diminishes any right of possession or enjoyment possessed by” a landowner. Similarly, *Nez Perce Tribe v. Idaho Power Co.*, 847 F. Supp. 791, 812 (D. Idaho 1993), held that the FPA does not preempt state law claims for damages arising from the use of state-owned land.
