**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the "Navigability for Title" Test by Focusing on the Rivers' Status at Statehood.**

The Montana Supreme Court properly determined that the relevant inquiry under the "equal footing" doctrine is whether the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable *at the time Montana achieved statehood in 1889*. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931) ("The question whether the river was navigable at the time of the admission of the state into the Union is the crucial inquiry."); *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) (reaffirming that the test for title under the equal footing doctrine turns on navigability *at statehood*). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the navigability inquiry is historical and fixed at the moment of statehood. *See, e.g., Bonelli Cattle Co. v. Arizona*, 414 U.S. 313, 329 (1973) ("The test is whether the body of water was navigable in fact at the time the State was admitted into the Union."); *Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Mississippi*, 484 U.S. 469, 477 (1988) ("The crucial time for determining navigability is the time the State entered the Union.").

PPL’s contrary argument—that courts should consider post-statehood changes in navigability—finds no support in this Court’s precedents. To the extent PPL invokes *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1 (1935), or *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49 (1926), those cases merely reiterate that the state’s title vests at statehood and remains unaffected by subsequent changes in navigability. *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 28 ("The title of the State to the lands under the navigable waters within her borders is subject to the paramount power of Congress to control navigation and to regulate or utilize the waters for purposes of interstate commerce."); *Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. at 55 ("The title of the State to lands under navigable waters within her borders is subject to the power of Congress to control navigation and to regulate or utilize the waters for purposes of interstate commerce."). Neither case suggests that the initial navigability determination should be revisited based on later developments.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on historical evidence to determine navigability at statehood was likewise proper. Courts routinely examine historical records—including legislative enactments, contemporaneous surveys, and expert affidavits—to ascertain navigability at the critical juncture. *See, e.g., Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82-83 (relying on historical evidence to determine navigability at statehood); *Bonelli*, 414 U.S. at 329 (same). The District Court did not err in admitting the State’s historical exhibits under Montana Rule of Evidence 902 (self-authenticating documents) and Rule 803(8) (public records exception to hearsay). *See Smith v. City of Great Falls*, 2005 MT 198, ¶ 39 (Mont. 2005) (affidavits must be based on personal knowledge or admissible hearsay); *Montana Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc.*, 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79-81 (admissibility of public records under Rule 803(8)).

**II. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Held That the Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt the State’s Claims.**

PPL’s preemption argument fails under both field and conflict preemption analyses. The Federal Power Act (FPA) does not "occupy the field" of state property rights. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 175-76 (1946) ("The Federal Power Act leaves to the state authorities the determination of the extent to which the lands under the navigable waters within the State are to be used for power purposes."); *Public Utility Dist. No. 1 of Pend Oreille County v. Washington Dep’t of Ecology*, 511 U.S. 700, 718 (1994) (FPA preserves state authority over water quality certification). The FPA’s licensing scheme explicitly contemplates state involvement in reviewing power projects, including consideration of state property rights. 16 U.S.C. § 802 (requiring applicants to demonstrate compliance with state law regarding "beds and banks" and water appropriation).

PPL’s reliance on *Federal Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239 (1954), is misplaced. *Niagara* addressed whether the FPA authorized the Federal Power Commission to regulate nonnavigable portions of a river, not whether the FPA preempts state property rights. *Id.* at 249-51. The Court in *Niagara* expressly declined to disturb state property rights, noting that the FPA "does not grant the Commission jurisdiction over the nonnavigable portions of the river." *Id.* at 251.

Nor does the federal navigational servitude preempt the State’s claims. The servitude "does not operate to defeat the State’s title to lands underlying navigable waters." *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. at 551. While the servitude permits the federal government to regulate navigation without compensating landowners, it does not divest states of their title to riverbeds or bar states from seeking compensation for private use of state-owned lands. *Id.* at 551-52; *see also United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596-97 (1941) (servitude does not extinguish state title).

**III. The State’s Title to the Riverbeds Under the Equal Footing Doctrine Is Not Subject to PPL’s Affirmative Defenses.**

PPL’s equitable defenses—prescriptive easement, estoppel, laches, and statute of limitations—fail as a matter of law. The equal footing doctrine vests absolute title in the state at statehood, and this title is not subject to adverse possession or prescriptive easement claims. *Bonelli*, 414 U.S. at 329 ("The State’s title to lands under navigable waters is not subject to prescription or adverse possession."); *Phillips Petroleum*, 484 U.S. at 477 (same). Similarly, estoppel and laches cannot defeat the State’s sovereign title. *See Utah*, 283 U.S. at 86 ("The State’s title to lands under navigable waters is not subject to prescription or adverse possession."); *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. at 551 (state title is not divested by nonuse or acquiescence).

The statute of limitations likewise does not bar the State’s claims. Sovereign immunity principles prevent the application of statutes of limitations to the State’s title claims. *See, e.g., County of Oneida v. Oneida Indian Nation of N.Y. State*, 470 U.S. 226, 244 (1985) ("The general rule is that statutes of limitations do not run against the sovereign."). Even if a limitations period applied, the State’s claims accrued no earlier than PPL’s first use of the riverbeds after statehood, and PPL has not shown any prejudice from the State’s delay.

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the navigability-for-title test by focusing on the rivers’ status at statehood and properly rejected PPL’s preemption and equitable defenses. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
