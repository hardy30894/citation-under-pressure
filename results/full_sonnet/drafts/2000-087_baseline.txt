# ARGUMENT

## I. THE POST-REMOVAL-PERIOD STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION OF A REMOVABLE ALIEN

### A. The Text of § 1231(a)(6) Contains No Express Authorization for Indefinite Detention

Section 1231(a)(6) provides that an alien ordered removed "may be detained beyond the removal period." 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). Petitioner submits that this text, standing alone, does not speak to *duration*. It says an alien "may be detained beyond the removal period," but it does not say for how long. Where Congress has elsewhere granted the Attorney General detention authority, it has typically cabined that authority with temporal limits or procedural checks. The absence of any such limit here should not be read as a grant of limitless authority, but rather as an ambiguity that this Court must resolve using the tools of statutory construction—including the canon that statutes should be construed, where possible, to avoid serious constitutional doubt. *See* *Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council*, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988) ("[W]here an otherwise acceptable construction of a statute would raise serious constitutional problems, the Court will construe the statute to avoid such problems unless such construction is plainly contrary to the intent of Congress.").

### B. Indefinite Civil Detention Raises Grave Constitutional Concerns That Require a Limiting Construction

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment applies to "all 'persons' within the United States, including aliens, whether their presence here is lawful, unlawful, temporary, or permanent." *Mathews v. Diaz*, 426 U.S. 67, 77 (1976). This Court has long recognized that "freedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty that Clause protects." *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992).

Civil detention, unmoored from any criminal proceeding, is constitutionally permissible only in narrow circumstances involving "a special justification" that outweighs the individual's liberty interest, and even then, "the Court has upheld preventive detention based on dangerousness only when limited to specially dangerous individuals and subject to strong procedural protections." *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 356–58 (1997). Critically, in every such case, detention was linked to a determination of dangerousness with meaningful periodic review, *see* *id.*; *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 747 (1987) (upholding pretrial detention where the Bail Reform Act "carefully limits the circumstances under which detention may be sought" and requires a "full-blown adversary hearing"). Section 1231(a)(6), by contrast, applies not to "a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals," but broadly to any alien ordered removed for myriad reasons—including, potentially, minor visa violations—without any showing of dangerousness in many cases and without any assurance that detention will ever end.

This Court's decision in *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), does not control. Mezei involved an alien stopped at the border who had never effected entry into the United States and who was therefore treated, for constitutional purposes, "as if stopped at the border." *Id.* at 215. Petitioner, by contrast, is a lawfully admitted resident alien who has resided in this country for decades. As this Court has recognized, "once an alien enters the country, the legal circumstance changes, for the Due Process Clause applies to all 'persons' within the United States." *Plyler v. Doe*, 457 U.S. 202, 210 (1982). The distinction between an alien seeking initial entry and one who has been admitted and resides within our borders is not a mere formalism; it reflects the fundamentally different constitutional status of the two classes of aliens.

### C. Indefinite Detention Bears No Reasonable Relation to the Statute's Purpose

Civil detention must bear a "reasonable relation to the purpose for which the individual [was] committed." *Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. 715, 738 (1972). The purpose of post-removal-period detention under § 1231(a)(6) is to secure the alien's presence at the moment removal becomes possible. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention no longer serves that purpose—it becomes indistinguishable from punishment imposed without trial, procedural protections, or fixed term. Petitioner Zadvydas's own case illustrates the problem starkly: no country would accept him. Germany rejected him because he was not a German citizen; Lithuania rejected him because he was neither a citizen nor a permanent resident. His detention, on the record before the district court, was not temporary custody incident to an approaching removal—it was, in the district court's words, effectively permanent, because "there is no place to send him." *Zadvydas v. Caplinger*, 986 F. Supp. 1011, 1027 (E.D. La. 1997).

### D. The Statute Must Be Construed to Contain an Implicit "Reasonable Time" Limitation

Given these serious constitutional concerns, this Court should construe § 1231(a)(6) to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to bring about the alien's removal—not indefinite detention without temporal limit. This construction follows directly from the canon of constitutional avoidance articulated in *DeBartolo*, 485 U.S. at 575, and honors Congress's evident purpose: to facilitate removal, not to authorize permanent civil incarceration untethered to any realistic prospect of removal.

Under this construction, habeas courts must ask whether detention has exceeded the period reasonably necessary to secure removal, measuring reasonableness against the statute's basic purpose of assuring the alien's presence at the moment removal becomes possible. Where, as here, removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute, and the alien is entitled to release, subject to appropriate conditions of supervision that protect the government's legitimate interests without offending the Constitution's core protections against arbitrary detention.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed.
