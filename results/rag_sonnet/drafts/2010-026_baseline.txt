# ARGUMENT

## THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED BY EXPANDING EXEMPTION 2 BEYOND ITS TEXT TO SHIELD DOCUMENTS THAT DO NOT RELATE TO "INTERNAL PERSONNEL RULES AND PRACTICES"

### A. FOIA's Exemptions Are Exclusive and Must Be Narrowly Construed

FOIA embodies "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." *Dep't of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). The nine statutory exemptions are "explicitly exclusive," *U.S. Dep't of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989), and this Court has consistently rejected efforts by agencies and courts to expand them through judicial gloss. In *Tax Analysts*, this Court reaffirmed that an agency "may refuse to make available documents in its control only if it proves that the documents fall within one of the nine disclosure exemptions." *Id.* This structural principle—that Congress, not courts, defines the scope of exemptions—forecloses the Ninth Circuit's approach here.

Exemption 2 exempts only matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Ninth Circuit did not apply this text. Instead, borrowing from the D.C. Circuit's decision in *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), the court adopted a judicially invented "predominantly internal" standard, holding that Exemption 2 "shields those personnel materials which are predominantly internal and disclosure of which would present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation." This atextual standard cannot be reconciled with this Court's repeated instruction that FOIA's exemptions "are to be interpreted narrowly" and applied according to their "clearly delineated statutory language." *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 360-61.

### B. The "High 2" Circumvention Theory Has No Grounding in This Court's Decisions and Improperly Substitutes Judicial Policy for Statutory Text

The Ninth Circuit's holding rests on a single sentence in *Rose*, in which this Court observed—in the context of rejecting a *broader* reading of Exemption 2 than the government sought—that the exemption might extend to rules "the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation." 425 U.S. at 369. But *Rose* did not hold that any document whose disclosure creates operational risk becomes an "internal personnel rule or practice." The documents at issue in *Rose*—honor and ethics hearing summaries—still concerned personnel matters; the circumvention language addressed only *which* personnel materials, within that category, might qualify for the exemption's more sensitive tier. *Rose* provides no license to stretch Exemption 2 to cover blast-radius calculations, engineering specifications, and munitions-storage arc maps, which have nothing to do with rules governing agency personnel.

The ESQD documents at issue here are technical data generated to calculate the physical effects of explosions. As the Navy's own witness explained, and the panel majority did not dispute, the Navy "use[s] these arcs to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety." That is engineering and safety-planning information—not a rule or practice governing Navy personnel in any sense contemplated by the statute. Extending Exemption 2 to cover this category of information, simply because an agency affidavit asserts a risk of misuse, converts a narrow personnel exemption into a general safety and security exemption that Congress never enacted.

This Court's decision in *Chrysler Corp. v. Brown*, 441 U.S. 281 (1979), confirms that lower courts may not fashion new grounds for withholding beyond the statutory exemptions Congress actually wrote. There, the Court held that FOIA's exemptions "permit an agency to withhold documents," but do not themselves compel withholding, and rejected efforts to read agency regulations or extrinsic statutes into the exemption scheme without clear congressional authorization. *Id.* The same principle forbids the Ninth Circuit from importing the D.C. Circuit's "predominantly internal" gloss into the statute's plain terms.

### C. Congress Provided the Proper Vehicle for Withholding Genuinely Sensitive Safety and Security Information, and the Navy Failed to Use It

Where Congress intended to protect information whose disclosure could endanger safety or security, it did so explicitly—through Exemption 1, covering material "kept secret in the interest of national defense or foreign policy," 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1), and Exemption 7, covering law-enforcement records whose release "could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual," *id.* § 552(b)(7)(F). This Court's decision in *Weinberger v. Catholic Action of Hawaii/Peace Education Project*, 454 U.S. 139 (1981), illustrates the proper channel: there, the Navy's classification of nuclear-storage information under Exemption 1 supplied the statutory basis for withholding, because "any material properly classified pursuant to Executive Order . . . is exempt from disclosure under Exemption 1." *Id.* at 143. The Navy made no such showing here. It did not classify the ESQD arc maps, and the district court did not resolve whether Exemption 7(F) applied. Having failed to invoke the exemptions Congress crafted for genuine safety and security concerns, the Navy cannot obtain the same result by stretching Exemption 2's personnel-rules language beyond recognition.

Permitting agencies to satisfy Exemption 2 merely by showing that internal use and a generalized "risk of circumvention" exist—without any requirement that the material actually concern personnel rules—effectively creates a tenth, judge-made exemption. That result cannot stand against this Court's repeated admonition that the "exemptions are explicitly exclusive" and must be "interpreted narrowly." *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151; *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
