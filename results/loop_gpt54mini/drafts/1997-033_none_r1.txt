The district court lacked authority to keep this case for trial.

Section 1407(a) authorizes transfer only “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and it then commands that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” to the transferor district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). Congress’s use of “shall” is mandatory. United States v. Illinois Central R.R. Co., 177 U.S. 514, 520 (1900). Courts may not convert a mandatory remand provision into a discretionary one simply because a different result seems efficient. Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282, 289 (1929); Thompson v. United States, 142 U.S. 471, 474 (1892).

That textual command controls here. The multidistrict transfer statute drew a sharp line between pretrial coordination and trial. Congress authorized transfer to centralize common pretrial proceedings, not to vest the transferee court with power to retain the case for trial. See In re Plumbing Fixture Cases, 298 F. Supp. 484, 495 (J.P.M.L. 1968). The district court’s contrary ruling nullified the statute’s remand requirement and effectively rewrote § 1407(a) to say that actions may be transferred for pretrial proceedings “and then kept for trial if the transferee court prefers.” Courts may not add to a statute what Congress omitted. Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 250-51 (1926); Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

Nor does § 1404(a) supply the missing authority. Section 1404(a) allows transfer “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only to a district where venue and jurisdiction would otherwise be proper. Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58 (1949); Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335, 344 (1960). This Court has repeatedly treated § 1404(a) as a limited venue-convenience provision, not a roving commission to disregard other venue statutes. Norwood v. Kirkpatrick, 349 U.S. 29, 32 (1955); Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585, 364 U.S. 19, 26-27 (1960). It follows that § 1404(a) cannot be used to defeat § 1407(a)’s mandatory remand command. Where one statute specifically requires remand, a general transfer provision cannot be used to evade that directive. See United States v. National City Lines, Inc., 334 U.S. 573, 581-82 (1948).

The Ninth Circuit suggested that transferee-court retention is supported by case law, but the cases it and Milberg invoked do not justify disregarding § 1407(a). The point of multidistrict litigation is coordinated pretrial management of common issues, not permanent reassignment of the case to the transferee court. The statute’s design is confirmed by the settled venue principle that litigants are entitled to have their case decided in the forum Congress selected, absent a lawful transfer. Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612, 636-37 (1964). A transferee court’s self-assignment for trial undermines that entitlement and deprives the original forum of the remand Congress required.

The district court also exceeded the proper role of a transferee court under the multidistrict scheme. Congress assigned the transfer-and-remand function to the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation, not to the transferee district judge. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The Court has long cautioned that statutory procedures governing venue and forum selection must be observed as written. Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. at 58; Hoffman, 363 U.S. at 344. The Arizona court’s order effectively nullified the Panel’s statutory duty to remand “at or before” the conclusion of pretrial proceedings.

Finally, the practical consequences confirm the error. Petitioners were haled from their chosen forum in Illinois to Arizona for limited pretrial coordination, only to have the transferee court keep the case for trial. That result is irreconcilable with the statute Congress enacted. As this Court explained in Van Dusen, transfer provisions are designed to change the courtroom, not to change the substantive rights Congress preserved. 376 U.S. at 636-37. Here, the district court changed the statute itself. Because § 1407(a) required remand, and § 1404(a) furnished no authority to override that command, the order assigning the case to the transferee court for trial must be vacated and the case remanded to the Northern District of Illinois.
