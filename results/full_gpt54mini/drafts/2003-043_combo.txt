The judgment below should be affirmed because the Internal Revenue Code ties the ten-year collection period to a timely assessment against the taxpayer whose liability the government seeks to collect. The statute says that, once “the assessment of any tax” has been made within the limitations period, the tax may be collected by levy or court proceeding within ten years after that assessment. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). Assessment, however, is the formal recording of “the liability of the taxpayer,” 26 U.S.C. § 6203, and the taxpayer must be identified. 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. Here, the IRS assessed the partnership, not the individual partners. That assessment therefore extended the collection period only as to the partnership itself.

This Court’s pre-1970 cases confirm that an assessment is a specific act directed to the person assessed, not a free-floating declaration that can be invoked against others whenever state law may impose substantive liability. In Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247 (1935), the Court described assessment as the official act by which tax liability is fixed and collected through the Government’s summary remedies. Id. at 259-60. Similarly, United States v. Updike, 281 U.S. 489 (1930), treated the assessment as the event that starts the collection period running. Id. at 495-97. That same principle appears in Bowers v. New York & Albany Lighterage Co., 273 U.S. 346 (1927), where the Court emphasized that the limitations periods in the tax laws are keyed to assessment and collection in the manner Congress prescribed. Id. at 350-52. None of these decisions suggests that an assessment against one taxpayer automatically operates as an assessment against another.

The Government relies on partnership law, but that argument conflates substantive liability with the separate procedural step Congress required before the IRS may use its extraordinary collection powers against a particular person. The Court has long recognized that federal tax collection proceeds according to the statutory machinery Congress chose. In United States v. Chamberlin, 219 U.S. 250 (1911), the Court held that the Government must proceed in the manner the revenue laws authorize; liability in the abstract does not itself dispense with statutory requirements. Id. at 261-63. In Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282 (1929), the Court reiterated that revenue statutes governing collection and suit are conditions on the Government’s remedy, not mere formalities. Id. at 288-89. Likewise, Davis v. Corona Coal Co., 265 U.S. 219 (1924), and Price v. United States, 269 U.S. 492 (1926), both reflect that tax enforcement depends on compliance with the procedure Congress set out, including the steps that mark the taxpayer and the tax at issue. Davis, 265 U.S. at 223-24; Price, 269 U.S. at 499-500.

The partnership cases do not help the United States. State-law joint liability may define who ultimately owes the debt, but it does not erase the federal requirement of a proper assessment against the person whose property the Government seeks to reach. See United States v. Updike, 281 U.S. 489 (1930), at 495-97; Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247 (1935), at 259-60. Nor does a later bankruptcy filing revive an expired federal limitations period. In Russell v. United States, 278 U.S. 181 (1929), the Court held that limitations in tax statutes are to be enforced according to their terms, because Congress chose finality as part of the tax system. Id. at 188-89. And in Stange v. United States, 282 U.S. 270 (1931), the Court again treated the Government’s collection power as bounded by the statutory time limit tied to assessment and the proper taxpayer. Id. at 276-77.

The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the IRS’s assessment against the partnership did not toll or extend the statute of limitations as to the individual partners. The partners were separate taxpayers; they were never assessed; and the time to assess them expired years before the bankruptcy claims were filed. Because the Government failed to take the step the statute requires against the respondents themselves, its claims are unenforceable. The judgment should be affirmed.
