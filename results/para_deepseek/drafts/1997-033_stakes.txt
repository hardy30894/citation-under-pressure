**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Text of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) Unambiguously Requires Remand After Pretrial Proceedings, Foreclosing Any District Court Authority to Retain a Transferred Case for Trial Under § 1404(a).**

The question presented is easily resolved by the unambiguous command of § 1407(a). That statute provides that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation may transfer civil actions “to any district for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). Critically, it then mandates that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings to the district from which it was transferred.” *Id.* (emphasis added). This language admits no exception, and it cannot be squared with the notion that a transferee court may retain a case for trial by invoking § 1404(a).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reasoning—that “Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) explicitly authorizes a transferee court to transfer the action to itself for trial pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a)”—cannot override the statute. A procedural rule of the Judicial Panel cannot expand the substantive authority of district courts beyond what Congress enacted. *Cf. Sibbach v. Wilson & Co.*, 312 U.S. 1, 13-14 (1941) (procedural rules must be consistent with enabling statutes). The Manual for Complex Litigation’s mention of a § 1404(a) transfer as a “standard option” similarly lacks binding force. As the dissent below recognized, “[s]eldom have so many been so consistently wrong.” *Lexecon, Inc. v. U.S. Dist. Ct.*, No. 95-70380, slip op. at 4 (9th Cir. July 21, 1995) (Kozinski, J., dissenting). This Court’s opportunity to correct that pervasive error is now.

**II. The Alleged Conflict Between § 1407 and § 1404 Is Illusory; Congress’s Policy Choice Must Be Respected.**

The courts below relied on cases such as *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 124-25 (2d Cir. 1971), and *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810, 820 (3d Cir. 1982), which held that a transferee court may consider § 1404(a) transfer motions as part of “pretrial proceedings.” But those decisions ignored the fundamental structure of the multidistrict litigation statute. Congress empowered the Panel—not individual district judges—to control the situs of trial. The Panel’s authority to transfer is “carefully limited to accomplish the statute’s purpose,” and the statute “precludes use of the general transfer section by district judges to subvert the panel’s responsibility.” *Lexecon*, slip op. at 5 (Kozinski, J., dissenting).

There is no genuine conflict between §§ 1407 and 1404. A transferee court may entertain a motion under § 1404(a) *after* the Panel remands the case to the transferor district. At that point, the transferor court—not the transferee court—possesses full authority to consider whether the case should be transferred elsewhere, including back to the original transferee district. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). A contrary rule, which allows the transferee court to short-circuit the remand process, effectively writes § 1407(a)’s mandate out of the statute. This Court has consistently rejected such statutory nullification. *See, e.g., Conn. Nat’l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992) (“courts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there”).

**III. Petitioner Suffered Concrete Prejudice from the Erroneous Transfer, and Mandamus Relief Is Appropriate.**

Petitioner was “dragged hundreds of miles from [its] home district in Illinois to appear before a judge in Arizona who had no familiarity with the case.” *Lexecon*, slip op. at 5 (Kozinski, J., dissenting). Petitioner participated in no common discovery and benefitted from no coordinated proceedings. The district court’s refusal to remand forced Petitioner to trial in a forum it never chose, contravening the “ancient right to select the forum” that Congress preserved in § 1407. *Id.*

Although the Ninth Circuit denied mandamus on grounds that Petitioner had an adequate remedy on appeal, the very nature of an erroneous venue order inflicts irreparable prejudice—forcing Petitioner to incur the expense and inconvenience of trial in the wrong district. *See Sunshine Beauty Supplies v. U.S. Dist. Ct.*, 872 F.2d 310, 311 (9th Cir. 1989) (recognizing such prejudice). When a district court’s order “is clearly erroneous as a matter of law” and “raises new and important problems,” as the court below acknowledged, mandamus is the appropriate vehicle to address it. *Bauman v. U.S. Dist. Ct.*, 557 F.2d 650, 653 (9th Cir. 1977). The error here—one that “the federal courts have simply disregarded the statutory mandate” for a quarter-century—is precisely the kind of “oft-repeated error” that warrants this Court’s intervention. *Lexecon*, slip op. at 4 (Kozinski, J., dissenting).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case should be remanded with directions to order the district court to vacate its § 1404(a) transfer and remand the action to the Northern District of Illinois pursuant to § 1407(a).
