# ARGUMENT

## THE THREE-YEAR LIMITATIONS PERIOD OF 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) REQUIRES ONLY THAT A PLAN PARTICIPANT HAD THE OPPORTUNITY TO ACQUIRE KNOWLEDGE OF THE DISCLOSED TRANSACTION, NOT THAT HE ACTUALLY READ OR RECALLS READING IT

### A. The Plain Text of Section 1113(2) Does Not Require Subjective Recollection

Section 1113(2) bars suit "three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This Court has repeatedly held that when a fiduciary discloses information in the manner ERISA requires, the participant is charged with the knowledge that disclosure conveys. Congress designed ERISA's disclosure regime "to ensure that the individual participant knows exactly where he stands with respect to the plan." *Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen*, 514 U.S. 73, 83 (1995) (quoting H.R. Rep. No. 93-1280, at 297 (1974)). That statutory purpose is defeated if a participant may simply assert, after litigation has commenced, that he never read what was placed before him.

The Ninth Circuit's rule—that "actual knowledge" requires proof the plaintiff "actually was aware" that the challenged investments occurred and were imprudent—collapses the distinction between disclosure and comprehension that this Court's precedents have consistently maintained. Sulyma, 909 F.3d at 1078. That approach effectively converts "actual knowledge" into "subjective recollection," a standard nowhere found in the statute's text.

### B. This Court's Fraud-Discovery Precedents Confirm That Actual Knowledge May Be Established Through Undisputed Receipt of Disclosures

This Court's decision in *Merck & Co. v. Reynolds*, 559 U.S. 633 (2010), addressing an analogous "discovery" accrual rule under the securities laws, recognized that actual knowledge is a factual showing satisfied when a plaintiff is placed in possession of the facts constituting the violation. *Id.* at 648. Nothing in *Merck* suggests that a defendant must prove the plaintiff subjectively processed and retained each disclosed fact; rather, the discovery rule is satisfied once the relevant facts were communicated to the plaintiff in a manner reasonably calculated to inform him.

Similarly, in *Tibble v. Edison International*, 575 U.S. 523 (2015), this Court examined ERISA's fiduciary duties in the context of ongoing disclosure obligations, reaffirming that "a fiduciary normally has a continuing duty ... to monitor investments and remove imprudent ones," 575 U.S. at 529, a duty that presumes participants are informed through the disclosures fiduciaries are statutorily obligated to provide. The statutory scheme Congress built around fiduciary disclosure obligations, see 29 U.S.C. § 1024(b), would be rendered meaningless if a participant's failure to read—or professed inability to recall—compliant disclosures could indefinitely extend the limitations period.

### C. The Ninth Circuit's Rule Cannot Be Reconciled With This Court's Treatment of Constructive-Notice Regimes

Congress's 1987 repeal of ERISA's constructive-knowledge provision, formerly codified at 29 U.S.C. § 1113(a)(2)(B) (1976), does not support the Ninth Circuit's holding. That provision addressed knowledge imputed from *third-party* filings with the Secretary of Labor—reports the participant never received. Its repeal reflects Congress's judgment that participants should not be charged with knowledge of documents filed with a government agency and never furnished to them. It says nothing about disclosures the fiduciary directly transmits to the participant himself. Cf. *Tibble*, 575 U.S. at 531 (looking to established principles of trust law to inform ERISA's fiduciary standards).

Indeed, the analogous concept of "inquiry notice" that this Court applied in *Merck* forecloses the Ninth Circuit's approach. A plaintiff cannot avoid the running of a discovery-based limitations period by declining to read materials placed directly before him that would have disclosed the relevant facts. *Merck*, 559 U.S. at 653 (Scalia, J., concurring in part) (observing that a plaintiff's willful blindness to available facts does not toll a discovery-based statute).

### D. The Rule Below Creates an Unworkable and Perverse Incentive Structure

The Ninth Circuit's construction rewards participants who ignore mandated disclosures over those who diligently review them, since only the inattentive participant can permanently forestall the running of the limitations period through self-serving deposition testimony that he does not "recall" reading a document. This inverts the statutory design: ERISA's disclosure requirements, 29 U.S.C. § 1024(b), exist precisely so that fiduciaries may discharge their obligations to participants through the furnished documents, not through proof of each participant's subjective retention of their contents.

This Court has cautioned against statutory constructions that produce such incongruous results. *See Lockheed Corp. v. Spink*, 517 U.S. 882, 887–888 (1996) (construing ERISA provisions to avoid results inconsistent with the statute's structure and purpose). A rule under which "actual knowledge" turns on unverifiable assertions of non-recollection invites exactly the kind of protracted, fact-intensive litigation over stale disclosures that Congress sought to avoid by enacting a firm three-year period running from the point of disclosure-based knowledge.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
