The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution, Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress. The Constitution's text and the intent of the Framers, as evidenced by historical context and judicial precedent, demonstrate that states may not impose additional qualifications beyond those enumerated.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are a constitutional ceiling, not a floor. In Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that Congress may not add to the qualifications specified in the Constitution. Similarly, in United States v. Smith, 286 U.S. 6 (1932), the Court stated that the Constitution's enumeration of qualifications is exclusive.

The historical context of the Constitution's drafting and ratification supports this interpretation. As James Madison explained in The Federalist No. 52, the Qualifications Clauses were intended to ensure uniformity in the qualifications for federal officeholders. Allowing states to impose additional qualifications would undermine this uniformity and create a patchwork of different requirements across the country.

The Court has consistently applied this principle in various contexts. In Ex parte Daigle, 307 U.S. 618 (1939), the Court reaffirmed that the Constitution's Qualifications Clauses are controlling. In Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham, 279 U.S. 597 (1929), the Court held that the Senate's power to judge the qualifications of its members is limited to the constitutional qualifications.

In this case, Section 3 was inextricably linked to Sections 1 and 2, and the voters were presented with an "all or nothing" package.

The decision of the lower court should be affirmed, as it correctly applied the relevant constitutional provisions and judicial precedent. The Qualifications Clauses establish a constitutional ceiling that precludes states from imposing additional qualifications for federal officeholders. The historical context and judicial precedent support this interpretation, and the severability clause in Amendment 73 is ineffective in saving the remaining provisions.
