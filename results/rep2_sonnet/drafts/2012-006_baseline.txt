# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE REQUIRES THE GOVERNMENT TO PROVE, BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT, THAT A DEFENDANT'S MEMBERSHIP IN A CONSPIRACY CONTINUED INTO THE LIMITATIONS PERIOD

### A. The Government Must Prove Every Fact Necessary to Constitute the Crime Charged

"[T]he Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged." *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). This principle is not a mere evidentiary nicety; it is the "prime instrument for reducing the risk of convictions resting on factual error." *Id.* at 363. Where timely prosecution is itself an element that the government must establish—because a conspiracy prosecuted outside the limitations period is no crime at all—the burden of proving that the offense occurred within the statutory period belongs to the prosecution, not the defense.

This Court has long recognized that a conspiracy is a continuing offense that terminates either upon accomplishment of its object or upon abandonment. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) ("[C]onspiracy . . . is a partnership in criminal purposes . . . that continues until the object of the conspiracy is accomplished or until the conspiracy itself is abandoned."). Because the statute of limitations bars prosecution unless an overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy occurred within the limitations window, the government's charge necessarily includes an implicit assertion that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy—or that the conspiracy itself persisted—into that window. That assertion is as much a "fact necessary to constitute the crime" as any element listed in the indictment. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364.

### B. Once a Defendant Produces Evidence of Withdrawal, the Burden of Persuasion Shifts to the Government

This Court's decisions in *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), and *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197 (1977), establish the framework for determining when a defense bears on an element the prosecution must disprove beyond a reasonable doubt, as opposed to an affirmative defense the defendant may be required to establish. In *Mullaney*, the Court held that Maine could not require a defendant to prove "heat of passion" to reduce murder to manslaughter, because the presence or absence of that fact went directly to "the degree of criminal culpability." 421 U.S. at 698. The Court explained that a State may not "reallocate the burden of proof" for a fact that is "critical" to the grading or existence of the offense simply "by labeling it a matter to be raised by the defendant." *Id.* at 699, 701.

Withdrawal prior to the limitations period is precisely such a fact. It does not merely mitigate culpability; it negates an element the government must prove—continued participation in an ongoing criminal agreement during the period Congress has deemed prosecutable. Unlike the affirmative defense at issue in *Patterson*, which concerned extreme emotional disturbance and did not "serve to negative any facts of the crime which the State is to prove," 432 U.S. at 207, evidence of withdrawal directly negates the temporal element the government must establish to bring the prosecution at all.

Consistent with these principles, this Court has repeatedly held that once a defendant comes forward with evidence sufficient to place a statute-of-limitations defense genuinely in issue, the government bears the burden of proving compliance with the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt. *See Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 114–15 (1970) (recognizing that statutes of limitations in criminal cases "are to be liberally interpreted in favor of repose," and that the government must establish the offense occurred within the statutory period). The same logic that animates *Toussie*'s protective construction of limitations periods requires that, once withdrawal evidence is introduced, the ultimate burden of persuasion on continued membership remains with the prosecution.

### C. Requiring the Government to Prove Continued Membership Beyond a Reasonable Doubt Comports with the Jury's Constitutional Role

The Sixth Amendment guarantees that "a jury, not a judge, will find each element of the offense" beyond a reasonable doubt. *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 510 (1995). Allowing a defendant to bear the burden of disproving continued membership—rather than requiring the government to prove it—effectively permits conviction on an essential element without the constitutionally required standard of proof. That result cannot be squared with *Gaudin*'s command that "the jury verdict required by the Sixth Amendment is a jury verdict of guilty beyond a reasonable doubt." *Id.* at 522–23.

### D. Application to This Case

Petitioners produced substantial evidence—undisputed by the government—that the alleged leaders of the conspiracy, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, had severed their relationship before the limitations period began to run. Under the framework set forth above, that evidence was sufficient to require the government to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that petitioners remained members of a single, continuing conspiracy that persisted into the limitations period. The government failed to do so. Instead, the courts below permitted the jury to infer continued membership from petitioners' earlier participation, effectively shifting to petitioners the burden of proving their own withdrawal—precisely the burden-shifting this Court's precedents forbid.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
