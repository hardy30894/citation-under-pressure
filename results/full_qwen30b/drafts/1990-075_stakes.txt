**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL IS CONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE CONGRESS PROVIDED AN INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TO GUIDE THE DELEGATEE’S EXERCISE OF POWER.

The Constitution vests all legislative power in Congress, U.S. Const. art. I, § 1, but does not prohibit Congress from delegating authority to other branches so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegatee’s exercise of discretion. *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This principle has been consistently reaffirmed by the Supreme Court, most recently in *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 377 (1989), where the Court upheld Congress’s delegation of authority to the Federal Sentencing Commission to promulgate guidelines, noting that “Congress may not abdicate or transfer its essential legislative functions,” but may “delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to act is directed to conform.” *Id.* at 377 (quoting *J.W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409).

Here, Congress clearly provided such an intelligible principle in 21 U.S.C. § 811(h). The statute authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance only when he determines that “there is a substantial likelihood that the substance presents an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is not vague or amorphous. It is a concrete, policy-driven threshold that requires a factual determination of public danger, grounded in the same factors Congress has long recognized as relevant to scheduling decisions: the substance’s potential for abuse, its pharmacological effects, its history and pattern of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. See *id.* § 811(c)(4), (5), (6). These factors are not mere suggestions—they are mandatory criteria that must be considered before any temporary scheduling action may be taken. The Attorney General is not free to act on whim or political preference; he must base his decision on a factual assessment of the substance’s threat to public safety.

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that this standard is insufficient is contrary to settled precedent. In *Mistretta*, the Court upheld a delegation that required the Sentencing Commission to consider “the nature and circumstances of the offense, the history and characteristics of the defendant, and the need for the sentence imposed,” 488 U.S. at 377, even though those factors were not defined with mathematical precision. The Court emphasized that “Congress need not spell out every detail of the policy to be implemented,” and that “the Constitution does not require that Congress write a detailed code for every administrative action.” *Id.* at 378. The same is true here. The standard “imminent hazard to the public safety” is not a blank check; it is a threshold that demands a reasoned, fact-based judgment, and Congress has provided the necessary framework for that judgment.

Moreover, the temporary scheduling provision is not a general delegation of legislative power. It is a targeted, emergency mechanism designed to address the rapid emergence of new synthetic drugs—like Euphoria—that can be manufactured and distributed before Congress can act through the slower permanent scheduling process. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 654. The temporary scheduling authority is precisely such a delegation—narrow in scope, time-limited, and subject to procedural safeguards.

The dissenting opinion in the Third Circuit misapprehends the nature of the delegation. It suggests that because the Attorney General may create new crimes through scheduling, the delegation is inherently unconstitutional. But this is a misreading of *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), the two cases the dissent cites. In both, the delegations were invalid not because they created crimes, but because they lacked any intelligible principle at all. In *Panama Refining*, the statute gave the President unfettered discretion to prohibit the interstate transport of petroleum “in such manner as he may deem necessary or proper,” a standard that “conveys no guidance at all.” 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter*, the President was empowered to approve “codes of fair competition” without any criteria for what constituted a “fair” code. 295 U.S. at 522. By contrast, § 811(h) requires the Attorney General to make a determination based on specific, measurable factors and to act only when a substantial likelihood of imminent hazard exists. The presence of such a standard renders the delegation constitutional.

II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S DELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA IS PROPER AND CONSISTENT WITH CONGRESS’S DELEGATION.

Congress has long authorized the Attorney General to delegate his responsibilities under the Controlled Substances Act. In 1973, the Attorney General issued a regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), subdelegating to the DEA Administrator the functions “vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.” This regulation was reaffirmed and expanded in 1987, when the Attorney General amended the regulation to include the temporary scheduling authority conferred by the 1984 amendments. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989). The DEA Administrator then issued the notice and order temporarily scheduling 4-methylaminorex as a Schedule I substance, in full compliance with the statutory requirements.
