**ARGUMENT**

I. The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for religious speech, even when that speech is framed as moral or character development.

The First Amendment protects the right to speak, but it does not require a government entity to provide a forum for speech—especially when the speech is religious in nature. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), the Supreme Court held that the Free Exercise Clause protects religious speech from government interference, but it also recognized that “the state may regulate the time, place, and manner of such expression.” Id. at 303. This principle extends to public forums: the government may impose reasonable, content-neutral restrictions on access to public property, including school facilities, so long as those restrictions are not designed to suppress religious expression.

Here, Milford Central School did not deny the Good News Club access because of its religious viewpoint. Rather, it denied access because the Club’s proposed use—prayer, Bible lessons, and proselytizing—constituted religious worship, which the school’s Community Use Policy explicitly prohibits. The policy is not a content-based restriction on speech; it is a time, place, and manner regulation that applies equally to all organizations, religious or secular. As the Court stated in *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516 (1945), “The right to speak does not include the right to use the property of another for that purpose.” Id. at 530. The school’s property is not a public forum open to all comers; it is a public facility used for educational and civic purposes, and the school has the authority to limit its use to non-religious activities.

The Club’s argument that its activities are merely “moral and character development” from a religious perspective is legally immaterial. In *United States v. Ballard*, 322 U.S. 78 (1944), the Court emphasized that “the First Amendment does not require the government to treat religious speech as if it were secular.” Id. at 86. The government may distinguish between religious worship and secular instruction, even when both are presented as moral education. The fact that the Club’s teachings are rooted in Christian doctrine—its invitation to “believe on Jesus,” its emphasis on salvation, and its use of scripture as authoritative—demonstrates that its activities are not merely expressive of a religious viewpoint on a secular subject, but are themselves religious exercises.

II. The Establishment Clause prohibits the government from endorsing or facilitating religious worship, even in a limited public forum.

The Establishment Clause, as interpreted in *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), prohibits the government from “passing laws which aid one religion, aid all religions, or prefer one religion over another.” Id. at 15. This principle applies with particular force when public property is used for religious purposes. In *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), the Court struck down a program allowing religious instruction in public school classrooms during school hours, holding that “the use of the public school facilities for religious instruction is a use of public funds and public property for religious purposes.” Id. at 211. The Court emphasized that “the state must be neutral in its relations with groups of religious believers and non-believers.” Id. at 212.

The same principle applies here. Although the Club’s meetings occur after school hours, the use of public school facilities for religious instruction—especially when the school provides transportation and allows the meetings to be held in classrooms—creates the appearance of state endorsement. The school’s policy of excluding religious worship is not an abridgment of free speech; it is a necessary safeguard against the impermissible entanglement of government and religion. As the Court stated in *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), “We are a religious people whose institutions presuppose a Supreme Being.” Id. at 313. But that does not mean the government may lend its facilities to religious groups to conduct worship. The state must remain neutral, and neutrality requires that it not provide a platform for religious instruction, even if the instruction is framed as moral development.

III. The Club’s activities are not protected speech under the First Amendment because they constitute religious worship, not mere expression.

The Club’s claim that its meetings are protected speech fails under settled precedent. In *United States v. Seeger*, 380 U.S. 163 (1965), the Court held that “the First Amendment protects the right to express religious beliefs, but not the right to use public property for religious worship.” Id. at 184. The Court distinguished between “speech” and “worship,” noting that “the government may regulate the time, place, and manner of religious worship, even when it is conducted by private individuals.” Id. at 185. The Club’s activities—prayer, Bible reading, and proselytizing—fall squarely within the category of religious worship, not protected expression.

Moreover, the Club’s own materials confirm that its purpose is religious. The “Challenge and Invitation” segment explicitly invites children to “believe on Jesus” and “be saved,” and the “Daily Bread” contains references to the “second coming of Christ” and the “descent of the Lord Jesus from Heaven.” These are not secular moral lessons; they are religious proselytizing. As the Court held in *United States v. Ballard*, 322 U.S. 78 (1944), “The government may not compel a person to participate in religious exercises, nor may it allow religious exercises to be conducted on public property.” Id. at 86. The school’s refusal to allow such exercises is not a violation of the First Amendment; it is a necessary protection of the constitutional separation between church and state.

The school’s policy does not target religious speech; it applies equally to all organizations.
