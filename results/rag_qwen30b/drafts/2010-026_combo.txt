**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Navy’s Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—technical data used to determine safe storage distances for ordnance at Naval Magazine Indian Island—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly internal” personnel rules and practices. That holding misapplies the statutory text, misinterprets the scope of Exemption 2, and contravenes binding Supreme Court precedent. The ESQD maps are not “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” of the Navy, nor do they present a risk of circumventing agency regulation. Rather, they are technical safety specifications developed for the purpose of protecting public safety and ensuring compliance with federal safety standards. Their disclosure would not jeopardize national security or agency operations, and the Navy has not classified them as such. The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard—without any basis in the text or history of FOIA—expands Exemption 2 beyond its statutory limits and violates the principle that FOIA exemptions must be narrowly construed.

**I. The ESQD Arc Maps Are Not “Related Solely” to Internal Personnel Rules and Practices**

The text of Exemption 2 is clear: it applies only to “matters which are related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The phrase “solely” is not a mere formality—it is a limiting term that excludes information with any significant external purpose. The Supreme Court has long recognized that FOIA exemptions are “explicitly exclusive,” and must be “narrowly construed.” *United States v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976); *United States Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989). The Ninth Circuit’s decision to exempt the ESQD maps under Exemption 2 rests on a misreading of the statute’s scope.

The ESQD maps were not created for internal administrative purposes. They were developed pursuant to the Navy’s *Ammunition and Explosives Ashore Safety Regulations for Handling, Storing, and Production Renovation and Shipping* (OP-5 manual), which is explicitly designed to “prescribe standardized safety regulations for the production, renovation, care, handling, storage, preparation for shipment, and disposal of these items.” The maps are used to determine safe distances between explosive storage facilities to prevent sympathetic detonations—a function that directly serves public safety and regulatory compliance. As such, they are not “internal” in any meaningful sense. They are technical specifications that govern the physical handling of hazardous materials, not internal employment policies.

This distinction is critical. In *United States v. Hendee*, 124 U.S. 309, 314 (1888), the Court held that a paymaster’s clerk was “an officer of the navy” within the meaning of a statute providing for increased pay based on length of service. The Court emphasized that the individual’s duties were “in the naval service of the United States,” not merely administrative. Similarly, the ESQD maps are not internal personnel tools; they are operational safety tools used in the Navy’s core mission of handling explosives. Their purpose is not to regulate internal conduct but to ensure that the Navy complies with federal safety standards and protects the surrounding communities. The maps are not “rules and practices” governing personnel—they are engineering data used to prevent catastrophic accidents.

**II. The “Predominantly Internal” Standard Is Not Supported by Pre-1970 Authority**

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard—first articulated in *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981)—is a post-1970 innovation. No Supreme Court decision before 1970 supports such a standard. Indeed, the Court has consistently rejected expansive interpretations of FOIA exemptions.

In *United States v. Alger*, 152 U.S. 384, 390 (1894), the Court emphasized that statutory construction must be grounded in the “meaning of the statute” and that “no practice inconsistent with that meaning can have any effect.” The meaning of Exemption 2, as written, is limited to matters “related solely” to internal personnel rules and practices. The phrase “solely” excludes any information with a significant external purpose. The D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test, which allows withholding of information if it is “predominantly” used internally, ignores this statutory language and expands the exemption beyond its text.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that information used to regulate public safety is not exempt under FOIA. In *Murray v. Joe Gerrick & Co.*, 291 U.S. 315, 318 (1934), the Court affirmed that a worker’s death at a Navy Yard was compensable under state law, even though the yard was a federal facility. The Court recognized that the Navy’s operations had direct consequences for public safety and that the law must account for that. Similarly, the ESQD maps are not internal personnel tools—they are safety tools that directly affect the public. Their disclosure would not harm the Navy’s operations; it would enhance public awareness and accountability.

**III. The Navy Has Not Demonstrated a Risk of Circumvention**

Even if the maps were internal, the Navy must show that their disclosure would “present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. The Navy’s claim rests on speculation: Commander Whitbred asserts that a person could “reverse engineer” the maps to discover information about specific weapons systems. But this is not a risk of circumvention—it is a risk of public knowledge. The maps are not classified, and the Navy has not taken steps to classify them. In *United States v. MacDaniel*, 32 U.S. 1, 10 (1833), the Court held that a claimant’s right to present evidence of offset was not defeated by the fact that the government had rejected it—because the court must determine what the head of the department “should have done.” Similarly, the Navy’s failure to classify the maps undermines its claim that their disclosure would endanger national security.
