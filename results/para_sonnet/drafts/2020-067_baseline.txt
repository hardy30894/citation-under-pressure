# ARGUMENT

## THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY CONSULTED THE ENTIRE RECORD, INCLUDING THE UNDISPUTED FACT OF PETITIONER'S PRIOR FELONY CONVICTIONS, IN CONDUCTING PLAIN-ERROR REVIEW UNDER RULE 52(b)

### A. Plain-Error Review Under Rule 52(b) Requires Examination of the Whole Record, Not an Artificially Truncated Version of It

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) permits appellate courts to correct a "plain error that affects substantial rights" even absent a timely objection below. This Court has repeatedly instructed that the plain-error standard "should not be too easy for defendants to satisfy," *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 82 (2004), precisely because it operates as an exception to the ordinary rule that claims must be preserved by objection. The third prong of the plain-error test asks whether the error "affected the outcome of the district court proceedings." *Id.* at 76. The fourth prong asks whether the error "seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 736 (1993) (quoting *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936)).

Nothing in *Olano* or its progeny confines this inquiry to the four corners of evidence presented to the jury. To the contrary, *Olano* itself directed courts to consider "the particular facts of the particular case" in determining whether the error affected the outcome. 507 U.S. at 737. Where, as here, a defendant challenges the omission of a knowledge-of-status element under *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), the "particular facts" bearing on whether that omission mattered inescapably include the defendant's own criminal history—a fact necessarily *outside* what the jury considered, since the *Rehaif* element was never submitted to it at all.

### B. Petitioner's Prior Convictions Are Part of the Record and Are the Most Probative Evidence Available on the Question the Fourth Prong Requires the Court to Answer

The very premise of a *Rehaif* claim is that the jury was never asked to find, and the government never had occasion to affirmatively prove, that the defendant knew he was a felon. It is therefore circular to insist that a court assessing prejudice may look only to what the jury heard when the deficiency is that the jury heard nothing on this element. This Court has recognized that where an unpreserved error concerns an element never litigated, the reviewing court must ask what the "record" reveals about whether the defendant *could* mount a defense—and there is no more direct evidence of a defendant's knowledge that he was a felon than the fact that he was, in fact, convicted of a felony and served a term of imprisonment for it. See *Rehaif*, 139 S. Ct. at 2198 (observing that a defendant's knowledge of status will "often be inferred from" the underlying record).

This mirrors the analysis Wyoming courts undertake when reviewing discretionary determinations for abuse of discretion: courts examine "the reasonableness of a decision in relation to the evidence presented," and do not artificially cabin their review to isolated portions of the proceeding. *Tracy v. Tracy*, 2017 WY 17, ¶ 46, 388 P.3d 1257, 1267 (Wyo. 2017). Just as a district court modifying custody must weigh the entirety of the circumstances—including facts developed outside any single hearing, such as a party's employment history or criminal record, see *id.* ¶ 17—an appellate court assessing plain error must weigh the entirety of the circumstances bearing on prejudice, including the defendant's undisputed criminal history reflected in the record of conviction.

### C. Limiting Review to the Trial Evidence Would Convert Plain-Error Review Into an Automatic-Reversal Rule for *Rehaif* Errors

Petitioner's proposed rule—that a reviewing court may consult only the evidence submitted to the jury—would guarantee reversal in every unpreserved *Rehaif* case, because by definition the jury was never presented with evidence of the defendant's knowledge of status. This would effectively convert a structural-error framework into the plain-error inquiry, contrary to this Court's admonition that Rule 52(b)'s standard is meant to be a "demanding" one that "should not be too easy" for defendants to satisfy. *Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. at 83 & n.9, 82. Courts should not construe a procedural rule in a manner that eliminates its function as a meaningful screen. See *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 732 (Rule 52(b) is meant to be applied so as to permit correction only of errors that are particularly egregious).

Analogously, this Court in matters of appellate review has consistently declined to impose artificial procedural blinders where doing so would produce results untethered to the actual facts of a case. Appellate courts, when review is not de novo, are expected to render judgments "in relation to the evidence presented," affording the prevailing party "every favorable inference." *Tracy*, 2017 WY 17, ¶ 46, 388 P.3d at 1267; see also *Jensen v. Milatzo-Jensen*, 2013 WY 27, ¶ 7, 297 P.3d 768, 772 (Wyo. 2013). The undisputed fact that petitioner was convicted of, and imprisoned for, prior felonies—a fact he has never once claimed he was unaware of—is not some extraneous consideration smuggled in from outside the case; it is part of the record of these very proceedings, developed at sentencing and in the presentence materials that the district court and both parties treated as accurate throughout.

### D. The Rule of Lenity and Sufficiency-of-Record Principles Support Deference to the Court of Appeals' Approach

Where, as here, a party bears the burden of demonstrating prejudice under the third and fourth prongs of *Olano*, that burden is not satisfied by pointing to gaps that the party's own failure to object created. Courts have long held that a party asserting error bears responsibility for providing a record sufficient to permit the appellate court's review; absent such a showing, courts "presume that there were no irregularities" and that the result below "was reasonably based on competent and sufficient evidence." Cf. principles discussed in *Golden v. Guion*, 2013 WY 45, ¶¶ 4–5, 299 P.3d 95, 96–97 (Wyo. 2013). Petitioner cannot satisfy his burden under *Olano*'s third and fourth prongs by insisting that the court ignore the only evidence in the record that speaks directly to the element he claims was omitted.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
