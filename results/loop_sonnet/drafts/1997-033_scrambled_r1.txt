# ARGUMENT

## I. The plain, mandatory text of Section 1407(a) forecloses any transferee court's use of Section 1404(a) to retain a case for trial.

Section 1407(a) provides that the Panel "shall" remand each transferred action "at or before the conclusion of" pretrial proceedings. That word is not a suggestion. This Court has treated the word "shall" in analogous procedural contexts as mandatory rather than permissive, admitting no judicial discretion to disregard it. *Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947). Congress's choice of mandatory phrasing must be given effect according to its terms, for "[i]t is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, ... the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Nothing in Section 1407(a) qualifies the remand duty for cases in which a transferee judge concludes, under the wholly separate discretionary standard of Section 1404(a), that retention would be convenient. The statute's own words supply the surest evidence of its purpose, for "[t]here is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress expressed its wish plainly: remand "at or before the conclusion" of pretrial proceedings, not "unless the transferee court prefers otherwise."

Respondent's countermotion asked the District of Arizona to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent one by invoking Section 1404(a)'s general convenience standard. But Congress did not leave the duration of a Panel transfer to the discretion of the transferee judge; it fixed that duration by statute, and courts have no license to nullify a mandatory command because they judge that departure serves efficiency.

## II. Because Congress specifically restricted multidistrict transfer to pretrial proceedings, the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a) cannot be used to override that specific limitation.

Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) serve different purposes and were drafted with different scope. Section 1404(a) traces its lineage to the historic equitable practice of transferring actions only where considerations of convenience and the interest of justice warranted, a practice this Court examined in *United States v. National City Lines, Inc.*, 334 U.S. 573, 583–84 (1948), and it operates by its own terms only where the transferee forum is one in which the action "might have been brought" in the first instance. This Court has enforced that limitation strictly, holding that the transferee district must satisfy that requirement regardless of the equities that might otherwise favor transfer. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343–44 (1960). Section 1407(a), by contrast, is a specialized mechanism enacted for the narrow purpose of coordinating pretrial proceedings among related actions pending in different districts, with the Panel's authority expressly bounded by the remand command.

Where a specific statutory provision addresses a particular subject, it is not displaced by a general provision addressing the same general subject matter. As this Court has explained, "however inclusive may be the general language of a statute, it 'will not be held to apply to a matter specifically dealt with in another part of the same enactment.'" *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (quoting *Rodgers v. United States*, 185 U.S. 83, 87–89 (1902)). Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the temporal scope of a Panel-ordered transfer; it cannot be "controlled or nullified" by resort to the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a), a statute of general applicability that says nothing about multidistrict litigation at all. *Id.*

Section 1404(a)'s general authority was designed to allow ordinary venue transfers between districts where an action could properly have been filed; it was never understood as a device for a court sitting only by virtue of a limited pretrial assignment to entrench itself permanently in a case and displace the plaintiff's chosen forum for trial. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 61 (1949). Lexecon selected the Northern District of Illinois as its forum. Nothing in Section 1404(a)'s convenience calculus permits a court sitting by virtue of the Panel's pretrial assignment to substitute its own trial-venue preference for the plaintiff's chosen forum, particularly when doing so directly contradicts the specific remand command of Section 1407(a).

## III. The Ninth Circuit erred in treating the conflict as unresolved.

The court below acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate" yet declined to enforce it, resting instead on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and non-binding circuit precedent. But an agency rule cannot rewrite the words Congress chose, and no court may relax a mandatory statutory command because compliance is inconvenient. Under *American Trucking*, 310 U.S. at 543, and *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485, the "sole function" of a reviewing court when statutory language is plain is to enforce it—not to weigh policy trade-offs Congress itself resolved. Because Section 1407(a) required remand of this action to the Northern District of Illinois "at or before" the conclusion of pretrial proceedings, and because Section 1404(a) supplies no authority to override that specific command, the judgment below should be reversed.
