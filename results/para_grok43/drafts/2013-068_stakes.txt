The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.

The Act constitutes a valid time, place, and manner regulation of speech in a traditional public forum. See Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (holding that such regulations are permissible if “they are justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech, . . . narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and . . . leave open ample alternative channels for communication”). The statute is content-neutral on its face. It “applies to all speech irrespective of content” and “does not make distinctions based on the content of the speech.” Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 719, 723 (2000). Exemptions for clinic employees and certain passersby do not render the law content-based, as they “are based on the nature of the speech, not its content.” McGuire v. Reilly, 386 F.3d 45, 57 (1st Cir. 2004).

The Act serves significant governmental interests in “protecting a woman’s freedom to seek pregnancy-related services” and “ensuring public safety and order.” Hill, 530 U.S. at 715. These interests are “unquestionably legitimate and important.” Id. at 728. The 35-foot fixed buffer zones are narrowly tailored because they “respond[] precisely to the substantive problem” of obstruction and harassment at clinic entrances without “significantly restricting a substantial quantity of speech that does not create the same evils.” Id. at 729, 730 (internal quotation marks omitted). The zones are fixed rather than floating, smaller than the 100-foot zone upheld in Hill, and permit speech immediately outside their boundaries.

Ample alternative channels remain available. Petitioners may “protest, educate, and distribute literature” from the perimeter of the zones, “display signs,” and “speak at a normal conversational distance” to patients who approach on foot. Hill, 530 U.S. at 729–30. The record confirms that these methods have permitted continued communication, including successful sidewalk counseling at each clinic.

Hill v. Colorado directly controls and should not be overruled. Its reasoning—that buffer zones around healthcare facilities can be content-neutral and narrowly tailored—remains sound and has not been undermined by subsequent decisions addressing unrelated contexts. See, e.g., Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc., 564 U.S. 552, 566–67 (2011) (distinguishing commercial-speech restrictions). Stare decisis considerations counsel against disturbing a precedent that has guided lower courts for more than a decade.

The law-of-the-case doctrine further bars relitigation of the facial validity already upheld in McCullen v. Coakley, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir. 2009). No intervening change in controlling authority justifies departure. See United States v. Matthews, 643 F.3d 9, 14 (1st Cir. 2011).

The First Circuit correctly sustained the Act as applied.
