**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Smoot's RLA Conspiracy Claim Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Remain as to Whether UTU and CSX Breached the Duty of Fair Representation.**

The Railway Labor Act imposes on the union a fiduciary-like obligation to represent all members of the craft "without hostile discrimination" and in good faith. *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co.*, 323 U.S. 192, 202 (1944); *see also Tunstall v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen & Enginemen*, 323 U.S. 210 (1944). Smoot's evidence—that Elswick removed himself only after being overruled by the Board of Appeals, that Earley was installed as his advocate and then disciplined for allegedly favoring Smoot's position at the very executive session from which Smoot was excluded, and that CSX's own representative sat on the panel that decided against him—raises a genuine question whether UTU and CSX acted in concert to defeat his claim rather than to adjudicate it fairly.

Summary judgment was particularly inappropriate given the nature of the claim. This Court's predecessor authority has long cautioned that "summary procedures should be used sparingly" in cases "where motive and intent play leading roles, the proof is largely in the hands of the alleged conspirators, and hostile witnesses thicken the plot." *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting Sys., Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). A conspiracy claim under the RLA presents precisely such a case: the agreement, if any, existed between Elswick, Earley, Emerick, and Cluster, all of whom controlled the very executive session evidence at issue. On summary judgment, "the inferences to be drawn from the underlying facts . . . must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion." *United States v. Diebold, Inc.*, 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962) (per curiam). The district court instead drew every inference against Smoot, resolving disputed questions of credibility and motive that should have gone to a jury. *See Sartor v. Arkansas Nat. Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627–28 (1944) (summary judgment is unwarranted where the evidence turns on the credibility of interested witnesses).

Moreover, a civil conspiracy "may be established by circumstantial evidence," and the acts of the alleged conspirators need not be examined in isolation but "as a whole." *Continental Ore Co. v. Union Carbide & Carbon Corp.*, 370 U.S. 690, 699 (1962). Viewed together, Elswick's initial denial of the claim, his abrupt withdrawal only after reversal by the Board of Appeals, and the subsequent disciplining of Earley for departing from "the official position of the UTU" during the very session Smoot was locked out of, permit a reasonable inference of coordinated action between UTU and CSX to deprive Smoot of his entitlement. The district court's contrary conclusion should be reversed.

**II. The District Court Erred in Awarding Punitive Damages Because There Is No Evidence of Malicious or Wanton Conduct.**

Punitive damages have never been available for simple, non-malicious wrongdoing. As the Supreme Court explained more than a century ago, punitive damages are reserved for cases where "the injury complained of has been inflicted maliciously or wantonly, and with circumstances of contumely or indignity." *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851). Smoot's recorders were left, openly and without concealment, in a room he had lawfully occupied moments before; he did not plant a device, tap a line, or otherwise engage in the clandestine conduct that Congress and the courts have treated as the paradigmatic wiretapping evil. *Cf. Berger v. New York*, 388 U.S. 41, 63 (1967) (electronic surveillance statutes address the danger of "indiscriminate use of such devices" through stealth). Nor did Smoot ever attempt to conceal the existence of the recording; he disclosed it to pursue internal union charges, hardly the conduct of one acting with malice. Where, as here, the defendant's conduct reflects, at most, a mistaken belief about his right to preserve a record of proceedings affecting his own economic interests, an award of punitive damages cannot stand.

**III. The Attorneys' Fees Award Should Be Vacated as Inconsistent With the American Rule.**

Fee-shifting is the exception, not the rule, in American jurisprudence, and statutory fee provisions must be construed with restraint. *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 717 (1967) (courts should not "resort to forfeiture of a fundamental host of unrecoverable litigation expenses" absent clear statutory command). Given that the underlying statutory violation, if any, was neither willful nor malicious, an award of $86,308.74 in fees—layered atop $250,000 in statutory damages and $100,000 in punitive damages—is grossly disproportionate to any culpability shown and should likewise be vacated or, at minimum, substantially reduced on remand.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment on Smoot's RLA conspiracy claim and vacate the awards of punitive damages and attorneys' fees.
