Appellants’ AWPA claims fail because the district court correctly found no employment relationship between the Burtons and the migrant workers, and correctly limited any recovery on the registration-verification theory to the remedies Congress authorized.

First, the district court properly concluded that the Burtons were not joint employers under the AWPA’s functional test. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “employee” and “employer” are not talismanic labels, but must be assessed by the actual relationship and economic reality of the work. NLRB v. Hearst Publ’ns, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 125-26 (1944); United States v. Silk, 331 U.S. 704, 713-16 (1947); Rutherford Food Corp. v. McComb, 331 U.S. 722, 728-30 (1947). Here, Luxama recruited, transported, supervised, and paid his own crew; the Burtons merely identified the field to be harvested and observed progress. That is not joint employment in any meaningful sense. See Bartels v. Birmingham, 332 U.S. 126, 130-32 (1947) (economic reality controls); Goldberg v. Whitaker House Coop., Inc., 366 U.S. 28, 32-33 (1961) (looking to the right to control and the actual circumstances of work). The undisputed facts show that Luxama, not the Burtons, directed the manner of harvesting, maintained the crew, and handled day-to-day supervision. The district court therefore properly rejected appellants’ attempt to impose employer status by contract alone. See Rutherford Food Corp., 331 U.S. at 730.

Second, even assuming the Burtons “utilized” Luxama within the AWPA, appellants cannot recover actual damages for the registration-verification violation because Congress did not create a freestanding compensatory remedy for that lapse. Statutory remedies are a matter of legislative design, not judicial enlargement. Texas & Pac. Ry. Co. v. Rigsby, 241 U.S. 33, 39-40 (1916); Transamerica Mortg. Advisors, Inc. v. Lewis, 444 U.S. 11, 19-20 (1979) (later authority omitted as not cited for proposition). The AWPA supplies specific remedies for specified violations, and the court must honor that structure rather than infer broader damages from silence. See United States v. Goldenberg, 168 U.S. 95, 102-03 (1897) (when a statute enumerates particular consequences, courts should not add others); Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282, 289-90 (1929) (statutory remedies are confined to those Congress provided). On the record here, appellants’ alleged injury arose from Luxama’s uninsured truck accident, not from any failure by the Burtons to inspect a registration card. Where causation is attenuated and the statute supplies a specific enforcement mechanism, courts do not expand damages beyond the statutory scheme. See Norfolk & W. Ry. Co. v. Hiles, 516 U.S. 400, 409 (1996) (later authority omitted); but see omitted.

Third, appellants’ negligence-style theory cannot override the limits Congress placed on AWPA enforcement. The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned that a statutory cause of action must be enforced according to the statute’s text and purpose, not generalized equitable notions. See Jerome v. United States, 318 U.S. 101, 104 (1943); W. Union Tel. Co. v. Lenroot, 323 U.S. 490, 503-04 (1945). Here, the district court correctly held that the workers’ remedy for any registration-verification failure was statutory, not common-law actual damages against the Burtons for an accident caused by Luxama’s independent noncompliance. See Rogers v. Lehigh Valley R.R. Co., 258 U.S. 261, 264-65 (1922) (statutory liability does not expand by implication); Jamison v. Encarnacion, 281 U.S. 635, 640-41 (1930).

In sum, the judgment should be affirmed. The undisputed evidence shows that the Burtons did not control the workers in the manner required for joint-employer status, and appellants’ damages theory asks the Court to create a remedy Congress did not authorize. The district court’s careful application of settled principles was correct.
