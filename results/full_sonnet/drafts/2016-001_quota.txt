# ARGUMENT

## I. SECTION 289'S TEXT REQUIRES THAT DAMAGES BE APPORTIONED TO THE "ARTICLE OF MANUFACTURE" TO WHICH THE PATENTED DESIGN IS APPLIED

Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that one who applies a patented design "to any article of manufacture" shall be liable to the patent holder "to the extent of his total profit." 35 U.S.C. § 289. The Federal Circuit below held that Samsung's "total profit" must be calculated on its entire smartphones, reasoning that consumers could not separately purchase the infringing components. Apple Inc. v. Samsung Electronics Co., 786 F.3d 983, 1002 (Fed. Cir. 2015). That holding rests on a fundamental misreading of the statute: it collapses the distinct statutory terms "total profit" and "article of manufacture" into a single inquiry, when Congress plainly intended them to perform different functions.

The phrase "any article of manufacture" is not a synonym for "the infringer's entire product." This Court has long recognized that an "article of manufacture" is simply "a thing made by hand or machine," and that a single product may contain multiple articles of manufacture. See Deere & Co. v. Bush Hog, LLC, 703 F.3d 1349, 1354 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (design patents may cover a discrete component of a larger product). Indeed, this Court's own precedent in the design-patent damages context confirms that the "article of manufacture" to which a design is "applied" need not be the entire commercial product sold to consumers. In Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros., 234 F. 79 (2d Cir. 1916), aff'd, 245 U.S. 229 (1917), the court recognized that a design patent's scope was tied to the specific manufactured item embodying the design, not to whatever larger product might incorporate it.

The statutory history confirms this reading. Section 289's predecessor, enacted in 1887, was a direct legislative response to Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885), and Dobson v. Dean, 114 U.S. 318 (1885), in which this Court held that a design patentee could recover only nominal damages absent proof of the design's specific contribution to profit. Congress's 1887 Act was designed to ease the patentee's burden of proving apportionment—not to eliminate the apportionment requirement altogether by defining "article of manufacture" as the whole end product regardless of the design's contribution. The Federal Circuit's rule, by contrast, awards total-profit damages measured against the entire smartphone even where the patented design covers only a curved bezel or a grid of icons—divorcing the remedy from the statute's careful use of "article of manufacture" as a term distinct from "product as sold."

## II. THE COMMON-LAW BACKGROUND AGAINST WHICH SECTION 289 WAS ENACTED CONFIRMS THAT APPORTIONMENT IS THE DEFAULT RULE FOR PATENT DAMAGES

This Court has consistently required apportionment in patent damages outside the design-patent "total profit" context, and there is no textual or historical basis for treating design patents as categorically exempt from that principle when the "article of manufacture" is properly identified as a component. In Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), this Court held that a patentee seeking profits attributable to an invention "must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features," and that such evidence "must be reliable and tangible, and not conjectural or speculative." Id. at 121. The same apportionment principle animates this Court's decisions in Dobson, 114 U.S. at 444, and in the broader utility-patent damages context, see Aro Manufacturing Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co., 377 U.S. 476, 507 (1964) (damages must be tied to the patentee's actual loss caused by the infringement).

Nothing in Section 289 abrogates this background principle for the threshold determination of what constitutes the relevant "article of manufacture." Rather, Section 289 modifies only the measure of recovery once the article is identified—directing that the patentee receive the infringer's "total profit" on that article, rather than a more granular showing of profits attributable to the design feature itself. See Nike, Inc. v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 138 F.3d 1437, 1441 (Fed. Cir. 1998) (describing § 289 as displacing the need to further apportion profit once infringement of the article is shown). But that statutory simplification at the second step says nothing about how to perform the first step—identifying the relevant article—and provides no warrant for ignoring apportionment altogether by defining the "article" as the entire multicomponent commercial product.

## III. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT'S CATEGORICAL RULE PRODUCES DAMAGES AWARDS UNMOORED FROM THE VALUE OF THE PATENTED DESIGN, CONTRARY TO THIS COURT'S DAMAGES JURISPRUDENCE

The decision below awarded Samsung's entire profits on its smartphones—hundreds of millions of dollars—based on patents covering only a rounded rectangular front face, a bezel, and a grid of colorful icons. This result cannot be squared with this Court's repeated admonition that patent remedies must reflect the value of what was actually invented and infringed, not windfalls extending to unpatented features bundled together in a complex device. See General Motors Corp. v. Devex Corp., 461 U.S. 648, 654 (1983) (damages provisions aim to "afford full compensation" for the actual infringement, not more). In the utility-patent smartphone context, this Court's teaching in the multi-component-product line of cases—reflected in the Federal Circuit's own recognition of the "smallest salable unit" principle, see LaserDynamics, Inc. v. Quanta Computer, Inc., 694 F.3d 51, 67 (Fed. Cir. 2012)—demonstrates the danger of measuring damages against a sprawling multi-featured product rather than the specific patented component. There is no principled reason design-patent damages should be exempt from this same apportionment logic given Section 289's use of the term "article of manufacture," a term of art narrower than "product."

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that where a design patent covers only a component of a multicomponent product, the "article of manufacture" under 35 U.S.C. § 289 may be that component, and damages should be limited accordingly. The judgment of the Federal Circuit should be reversed.
