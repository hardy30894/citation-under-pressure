**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) borrows state law as federal law only to the extent necessary to fill gaps left by existing federal law. The statute, its history, and this Court’s precedent all confirm that Congress did not intend state law to operate as a freestanding source of rights on the Shelf whenever it is merely topically relevant and not directly contradicted by a federal statute.

**I. The Text and Purpose of OCSLA Establish a Federal, Gap-Filling Regime.**

OCSLA declares that “the Constitution and laws and civil and political jurisdiction of the United States” extend to fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). Only after establishing this comprehensive federal jurisdiction does the Act provide that adjacent-state law is “declared to be the law of the United States” to the extent “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This structure shows that state law is not an independent regulatory regime running alongside federal law—it is *adopted as* federal law, and only where federal law leaves an area unaddressed.

This Court has recognized that OCSLA was designed “to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf.” Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). Congress’s objective was to ensure that some law—not necessarily state law—governed every aspect of activity on the Shelf, because federal law alone “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). State law was borrowed only as a stopgap, to supply rules where none otherwise existed. Treating an artificial island as “an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State,” § 1333(a)(1), necessarily means that federal jurisdiction is primary and state law is subordinate and interstitial—precisely the model this Court applies to ordinary federal enclaves.

**II. Rodrigue Confirms that State Law Applies Only Where Federal Law Would Otherwise Leave a Void.**

In Rodrigue, the Court permitted Louisiana wrongful-death law to apply on a fixed platform only after concluding that the federal Death on the High Seas Act did not reach the accidents at issue because the platforms were not vessels and the deaths did not arise from traditional maritime activity. 395 U.S. at 359–60. The Court explained that because no federal remedy governed, this removed “any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through § 1333(a)(2).” Id. at 366. Rodrigue thus did not hold that state law applies whenever it is not directly contradicted by federal law; it held that state law fills the field only where federal law supplies no answer at all. Had a federal remedy existed, the Court’s analysis makes plain that the federal remedy—not the state one—would have controlled.

Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), which involved a State-borrowed limitations period on the Shelf, likewise proceeds from the premise that once state law is incorporated under § 1333(a)(2)(A), it operates as *federal* law filling an admitted gap, not as an independently enforceable state cause of action running concurrently with a comprehensive federal scheme. See id. at 99–100 (applying Rodrigue’s framework). Nothing in this Court’s decisions suggests that a comprehensive federal statute like the FLSA, which already regulates the precise subject of wages and hours, leaves room for supplemental, more generous state regulation simply because the state law is not technically inconsistent.

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s Longstanding Gap-Filling Test Correctly Implements Congress’s Design.**

The Fifth Circuit—which has the longest and most extensive experience applying OCSLA to the Shelf’s oil and gas platforms—has consistently required a showing of “necessity to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law before state law may be adopted as surrogate federal law. Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). That test respects OCSLA’s statutory design: federal law governs the Shelf comprehensively, and state law serves only the limited, subordinate function of supplying substantive content where federal law is silent. The Ninth Circuit’s newly minted “applicable and not inconsistent” standard, divorced from any gap requirement, effectively converts OCSLA into a preemption statute in which state law presumptively applies unless affirmatively displaced. That approach inverts OCSLA’s structure, under which federal law is the baseline and state law is the exception invoked only in its absence.

This inversion also undermines OCSLA’s core purpose of ensuring uniform, predictable federal regulation of an area Congress placed under exclusive federal jurisdiction. See Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19, 26 (1988) (describing OCSLA as resolving the “dispute … over territorial jurisdiction” between the States and the Federal Government by establishing federal control of the Shelf). Permitting each adjacent State’s wage-and-hour code to operate concurrently with the FLSA whenever not technically inconsistent would produce exactly the patchwork of local regulation Congress sought to avoid by placing the Shelf under federal, not state, dominion.

Because the FLSA comprehensively regulates minimum wages, overtime, and related workplace protections for Shelf workers, and because Congress addressed the interaction between federal and more generous state wage laws through the FLSA’s own savings clause, no gap exists for California law to fill here. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
