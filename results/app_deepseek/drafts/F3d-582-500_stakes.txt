**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Genuine Disputes of Material Fact Exist Regarding Erdman’s FMLA Eligibility.**

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment *de novo*, applying the same standard as the district court. *McTernan v. City of York*, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009). Summary judgment is appropriate only when “the movant shows that there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). All inferences must be drawn in favor of the nonmoving party—here, Erdman. *Id.*

The sole basis for the District Court’s grant of summary judgment on Erdman’s FMLA claims was its determination that she had not worked 1,250 hours in the 12 months preceding her leave request. That determination was premature because it resolved material factual disputes that should have been left to a jury.

**A. The Record Contains Evidence That Erdman Worked Substantial Unrecorded Overtime, Which Must Be Counted Toward Her FMLA Eligibility.**

Under the FMLA, an eligible employee must have “been employed for at least 1,250 hours of service during the previous 12-month period.” 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(ii). The implementing regulations define “hours of service” as “actual hours worked,” including overtime hours, unless the employer properly excluded them under the Fair Labor Standards Act. 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c) (2003). Critically, “hours that an employee works beyond the normal workweek” count toward the 1,250-hour threshold, even if the employer did not formally authorize or record them. *Crenshaw v. DeKalb County*, 198 F.3d 832, 834 (11th Cir. 1999) (holding that “comp time” and unrecorded overtime must be included). This Court has similarly emphasized that the FMLA’s eligibility requirement is calculated based on “the actual hours worked,” not merely those reflected in an employer’s payroll system. *Sommer v. The Vanguard Group*, 461 F.3d 397, 400 n.2 (3d Cir. 2006).

Here, Erdman presented uncontroverted evidence that she regularly worked extra hours outside the office with the knowledge and approval of her supervisor, Patty Sarno. App. 12. Sarno authorized payment for these hours or allowed Erdman to use them as “comp” time. *Id.* Even after Sarno told Erdman to limit her hours, Erdman continued working additional hours, and she e-mailed her new supervisor, Stella Getgen, in September 2002 to clarify whether she could still accrue “comp” time. App. 14. Getgen never responded to that e-mail. *Id.* When Getgen later conceded Erdman’s vacation time calculation was correct, she made no objection to Erdman’s use of “comp” time. App. 15.

Critically, Erdman’s unrebutted testimony and Getgen’s acknowledgment of the “comp” time system create a genuine factual dispute about how many hours Erdman actually worked. The District Court improperly resolved this dispute by accepting Nationwide’s payroll records as dispositive. But payroll records are not conclusive; they merely reflect what the employer chose to record. As the Eleventh Circuit recognized in *Crenshaw*, “the employer cannot avoid counting hours worked simply because it failed to record them.” 198 F.3d at 834. Because Erdman testified that she worked additional hours beyond those recorded, and because Nationwide’s own supervisor never disavowed the “comp” time system until February 10, 2003, a reasonable jury could find that Erdman accumulated the required 1,250 hours.

**B. Nationwide’s Actions May Have Improperly Prevented Erdman from Accruing the Hours Necessary for FMLA Eligibility, Further Precluding Summary Judgment.**

Even assuming arguendo that Erdman’s recorded hours fell short of 1,250, the FMLA’s implementing regulations prohibit an employer from “taking any action to avoid the responsibilities under the Act,” including “manipulating the employee’s hours” to prevent eligibility. 29 C.F.R. § 825.220(b) (2003). This Court has recognized that an employer cannot “interfere with, restrain, or deny the exercise of” FMLA rights. *Callison v. City of Philadelphia*, 430 F.3d 117, 119 (3d Cir. 2005) (quoting 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(1)).

The record suggests that Nationwide took precisely such actions. Within weeks of Erdman’s January 2003 meeting about “comp” time, Getgen admonished her for unapproved overtime and then, on February 10, 2003, abruptly prohibited any further use of “comp” time. App. 15-16. Shortly thereafter, Nationwide eliminated Erdman’s part-time position, forcing her to accept full-time status—a change that disrupted her ability to care for her disabled daughter. App. 17. When Erdman then sought clarification about her August vacation—and indicated she would request FMLA leave if vacation were denied—Nationwide fired her on May 9, 2003, just weeks after she submitted her FMLA paperwork. App. 18.

A reasonable jury could infer that Nationwide’s sudden restrictions on overtime, the elimination of her part-time role, and her subsequent termination were all designed to prevent Erdman from accumulating the hours needed for FMLA leave—or to retaliate against her for seeking it. Because material facts remain in dispute on both the hours-worked question and the issue of interference, summary judgment was improper.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the District Court’s grant of summary judgment on Erdman’s FMLA claims and remand for further proceedings.
