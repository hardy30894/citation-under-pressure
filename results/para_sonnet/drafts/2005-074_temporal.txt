**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered the United States Before Its 1997 Effective Date**

This Court has long adhered to the rule that a statute will not be read to reach backward and attach new consequences to conduct already completed unless Congress has made that purpose unmistakably clear. In *United States v. Heth*, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399 (1806), the Court refused to apply a later statute reducing customs commissions to commissions already earned, reasoning that legislation is presumed to speak only to the future absent a clear direction otherwise. The Court reaffirmed that presumption in *Sohn v. Waterson*, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 596 (1873), holding that a statute of limitations would not be applied to revive or extinguish rights accrued before its passage without unequivocal statutory language, and again in *William Danzer & Co. v. Gulf & Ship Island R.R. Co.*, 268 U.S. 633 (1925), where the Court declined to give retroactive effect to an amendment absent "explicit words or necessary implication." The rule was crystallized in *Union Pacific R.R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913): a statute affecting antecedent rights will not be applied retroactively "unless such be the unequivocal and inflexible import of the terms, and the manifest intention of the legislature."

This presumption applies with full force to immigration legislation. In *Chew Heong v. United States*, 112 U.S. 536 (1884), the Court refused to construe the Chinese exclusion laws to bar the reentry of an alien whose departure predated the statute's requirements, holding that Congress must speak plainly before a court will read an immigration statute to disturb expectations formed under the prior law. Section 241(a)(5) contains no comparable statement addressing reentries that occurred, as Fernandez-Vargas's did, fifteen years before the statute existed. Nothing in its text or structure evinces the "unequivocal and inflexible" congressional purpose that *Union Pacific* and *Chew Heong* require before a court may reach back to conduct completed under an entirely different legal regime.

Any remaining doubt must be resolved in petitioner's favor under the settled rule that deportation statutes are construed narrowly against the government. In *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948), this Court explained that "we resolve the doubts in favor of that construction because deportation is a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile." That interpretive rule, applied to a statute that strips an alien of every avenue of relief and revives a decades-old order without further hearing, compels the conclusion that Congress did not intend Section 241(a)(5) to reach reentries predating its enactment.

**II. Even If the Reinstatement Statute Applies to Pre-Enactment Reentries, Its Application to Petitioner Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

Should the statute be read to reach petitioner's 1982 reentry, that application impermissibly attaches a new and severe disability to conduct already completed and to rights already accruing under the law as it then stood. The general principle that vested rights and settled expectations may not be casually undone by subsequent legislation is longstanding. In *Ochoa v. Hernandez y Morales*, 230 U.S. 139 (1913), this Court examined the limits that due process places on legislative interference with rights that have already attached under prior law, recognizing that a legislature's power to alter the legal landscape going forward does not extend to nullifying rights already fixed. Fernandez-Vargas reentered in 1982, married a United States citizen, and initiated adjustment-of-status proceedings—all before Section 241(a)(5) existed and while the law permitted precisely the relief he sought. Only by operation of a statute enacted in 1997 was that avenue closed and the 1981 order revived without any new hearing.

This Court has also insisted that aliens be afforded genuine process before being stripped of a status or claim they have asserted. In *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276 (1922), the Court held that an alien asserting a claim to a protected status could not be summarily deported without an opportunity to have that claim heard, explaining that deportation proceedings touching such interests demand more than administrative fiat. So too here: petitioner's pending application for adjustment, filed in reliance on the law as it existed when he reentered and as it existed when he married, was extinguished not through adjudication of its merits but through the mechanical reinstatement of a twenty-year-old order.

The Tenth Circuit's suggestion that petitioner had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" cannot be squared with these principles. The relevant expectation is not merely that adjustment would ultimately be granted, but that his conduct—reentry, marriage, and pursuit of lawful status—would be judged under the law in force when it occurred, consistent with *Heth*, *Sohn v. Waterson*, and *Danzer*. Because Section 241(a)(5), so applied, reaches back to erase both the process and the substantive avenue of relief available to petitioner at the time he acted, its application here is impermissibly retroactive, and the judgment below should be reversed.
