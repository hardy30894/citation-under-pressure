ARGUMENT

I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE PRECLUDES RETRIAL OF A PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION ONCE A COURT HAS FINALLY DETERMINED THAT THE EVIDENCE IS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT IT.

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment is binding on the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The Clause protects against both a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal and multiple punishments for the same offense. *Id.* A prior conviction allegation, when proved, exposes a defendant to a doubled sentence and an additional one-year enhancement. It is a fact that authorizes increased punishment. When the State has failed to prove that allegation beyond a reasonable doubt, and an appellate court has finally reversed the true finding for insufficient evidence, that determination is the functional equivalent of an acquittal. The State may not relitigate that fact without exposing the defendant to double jeopardy.

This Court has long held that once a defendant has been acquitted, the Double Jeopardy Clause absolutely bars any further prosecution for the same offense. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 669 (1896) (the accused, whether convicted or acquitted, has been placed in jeopardy, and an acquittal is final). In *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 134 (1904), the Court held that the government may not appeal an acquittal to obtain another trial on the same charge. The same reasoning governs here. An appellate court’s reversal for insufficiency of the evidence is an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes because it is a final determination that the State failed to satisfy its burden. *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671. Just as the State may not retry a defendant on a substantive offense after such a ruling, it may not retry a sentencing allegation after the same determination.

The Double Jeopardy Clause likewise protects a defendant from repeated attempts to obtain a harsher sentence based on the same alleged facts. *In re Nielsen*, 131 U.S. 176, 187-188 (1889) (the Clause is not confined to cases affecting life or limb, but extends to all cases where a second punishment is attempted for the same offense). The principle is one of finality. *United States v. Perez*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 579, 580 (1824) (recognizing the interest of the accused in being protected from repeated prosecutions for the same alleged offense). If the State could retry a prior conviction allegation after an insufficiency finding, the defendant would face the risk of multiple prosecutions for the same factual issue, and ultimately the risk of a sentence enhanced on the basis of facts that one court already found unproved. That is precisely the evil the Clause forbids.

The California Supreme Court erred in holding that double jeopardy protections do not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Clause draws no such line. *Nielsen*, 131 U.S. at 187. In *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 188 (1957), the Court explained that the Double Jeopardy Clause forbids a second trial for the same offense, whether the first trial ended in an acquittal or a conviction. In *Coffey v. United States*, 116 U.S. 436, 443 (1886), the Court held that an acquittal on a criminal charge bars a subsequent attempt to impose punishment based on the same conduct. Those cases rest on the fundamental principle that the State should not be permitted to make repeated attempts to convict or to punish. *See United States v. Sanges*, 144 U.S. 310, 320 (1892) (the government may not appeal from a judgment for the defendant in a criminal case absent express statutory authority).

California has chosen to provide a trial-like proceeding for prior conviction allegations. It grants a right to a jury trial, requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and permits confrontation of witnesses. Those procedural protections make the determination of a prior conviction allegation resemble a trial on guilt or innocence. When a State provides such a proceeding, it must accept the consequences of an adverse determination. The State cannot label a proceeding "sentencing" and thereby escape the Double Jeopardy Clause after it has lost. *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 134.

Petitioner Monge was subjected to a full proceeding on the prior conviction allegation. The trial court found the allegation true, but the Court of Appeal reversed, holding that the evidence was insufficient to prove that Monge personally used a deadly weapon—a necessary element of the serious felony enhancement. That reversal was a final judgment of acquittal on the enhancement issue. The State may not retry that allegation. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars the door.

The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
