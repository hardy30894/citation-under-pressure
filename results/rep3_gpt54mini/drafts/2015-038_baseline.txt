**Argument**

ANILCA does not strip the National Park Service of authority to regulate non-federal lands and waters merely because they lie within the exterior boundaries of a conservation system unit. The statute’s text, structure, and history show that § 103(c) draws a narrow jurisdictional line: Alaska lands and waters that are not federal “public lands” remain outside the special federal management regime created by ANILCA, but they are not placed beyond generally applicable federal regulation. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the hovercraft ban may be enforced against Sturgeon on the Nation River.

1. **Section 103(c) limits ANILCA’s own “public lands” provisions; it does not categorically bar NPS regulation of inholdings and waters.**

ANILCA § 103(c) provides that, subject to specific exceptions, “only the public lands within such units are deemed to be included as part of the National Park System.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). That language does not say that non-federal lands within park boundaries become regulatory black holes. Rather, it identifies what counts as part of the National Park System for purposes of ANILCA’s special land-management rules. As the Ninth Circuit recognized, a regulation of general applicability is not transformed into a prohibited regulation of “public lands” simply because it has incidental application on state-owned waters within a park boundary.

That reading is consistent with the ordinary meaning of “public lands” in ANILCA. Congress used that term to refer to federally owned lands, not to all lands located within the exterior boundaries of a unit. See Alaska v. Babbitt, 72 F.3d 698, 701-03 (9th Cir. 1995). Section 103(c) therefore limits the scope of lands “deemed to be included” in the park system; it does not immunize state, Native, corporate, or private property from federal law. See Sturgeon v. Frost, 136 S. Ct. 1061, 1068-69 (2016) (explaining that § 103(c) does not clearly foreclose all federal regulation of non-federal inholdings).

2. **NPS’s hovercraft ban is a law of general applicability, not a regulation targeted only at state lands.**

The challenged regulation bars hovercraft use on “NPS-administered lands and waters.” 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e). It applies across the National Park System and does not single out Alaska’s non-federal lands. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that agencies may adopt generally applicable rules governing conduct within federal enclaves and system units so long as Congress has delegated such authority. See Alaska v. Babbitt, 72 F.3d at 703-05. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the ban applies to Sturgeon because the Nation River segment at issue lies within the Yukon-Charley unit and because the regulation is not limited to “public lands” in the ANILCA sense. Sturgeon v. Frost, 136 S. Ct. at 1069-70.

Sturgeon’s contrary reading would create an anomalous regime in which Alaska inholdings and waters inside national park boundaries would be exempt from many generally applicable safety, resource-protection, and access regulations, even when Congress said only that such lands are not “deemed to be included” in the system. Nothing in the statute supports that sweeping result. Courts “must give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute.” Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 362, 404 (2000). Sturgeon’s interpretation would erase the distinction between ownership and location that Congress preserved.

3. **The statute’s structure and related provisions confirm that Congress did not bar all federal regulation.**

ANILCA elsewhere preserves existing federal authority. Section 1319 states that “[n]othing in this Act shall be construed as … superseding, modifying, or repealing” existing laws authorizing federal agencies to exercise regulatory functions. 16 U.S.C. § 3207. That savings clause cuts directly against Sturgeon’s position. Congress knew how to cabin federal power when it wanted to, and it did not broadly divest agencies of general regulatory authority over non-federal lands merely because they sit inside a park boundary. See Sturgeon v. Frost, 136 S. Ct. at 1069-70.

The legislative history points the same way. Congress focused § 103(c) on the special treatment of federal “public lands” within Alaska conservation units, not on creating unregulated pockets of state jurisdiction inside park boundaries. See H.R. Rep. No. 94-1569, at 24 (1976), reprinted in 1976 U.S.C.C.A.N. 4290, 4310. The Ninth Circuit’s construction thus harmonizes the statute’s text with its overall design. See Sturgeon v. Frost, 136 S. Ct. at 1070-71.

4. **At minimum, Sturgeon cannot obtain the sweeping declaration he seeks.**

Sturgeon asks for a rule that NPS lacks regulatory authority over any state, Native, corporate, or private land physically inside park boundaries. That proposition is far broader than § 103(c) or any controlling case permits. The proper question is whether the challenged regulation, as applied here, is barred by ANILCA. The answer is no. The hovercraft prohibition is a generally applicable NPS regulation validly enforced within the Yukon-Charley unit, even as applied to the Nation River segment over which Alaska asserts ownership. See Sturgeon v. Frost, 136 S. Ct. at 1068-71.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
