ARGUMENT

I. The Bankruptcy Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Determine Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs

The bankruptcy court's determination of post-petition interest and collection costs was improper because it lacked jurisdiction to do so. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1334, a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is limited to matters that are "related to" the bankruptcy case. See Celotex Corp. v. Edwards, 514 U.S. 300, 308 (1995) (holding that "related to" jurisdiction is not unlimited and requires a "close nexus" between the proceeding and the bankruptcy case). The Supreme Court has further clarified that a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is limited to "matters that affect the administration of the estate or the interpretation or enforcement of the debtor's plan." See In re Seven Fields Dev. Corp., 505 F.3d 237, 265 (3d Cir. 2007) (citing In re Resorts Int'l, Inc., 372 F.3d 154, 167 (3d Cir. 2004)).

In this case, the bankruptcy estate was closed, and Kirkland was discharged in February 2006. The dispute over post-petition interest and collection costs arose after the bankruptcy case was closed, and therefore, it is not "related to" the bankruptcy case. See In re Pegasus Gold Corp., 394 F.3d 1189, 1193-94 (9th Cir. 2005) (holding that a bankruptcy court lacks jurisdiction over a dispute that arises after the bankruptcy case is closed). The bankruptcy court's determination of post-petition interest and collection costs was thus an overreach of its jurisdiction.

Furthermore, the bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is also limited by the "core" or "non-core" distinction under 28 U.S.C. § 157. See Stern v. Marshall, 131 S. Ct. 2594, 2603-04 (2011) (holding that a bankruptcy court's authority to enter final judgments is limited to "core" proceedings). The determination of post-petition interest and collection costs is not a "core" proceeding, as it does not involve the administration of the estate or the interpretation of the debtor's plan. See In re Exide Techs., 544 F.3d 196, 206 (3d Cir. 2008) (holding that a proceeding is "core" if it "invokes a substantive right provided by title 11 or if it is a proceeding that, by its nature, could arise only in the context of a bankruptcy case").

II. The Bankruptcy Court Erred in Failing to Award ECMC Collection Costs

The bankruptcy court erred in failing to award ECMC collection costs. Under 20 U.S.C. § 1091a(b)(1), a guaranty agency is entitled to recover collection costs from a debtor who defaults on a student loan. See United States v. Phillips, 17 F.3d 1225, 1227-28 (9th Cir. 1994) (holding that a guaranty agency is entitled to recover collection costs under § 1091a(b)(1)). ECMC provided documentation of the collection costs it incurred, and Kirkland did not dispute the amount. The bankruptcy court's failure to award ECMC collection costs was therefore an abuse of discretion. See In re R & G Properties, Inc., 79 F.3d 163, 166 (1st Cir. 1996) (holding that a bankruptcy court's failure to award costs and fees under a statutory provision is reviewed for abuse of discretion).

In conclusion, the bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to determine post-petition interest and collection costs, and erred in failing to award ECMC collection costs. This Court should reverse the bankruptcy court's determination and remand for further proceedings.
