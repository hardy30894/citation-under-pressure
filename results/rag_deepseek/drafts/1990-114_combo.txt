**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Prohibit Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement of State Judges**

Missouri’s constitutional provision requiring state judges to retire at age 70 is a traditional exercise of state authority over its judiciary. This Court has long recognized that “[t]he judicial power of a State” is a matter of state concern, and that the Constitution “does not interfere with the power of a State to distribute the business of its judicial department.” *Dreyer v. Illinois*, 187 U.S. 71, 84 (1902). The people of Missouri possess the sovereign right to define the qualifications and tenure of their judges, including mandatory retirement. *See Duncan v. Missouri*, 152 U.S. 377, 382 (1894) (upholding state constitutional amendment altering judicial jurisdiction). Congress must therefore speak with unmistakable clarity before displacing such a fundamental state interest. *Railroad Retirement Bd. v. Alton R.R. Co.*, 295 U.S. 330, 346–47 (1935) (federal power is limited; “the Constitution is not a statute” and Congress cannot exceed enumerated grants).

The ADEA does not clearly cover appointed state judges. The Act’s definition of “employee” expressly excludes “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Judges are quintessential policymakers. In deciding cases, they “necessarily will resolve issues previously unsettled and thus will create law.” *See Booth v. United States*, 291 U.S. 339, 350 (1934) (judicial office continues after retirement; judges exercise continuing judicial power). The Missouri Supreme Court has established common law for the entire State, a core policymaking function. *See Hannum v. United States*, 226 U.S. 436, 440 (1913) (recognizing different classes of judicial officers based on retirement status). Judges also exercise supervisory and administrative policymaking over the court system, further demonstrating their role as “appointees on the policymaking level.” *Dreyer*, 187 U.S. at 84.

The alternative reading—that the policymaking-level exception applies only to executive or legislative appointees—would leave appointed judges entirely without an ADEA exemption, a result Congress could not have intended. *Duncan*, 152 U.S. at 382 (state constitutional amendments are entitled to respectful interpretation). Because the ADEA does not clearly preempt Missouri’s constitutional retirement provision, the district court properly dismissed the statutory claim.

**II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate Equal Protection**

Age classifications are subject only to rational-basis review. This Court has repeatedly upheld mandatory retirement ages for state employees, reasoning that such laws are permissible where they serve legitimate state interests. *Dodge v. Bd. of Educ. of Chicago*, 302 U.S. 74, 78 (1937) (upholding compulsory retirement of teachers at age 65; “the legislature may determine . . . the age at which public employees should be retired”). There is no fundamental right to public employment. *Layton v. Missouri*, 187 U.S. 356, 361 (1902) (state courts’ jurisdiction over removal of judges is a matter of state law).

Missouri’s mandatory retirement at 70 is rationally related to several legitimate state interests. It ensures that judges retire before age may impair their faculties, reduces the need for costly and humiliating removal proceedings, promotes turnover infusing the judiciary with fresh perspectives, and increases opportunities for women and minorities to serve. *Dodge*, 302 U.S. at 78 (recognizing administrative convenience as a rational basis). The state’s interest in a competent and respected judiciary is unquestionably legitimate. *Ohio ex rel. Bryant v. Akron Metro. Park Dist.*, 281 U.S. 74, 80 (1930) (states have “wide discretion” in structuring their governmental institutions). The fact that some judges remain fully competent after 70 does not render the classification irrational. *Finch & Co. v. McKittrick*, 305 U.S. 395, 399 (1939) (a classification is not unconstitutional simply because it is not “scientific or logical” so long as it rests on a “reasonable basis”).

Petitioners’ due process claim fares no better. They assert no fundamental right to serve as a judge beyond age 70. *Dodge*, 302 U.S. at 78. Because mandatory retirement at 70 is rationally related to legitimate state objectives, it comports with the Fourteenth Amendment.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
