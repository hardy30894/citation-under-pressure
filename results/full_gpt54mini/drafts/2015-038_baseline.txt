**Argument**

I. **ANILCA Does Not Withdraw the National Park Service’s Ordinary Regulatory Authority Over Non-Federal Lands and Waters Within Alaska Park Units**

Sturgeon’s claim turns on the premise that Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (“ANILCA”) categorically bars the National Park Service (“NPS”) from regulating any state, Native, corporate, or private lands or waters that happen to lie within the exterior boundaries of a national park unit in Alaska. That reading cannot be squared with the statute’s text, structure, or this Court’s precedent.

ANILCA § 103(c) provides that “only those lands within any conservation system unit which are owned by the United States” are deemed part of the “public lands” for purposes of federal land management, and that “non-public lands” within those units “shall not be deemed to be included” within the unit “for the purposes of the laws enacted after” ANILCA, “except as may be otherwise provided by this Act.” 16 U.S.C. § 3102(3), (4). But the question here is not whether nonfederal lands are “public lands.” The question is whether a regulation that applies to conduct occurring on waters and lands inside a park unit, and that operates only because of the location of that conduct within the unit, is impermissibly directed at “public lands” alone. It is not.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that NPS’s hovercraft ban is not a regulation “that applies solely to public lands within CSUs in Alaska.” *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 577 U.S. 424, 430 (2016) (summarizing the Ninth Circuit’s holding). The Park Service’s general regulatory authority in national park units is broad. Under the National Park Service Organic Act, the Secretary is charged to “promote and regulate the use of the Federal areas known as national parks.” 16 U.S.C. § 1. The Supreme Court has recognized that the Organic Act is a sweeping grant of authority to protect park resources. *United States v. New Mexico*, 438 U.S. 696, 708 (1978). Nothing in ANILCA repeals that baseline authority by implication. Repeals by implication are disfavored. *Posadas v. National City Bank*, 296 U.S. 497, 503 (1936).

Indeed, ANILCA itself confirms that Congress preserved preexisting federal authority unless expressly displaced. Section 1319 states that “[n]othing in this Act shall be construed as … superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to … exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. That savings clause is fatal to Sturgeon’s broad theory. See *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 577 U.S. at 438–39 (explaining that ANILCA “leaves intact” other federal authority unless specifically altered).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in the earlier “Katie John” litigation confirms the same point. In *State of Alaska v. Babbitt*, 72 F.3d 698 (9th Cir. 1995), *John v. United States*, 247 F.3d 1032 (9th Cir. 2001) (en banc) (per curiam), and *John v. United States*, 720 F.3d 1214 (9th Cir. 2013), the court addressed ANILCA’s treatment of navigable waters and federal subsistence regulation. Those cases did not read § 103(c) as a categorical ban on all federal regulation touching nonfederal lands or waters within Alaska park units. To the contrary, they reflect a careful distinction between title to land and the scope of federal regulatory power.

Sturgeon’s position also conflicts with settled administrative law. Agency inconsistency does not defeat Chevron review. *National Cable & Telecommunications Ass’n v. Brand X Internet Services*, 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005). Even if NPS’s views changed over time, that does not alter the meaning Congress enacted. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843–44 (1984).

Finally, the practical consequences of Sturgeon’s theory underscore its error. A rule limiting hovercraft use, firearms discharge, or commercial activity only because such conduct occurs within a park unit is not a regulation of “non-public lands” as such; it is regulation of conduct within a federal conservation system unit. Congress knew how to forbid such regulation when it wished to do so, but it did not do so here. See *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 577 U.S. at 441–42.

II. **The Ninth Circuit’s Judgment Should Be Affirmed**

This Court should affirm because ANILCA’s text, read as a whole, does not deprive NPS of authority to enforce generally applicable park regulations within Alaska park units merely because the regulated conduct occurs over state-owned waters or other nonfederal lands. The decision below is consistent with the statute’s savings clause, with the Organic Act, and with this Court’s instruction that courts should not infer sweeping repeals of federal authority absent clear congressional command. *Posadas*, 296 U.S. at 503; *United States v. New Mexico*, 438 U.S. at 708.

At minimum, Sturgeon’s reading cannot prevail where the statute is reasonably read to preserve federal regulation of conduct in park units while limiting only the classification of nonfederal lands as “public lands.” *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 577 U.S. at 438–42. Because that is the better reading, and the only reading consistent with ANILCA’s structure, the judgment should be affirmed.
