# ARGUMENT

## I. RESPONDENT HAS NO LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST PROTECTED BY DUE PROCESS IN OHIO'S CLEMENCY PROCEDURE

Executive clemency is an act of grace lying wholly outside the judicial function. This Court has long recognized that "[a] pardon is an act of grace." United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833). The power to commute a sentence or grant a pardon has traditionally resided in the unfettered discretion of the executive, and no person under sentence has ever possessed a cognizable right to receive that grace.

This principle finds firm support in Biddle v. Perovich, 274 U.S. 480 (1927), where the Court explained that a commutation is "the exercise of the executive discretion" and does not depend upon the consent of the condemned man. Id. at 486. If clemency may be granted without the prisoner's consent, it follows a fortiori that the prisoner possesses no entitlement to any particular procedure by which it is granted or withheld.

Respondent cannot manufacture a liberty interest simply because Ohio has established procedural mechanisms surrounding its clemency process. This Court's decision in Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), teaches that due process protections attach to deprivations of interests the Constitution or laws independently secure—not to every procedural framework a State chooses to erect. The mere existence of state regulations governing the mechanics of clemency review does not transform an act of executive grace into an entitlement. As Justice Holmes observed in a related context, "the Constitution does not require order without protection." Buck v. Bell, 274 U.S. 200, 208 (1927) (analogously recognizing broad executive and state discretion in matters affecting life itself).

Moreover, this Court has consistently distinguished between the trial and appellate process—where due process safeguards attach because the State has committed itself to an adjudicatory function—and post-conviction clemency, which serves an entirely different constitutional role. In Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. 87 (1925), Chief Justice Taft explained that the pardon power exists precisely to correct the harshness that can result from "the ordinary operation of the courts," id. at 120, and functions as a check *outside* the judicial system, not an extension of it. Clemency is not part of "the machinery of the [judicial] process," Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. at 121, but a wholly separate constitutional safety valve.

The Sixth Circuit's reasoning—that because Ohio's clemency procedure occurs in "distance" from trial it nonetheless merits "minimal" due process protection—finds no footing in this Court's precedent. In Fahy v. Connecticut, 375 U.S. 85 (1963), this Court emphasized that due process analysis is tethered to specific deprivations occasioned by identifiable governmental action within the adjudicative process; it was never extended to discretionary grace extended after that process has concluded. To hold otherwise would, as this Court warned in a related setting, invite unwarranted "intrusion into the process" reserved to another branch. See Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455, 462 (1942) (cautioning against imposing rigid constitutional procedural requirements onto matters historically left to case-by-case discretion).

## II. THE VOLUNTARY INTERVIEW OPTION DOES NOT VIOLATE RESPONDENT'S FIFTH AMENDMENT RIGHTS

The Fifth Amendment's protection against compelled self-incrimination applies only where the government compels a person to speak. See Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 597 (1896) (the privilege protects a witness from being "compelled to be a witness against himself"). Ohio's clemency interview is, by its own terms, voluntary: Respondent may request it or decline it, and having requested it, remains free to answer as much or as little as he wishes.

This Court's decision in Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), confirms that the privilege is concerned with compulsion, not with the mere presentation of an opportunity to speak that carries some incidental risk. Where, as here, an inmate voluntarily elects to participate in a beneficial proceeding, no compulsion within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment can be said to exist. The choice to seek the possibility of clemency by speaking to the parole board is precisely the sort of choice that this Court has held does not rise to constitutional compulsion. See Adamson v. California, 332 U.S. 46, 54 (1947) (privilege against self-incrimination protects against compelled testimony, not against the consequences of an accused's own voluntary choices).

Nor can the "unconstitutional conditions" doctrine rescue Respondent's claim. That doctrine, articulated in cases such as Frost & Frost Trucking Co. v. Railroad Commission, 271 U.S. 583 (1926), applies where the State conditions a benefit on the surrender of an independently existing constitutional right. But Respondent has no independent liberty interest in clemency, see Part I, supra; the interview is an additional voluntary opportunity layered atop an already-discretionary process. There is no "condition" imposed at all—Respondent may seek clemency through counsel, through affidavits, and through any other means without ever setting foot in an interview room. The option to speak directly to the parole board is an added avenue for relief, not a Hobson's choice extracting a constitutional sacrifice.

This Court's reasoning in Ullmann v. United States, 350 U.S. 422 (1956), further demonstrates that the privilege is not offended by procedures that merely create incidental, speculative risks of future incrimination; some concrete compulsion must be shown. Respondent's own complaint disclaims any concern that this proceeding will affect his existing convictions, rendering any Fifth Amendment injury wholly speculative and premature—precisely the sort of premature constitutional claim this Court has consistently declined to entertain in analogous contexts. See Poe v. Ullman, 367 U.S. 497, 508 (1961) (courts should not adjudicate constitutional questions resting on speculative, hypothetical future harm).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
