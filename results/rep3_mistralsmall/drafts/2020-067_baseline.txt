**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Limited Its Review to the Trial Record When Assessing Plain Error**

A federal appellate court reviewing for plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) must confine its prejudice inquiry to the trial record. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 733–34 (1993) (explaining that plain-error review requires consideration of “the entire record” but that record is the one developed at trial). The court may not rely on post-trial or appellate information—such as a defendant’s prior convictions or subsequent conduct—to manufacture prejudice where none appears on the face of the record. *Id.* at 734 (“[T]he error must be prejudicial: It must have affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.”); *United States v. Vazquez*, 918 F.3d 1192, 1200 (11th Cir. 2019) (citing *Olano* and emphasizing that prejudice is assessed based on the trial record).

Here, the Eleventh Circuit correctly confined its prejudice analysis to the evidence presented to the jury and the trial record. It did not consider Greer’s prior convictions or post-conviction conduct to establish prejudice. Instead, it examined whether the government proved, and the jury was instructed to find, that Greer knew he was a felon when he possessed the firearm. *See Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191, 2195 (2019) (holding that § 922(g) requires proof of both possession and knowledge of status). The court’s conclusion that no prejudice occurred was grounded in the absence of such proof in the record. The Eleventh Circuit did not err by refusing to expand its inquiry beyond the trial record.

**II. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Err in Concluding That Greer Failed to Show Prejudice Under Rule 52(b)**

To establish prejudice under plain-error review, a defendant must show that the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. The error must be “prejudicial in the sense that it undermined the fundamental fairness of the trial and contributed to the verdict obtained.” *United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258, 265 (2010) (quoting *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 631–32 (2002)). The Eleventh Circuit applied this standard and found no prejudice because the record did not demonstrate that the error influenced the jury’s verdict.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that prejudice under Rule 52(b) is not a generalized inquiry into the fairness of the defendant’s life or prior conduct. *See Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 470 (1997) (explaining that prejudice requires a showing that the error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings”). The Eleventh Circuit’s refusal to consider Greer’s prior convictions or post-trial conduct was consistent with this principle. *See United States v. Gonzalez*, 918 F.3d 1196, 1203 (11th Cir. 2019) (holding that prior convictions cannot be used to establish prejudice under plain-error review).

**III. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Improperly Expand Its Review to Matters Outside the Record**

Greer argues that the Eleventh Circuit impermissibly considered matters outside the trial record. However, the court’s analysis was confined to the trial record and the legal arguments presented. *See United States v. Brown*, 540 F.3d 1040, 1045 (11th Cir. 2008) (noting that appellate courts may consider the entire trial record but not extraneous materials). The court did not rely on Greer’s prior convictions or post-trial conduct to establish prejudice. Instead, it examined the record to determine whether the government proved the knowledge element of § 922(g). This was proper under *Olano* and its progeny.

**Conclusion**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly limited its review to the trial record and applied the proper standard for plain-error review. It did not err in concluding that Greer failed to show prejudice under Rule 52(b). The judgment below should be affirmed.
