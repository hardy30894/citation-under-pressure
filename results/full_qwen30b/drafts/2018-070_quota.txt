**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the precise language and legislative intent behind OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that California’s wage and hour laws apply on the OCS so long as they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, misreads the statute and ignores binding precedent. The correct rule is that state law may only be applied on the OCS to fill a gap in federal law—*not* as a substitute for federal law where Congress has already established a comprehensive regulatory scheme. The Court should reverse.

### I. OCSLA’s Choice-of-Law Provision Is Not a Blanket Adoption of State Law; It Is a Gap-Filling Mechanism

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of § 1333(a)(2)(A) as a general invitation to apply any “applicable and not inconsistent” state law—regardless of whether federal law already governs the subject—contradicts the statute’s text, structure, and history. The provision does not say “state law applies unless preempted.” It says state law applies “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (emphasis added). The phrase “to the extent” is critical: it limits application to those areas where federal law is silent or incomplete. As the Supreme Court has long held, “where Congress has legislated in a field, the States may not supplement or alter the federal scheme.” *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941); see also *Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Ass’n*, 505 U.S. 88, 100 (1992) (“Congress may pre-empt state law either by occupying a field or by creating an irreconcilable conflict.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s reading would render the “gap” requirement superfluous. If state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent,” then the phrase “to the extent” becomes meaningless. This violates the cardinal principle of statutory interpretation that “no clause of a statute should be treated as surplusage.” *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538 (1955). The legislative history confirms that Congress intended OCSLA to fill gaps, not to expand federal jurisdiction. As the Senate Report explained, Congress rejected extending state law to the OCS “because the Federal Government had not yet developed a comprehensive body of law for the OCS.” S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6 (1953). The Act was designed to “provide a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as drilling platforms” *where federal law was insufficient*. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). That is, state law was to be a *supplement*, not a *substitute*.

### II. The Supreme Court Has Repeatedly Held That Federal Law Preempts State Law Where Congress Has Enacted a Comprehensive Scheme

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is precisely such a comprehensive scheme. Congress has “established a detailed and comprehensive regulatory framework” for wage and hour protections. *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002). The FLSA’s “savings clause,” 29 U.S.C. § 218, explicitly permits states to impose *more protective* wage and hour standards, but it does not authorize state law to *supersede* or *duplicate* federal law where federal law already governs. The clause is not a license to apply state law in every case; it is a narrow exception to preemption. *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 187; *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. at 187 (“The savings clause does not create a general right to apply state law in the absence of federal law.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that California’s meal period laws are “not inconsistent” with the FLSA ignores the fact that the FLSA *already governs* the subject. The FLSA establishes minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements. 29 U.S.C. § 206, § 207. The Department of Labor (DOL) has issued detailed regulations interpreting these provisions, including rules on compensable time and work hours. *Brigham v. Eugene Water & Elec. Bd.*, 357 F.3d 931, 940 (9th Cir. 2004). These regulations are “substantive agency regulations” with the “force and effect of law.” *Chrysler Corp. v. Brown*, 441 U.S. 281, 295–96 (1979). Where federal law is already in place and enforced, state law cannot be applied as a parallel remedy. *Hines*, 312 U.S. at 67.

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Would Create a Federal-Local Legal Hybrid That Congress Never Intended

The Ninth Circuit’s approach would allow state law to apply on the OCS even when federal law is fully operative, creating a patchwork of overlapping and potentially conflicting standards. This is precisely the kind of “inconsistent” regime Congress sought to avoid. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 366 (“Any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)] was remove[d].”); *Tallentire v. United States*, 477 U.S. 208, 218 (1986) (OCSLA applies state law only where federal law is “inadequate”).
