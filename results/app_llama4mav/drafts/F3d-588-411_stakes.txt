## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in instructing the jury on the meaning of "shielding" under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii), and the evidence was insufficient to prove Xiang Hui Ye intended to prevent the government from detecting his illegal alien employees.

### A. The District Court's Supplemental Jury Instruction on "Shielding" Was Erroneous

The district court's definition of "shielding" was overly broad and vague, and did not accurately reflect the statutory meaning. The term "shielding" is not defined in the statute, and the district court was required to provide a definition that was consistent with the case law.

Several circuits have addressed the meaning of "shielding" in the context of § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). The Ninth Circuit, in United States v. You, 382 F.3d 958, 966 (9th Cir. 2004), held that "shielding" requires "conduct that prevents detection by immigration authorities." The Fifth Circuit, in United States v. Rubio-Gonzalez, 674 F.2d 1067, 1072 (5th Cir. 1982), similarly held that "shielding" involves "acts that tend to prevent detection" of illegal aliens.

In this case, the district court defined "shielding" as "protecting or keeping safe from detection or danger." (R. at 123). This definition is too broad and does not accurately capture the statutory meaning. By defining "shielding" in this way, the district court allowed the jury to convict Ye based on conduct that did not necessarily involve an intent to prevent detection by immigration authorities.

The Seventh Circuit has not previously addressed the meaning of "shielding" in the context of § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). However, in United States v. Varkonyi, 645 F.2d 453, 459 (5th Cir. 1981), the Fifth Circuit held that the government must prove that the defendant "knowingly or in reckless disregard of the fact that an alien has come to, entered, or remained in the United States in violation of law, concealed, harbored, or shielded from detection such alien." The district court's instruction did not require the jury to find that Ye had the requisite intent to shield the illegal aliens from detection.

The erroneous instruction was not harmless, as it allowed the jury to convict Ye based on conduct that may not have been intended to prevent detection. For example, Ye's provision of housing to the illegal aliens could be seen as "protecting or keeping safe" them, even if it was not done with the intent to prevent detection. (R. at 78-80).

The court should reverse Ye's conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) because the district court's definition of "shielding" was overly broad and did not accurately reflect the statutory meaning.

### B. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Ye Intended to Prevent the Government from Detecting His Illegal Alien Employees

The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to prove that Ye intended to prevent the government from detecting his illegal alien employees. While Ye admitted to hiring and providing housing to illegal aliens, the evidence did not show that he took steps to actively conceal their presence from immigration authorities.

In United States v. Lopez, 590 F.3d 1238, 1244 (11th Cir. 2009), the Eleventh Circuit held that "mere failure to report the presence of an illegal alien is not sufficient to establish harboring or shielding." The government must prove that the defendant took affirmative steps to conceal the alien's presence. See also United States v. Ozcelik, 527 F.3d 88, 100 (3d Cir. 2008) ("To prove 'harboring,' the government must show that the defendant 'acted with the intent to prevent detection by immigration authorities.'").

In this case, while Ye failed to maintain proper employment records and provided housing to the illegal aliens, the evidence did not show that he took affirmative steps to conceal their presence. Ye's statement to the agents that he did not have I-9 forms for the Hispanic employees because they did not have Social Security numbers (R. at 45) suggests that he was simply failing to comply with employment laws, rather than actively concealing the aliens' presence.

The court should reverse Ye's conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) because the evidence was insufficient to prove that he intended to prevent the government from detecting his illegal alien employees.
