**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Requires Only That a Defendant Be Competent Under the Rational-Understanding Standard of *Pate v. Robinson*, Not Under a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Test.**

This Court has long defined the constitutional floor of competency in a single, uniform way. In *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court held that due process forbids the conviction of a person who lacks "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and who does not have "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 385. *Pate* did not purport to create a floor applicable only to standing trial; it announced the constitutional measure of mental competency itself, explaining that a defendant's competency concerns his "capacity ... to understand the nature of the proceedings against him" and to participate rationally. *Id.* at 384–85.

Nothing in the competency inquiry changes when the defendant, rather than proceeding to trial, chooses to plead guilty or to represent himself. The Ninth Circuit's demand for a distinct "reasoned choice" standard has no textual or doctrinal source in this Court's due process jurisprudence. It rests instead on the mistaken premise that the *decision* to waive rights requires greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But the competency inquiry has always asked whether the defendant possesses the mental capacity to understand and participate—not whether the choice he makes is wise, or is the choice a rational and counseled defendant would make. *Moore v. Michigan*, 355 U.S. 155 (1957), confirms this focus: the Court there examined whether the record supported a finding that the defendant understandingly relinquished his right to counsel, not whether he satisfied some elevated capacity beyond that required to stand trial. Competency doctrine safeguards a defendant against being subjected to proceedings he cannot comprehend or participate in; it does not superintend the soundness of the particular decisions a competent defendant elects to make.

**II. The Standards Governing Waiver of Counsel and Guilty Pleas Concern the Voluntariness and Knowledge of the Waiver, Not a Separate, Elevated Mental-Capacity Threshold.**

The decisions of this Court addressing waiver of counsel and guilty pleas confirm that the constitutional inquiry is whether the waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent—not whether the defendant met some heightened capacity beyond the *Pate* standard. In *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), the Court examined whether the record demonstrated that the accused, aware of his right to counsel, understandingly declined it, and held that the presumption runs against finding such a waiver absent an adequate record. *Id.* at 516. That inquiry concerns the defendant's knowledge and intent respecting his rights, a question distinct from the *Pate* capacity inquiry, and one that in no way implies a separate, more demanding competency standard. *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required that a waiver of counsel be made "with eyes open," *id.* at 724, emphasizing the defendant's awareness of the risks of self-representation—again a voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry, not a mental-capacity inquiry.

*Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), confirms that the focus is on whether the record shows an informed choice, explaining that "want of counsel at ... arraignment ... does not vitiate ... proceedings" where the record shows the plea was "entered understandingly." *Id.* at 174–75. And *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), recognized that a defendant may competently elect to conduct his own defense without the assistance of counsel—an election this Court treated as a matter of intelligent waiver, not of superior mental competence. Similarly, *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), required that the record affirmatively show that a guilty plea was voluntary and intelligent because entry of such a plea implicates the waiver of multiple constitutional protections, including the privilege against self-incrimination, the right to trial by jury, and the right to confront one's accusers, *id.* at 243, but *Boykin* nowhere suggested that the defendant must meet a heightened standard of rationality beyond that required to stand trial.

**III. *Westbrook v. Arizona* Does Not Establish a Substantive Competency Standard Higher Than the *Pate* Standard.**

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard cannot be traced to *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam). *Westbrook* remanded because the record did not show that the trial court had determined "whether petitioner had the capacity to waive his constitutional right to the assistance of counsel," *id.* at 150, but the Court did not articulate any standard different from the one already governing competency generally, nor did it suggest that waiver of counsel demands a quantum of rationality beyond the ability to consult with counsel and understand the proceedings described in *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. at 385. *Westbrook* stands only for the unremarkable proposition that a court must separately consider whether a waiver occurred at all—a voluntariness question, not a new mental-capacity threshold.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard that governs whether a defendant may stand trial, articulated in *Pate v. Robinson*, likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional inquiries required by *Carnley*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, *Boykin*, and *Moore v. Michigan* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
