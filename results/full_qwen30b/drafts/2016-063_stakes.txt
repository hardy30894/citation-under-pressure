**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to resolve a conflict among the circuits and to clarify the proper interpretation of 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A), which defines the scope of “mixed cases” for purposes of judicial review. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Perry v. Merit Systems Protection Board*, 877 F.3d 1001 (D.C. Cir. 2017), wrongly concludes that *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596, 184 L. Ed. 2d 433 (2012), “eviscerates” its prior precedent in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), and thus compels transfer of this case to the Federal Circuit. That conclusion is incorrect. *Kloeckner* did not overrule *Powell*, nor does it compel a different result in this case. On the contrary, *Kloeckner* confirms the continued vitality of the jurisdictional/procedural distinction that underpins *Powell*, and the D.C. Circuit’s refusal to follow that precedent violates settled principles of stare decisis and statutory interpretation.

The statutory framework governing judicial review of MSPB decisions is clear. Under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A), “the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit shall have exclusive jurisdiction to review decisions of the Board.” However, § 7703(b)(2) creates an exception: “a case of discrimination” under § 7702(a)(1) “shall be filed in a United States district court.” The key to determining which forum applies is whether the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). This language is not a mere formality—it defines the threshold for a “mixed case.” As the Supreme Court emphasized in *Kloeckner*, the statute “requires that the employee be affected by an action appealable to the MSPB.” 133 S. Ct. at 604. That is, the action must be one that, if properly challenged, would fall within the Board’s jurisdiction.

In *Kloeckner*, the Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a claim on a *procedural* ground—such as untimeliness—judicial review lies in district court. 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Court did so because the employee had been “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB”—her removal was a final, appealable action—and the Board’s dismissal was not based on a lack of jurisdiction, but on a failure to meet a procedural deadline. Id. The Court explicitly limited its holding to such cases: “We address only the proper forum for appealing MSPB dismissals on procedural grounds.” Id. at 603. The Court cited decisions from the Second and Tenth Circuits that had drawn a distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals, and it did so with awareness of the legal significance of that distinction. See id. at 603 n.3. The Court’s focus on *procedural* grounds, and its repeated reference to the employee’s status as “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB,” confirms that the statutory threshold for a “mixed case” is met when the underlying action is appealable, even if the appeal is dismissed for a procedural defect.

By contrast, in *Powell*, the D.C. Circuit held that when the MSPB dismisses a case for lack of *jurisdiction*—because the employee voluntarily accepted the action—the case is not a “mixed case” under § 7702(a)(1)(A), and review lies in the Federal Circuit, not district court. 158 F.3d at 599–600. The Court in *Powell* correctly recognized that the statutory language turns on whether the action is *appealable* to the MSPB. If the action is not appealable—because it was voluntary—the employee was not “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB,” and thus the case does not qualify as a “mixed case.” Id. at 599. The D.C. Circuit’s reasoning in *Powell* is consistent with *Kloeckner* and does not conflict with it. Indeed, *Kloeckner* reinforces the distinction: the Court’s holding applies only where the action *is* appealable, but the appeal is dismissed for a procedural reason. Where the action is not appealable at all—because it was voluntary—the case falls outside the scope of § 7702(a)(1)(A), regardless of the employee’s allegations of discrimination.

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* from *Powell* on the ground that the former “eviscerates” the latter is legally unsound. The Court in *Kloeckner* did not purport to address jurisdictional dismissals. It did not consider whether a voluntary retirement could ever be the subject of a mixed case. It did not suggest that the Board’s jurisdictional determination is irrelevant to the question of forum. To the contrary, the Court’s analysis presupposes that the Board has jurisdiction over the underlying action. See 133 S. Ct. at 604 (“Kloeckner brought a mixed case—that she was affected by an action (i.e., removal) appealable to the MSPB”). That is precisely the opposite of the situation here: Perry’s retirement was not appealable because it was voluntary. The Board’s jurisdictional dismissal was not a procedural error that could be waived; it was a determination that the action was not subject to Board review at all.
