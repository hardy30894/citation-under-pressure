**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 875(c) Requires Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Communications as Threats.**

A conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) demands that the government prove the defendant “means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). This Court has long held that the First Amendment exception for “true threats” turns on the speaker’s purpose: “What is a threat must be distinguished from what is constitutionally protected speech.” Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 707 (1969) (per curiam). The objective “reasonable speaker” test applied below cannot satisfy that constitutional command.

In Black, the Court defined true threats by reference to the speaker’s own understanding: the prohibition reaches only those statements in which the speaker intends the words to convey a serious expression of violent intent. 538 U.S. at 359. An objective standard that asks only whether a hypothetical listener would feel threatened severs the required link between the speaker’s mens rea and the unprotected category. See also R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, 505 U.S. 377, 388 (1992) (threats are proscribable because of the “particularly intolerable” nature of the speaker’s chosen message). Because the district court instructed the jury solely on objective foreseeability, the convictions rest on an erroneous legal standard.

**II. The Statute’s Text and Constitutional Avoidance Principles Confirm the Need for Subjective Intent.**

Section 875(c) punishes transmission of “any threat to injure the person of another.” 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). This Court presumes that criminal statutes require proof of scienter when the prohibited conduct lies “close to the line separating constitutionally protected speech from unprotected speech.” United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., 513 U.S. 64, 71-72 (1994). Interpreting “threat” to require only objective foreseeability would criminalize protected speech uttered without any intent to threaten, raising serious First Amendment doubts that the statute’s text readily avoids. See also Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (offenses derived from common-law antecedents ordinarily incorporate mens rea).

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule, drawn from United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), cannot stand after Black. That decision’s objective formulation is incompatible with Black’s insistence that the speaker “means to communicate” a threat. 538 U.S. at 359. The convictions must therefore be reversed.
