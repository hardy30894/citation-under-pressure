# ARGUMENT

## THE BOP'S PROGRAM STATEMENT CLASSIFYING ROUSSOS'S OFFENSE AS A "CRIME OF VIOLENCE" IS INCONSISTENT WITH THE GOVERNING STATUTE AND REGULATION

### A. The Categorical Approach Governs the Definition of "Crime of Violence"

Congress defined "crime of violence" in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3) by reference to the elements of the offense, not the facts underlying a particular defendant's conduct. Section 924(c)(3) asks whether the offense "has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force," or whether the offense "by its nature, involves a substantial risk" that force will be used. 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). This Court and the Supreme Court have consistently construed such statutory language to require a categorical, offense-based inquiry rather than a case-specific factual inquiry. *See Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 600 (1990) (holding that determination of whether a prior conviction constitutes a predicate "violent felony" requires courts to look "only to the fact of conviction and the statutory definition of the prior offense," not to the underlying facts). The Supreme Court's rationale in *Taylor*—that a categorical approach avoids unfairness and unpredictability that would result from relitigating the facts of old convictions—applies with equal force here. *Id.* at 601.

Conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846 does not have as an element the use of physical force. Nor does the offense, "by its nature," involve a substantial risk of force. The elements of drug distribution conspiracy require only an agreement to distribute a controlled substance; no element concerns firearms, violence, or force of any kind. The BOP's own regulation borrows the § 924(c)(3) definition verbatim, 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, and thus imports the categorical elements-based test that Congress intended.

### B. The BOP's Program Statement Improperly Converts a Sentencing Enhancement Into an Element of the Offense

Program Statement 5162.02 abandons the categorical approach mandated by the statute and regulation. Instead of examining whether the offense of conviction has an element of force, the Program Statement directs BOP officials to look behind the conviction to any sentencing enhancement imposed under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1 for weapon possession, and to treat that enhancement as automatically converting the underlying drug offense into a "crime of violence."

This approach cannot be reconciled with § 924(c)(3)'s plain text. A sentencing enhancement is not an "element" of the offense; it is a fact found by the sentencing court for purposes of calculating a guideline range, applying a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard entirely distinct from the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard applicable to offense elements. *Cf. United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 157 (1997) (recognizing that sentencing enhancements may rest on conduct never proven to a jury and subject only to a preponderance standard). Roussos pleaded guilty to conspiracy under § 846; he was never charged with, nor did he plead guilty to, any offense involving a firearm. The gun was found not on his person, not at his arrest, and not during commission of any act of distribution, but in a search of his separate vacation residence. The sentencing court's enhancement reflected a factual finding about the vacation-home firearm's connection to the conspiracy—not a determination that the offense of conviction, § 846, requires or involves force as an element.

The BOP's Program Statement thus violates the settled principle, most recently reaffirmed by this Court's sister circuits construing identical statutory language, that an agency's interpretation of a statute cannot stand where it contradicts the plain and unambiguous terms Congress enacted. *See Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984) ("If the intent of Congress is clear, that is the end of the matter; for the court, as well as the agency, must give effect to the unambiguously expressed intent of Congress."). Congress's use of the categorical, elements-based language in § 924(c)(3)—which the BOP itself incorporated in 28 C.F.R. § 550.58—leaves no room for the agency to substitute a facts-and-circumstances test through subregulatory guidance.

### C. The Program Statement Is Not Entitled to Deference Because It Conflicts With the BOP's Own Regulation

Even under deferential review, an agency interpretation that contradicts the agency's own duly promulgated regulation is not entitled to deference. *See Christensen v. Harris County*, 529 U.S. 576, 588 (2000) (interpretations contained in agency guidance documents "do not warrant Chevron-style deference" where they conflict with the plain language of the regulation they purport to interpret). Section 550.58 unambiguously defines "crime of violence" by cross-reference to § 924(c)(3)'s elements-based test. Program Statement 5162.02, by contrast, directs BOP staff to treat a sentencing enhancement—untethered to any offense element—as dispositive. That directive does not interpret § 550.58; it rewrites it.

### D. Roussos's Offense of Conviction Is Not a Crime of Violence

Applying the categorical approach required by *Taylor* and by the plain text of § 924(c)(3), Roussos's conviction for conspiracy to distribute narcotics under § 846 is not a crime of violence. The offense has no element of force, and there is no dispute, as the record reflects, that "guns were not a factor in his arrest and conviction." The BOP's classification rests entirely on a sentencing enhancement addressing a firearm discovered at a location separate from the offense conduct itself.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that Program Statement 5162.02 is inconsistent with 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, and 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), reverse the judgment of the district court, and remand with instructions to grant the writ of habeas corpus.
