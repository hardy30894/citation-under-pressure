# ARGUMENT

## I. Congress's Delegation of Temporary Scheduling Authority to the Attorney General Satisfies the Intelligible Principle Test

Article I vests "all legislative powers" in Congress, but this Court has long recognized that Congress may enlist the assistance of the Executive Branch "under limitations of a rightful exercise of its own powers." Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892). The touchstone of a constitutional delegation is whether Congress has laid "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). Section 811(h) readily satisfies this standard.

Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. The statute confines temporary scheduling to substances that pose "an imminent hazard to the public safety," and directs the Attorney General to consider specified statutory factors bearing on the substance's history and pattern of abuse, its scope and significance, and the risk to public health. These are precisely the kind of ascertainable standards this Court has repeatedly approved. In Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), the Court sustained a delegation to fix commodity prices that would be "generally fair and equitable" and would effectuate the purposes of the Act, explaining that Congress "is not confined to that method of executing its policy which involves the least possible delegation of discretion." Id. at 424-25. Similarly, in Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245 (1947), the Court upheld a delegation permitting the Home Loan Bank Board to appoint conservators for savings associations upon a finding of "unsafe" practices, reasoning that the standard drew upon "well-known and generally acceptable standards" already familiar in the regulated field. Id. at 250. The "imminent hazard" standard here draws upon the same well-established body of pharmacological and law-enforcement expertise that already governs permanent scheduling determinations under 21 U.S.C. § 811(c).

The complexity and volatility of the problem Congress confronted further supports the delegation. As this Court explained in sustaining broad regulatory delegations, "the modern complexities of our economic and governmental structure" require that Congress be permitted to delegate authority under general standards rather than exhaustive detail. American Power & Light Co. v. SEC, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946). The same is true of the illicit drug trade, where clandestine chemists design new substances more rapidly than Congress can legislate. This Court has recognized that the pace of change in a regulated field is itself relevant to the permissible breadth of delegation. See Yakus, 321 U.S. at 424 (delegation upheld where "the definition, though general, sufficiently marks the field within which the [delegate] is to act"). The same logic that permitted price controls responsive to wartime economic emergencies, Lichter v. United States, 334 U.S. 742, 778-79 (1948) (upholding delegation to fix "excessive profits" because Congress could not "anticipate and to appraise specifically" every contingency), permits Congress to arm the Attorney General with authority to respond swiftly to newly designed narcotics like "Euphoria."

Petitioners' reliance on Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In both cases the delegating statute supplied no standard at all: the President was left to determine, in Panama Refining, whether to prohibit interstate shipment of "hot oil" without any legislative "criterion to govern the President's course," 293 U.S. at 415, and in Schechter Poultry to approve industry-fashioned codes of "fair competition" untethered to any legislative policy. 295 U.S. at 541-42. Section 811(h), by contrast, specifies both the harm to be avoided ("imminent hazard to the public safety") and the factors relevant to that determination. That is materially more confining than the standardless delegations struck down in 1935, and it is comparable to delegations this Court has consistently sustained. See Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952) (upholding delegation of discretion to detain aliens pending deportation where statute supplied general but adequate guidance).

## II. Limited Judicial Review Does Not Render the Delegation Unconstitutional

Nor does the temporary, time-limited character of an order under § 811(h) undermine the delegation's validity. This Court has never required that every exercise of delegated authority be subject to full judicial review before the delegation itself may stand; rather, the adequacy of the standard controls. See Yakus, 321 U.S. at 444 (sustaining delegation notwithstanding restrictions on review, because the statutory standards themselves confined the delegate's discretion). The one-year sunset provision, renewable by only six months, itself operates as a structural check by forcing the Attorney General to proceed to permanent scheduling under the full procedural protections of 21 U.S.C. § 811(a)-(c) — including notice, hearing, and the binding scientific evaluation of the Secretary of Health and Human Services — or else allow the temporary designation to lapse.

## III. The Attorney General Lawfully Subdelegated Temporary Scheduling Authority to the DEA

Finally, the subdelegation to the DEA Administrator was authorized and proper. Congress expressly empowered the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Act, 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), and this Court has long approved an agency head's redistribution of delegated authority to subordinate officials absent a clear congressional prohibition. In Fleming v. Mohawk Wrecking & Lumber Co., 331 U.S. 111 (1947), the Court upheld the Price Administrator's subdelegation of rent-control authority to local boards, holding that "unless" Congress "explicitly" restricts the power to redelegate, an agency head may distribute his statutory duties as he sees fit. Id. at 121. Section 871(a)'s broad grant of redelegation authority, coupled with the DEA's evident competence in drug enforcement, forecloses petitioners' subdelegation challenge.
