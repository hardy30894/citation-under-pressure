**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Pakistan’s Expropriation of Appellant’s Property Did Not Constitute Commercial Activity Under the FSIA.**

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (“FSIA”) provides that a foreign state is immune from suit in United States courts unless one of its enumerated exceptions applies. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 1604. One such exception is the “commercial activity” exception, which waives immunity where the action is “based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state” or where the action is “based upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere.” *Id.* § 1605(a)(2). The FSIA defines “commercial activity” as “either a regular course of commercial conduct or a particular commercial transaction or act.” *Id.* § 1603(d).

The district court erred in holding that Pakistan’s expropriation of Appellant’s land did not constitute commercial activity. This Court has recognized that “expropriation of property by a foreign state is a commercial act when the property taken is used for a commercial purpose.” *See Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992) (citing *Texas Trading & Milling Corp. v. Federal Republic of Nigeria*, 647 F. Supp. 300, 308 (S.D.N.Y. 1986), *aff’d*, 801 F.2d 340 (2d Cir. 1986)). Here, Appellant alleges that his property was expropriated and then used for military housing—a quintessentially commercial purpose. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614 (noting that “a state’s issuance of debt securities for the purpose of raising capital to fund its governmental programs is a commercial act”); *see also Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993) (explaining that the commercial activity exception applies where the act is “of the kind that a private person would engage in”).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has made clear that the commercial nature of an act is determined by its “nature,” not its “purpose.” *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. The expropriation of land for military housing is an act that a private person could perform—namely, acquiring property for resale or use in a business enterprise. *See id.* The fact that the ultimate beneficiary was the Pakistani military does not alter the commercial character of the act itself. *See id.* at 615 (holding that the issuance of bonds for governmental purposes was still commercial because the act itself was of a type a private person could perform).

The district court’s contrary conclusion misapplied the FSIA’s commercial activity exception. As this Court has held, “[t]he FSIA’s commercial activity exception is to be construed broadly in favor of exercising jurisdiction.” *See Rubin v. Islamic Republic of Iran*, 830 F.3d 1317, 1324 (11th Cir. 2016) (quoting *Hernandez v. Republic of Cuba*, 763 F.3d 119, 124 (D.C. Cir. 2014)). The district court’s restrictive interpretation of “commercial activity” contravenes this principle and must be reversed.

**II. The District Court Erred in Holding That the Expropriation Did Not Have a Direct Effect in the United States.**

The FSIA’s commercial activity exception also applies where the action “causes a direct effect in the United States.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The district court found that the expropriation did not have a direct effect in the United States because Appellant’s property was located in Pakistan and the alleged injury occurred there. This was error.

This Court has held that a “direct effect” occurs where the action “has a substantial, immediate, and foreseeable effect” within the United States. *See Rubin*, 830 F.3d at 1325 (quoting *U.S. Aviation Underwriters, Inc. v. Okeanos Travel Agency, Inc.*, 66 F.3d 144, 149 (11th Cir. 1995)). The Supreme Court has further explained that a foreign state’s commercial activity need not occur in the United States to have a direct effect here; rather, the focus is on whether the effects of the activity are felt domestically. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618.

Here, Appellant alleges that he was offered an alternative parcel of land in the United States as part of the expropriation scheme. Even if that offer was ultimately rejected, the fact that the Pakistani government engaged in negotiations in the United States regarding compensation for the expropriation constitutes a direct effect in this country. *See id.* at 618 (finding a direct effect where the foreign state’s commercial activity involved transactions with U.S. entities). Moreover, Appellant’s decision to abandon further litigation in Pakistan and seek compensation in U.S. courts is a foreseeable consequence of the expropriation, which further supports jurisdiction under the FSIA. *See id.*

The district court’s narrow interpretation of “direct effect” conflicts with the FSIA’s remedial purpose and this Court’s precedent. *See Rubin*, 830 F.3d at 1325. Accordingly, the district court’s dismissal for lack of subject matter jurisdiction must be reversed.
