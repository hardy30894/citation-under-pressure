## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Bryant's Claim to Enforce the Arbitration Award

The district court erred in dismissing Bryant's claim seeking enforcement of the arbitration award. As an employee covered by a collective bargaining agreement, Bryant has standing to enforce the arbitration award won against his employer, Bell Atlantic. The Supreme Court has long recognized that an individual employee has a federal right to enforce arbitration awards won pursuant to a collective bargaining agreement. See United Steelworkers of America v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp., 363 U.S. 593 (1960). In Enterprise Wheel, the Court held that an arbitration award is final and binding on the parties, and that federal courts have the authority to enforce such awards. Id. at 599. Therefore, Bryant has standing to seek enforcement of the arbitration award.

### II. The District Court Erred in Failing to Recognize the Proof Requirements Under the Burden-Shifting Standard in Title VII and Section 1981 Cases

The district court erred in failing to recognize the proof requirements under the burden-shifting standard in Title VII and Section 1981 cases. The Supreme Court has established that a plaintiff can establish a prima facie case of employment discrimination by showing that he is a member of a protected class, was qualified for the job, suffered an adverse employment action, and that the circumstances give rise to an inference of discrimination. See McDonald v. Santa Fe Trail Transp. Co., 427 U.S. 273 (1976) (citing McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973)). Under this standard, Bryant has established a prima facie case of employment discrimination. The burden then shifts to Bell Atlantic to articulate a legitimate, non-discriminatory reason for terminating Bryant. See Texas Dept. of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248 (1981) (citing McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973) and Furnco Construction Corp. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567 (1978)). However, the district court failed to properly apply this burden-shifting standard.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has held that a plaintiff can establish a claim of employment discrimination under Section 1981 by showing that the defendant's actions were motivated by discriminatory intent. See Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co., 392 U.S. 409 (1968). Bryant has presented evidence that Bell Atlantic's actions were motivated by discriminatory intent, including the refusal to accommodate his child care responsibilities while accommodating other employees.

### III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because There Are Genuine Issues of Material Fact Still in Dispute

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because there are genuine issues of material fact still in dispute. The Supreme Court has held that summary judgment is only appropriate when there is no genuine issue of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. See Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620 (1944). In this case, there are genuine issues of material fact regarding Bell Atlantic's motivations for terminating Bryant and whether the company accommodated his child care responsibilities. Therefore, the district court erred in granting summary judgment.

In conclusion, the district court erred in dismissing Bryant's claim to enforce the arbitration award, failed to properly apply the burden-shifting standard in Title VII and Section 1981 cases, and erred in granting summary judgment. This Court should reverse the district court's decision and remand the case for further proceedings.
