The issue before this Court is whether the rule announced in Johnson v. United States regarding what constitutes a violent offense applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. Although Johnson was decided after Welch's conviction became final, the principle that the Supreme Court's decisions construing federal statutes apply retroactively supports the retroactive application of Johnson. See United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103 (1801), where the Court stated that "if subsequent to the judgment and before the decision of the appellate court, a law intervenes and positively changes the rule which governs, the law must be obeyed, or its obligation denied."

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) imposes a minimum sentence of 15 years for defendants with three prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). The ACCA defines a "violent felony" as a crime that has certain characteristics. The Supreme Court has held that a statute is void for vagueness if it fails to provide adequate notice of the conduct it prohibits. See Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) (stating that a penal statute must be "sufficiently explicit to inform those who are subject to it what conduct on their part will render them liable to its penalties").

In Johnson v. United States, the Court held that the residual clause of the ACCA was unconstitutionally vague because it failed to provide clear guidance on what constitutes a violent felony. The Court's decision in Johnson was based on the principle that a statute must provide fair notice of the conduct it prohibits, as established in Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). The Court in Connally noted that a statute must be "sufficiently explicit" to inform those subject to it of the conduct that will render them liable to its penalties.

The Court has also held that a statute is unconstitutional if it is so vague that it allows for arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement. See Cline v. Frink Dairy Co., 274 U.S. 445 (1927), where the Court considered the validity of a statute under the due process clause. The decision in Johnson applies retroactively to Welch's case because it is a substantive rule that changes the definition of a violent felony under the ACCA. See Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1974) (holding that a new rule that narrows the scope of a criminal statute applies retroactively).

Furthermore, the Court has held that a new rule applies retroactively if it is a substantive rule. The rule announced in Johnson is a substantive rule that alters the range of conduct that the ACCA punishes. See United States v. Chambers, 291 U.S. 217, 226 (1934) (stating that a change in the law that affects the substantive rights of the parties must be given effect).

In light of the above authorities, the rule announced in Johnson v. United States applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. The residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague, and Welch's prior conviction for strong arm robbery does not qualify as a predicate offense under the ACCA. Therefore, Welch's sentence should be vacated, and his case should be remanded for resentencing.

The relevant case authorities that support this argument are: United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103 (1801); Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451 (1939); Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385 (1926); Cline v. Frink Dairy Co., 274 U.S. 445 (1927); Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333 (1974); United States v. Chambers, 291 U.S. 217 (1934); Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945); and United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81 (1921).
