# ARGUMENT

## The Rule of *Johnson v. United States* Does Not Apply Retroactively to Welch's Final Conviction

This Court has never held that every new constitutional rule of criminal procedure must be applied to convictions that are already final. To the contrary, in *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965), the Court held that "the Constitution neither prohibits nor requires retrospective effect," *id.* at 629, and that retroactivity must instead be determined by a case-specific inquiry into the nature of the new rule and the consequences of applying it to closed cases. That framework, refined in *Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott*, 382 U.S. 406 (1966), *Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. 719 (1966), *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967), *DeStefano v. Woods*, 392 U.S. 631 (1968), and *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244 (1969), governs here and forecloses retroactive application of the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015).

### A. The Governing Standard Requires Weighing Purpose, Reliance, and Administrative Burden

In *Stovall v. Denno*, this Court explained that resolution of a retroactivity question turns on "(a) the purpose to be served by the new standards, (b) the extent of the reliance by law enforcement authorities on the old standards, and (c) the effect on the administration of justice of a retroactive application of the new standards." 388 U.S. at 297. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the first of these factors—the purpose of the rule—is the "most significant" consideration, and that rules serving primarily to safeguard "the very integrity of the fact-finding process" at trial are the strongest candidates for retroactive application. *Tehan*, 382 U.S. at 416. Where, by contrast, a new rule does not bear on the accuracy of the determination of guilt, this Court has consistently withheld retroactive effect. *See DeStefano*, 392 U.S. at 633–34 (declining to apply *Duncan v. Louisiana* retroactively because the jury-trial right did not concern "the basic accuracy of the fact-finding process"); *Desist*, 394 U.S. at 249 (denying retroactive effect to *Katz v. United States* because the rule did not go to "the fairness of the trial" or the reliability of the determination of guilt).

### B. Each *Stovall* Factor Counsels Against Retroactivity

**1. Purpose.** The rule of *Johnson v. United States* rests on the void-for-vagueness doctrine of the Due Process Clause, which serves to give "fair warning" of prohibited conduct and to curb arbitrary enforcement—not to protect the integrity of the trial's fact-finding function. Welch's sentence enhancement did not concern whether he was guilty of the offense of conviction; it concerned only the legal classification of a prior conviction for sentencing purposes. Because the residual clause's infirmity did not "impair the truth-finding function" in the way that, for example, the right to counsel does, *see Tehan*, 382 U.S. at 416, the purpose factor does not favor retroactivity. Just as this Court declined to retroactively apply *Griffin v. California*'s bar on comment upon a defendant's silence because that rule did not concern "the accuracy of the truth-determining process," *id.*, the rule here likewise stands apart from the trial's core function of separating the guilty from the innocent.

**2. Reliance.** For decades, sentencing courts, prosecutors, and probation officers structured guilty pleas, plea negotiations, and sentences around the residual clause as this Court had construed and applied it. Just as *Linkletter* recognized that law enforcement's reasonable reliance on then-prevailing constitutional standards weighs against disturbing final judgments, 381 U.S. at 636–37, so too does the settled reliance on the residual clause here counsel against unsettling thousands of final sentences imposed under a standard that stood unchallenged for years.

**3. Effect on the Administration of Justice.** This Court has consistently recognized that retroactive application of a new rule that would require reopening numerous final judgments imposes a substantial burden on the administration of justice. *See Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. at 731–32 (declining to apply *Escobedo* and *Miranda* retroactively in part because of the "impact of retroactivity" on the "administration of ... justice"); *Tehan*, 382 U.S. at 419 (noting the "disruptive effect" of retroactive rules "on the administration of justice"). Extending *Johnson* retroactively would require federal courts to reopen collateral proceedings in a large number of ACCA cases nationwide, disturbing sentences long final and burdening courts with resentencing determinations untethered to any question of Welch's guilt.

### C. Welch's Contrary Position Would Erase the Distinction This Court Has Long Drawn

Welch's argument—that any new rule bearing on a sentencing statute must apply retroactively because it is "substantive"—cannot be reconciled with this Court's repeated refusal to treat every new constitutional holding as automatically retroactive merely because of the constitutional provision invoked. As *Linkletter* itself makes plain, the constitutional source of a rule does not dictate its temporal reach; the consequences of retroactivity do. 381 U.S. at 628–29. Nothing about the residual clause's vagueness, however serious a defect prospectively, transforms the *Stovall* balancing test into a foregone conclusion favoring retroactivity.

### Conclusion

Applying the settled retroactivity framework of *Linkletter*, *Tehan*, *Johnson v. New Jersey*, *Stovall*, *DeStefano*, and *Desist*, the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* does not apply to Welch's final conviction. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
