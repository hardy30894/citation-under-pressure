The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a reviewing court conducting plain-error analysis under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) may examine the entire record, not merely the trial evidence, to determine whether a Rehaif error affected substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings.

Plain-error review requires the defendant to show an error that is plain, affects substantial rights, and seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). In making these assessments, “a reviewing court must consider the error’s effect on the jury’s verdict in light of the entire record.” United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55, 59 (2002). This Court has repeatedly confirmed that the plain-error inquiry is not confined to the evidence presented at trial. See Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 469-470 (1997) (examining post-trial developments in the record); Puckett v. United States, 556 U.S. 129, 142 (2009) (considering the full procedural history).

The same principle governs omitted-element errors. In Neder v. United States, 527 U.S. 1, 19 (1999), the Court held that whether the omission of an element is harmless turns on whether the record contains “overwhelming” evidence of the missing element. Although Neder addressed harmless-error review, the Court has applied an analogous record-wide approach to plain-error claims involving omitted elements. See, e.g., United States v. Cotton, 535 U.S. 625, 632-633 (2002) (reviewing indictment and sentencing record to conclude that an Apprendi error did not affect substantial rights or the fairness of the proceedings).

Rehaif errors are no exception. Because the knowledge-of-status element is typically proved by evidence of prior convictions that may not have been introduced at trial, limiting review to the trial record would prevent courts from performing the very inquiry Rule 52(b) demands. The Eleventh Circuit therefore acted within established precedent when it consulted Greer’s undisputed prior convictions to conclude that he could not satisfy the third or fourth prongs of plain-error review.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
