ARGUMENT

Section 875(c) requires the Government to prove that the defendant subjectively intended his communication as a threat. The statute’s text—“any threat to injure the person of another”—imports the common-law requirement of intent to threaten. See Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952) (“mere omission from a criminal enactment of any mention of criminal intent should not be read as elimination of that element”). This Court has applied the same presumption to analogous threat statutes. See United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994).

The First Amendment independently demands subjective intent. In Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), the Court defined “true threats” as “those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.” Id. at 359. The phrase “means to communicate” refers to the speaker’s purpose, not a reasonable listener’s perception. See also R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, 505 U.S. 377, 388 (1992) (threats unprotected because of “the intention of the speaker”).

An objective standard, as applied below under United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), cannot be reconciled with Black. It would criminalize “political hyperbole” and “vehement, caustic” speech that the First Amendment protects. Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam). The Third Circuit’s rule therefore fails to “distinguish what is a threat” from protected expression. See NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co., 458 U.S. 886, 927 (1982).

Because the jury was instructed only on objective foreseeability, the convictions rest on an erroneous legal standard and must be reversed.
