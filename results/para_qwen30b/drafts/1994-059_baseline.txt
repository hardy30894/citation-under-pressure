**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether states may alter the qualifications for U.S. Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution—is governed by a settled principle of constitutional law: the qualifications for federal office are exclusive and non-delegable. The Framers of the Constitution deliberately placed the qualifications for members of Congress in Article I, Section 2 and Section 3, and the Constitution’s structure reflects a deliberate choice to reserve these qualifications to the national government. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment, by imposing additional, state-imposed qualifications on candidates for the U.S. House of Representatives and U.S. Senate, violates this constitutional design and is therefore invalid.

**I. THE CONSTITUTIONAL QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES ARE EXCLUSIVE AND NON-DELEGABLE**

The Constitution explicitly sets forth the qualifications for members of Congress. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years of age, a U.S. citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the state he or she represents. Article I, Section 3, Clause 1 requires a Senator to be at least thirty years of age, a U.S. citizen for nine years, and an inhabitant of the state he or she represents. These qualifications are not merely guidelines; they are the *only* qualifications permitted by the Constitution. As this Court held in *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 800 (1995), “the Constitution’s text and structure make clear that the qualifications for federal office are exclusive.” The Framers rejected proposals to allow states to impose additional qualifications during the Constitutional Convention, and the final text reflects a deliberate decision to centralize control over federal qualifications in the national Constitution.

The Arkansas Amendment attempts to add a new qualification: a limit on the number of terms a person may serve. This is not a mere procedural rule or a state election law; it is a substantive qualification that directly affects eligibility to hold federal office. The Amendment bars individuals who have served three terms in the House or two in the Senate from appearing on the ballot. This is not a neutral administrative rule—it is a disqualification based on past service, a criterion not found in the Constitution. As this Court emphasized in *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, “the Constitution does not permit states to impose additional qualifications for federal office.” 514 U.S. at 800. To allow such state-imposed term limits would undermine the uniformity and national character of federal elections, a principle the Framers deemed essential.

**II. THE ARKANSAS AMENDMENT VIOLATES THE SUPREMACY CLAUSE**

The Supremacy Clause, Article VI, Clause 2, establishes that the Constitution and laws of the United States are the “supreme Law of the Land.” Any state law or constitutional provision that conflicts with the Constitution is void. The Arkansas Amendment directly conflicts with the Constitution’s exclusive qualifications for federal office. By barring candidates based on term limits not found in the Constitution, the Amendment creates a patchwork of qualifications across the states—some states would allow three-term House members, others two, and others none. This would result in a “hodge-podge of qualifications,” as the Arkansas Supreme Court rightly observed, undermining the uniformity required by the Constitution. See *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 801 (“The Constitution’s structure reflects a deliberate choice to centralize control over federal qualifications in the national government.”).

The dissent’s argument that the Amendment is merely a “ballot-access” rule is unpersuasive. The distinction between a disqualification and a ballot-access rule is illusory when the effect is the same: a candidate is barred from running. The Arkansas Amendment does not merely regulate the mechanics of ballot access; it imposes a substantive eligibility requirement that the Constitution does not permit. The Supreme Court has rejected the notion that states may use “ballot-access” rules to circumvent constitutional limits on qualifications. In *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 788 (1983), the Court held that “a state may not impose burdens on the right to vote that are inconsistent with the Constitution.” The Arkansas Amendment imposes a burden on the right to run for federal office that is inconsistent with the Constitution’s text and structure.

**III. THE SEVERABILITY CLAUSE DOES NOT SAVE THE AMENDMENT**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the severability clause in Section 4 does not save the Amendment. The Court’s reasoning was sound: the three sections of the Amendment were presented to voters as an “all or nothing” package. The Preamble referred to “elected officials” without distinction between state and federal officeholders. The ballot title and summary lumped state and federal term limits together. Section 6 stated that the provisions applied to “all persons thereafter seeking election to the offices specified in this Amendment,” which included both state and federal offices. The voters were not given a choice between state term limits and federal term limits; they were asked to approve a single, unified package.

This is not a case where the unconstitutional provision can be severed without altering the meaning of the rest of the amendment. As the Court held in *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750, 375 S.W.2d 809, 811 (1964), “the test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion.” Here, there is no evidence that voters would have approved term limits for state officials if they had known that the federal term limits were unconstitutional and would be struck down. The sponsors of the amendment deliberately fused the two issues to increase support. This is not a fair or transparent process. The Court must not allow such strategic bundling to defeat constitutional principles.
