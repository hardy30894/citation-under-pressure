The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff's claims "arise out of or relate to" the defendant's contacts with the forum state. Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of California, San Francisco County, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1780 (2017). The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this standard in affirming the district court's denial of Ford's motion to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction.

Ford's activities in Montana, including advertising and selling parts within the state, demonstrate a sufficient connection to the claims at issue. The Court has long recognized that a defendant's "continuous and systematic" contacts with a forum state can give rise to specific jurisdiction, even if those contacts are not directly related to the plaintiff's claims. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 317 (1945). In this case, Ford's contacts with Montana are not merely incidental or attenuated; rather, they reflect a deliberate effort to serve the Montana market and avail itself of the privilege of doing business in the state.

The "arise out of or relate to" standard is not limited to cases where the defendant's in-state activities directly caused the plaintiff's injury. Rather, it encompasses a broader range of connections between the defendant's contacts and the plaintiff's claims. See Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462, 472-73 (1985) (noting that the defendant's contacts with the forum state must have a "substantial connection" to the plaintiff's claims). In this case, Ford's Montana activities are sufficiently connected to the claims at issue, as they reflect the company's efforts to serve the state's market and promote its products.

The exercise of specific jurisdiction over Ford in Montana is consistent with the principles of due process. Ford has purposefully availed itself of the privilege of doing business in Montana, and it is therefore subject to the jurisdiction of the state's courts. See Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958) (noting that a defendant's purposeful availment of a forum state's laws and privileges is a key factor in determining whether the exercise of jurisdiction is constitutional). The Montana Supreme Court's decision affirming the district court's denial of Ford's motion to dismiss is therefore entitled to deference.

In contrast, adopting Ford's position would undermine the principles of due process and create an unjustified limitation on the exercise of specific jurisdiction. The Court should reject Ford's argument and affirm the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court.
