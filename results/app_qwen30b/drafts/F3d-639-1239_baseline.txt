**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Mr. Lucero’s class-action complaint for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction on the ground of mootness. The court’s conclusion—that a defendant’s Rule 68 offer of judgment to a named plaintiff automatically extinguishes the case, even where a motion for class certification remains pending—contravenes well-established principles of federal jurisdiction and the Supreme Court’s recognition of the unique nature of class actions. This case presents a clear instance in which the named plaintiff’s individual claim, though satisfied, remains part of a larger, ongoing controversy that continues to present a justiciable dispute. As the Supreme Court has held, “a case is not moot simply because the named plaintiff has received all the relief he seeks,” *Sosna v. Iowa*, 419 U.S. 393, 402, 95 S.Ct. 553, 42 L.Ed.2d 532 (1975). Here, the relief sought by Mr. Lucero extends beyond his individual claim to the vindication of rights shared by a class of similarly situated individuals. The district court’s rigid application of a “general rule” of mootness, without regard to the class-action context, misapplies the law.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a class action is not merely a vehicle for individual redress but a mechanism for resolving issues of broad public importance. In *Sosna*, the Court emphasized that “the mere fact that the named plaintiff has obtained the relief he seeks does not necessarily render the case moot when the class action is still pending and the class members have not yet been afforded their day in court.” Id. at 402, 95 S.Ct. 553. The Court further noted that “the interests of the class members may be sufficiently distinct from those of the named plaintiff to justify the continuation of the suit even after the named plaintiff’s individual claim has been satisfied.” Id. This principle is particularly salient in the context of statutory enforcement actions like the FDCPA, where Congress intended to protect a broad class of consumers from abusive debt collection practices. The purpose of the FDCPA is not merely to compensate individual victims but to deter widespread violations of consumer rights. As the Court stated in *United States Parole Commission v. Geraghty*, 445 U.S. 388, 402, 100 S.Ct. 1202, 63 L.Ed.2d 479 (1980), “a case is not moot merely because the named plaintiff has obtained all the relief he seeks, if the class members’ claims remain live and the class action is still pending.”

The district court’s reliance on *Clark v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins.*, 590 F.3d 1134, 1138 (10th Cir.2009), is misplaced. *Clark* involved a situation where the named plaintiff had already obtained a favorable judgment and no class certification motion was pending. There, the court correctly concluded that the case was moot because the plaintiff’s individual claim had been fully resolved and no further relief was sought on behalf of a class. But here, the situation is fundamentally different: Mr. Lucero filed his motion for class certification on February 22, 2010—well within the discovery period set by the court—and the class certification hearing was vacated only after the court dismissed the case. The motion was timely, properly supported, and pending at the time of dismissal. Under *Sosna*, the mere fact that the defendant offered to satisfy the named plaintiff’s individual claim does not extinguish the court’s jurisdiction when a class action is pending and the class certification motion is still before the court.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has rejected the notion that a defendant may “buy off” a class action by offering full relief to the named plaintiff before class certification. In *Geraghty*, the Court held that “a case is not moot simply because the named plaintiff has obtained all the relief he seeks,” and that “the interests of the class members may be sufficiently distinct from those of the named plaintiff to justify the continuation of the suit.” 445 U.S. at 402, 100 S.Ct. 1202. The Court has consistently warned against allowing defendants to “evade the consequences of class-wide liability” by settling with the named plaintiff before class certification. *See, e.g.,* *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 40, 71 S.Ct. 104, 95 L.Ed. 36 (1950) (noting that “the court may not permit a party to avoid the consequences of a class action by settling with the named plaintiff before certification”).

The Tenth Circuit has previously recognized that a Rule 68 offer of judgment does not automatically moot a class action. In *Reed v. Heckler*, 878 F.2d 1277, 1280 (10th Cir.1989), we held that “a class action may survive the satisfaction of the named plaintiff’s individual claim if the class members’ claims remain live and the class action is still pending.” The court in *Reed* rejected the notion that a defendant could “buy off” a class action by offering full relief to the named plaintiff. The same principle applies here. Mr. Lucero’s motion for class certification was timely, and the class claims were not resolved. The district court’s failure to recognize this distinction renders its dismissal of the case for lack of jurisdiction erroneous.

Finally, the district court’s refusal to compel acceptance of the Rule 68 offer was proper, but that does not justify dismissal. The offer terminated only upon the court’s dismissal, and Mr. Lucero never waived his right to pursue class certification. The Supreme Court has made clear that “a party may not be deprived of the right to litigate a claim simply because the defendant has offered to satisfy it.” *Sosna*, 419 U.S. at 402, 95 S.Ct. 553. The court’s jurisdiction over a class action is not contingent on the named plaintiff’s individual claim remaining unresolved, but on the existence of a live controversy involving the class. Here, that controversy persists.

For these reasons, the district court erred in dismissing the complaint for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The case remains justiciable, and the court retains jurisdiction to consider Mr. Lucero’s motion for class certification.
