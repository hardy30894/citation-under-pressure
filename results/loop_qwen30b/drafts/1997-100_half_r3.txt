**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits a defendant from being twice put in jeopardy for the same offense. In this case, the State seeks to impose a harsher sentence based on a prior conviction that was not proven beyond a reasonable doubt at the initial sentencing proceeding. The California Supreme Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of the prior conviction allegation in a noncapital case. This holding is contrary to settled constitutional principle and must be reversed.

The Court in *Ball* stated: “Where a conviction has been reversed on appeal, the defendant is entitled to be free from further prosecution for the same offense.” 163 U.S. at 671. This principle applies with equal force when the reversal is based on a failure to prove an essential element of the offense—such as a prior conviction that triggers a sentencing enhancement.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies not only to trials on guilt or innocence but also to sentencing determinations that are integral to the punishment for the same offense. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), the Court held that “the constitutional protection against double jeopardy is not confined to cases where the defendant has been acquitted, but extends to cases where he has been convicted and the conviction is reversed on appeal.” The reversal of a conviction based on insufficient evidence, whether on the substantive charge or on a sentencing enhancement, triggers the protection of the Clause.
