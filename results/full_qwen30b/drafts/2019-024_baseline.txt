**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations under ERISA, which runs “from the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” bars a suit where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to resolve a critical tension between ERISA’s remedial purpose and the strict application of its time-bar. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that a plaintiff may avoid the statute of limitations by claiming ignorance of information he had the opportunity to access, undermines Congress’s intent to provide timely and certain resolution of fiduciary disputes. This Court should reverse.

### I. The Ninth Circuit Misapplied the “Actual Knowledge” Standard by Failing to Require Knowledge of the Breach Itself

ERISA’s three-year limitations period under § 1113(2) is triggered only when a plaintiff has “actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This is not a mere notice-of-transaction rule. The Supreme Court has made clear that “actual knowledge” means more than awareness of a fact; it requires knowledge of the *wrongfulness* of the conduct. In *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1832 (2015), the Court emphasized that the statute of limitations “does not begin to run until the plaintiff knows or should know of the breach.” The Court further explained that “a plaintiff cannot be said to have actual knowledge of a breach unless he knows that the conduct in question violates ERISA’s fiduciary standards.” Id. at 1833. Thus, knowledge of the *existence* of an investment is not equivalent to knowledge of its *imprudence*—a distinction central to this case.

The Ninth Circuit erred by conflating knowledge of a transaction with knowledge of a breach. It held that “actual knowledge” requires only awareness that the defendant acted and that those acts were imprudent. But this formulation is circular: it assumes the very breach that must be proven. The Court in *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988), correctly held that “the statute of limitations is triggered by [a claimant’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law.” But that principle applies only when the transaction itself is the breach. Here, the alleged breach is not the mere *existence* of alternative investments, but their *imprudent allocation*—a question of fiduciary judgment, not mere fact. As the Court in *Tibble* recognized, “a fiduciary’s decision to invest in a particular asset class is not a breach of duty unless it is imprudent.” 135 S. Ct. at 1832. A plaintiff cannot be deemed to have “actual knowledge” of an imprudence he never perceived.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s Rule Encourages Forum Shopping and Creates Unpredictability

The Ninth Circuit’s approach—where a plaintiff may avoid the statute of limitations by claiming he “did not recall” or “did not read” disclosures—creates a dangerous incentive to delay litigation. It allows plaintiffs to benefit from the very information they ignore, while shielding themselves from the consequences of their inaction. This contradicts the foundational principle that “a statute of limitations is not a mere technicality, but a substantive right designed to promote fairness and finality.” *Curtis v. Loether*, 415 U.S. 189, 196 (1974).

The Court has long held that “a plaintiff cannot sit on his rights and then complain that he did not know of the breach.” *Tibble*, 135 S. Ct. at 1833. The Ninth Circuit’s rule, by contrast, permits a plaintiff to “wait and see” whether a fund performs poorly, then claim ignorance of the investment strategy that caused the loss. This is precisely the kind of conduct Congress sought to prevent by enacting a clear, objective limitations period. As the Court explained in *Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Serv., Inc.*, 486 U.S. 825, 833 (1988), “the statute of limitations serves to protect defendants from stale claims and to encourage prompt assertion of rights.” The Ninth Circuit’s decision undermines that purpose by making the statute of limitations contingent on a plaintiff’s subjective memory or reading habits.

### III. The District Court Correctly Determined That Sulyma Had Actual Knowledge

The district court properly concluded that Sulyma had actual knowledge of the investments more than three years before filing suit. Intel provided multiple, contemporaneous disclosures—Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, Annual Disclosures, and Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notices—detailing the composition of the funds, including the allocation to hedge funds, private equity, and commodities. These documents were accessible via Intel’s website and were available to Sulyma during his employment. The fact that Sulyma claims he “did not recall” seeing them is not a defense to the statute of limitations. As this Court has held, “a plaintiff cannot avoid the statute of limitations by failing to read documents that were available to him.” *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002) (per curiam). The availability of information, not the plaintiff’s recollection of it, determines when the limitations period begins.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—requiring a fact-finder to determine whether Sulyma “had actual knowledge”—creates an untenable burden on courts and invites speculation. It transforms a statutory deadline into a subjective inquiry into a plaintiff’s mental state. That is not the law. As the Court stated in *Tibble*, “the limitations period begins when the plaintiff knows or should know of the breach.” 135 S. Ct. at 1832. Sulyma had the opportunity to know. He did not. That is enough.

### Conclusion

The Ninth Circuit’s decision misreads ERISA’s limitations period by equating awareness of a transaction with knowledge of a breach. It permits plaintiffs to evade the statute of limitations through inaction and selective memory.
