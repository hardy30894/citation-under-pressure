The Ninth Circuit erred in exempting documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA because they are "predominantly" for internal use and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard is unfounded in the statutory text and Supreme Court precedent.

The FOIA exemptions are "explicitly exclusive" and must be "narrowly construed," as established in Administrator, Federal Aviation Administration v. Robertson, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975) (quoting S.Rep. No. 813-89, at 3 (1965)) and Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976). The statutory language of Exemption 2, which exempts matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency," 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2), does not support a broad interpretation that encompasses documents like the ESQD arc maps.

In Rose, the Supreme Court considered the scope of Exemption 2 and noted that it was intended to cover "minor or trivial administrative matters" of no genuine public interest, citing S.Rep. No. 813-89, at 8 (1965). The Court also observed that the exemption might apply to more sensitive information, but only if its disclosure would "risk circumvention of agency regulation," 425 U.S. at 369. The ESQD arc maps, which are used for safety and risk mitigation purposes, do not fit within the narrow construction of Exemption 2.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on the "predominantly internal" standard, borrowed from the D.C. Circuit's decision in Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C.Cir.1981), is misplaced. This standard has no basis in pre-1970 Supreme Court precedent or the statutory text. Instead, it represents an unwarranted expansion of Exemption 2.

The correct interpretation of Exemption 2 is guided by the principles enunciated in Rose, which emphasize the need for a narrow construction of the exemptions. The ESQD arc maps do not relate "solely" to internal personnel rules and practices, and their disclosure does not risk circumvention of agency regulation in the manner contemplated by the statute. Therefore, the Ninth Circuit's decision to exempt these documents from disclosure under Exemption 2 was incorrect.
