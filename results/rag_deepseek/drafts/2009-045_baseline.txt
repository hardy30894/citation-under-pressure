## Argument

The question presented asks whether a reasonable attorneys’ fee under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes—in the rare and exceptional case supported by specific evidence. The decision below should be affirmed.

The lodestar method—hours reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate—is the starting point for determining a reasonable fee. *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433 (1983). But the lodestar is not conclusive. This Court has consistently recognized that upward adjustments may be justified in extraordinary circumstances. In *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), the Court expressly rejected the argument “that an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988.” *Id.* at 901. Quoting *Hensley*, the Court stated that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Id.* (internal quotation marks omitted). The Court required only that the fee applicant offer “specific evidence” that the quality of service was superior to what one reasonably should expect given the hourly rates charged, and that the success was exceptional. *Id.* at 899.

*Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air* (Delaware Valley I), 478 U.S. 546 (1986), reaffirmed this principle. The plurality explained that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible . . . in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” *Id.* at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901). Although the Court noted that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” it left open the possibility of an extraordinary case. *Id.* at 566 (emphasis added). Nothing in *Delaware Valley I* overruled *Blum*; indeed, the plurality cited *Blum* with approval throughout. *Id.* at 564, 568.

Petitioner’s argument that enhancements based on quality and results are categorically forbidden misreads these precedents. *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* do not erect a per se bar; they establish a high threshold. The enhancement is permissible only when the applicant presents specific evidence that the representation was exceptional and the results extraordinary. That is precisely what occurred here.

The district court made detailed findings based on its “own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The court noted that counsel’s “superb quality of representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee.” *Id.* at 1290. The results obtained were exceptional: a comprehensive injunction reforming Georgia’s foster care system, affecting thousands of children. The court’s findings were specific, detailed, and grounded in the record. This is precisely the “rare” and “exceptional” case that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* contemplate.

The circuits uniformly agree. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits all hold that a district court may enhance the lodestar for exceptional performance and results in appropriate cases. *See, e.g., Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008); *Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000). No circuit has adopted petitioner’s categorical rule.

Petitioner’s reliance on *City of Burlington v. Dague*, 505 U.S. 557 (1992), is misplaced. *Dague* addressed contingency enhancements, not quality-of-performance enhancements. The Court distinguished between the two, noting that contingency adjustments are generally impermissible because the lodestar already accounts for the risk of nonpayment. *Id.* at 565-66. But *Dague* did not overrule *Blum* or *Delaware Valley I* on quality enhancements. To the contrary, the Court reaffirmed that the lodestar is “presumed to be the reasonable fee,” but did not foreclose all adjustments. *Id.* at 562.

The district court’s enhancement was not an abuse of discretion. The court provided a “concise but clear explanation of its reasons,” as *Hensley* requires. 461 U.S. at 437. The enhancement was supported by specific evidence of superior performance and exceptional results. This Court should affirm the judgment below and hold that, in rare and exceptional cases, a fee enhancement based on quality of performance and results obtained is permissible under federal fee-shifting statutes.
