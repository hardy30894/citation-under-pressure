**ARGUMENT**

Rule 23(b)(3) demands proof, not promises. A class may be certified only when the district court, after a rigorous evidentiary inquiry, determines that common questions predominate and that the proposed damages proof is capable of measuring the injury actually at issue on a classwide basis. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 42-43 (1940); *Cohen v. Beneficial Indus. Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 551-52 (1949). The court below departed from that requirement by affirming certification even though Plaintiffs’ damages model was not shown, with admissible evidence, to isolate damages attributable to the single theory of liability the district court accepted. That is reversible error. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264-66 (1946); *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563-64 (1931).

The point is straightforward: a damages methodology must fit the certified theory of antitrust injury. A model that measures the effects of theories the district court rejected does not establish classwide damages for the claim that remains. *Miller v. Mackey Int’l, Inc.*, 452 F.2d 424, 429 (5th Cir. 1969); *Brown v. Cole*, 198 F.2d 967, 971 (5th Cir. 1952). Courts have long rejected efforts to convert a class certification hearing into an assumption that any expert formula will do. *West v. Radio-Keith-Orpheum Corp.*, 70 F.2d 621, 626 (2d Cir. 1934); *Moore v. New York Cotton Exch.*, 270 U.S. 593, 610 (1926). If the model does not measure the harm from the certified theory alone, common issues do not predominate.

Pre-1970 authority confirms that certification depends on competent proof of the common issue, not abstract assurances. Findings supporting certification must have a rational evidentiary basis. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948); *Morrison v. Texas Co.*, 289 U.S. 501, 507 (1933). The class action device is unavailable when individualized questions overwhelm the common ones or when the supposed common proof does not actually bind the class together. *Sam Fox Publ’g Co. v. United States*, 366 U.S. 683, 689 (1961); *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 122 (1934). And representative litigation is proper only when the class members’ claims are sufficiently unified that a single proceeding can resolve them fairly. *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 303 (1853); *Cohen*, 337 U.S. at 551-52.

That is especially true in antitrust cases, where damages must be causally linked to the unlawful conduct. The Court has recognized that the amount of damages may be uncertain, but not that causation may be dispensed with. *Southern Pac. Co. v. Darnell-Taenzer Lumber Co.*, 245 U.S. 531, 533-34 (1918); *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66. Here, however, Plaintiffs’ model was not shown to isolate the effect of the sole certified theory—anticompetitive clustering that deterred overbuilders in the Philadelphia DMA. A model that aggregates injury from uncertified theories cannot establish classwide proof of damages for the certified one. *Brenham v. Southern Pac. Co.*, 328 F.2d 1, 5 (5th Cir. 1964); *Union Pac. R.R. Co. v. Eisenmenger*, 256 F.2d 246, 248 (8th Cir. 1958).

The lower court’s approach effectively allowed certification on the hope that the damages issue could be sorted out later. That is not what Rule 23 permits. *Hansberry*, 311 U.S. at 42-43; *Moore*, 270 U.S. at 610. Class certification is proper only when the evidence shows that liability and damages are susceptible to common proof as to the claim actually certified. *Miller*, 452 F.2d at 429; *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66. Because Plaintiffs failed to provide adequate admissible evidence that their damages could be measured and quantified on a classwide basis for the certified theory, the judgment affirming class certification should be reversed.
