**ARGUMENT**

The District Court properly denied habeas relief because any error in failing to advise Petitioner of his right to appeal was harmless. Rule 32(a)(2) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure requires district courts to advise defendants of their appellate rights. *See* Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2) (1992). However, this Court has long held that procedural errors do not warrant reversal absent prejudice to the defendant. *See Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884) ("[A]n error committed in the course of a trial, which does not affect the substantial rights of the accused, is not ground for reversal."); *see also Lewis v. United States*, 279 U.S. 637, 640 (1929) ("[A]n error which does not affect substantial rights is not ground for reversal.").

The District Court found—and the record supports—that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal at sentencing. This factual finding is entitled to deference. *See Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573-74 (1985) (factual findings reviewed for clear error). Given Petitioner’s knowledge, the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) was satisfied, and any error was harmless. *See Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) ("If, when all is said and done, the conviction is sure that the error did not influence the jury, or had but very slight effect, the verdict and the judgment should stand.").

This Court has repeatedly applied harmless-error review to procedural violations, including those under Rule 32(a)(2). *See Bruno v. United States*, 308 U.S. 287, 293 (1939) ("The error, if any, was harmless."); *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946) ("The inquiry cannot be merely whether there was error in the abstract or whether there was enough to raise a doubt of guilt."). The harmless-error standard applies regardless of whether the error is constitutional or procedural. *See Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 21 (1967) ("[B]efore a federal constitutional error can be held harmless, the court must be able to declare a belief that it was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.").

The Court of Appeals correctly concluded that Petitioner’s awareness of his appellate rights rendered any Rule 32(a)(2) violation harmless. This aligns with this Court’s recognition that procedural rules are designed to promote justice, and their purpose is satisfied when the defendant’s rights are otherwise protected. *See United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954) ("[T]he purpose of a procedural rule is to promote the proper administration of justice, and if that purpose is otherwise served, the rule may be deemed satisfied.").

Finally, the District Court’s harmless-error analysis is consistent with this Court’s longstanding precedent. *See Salinger v. Loisel*, 265 U.S. 224, 231-32 (1924) ("[A]n error which does not prejudice the accused is not ground for reversal."); *Salinas v. United States*, 276 U.S. 158, 163 (1928) ("[A]n error which does not prejudice the rights of the accused is not ground for reversal.").

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
