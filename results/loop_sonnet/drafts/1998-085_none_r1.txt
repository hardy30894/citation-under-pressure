ARGUMENT

I. The common law has always required conduct of an aggravated character before punitive damages may be awarded—proof of intent or recklessness alone has never sufficed.

This Court's punitive-damages jurisprudence has, from its earliest expressions, focused on the character of the wrong committed, not merely on the state of mind that accompanied it. In Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1851), the Court explained that a jury awarding exemplary damages looks to "the enormity of [the defendant's] offence rather than the measure of compensation to the plaintiff." Id. at 371. That formulation is telling: it is the enormity of the offense—its objective gravity—that justifies punishment, not simply the presence of some culpable mental state that might accompany conduct falling well short of that threshold.

This Court's decisions in the years that followed confirmed the same understanding. In Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore Railroad Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202 (1858), and in Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Humes, 115 U.S. 512 (1885), the Court treated exemplary damages as a penal remedy, appropriate only where the defendant's conduct carried the aggravated quality that has traditionally distinguished punishable wrongs from ordinary civil violations. See Humes, 115 U.S. at 521. And in Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), the Court likewise recognized exemplary damages as compensation-plus-punishment, available only where circumstances of aggravation attended the wrong—not wherever a plaintiff proved intent or disregard of another's rights standing alone.

This distinction between mental state and the independent requirement of egregious conduct is nowhere clearer than in Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893). There, this Court held that a railroad corporation could not be held liable for punitive damages arising from a conductor's wrongful ejection of a passenger absent proof that the corporation itself had authorized, participated in, or ratified conduct of a sufficiently aggravated character. See id. at 108–09. The conductor's own motive, whatever it may have been, was not enough; something more—an aggravated wrong attributable to the defendant whose punishment was sought—was required. Prentice thus stands for the proposition, directly applicable here, that punitive liability cannot rest on state of mind divorced from the objective seriousness of the conduct and the defendant's relationship to it.

Finally, Huntington v. Attrill, 146 U.S. 657 (1892), reinforces the penal character of exemplary damages by distinguishing remedial awards, which merely compensate, from penal awards, which punish and deter. See id. at 667–68. That penal label has traditionally attached only to conduct the community regards as warranting punishment—not to every instance of intentional or even reckless wrongdoing.

II. Congress legislated against this settled common-law backdrop, and its choice of the terms "malice" and "reckless indifference" should be read accordingly.

Congress did not write § 1981a of Title VII on a clean slate. It selected words—"malice" and "reckless indifference"—that carry more than a century of common-law meaning behind them. This Court has long instructed that "where Congress borrows terms of art in which are accumulated the legal tradition and meaning of centuries of practice, it presumably knows and adopts the cluster of ideas that were attached to each borrowed word in the body of learning from which it was taken." Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952). The common-law tradition surrounding punitive damages—reflected in Day, Quigley, Humes, Barry, Prentice, and Huntington—consistently demanded conduct of an aggravated, egregious character before punishment, rather than compensation, was permitted.

This Court should presume that Congress intended the same limitation when it employed that vocabulary in § 1981a. To read the statute otherwise—permitting punitive damages upon nothing more than proof of intentional discrimination—would sever the words Congress chose from the accumulated legal tradition that gives them meaning, and would convert every ordinary intentional-discrimination verdict into a potential punitive-damages case. Nothing in the statute suggests Congress intended so dramatic a departure from settled practice.

III. Imputing punitive liability to an employer requires independent proof of aggravated corporate conduct, not merely an individual agent's state of mind.

Even where an individual actor's state of mind might satisfy § 1981a's malice or recklessness standard, imputing that mental state to the employer for purposes of punishment demands more. This Court's decision in New York Central & Hudson River Railroad Co. v. United States, 212 U.S. 481 (1909), recognized that corporate responsibility for an agent's wrongful acts depends on whether those acts were committed within the scope of the agent's authority and in furtherance of the corporation's business. See id. at 493–95. Read together with Prentice, that principle confirms that an employer's exposure to the penal sanction of punitive damages cannot rest on an isolated employee's state of mind alone; it must be tied to conduct—and to the employer's own connection to that conduct—sufficiently aggravated to justify visiting punishment on the organization itself.

For these reasons, an award of punitive damages under Title VII requires proof that the employer's conduct was egregious, independent of the actor's state of mind, and this Court should affirm.
