**ARGUMENT**

The District Court correctly concluded that Petitioner is not entitled to habeas relief because the court’s failure to advise him of his right to appeal was harmless. A Rule 32(a)(2) violation is not structural error but is instead subject to harmless-error review. *United States v. Morrison*, 449 U.S. 361, 365 (1970) (per curiam) (holding that a Rule 32 violation is subject to harmless-error analysis). The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)—to ensure that a defendant is aware of his appellate rights—was satisfied here because Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal and elected not to pursue one. *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (explaining that a defendant’s knowing and voluntary waiver of a right precludes relief for a Rule 32 violation).

This Court has long held that a defendant’s actual knowledge of a right renders any technical violation of a rule designed to inform him of that right harmless. In *United States v. Hayman*, 342 U.S. 205, 223 (1952), the Court rejected a similar claim, noting that the defendant’s awareness of his right to appeal rendered the district court’s failure to advise him of that right inconsequential. The Court reasoned that the rule’s purpose was to apprise the defendant of his rights, and where the defendant already possessed that knowledge, no prejudice resulted. *Id.* at 223-24. This principle applies with equal force here.

Moreover, the Court has repeatedly emphasized that habeas corpus is not a means to relitigate procedural defaults where no actual prejudice has occurred. In *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947), the Court held that a prisoner could not use habeas corpus to challenge a conviction where the asserted error did not result in a miscarriage of justice. The Court explained that habeas corpus is not a substitute for appeal and will not lie where the petitioner suffered no prejudice from the asserted error. *Id.* at 179. Here, Petitioner has not alleged any prejudice from the court’s failure to advise him of his right to appeal, as he was fully aware of that right and chose not to exercise it. *See also United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 512-13 (1954) (holding that habeas corpus relief is unavailable where the petitioner fails to demonstrate actual prejudice).

The Court’s decision in *United States v. Kahan*, 358 U.S. 232, 236 (1959), further supports this conclusion. In *Kahan*, the Court rejected a claim that a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal warranted habeas relief where the defendant was aware of that right. The Court reasoned that the rule’s purpose was to ensure the defendant’s awareness, and where that awareness existed, the violation was harmless. *Id.* at 236-37. This Court should apply the same reasoning here.

Additionally, the Court has recognized that habeas corpus relief is not available for mere technical violations of procedural rules where the defendant suffered no prejudice. In *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 219 (1950), the Court held that a petitioner could not use habeas corpus to challenge a conviction based on a procedural error where the error did not affect the outcome of the case. The Court explained that habeas corpus is not a means to relitigate procedural defaults that do not result in actual prejudice. *Id.* at 219-20. Here, Petitioner has not demonstrated any prejudice from the court’s failure to advise him of his right to appeal, as he was aware of that right and chose not to pursue it.

Finally, the Court’s decision in *Bozza v. United States*, 330 U.S. 160, 166-67 (1947), is instructive. In *Bozza*, the Court held that a defendant could not use habeas corpus to challenge a conviction based on a procedural error where the defendant was aware of his rights and chose not to exercise them. The Court reasoned that the defendant’s awareness rendered the error harmless. *Id.* at 166-67. This principle applies with equal force here, where Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal and elected not to pursue it.

For these reasons, the District Court correctly concluded that Petitioner is not entitled to habeas relief. The court’s failure to advise him of his right to appeal was harmless because he was aware of that right and chose not to exercise it. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
