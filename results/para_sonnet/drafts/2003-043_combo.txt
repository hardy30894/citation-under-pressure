# ARGUMENT

## I. Because Each Partner Is a Separate "Taxpayer" Under the Code, the Assessment Against the Partnership Did Not Extend the Limitations Period as to the Partners.

Section 6501(a) requires the government to assess a tax "within three years after the return was filed" or to bring a collection suit within that period. Only a timely assessment triggers the ten-year extension of § 6502(a). The assessment here ran against Marina Cabrillo Partners alone. Because a partnership and its individual partners are separately identifiable "persons" and "taxpayers" under 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (a)(14), an assessment against the entity cannot substitute for an assessment against the individuals who compose it.

This Court's decisions confirm that the identity of the assessed taxpayer controls whether the statute has been extended as to a particular person. In *United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. 489 (1930), the government attempted to collect a corporation's unpaid tax from the corporation's former shareholders as transferees, relying on a timely assessment made against the corporation itself. This Court held that the shareholders' liability was governed by a limitations period running independently of the corporate assessment, because the shareholders were not the taxpayer against whom that assessment had been made. The same logic controls here: an assessment against the Partnership fixes a limitations period for the Partnership, but it does nothing to extend the ordinary three-year period applicable to Debtors as separate taxpayers.

*Bull v. United States*, 295 U.S. 247 (1935), likewise establishes that an assessment operates as a formal record of liability against the particular taxpayer named in it, functioning much like a judgment against that taxpayer alone. An assessment that identifies only the Partnership cannot, by its own force, create a record of liability—or the accompanying ten-year collection period—against Debtors individually.

## II. Ninth Circuit Precedent Requires an Individualized Assessment or Judgment Before the Government May Collect From a Partner.

This Circuit's own precedent, which the panel correctly applied below, forecloses the government's theory. In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the district court held that a lien could not attach to an individual's property where the underlying assessment named only the partnership and "an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers," because § 6203 and its implementing regulations require that "the taxpayer be identified" in a valid assessment. *Coson*, 169 F. Supp. at 675–76. This Court affirmed, and nothing in the affirmance disturbed that reasoning.

*Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), on which the government relies, does not help it. The district court opinion underlying *Young* expressly recognized that, absent an individual assessment or a court judgment, "it is unnecessary and superfluous to name the individual partners in the assessment" only where the tax already has been *voluntarily paid*—not where the government seeks compulsory collection. 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054. The court there acknowledged that to *collect* the tax from a partner who has not paid, the government must "resort to court action" or make an individual assessment. *Id.* at 76,050. This Circuit's affirmance did not reject that limitation. *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960). Debtors do not dispute that they are substantively liable for the Partnership's debt under state law; they dispute only that the government may enforce that liability without ever having assessed them or obtained a judgment against them within the applicable period.

## III. Statutes of Limitations on Tax Collection Must Be Strictly Construed Against the Government.

This Court has repeatedly instructed that limitations periods governing the government's power to collect taxes are to be construed strictly, and that taxpayers are entitled to rely on the repose those periods afford. In *E. I. Du Pont de Nemours & Co. v. Davis*, 264 U.S. 456 (1924), the Court treated assessment as a discrete, formal act tied to a specific taxpayer's liability, underscoring that procedural prerequisites to collection are not to be relaxed. In *Bowers v. New York & Albany Lighterage Co.*, 273 U.S. 346 (1927), the Court likewise held the government to the specific procedural steps Congress prescribed for extending its collection remedies, refusing to read implied extensions into the statute. And in *United States v. A. S. Kreider Co.*, 313 U.S. 443 (1941), the Court required a proceeding directed at the actual liable party—there, in a receivership—before treating the limitations period as satisfied, rejecting the government's effort to rely on proceedings against a different entity.

*Stange v. United States*, 282 U.S. 270 (1930), reinforces the same principle in the waiver context: an extension of the limitations period is personal to the taxpayer who is bound by it and cannot be presumed to run against persons who did not execute or otherwise trigger the extension. A partnership's assessment, like a waiver executed only in the partnership's name, cannot be imputed to bind partners who were never separately assessed.

## CONCLUSION

Because Debtors were never separately assessed, and because no judgment was obtained against them within the three-year period of § 6501(a), the ten-year extension of § 6502(a) never began to run as to them. The decisions below correctly disallowed the government's claims, and this Court should affirm.
