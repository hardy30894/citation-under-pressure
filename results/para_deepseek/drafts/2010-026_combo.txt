**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by exempting the ESQD arc maps under FOIA Exemption 2. That exemption covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The court’s “predominantly internal” standard rewrites the statute, substituting “predominantly” for Congress’s chosen word “solely.” This Court has long held that “solely” means exclusively—nothing else. *United States v. Standard Brewery*, 251 U.S. 210, 218 (1920). The maps at issue are technical data about explosive safety distances; they are not personnel rules or practices. They therefore fall outside Exemption 2’s plain text.

**I. The Plain Language of Exemption 2 Forecloses the Ninth Circuit’s “Predominantly Internal” Test.**

FOIA’s exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be “narrowly construed.” *American Mail Line v. Gulick*, 411 F.2d 696, 699 (D.C. Cir. 1969). The statute’s command that exempt matters be “related solely” to internal personnel rules and practices leaves no room for a “predominantly” standard. When Congress uses “solely,” it means “exclusively” and “only.” *Standard Brewery*, 251 U.S. at 218. The Ninth Circuit’s test dilutes that requirement, permitting withholding whenever a document is *mostly* internal—even if it also concerns external operations, safety, or the public. That contravenes the fundamental principle that exemptions from disclosure statutes are to be strictly confined. *Piedmont & Northern Ry. v. ICC*, 286 U.S. 299, 311 (1932); *St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. Bachmann*, 285 U.S. 112, 119 (1932).

The ESQD arc maps are not “personnel rules and practices.” They are technical calculations and maps used to design ammunition storage facilities and to mitigate explosive chain reactions. They govern the physical layout of ordnance, not the conduct of agency employees. Early FOIA decisions uniformly held that Exemption 2 covers only trivial internal administrative matters—parking, lunch hours, sick leave—not substantive operational data. *Epstein v. Resor*, 296 F. Supp. 214, 216 (N.D. Cal. 1969) (Exemption 2 “was intended to exempt from disclosure only such matters as are trivial and of no genuine public interest”); *Sonderegger v. United States*, 298 F. Supp. 625, 627 (D. Idaho 1969) (same); *Consumers Union v. Veterans Administration*, 301 F. Supp. 796, 800 (S.D.N.Y. 1969) (Exemption 2 “does not apply to matters of genuine public interest”). The maps here are of profound public interest—they reveal the blast zones of explosives stored near populated communities. They cannot be trivial internal rules.

**II. The Risk-of-Circumvention Test Has No Statutory Basis and Cannot Salvage the Ninth Circuit’s Error.**

The Ninth Circuit also required that disclosure “present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation.” That test appears nowhere in Exemption 2. Congress did not condition Exemption 2 on any showing of harm; it simply exempted matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices.” The court imported the circumvention requirement from later judicial gloss, but the statute’s plain meaning controls. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (“where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning, the duty of interpretation does not arise”). Even if a risk-of-circumvention inquiry were permissible, it cannot transform non-personnel data into personnel rules. The maps are not rules at all; they are scientific measurements. No amount of agency concern about misuse can change what the document is.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Conflicts with the Structure and Purpose of FOIA.**

FOIA reflects “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure.” *American Mail Line*, 411 F.2d at 699. Exemptions are to be “narrowly construed” to avoid swallowing the rule. *Id.* The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test invites agencies to withhold any document that is used primarily inside the agency, regardless of its subject matter. That would eviscerate FOIA’s core purpose. The maps here are not internal personnel matters; they are operational safety data that directly affect the public. The dissent below correctly observed that the Navy’s failure to classify the maps under Exemption 1 undermines its claim that they are too sensitive for Exemption 2. The majority’s expansion of Exemption 2 distorts Congress’s careful balance.

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit erred by adopting a “predominantly internal” standard that contradicts the plain language of Exemption 2 and the narrow construction required by this Court’s precedents. The ESQD arc maps are not “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” of the Navy. The judgment should be reversed.
