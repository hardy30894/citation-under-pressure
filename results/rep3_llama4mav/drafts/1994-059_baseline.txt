## ARGUMENT

I. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT AMENDMENT 73'S TERM LIMITS ON FEDERAL CONGRESSIONAL CANDIDATES VIOLATE THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.

The Qualifications Clauses of the U.S. Constitution, Art. I, § 2, cl. 2 and Art. I, § 3, cl. 3, establish exclusive and uniform requirements for membership in Congress. The Constitution sets forth specific qualifications for Representatives and Senators, and the States may not add to or alter these qualifications. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969). In Powell, this Court held that the House of Representatives could not exclude a duly elected Representative who met the constitutional qualifications, emphasizing that the Constitution's Qualifications Clauses are exclusive.

The framers of the Constitution deliberately chose to establish uniform national qualifications for Congress to prevent the fragmentation that would result from allowing each State to impose its own additional qualifications. See The Federalist No. 52 (J. Madison). Amendment 73's attempt to limit ballot access for federal congressional candidates based on their service history is, in effect, an additional qualification that contravenes the Constitution's exclusive requirements.

The Tenth Circuit's decision in Thorsted v. Gregoire, 841 F. Supp. 1068 (W.D. Wash. 1994), aff'd sub nom. Term Limits Leadership Council, Inc. v. Gregoire, 45 F.3d 1075 (9th Cir. 1994), and this Court's precedents make clear that state-imposed term limits on federal congressional candidates are unconstitutional. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly applied these principles in holding that Section 3 of Amendment 73 is unconstitutional.

II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT THE UNCONSTITUTIONALITY OF SECTION 3 RENDERS THE ENTIRE AMENDMENT 73 INVALID.

The severability clause in Amendment 73 cannot save the remainder of the amendment because the provisions are inextricably linked. The ballot title and the Preamble to Amendment 73 presented term limits for state and federal officeholders as a unified package, making it impossible for voters to approve one without the other. The sponsors of Amendment 73 created this uncertainty and failed to provide a clear, separate vote on term limits for state officeholders.

In Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964), the Arkansas Supreme Court established that the test for severability is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion. Here, it is impossible to determine whether the voters would have approved term limits for state officeholders without the inclusion of federal congressional term limits. The lack of a clear, separate vote on state term limits renders the entire amendment invalid.

The practice of coupling a legitimate objective with one of doubtful legality, as seen in Amendment 73, is misleading and unfair to voters. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the unconstitutionality of Section 3 renders the entire Amendment 73 invalid.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed. Amendment 73's term limits on federal congressional candidates are unconstitutional, and the entire amendment is invalid due to the inextricable link between its provisions.
