**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON APPELLANT’S CONSPIRACY CLAIM UNDER THE RAILWAY LABOR ACT (“RLA”), 45 U.S.C. § 153.**

The RLA provides a comprehensive scheme for the resolution of labor disputes in the railroad industry, including the enforcement of collective bargaining agreements through private arbitration. *See Atchison, T. & S.F. Ry. Co. v. United Transp. Union, 473 U.S. 380, 386 (1985)* (“The RLA establishes a mandatory, exclusive method for resolving disputes between railroads and their employees.”). To state a claim for conspiracy under the RLA, a plaintiff must allege a combination or agreement between a union and an employer to deprive the plaintiff of rights under the RLA. *See Hawaiian Airlines, Inc. v. Norris, 512 U.S. 246, 261 (1994)* (“A conspiracy claim under the RLA requires a showing of a combination or agreement to deprive an employee of rights under the Act.”).

Here, Appellant presented evidence that UTU and CSX conspired to deprive him of his right to a fair arbitration hearing under the RLA. Specifically, Appellant demonstrated that UTU’s representative, Earley, failed to advocate for his position during the executive session and that CSX’s representative, Emerick, participated in the improper exclusion of Appellant from that session. *See id.* at 262 (noting that a union’s breach of its duty of fair representation may give rise to liability under the RLA). The district court erred in disregarding this evidence and granting summary judgment. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986)* (“Summary judgment is appropriate only if there is no genuine issue of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”).

II. **THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN AWARDING DAMAGES AND ATTORNEYS’ FEES ON DEFENDANTS’ COUNTERCLAIMS UNDER THE FEDERAL WIRETAP ACT (“THE ACT”), 18 U.S.C. § 2510 ET SEQ.**

The Act prohibits the intentional interception of oral communications where the parties have a reasonable expectation of privacy. *See 18 U.S.C. § 2511(1)(a)*; *Babcock v. Schindler, 789 F.2d 439, 442 (6th Cir. 1986)* (“The Act creates a private right of action for damages against any person who intercepts a wire, oral, or electronic communication.”). However, the Act does not apply where the interception occurs in a setting where there is no reasonable expectation of privacy. *See United States v. Amen, 831 F.2d 373, 378 (2d Cir. 1987)* (“The Act does not prohibit the recording of conversations in public places or where the parties have no reasonable expectation of privacy.”).

In this case, the executive session was a private, internal deliberation among the PLB members, UTU, and CSX. Appellant had no reasonable expectation of privacy in the executive session, as he was not a party to the deliberations and was excluded from the room. *See id.* at 379 (finding no reasonable expectation of privacy in a private meeting where the plaintiff was not present). The district court erred in concluding that Appellant’s recording of the executive session violated the Act. *See also United States v. Jones, 542 F.3d 385, 391 (6th Cir. 2008)* (holding that the Act does not apply where the interception occurs in a setting where there is no reasonable expectation of privacy).

Moreover, even if the Act applied, the district court erred in awarding damages and attorneys’ fees. The Act permits recovery of statutory damages only where the interception was willful or intentional. *See 18 U.S.C. § 2520(c)(2)(A)*; *Babock, 789 F.2d at 443* (“Statutory damages are available only where the interception was willful or intentional.”). Here, Appellant did not willfully or intentionally violate the Act; he merely recorded a conversation that he believed was not private. *See Amen, 831 F.2d at 379* (finding no willful or intentional violation where the plaintiff did not know the conversation was private).

Finally, the district court erred in awarding attorneys’ fees. The Act permits recovery of attorneys’ fees only where the prevailing party has demonstrated that the opposing party’s claim was frivolous, unreasonable, or groundless. *See 18 U.S.C. § 2520(b)(3)*; *Babock, 789 F.2d at 444* (“Attorneys’ fees are available only where the opposing party’s claim was frivolous, unreasonable, or groundless.”). Here, Appellant’s claim was not frivolous, unreasonable, or groundless; he had a good faith belief that the executive session was not private. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s orders granting summary judgment on his RLA conspiracy claim and awarding damages and attorneys’ fees on Defendants’ counterclaims under the Act.
