**ARGUMENT**

The case is moot. Article III’s case-or-controversy requirement demands that a live dispute persist throughout litigation. *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895). When the challenged policy is repealed and no plaintiff remains subject to it, federal courts lack jurisdiction. *Hall v. Beals*, 396 U.S. 45, 48 (1969) (per curiam); *Golden v. Zwickler*, 394 U.S. 103, 108 (1969). Here, Georgia Gwinnett College revised its speech policies while the suit was pending, and Uzuegbunam graduated. The only remaining claim is for nominal damages. That is insufficient to avoid mootness.

**I. The Policy Change Extinguished All Ongoing Controversy**

A plaintiff must present a “definite and concrete” controversy, not one that is hypothetical or speculative. *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240 (1937). Once the law that allegedly caused injury has been repealed, no controversy remains as to prospective relief. *Golden*, 394 U.S. at 108; *California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R. Co.*, 149 U.S. 308, 314 (1893) (dismissing as moot where the challenged rates had ceased to operate). The new college policy permits expressive activity anywhere on campus without a permit, and the challenged code-of-conduct provision has been removed. Because the policies are gone, both the facial and as-applied challenges to prospective relief are moot. *Hall*, 396 U.S. at 48.

**II. Nominal Damages Do Not Revive a Moot Case**

A plaintiff cannot “maintain a suit” solely to recover nominal damages after the underlying controversy has ended. *Mills*, 159 U.S. at 653. Nominal damages are a technical remedy that presupposes a live controversy; they cannot create one where none exists. *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42 (1943) (per curiam). In *Atherton Mills v. Johnston*, 259 U.S. 13, 16 (1922), the Court dismissed an otherwise moot challenge to an expired federal statute even though the plaintiff sought damages. Likewise, in *Brownlow v. Schwartz*, 261 U.S. 216, 218 (1923), the demolition of the subject building made the case moot despite a claim for damages. The only exception is when the plaintiff suffers a concrete, continuing injury through collateral consequences. *Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 55–56 (1968). Here, the complaint alleged only an abstract violation of constitutional rights, not any actual injury. Abstract injury cannot sustain a case after the policy has been repealed. *Golden*, 394 U.S. at 108–09.

**III. The Voluntary Cessation Doctrine Does Not Apply**

The voluntary cessation doctrine can avoid mootness only where the defendant remains free to resume the challenged conduct. *Southern Pac. Terminal Co. v. ICC*, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911). Here, the college’s revision was formal and permanent, removing the challenged provisions from the student handbook. The college has not paused enforcement; it has adopted a new policy governing all speech on campus. Nothing suggests the college will reinstate an unconstitutional restriction. *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301, 305 (1964). Thus, there is no live controversy.

**Conclusion**

Because the challenged policies were repealed and no plaintiff remains subject to them, the case is moot. A prayer for nominal damages cannot supply the ongoing controversy required by Article III. The district court properly dismissed the complaint.
