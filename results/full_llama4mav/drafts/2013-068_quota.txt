The Massachusetts statute creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that does not violate the First Amendment. The First Circuit correctly upheld the law, and its decision should be affirmed.

The buffer zone is content-neutral, as it applies to all individuals, regardless of their viewpoint or message. The law does not burden speech based on its content, but rather regulates the place and manner of speech to ensure public safety and facilitate access to healthcare facilities. See Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 719-20 (2000) (upholding a similar Colorado statute as content-neutral); Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (explaining that a regulation is content-neutral if it is "justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech").

The buffer zone is also narrowly tailored to serve significant government interests. The state has a legitimate interest in protecting the safety and well-being of patients and staff at healthcare facilities, as well as ensuring that individuals can access these facilities without obstruction or harassment. See Madsen v. Women's Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753, 768 (1994) (recognizing the government's interest in protecting healthcare facilities and their patients); Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western New York, 519 U.S. 357, 376 (1997) (upholding a floating buffer zone around healthcare facilities as a reasonable means of protecting patients and staff).

The buffer zone leaves open ample alternative channels of communication for individuals to express their views. Petitioners can still engage in oral speech, distribute literature, display signs, and pray outside the buffer zone. See Hill, 530 U.S. at 726-27 (noting that the Colorado statute left open alternative channels of communication); Ward, 491 U.S. at 802 (explaining that a regulation is valid if it leaves open "ample alternative channels of communication").

Petitioners' argument that the buffer zone is not narrowly tailored because it burdens their ability to engage in effective "sidewalk counseling" is unpersuasive. The First Amendment does not guarantee a right to engage in speech in a particular location or manner. See Heffron v. International Soc'y for Krishna Consciousness, Inc., 452 U.S. 640, 647 (1981) (explaining that the First Amendment does not guarantee a right to engage in speech in a particular location). The buffer zone is a reasonable regulation that balances the competing interests of public safety, access to healthcare facilities, and free speech.

The First Circuit's decision is consistent with this Court's precedent, including Hill v. Colorado, which upheld a similar buffer zone statute. See also McCullen v. Coakley, 571 F.3d 167, 176-81 (1st Cir. 2009) (upholding the Massachusetts statute as content-neutral and a valid time, place, and manner regulation). The Court should affirm the First Circuit's decision and uphold the Massachusetts statute as constitutional.

Furthermore, the law of the case doctrine supports the First Circuit's decision, as the issue of the statute's facial constitutionality was previously decided in McCullen I. See United States v. Matthews, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011) (explaining the law of the case doctrine). Petitioners have not shown that there has been a significant change in controlling legal authority or that the earlier decision was blatantly erroneous. See id. at 14 (listing exceptions to the law of the case doctrine).

In conclusion, the Massachusetts statute is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that does not violate the First Amendment. The First Circuit's decision upholding the law should be affirmed, as it is consistent with this Court's precedent and the law of the case doctrine. See Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310, 340 (2010) (explaining the importance of stare decisis); Frisby v. Schultz, 487 U.S. 474, 488 (1988) (upholding a municipal ordinance prohibiting picketing outside a residence as a valid time, place, and manner regulation).
