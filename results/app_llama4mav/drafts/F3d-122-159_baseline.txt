## Argument

The Bureau of Prisons ("BOP") erred in denying Victor Roussos eligibility for early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) after he successfully completed a 500-hour drug treatment program. The BOP's decision was based on its classification of Roussos' drug conspiracy conviction as a "crime of violence" due to a two-level sentence enhancement for possession of a firearm during a drug trafficking offense. However, this classification is inconsistent with the congressional statute and the BOP's own regulations.

The statute authorizing early release, 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), allows for a reduction in sentence for prisoners convicted of "nonviolent offenses." Although the statute does not define "nonviolent offense," the BOP regulations define it by referencing the term "crime of violence" as used in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). See 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 (1995). Section 924(c)(3) defines a "crime of violence" as an offense that is a felony and either (A) has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person or property of another, or (B) involves a substantial risk that physical force may be used in the course of committing the offense.

The BOP's Program Statement 5162.02, which further defines "crime of violence," is overly broad and inconsistent with the statute and regulations. The Program Statement provides that a conviction under 21 U.S.C. § 846 (conspiracy to distribute narcotics) is a crime of violence if the sentencing court enhanced the sentence for possession of a dangerous weapon. However, this interpretation ignores the fact that Roussos' underlying offense, conspiracy to distribute narcotics, does not inherently involve violence.

In Davis v. Crabtree, 109 F.3d 566 (9th Cir. 1997), the Ninth Circuit held that a sentencing enhancement for possession of a firearm does not render a drug conspiracy conviction a crime of violence for purposes of § 3621(e)(2)(B). The court reasoned that the BOP's interpretation would "effectively nullify the statute" by allowing the BOP to deny early release to prisoners who have committed nonviolent offenses. Id. at 568.

Similarly, in Byrd v. Hasty, 142 F.3d 1395 (11th Cir. 1998), the Eleventh Circuit held that a drug conspiracy conviction is not a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3) simply because the sentencing court enhanced the sentence for possession of a firearm. The court noted that the BOP's interpretation would lead to an "absurd result" where prisoners convicted of nonviolent offenses are denied early release due to a sentencing enhancement. Id. at 1398.

The Third Circuit should follow the reasoning of the Ninth and Eleventh Circuits and hold that Roussos' drug conspiracy conviction is not a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3). The BOP's classification of Roussos' offense as a crime of violence based on a sentencing enhancement is inconsistent with the statute and regulations. Therefore, Roussos is eligible for early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), and the district court's denial of his habeas petition should be reversed.

## Conclusion

The BOP's denial of Roussos' eligibility for early release was erroneous, and the district court's denial of his habeas petition should be reversed. The Third Circuit should hold that a drug conspiracy conviction is not a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3) simply because the sentencing court enhanced the sentence for possession of a firearm, and that Roussos is eligible for early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B).
