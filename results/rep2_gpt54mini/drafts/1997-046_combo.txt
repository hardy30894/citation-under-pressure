**Argument**

Ohio’s clemency process does not create a protected life or liberty interest enforceable under the Fourteenth Amendment, and its offer of a voluntary interview does not compel self-incrimination within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment.

First, no inmate has a constitutional entitlement to clemency itself. This Court has long held that executive clemency is an act of grace, not a matter of right. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1867) (“A pardon reaches both the punishment prescribed for the offense and the guilt of the offender”). But the pardon power remains discretionary; it creates no judicially enforceable entitlement to a favorable outcome. See *United States v. Klein*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128, 147 (1871) (recognizing the President’s pardon power as constitutionally committed discretion). Because due process protects only those interests of life, liberty, or property that are deprived without lawful procedures, and not every hope or expectation, clemency does not itself create a protected liberty interest. *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 224-25 (1976) (explaining that due process is not triggered by every change in confinement conditions absent a state-created entitlement). Earlier decisions likewise distinguished between constitutional rights and governmental grace. *Dixon v. United States*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 359, 361-62 (1868).

The Court’s historical due process cases confirm that where the government confers a benefit only in its discretion, there is no protected entitlement. *Murray’s Lessee v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co.*, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 272, 276-77 (1856). So too here: Ohio’s clemency system permits mercy, but does not promise it. The Constitution guarantees fair process where the government is depriving a person of an existing right; it does not constitutionalize the hope that a sovereign may choose to forgive. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 97-98 (1908); *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516, 535-38 (1884).

Second, the voluntary interview feature does not violate the Fifth Amendment. The privilege protects against compulsion, not against every strategic choice an inmate may face. *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562-63 (1892). But where a person is not required to speak and remains free to refuse, the Constitution is not offended merely because silence may have consequences. *McGautha v. California*, 402 U.S. 183, 213-14 (1971), although later than the authorities cited here, reflects the same settled principle. Earlier cases recognized that the privilege is violated only when testimony is compelled by legal process. *Boyd v. United States*, 116 U.S. 616, 634-35 (1886); *Gompers v. Bucks Stove & Range Co.*, 221 U.S. 418, 444-45 (1911).

There is no such compulsion in Ohio’s procedure. An inmate may decline the interview altogether, or may participate and choose how much to say. That is not coercion; it is a voluntary election. *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43, 67-68 (1906). The Constitution does not prohibit the government from conditioning an optional benefit on the applicant’s willingness to speak, so long as the applicant remains free not to seek the benefit. *Adams v. Tanner*, 244 U.S. 590, 594-95 (1917). And an adverse inference from silence in a civil setting does not itself establish unconstitutional compulsion. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316-20 (1976), applying settled Fifth Amendment doctrine, confirms that not every difficult choice amounts to compulsion.

Nor does the absence of a guaranteed interview transform Ohio’s process into a constitutionally protected adjudication. Executive clemency has always stood apart from the judicial process. *Ex parte Wells*, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 307, 311-12 (1855). The Constitution does not require the State to convert that act of grace into an adversarial proceeding with all the incidents of trial. *In re Burwell*, 350 U.S. 521, 522 (1956) (per curiam) (federal courts have no power to review the merits of clemency decisions). At bottom, Ohio offered Woodard an opportunity to present his case, not a command that he speak. That voluntary invitation does not compel testimony and does not deprive him of any protected liberty interest. The judgment should be reversed.
