**ARGUMENT**

**I. Clemency proceedings create no protected life or liberty interest.**

In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), this Court held that a state’s clemency procedures confer no constitutionally protected liberty interest. The Court explained that “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Id.* at 464. Because the power to grant or withhold clemency “is rarely, if ever, appropriate” for judicial oversight, *id.*, an inmate possesses no life or liberty interest arising from the clemency process itself.

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that Ohio’s clemency procedures are “an integral part of the . . . system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant”—rests on a misreading of *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985). *Evitts* addressed the right to effective assistance of counsel on a first appeal as of right; it did not purport to constitutionalize executive clemency. *Dumschat* remains controlling on that distinct question. *See also Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976) (matters traditionally outside judicial cognizance do not trigger due-process scrutiny).

**II. The voluntary interview option does not compel self-incrimination.**

Ohio’s regulation permits, but does not require, an inmate to request a personal interview. The inmate “remains free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes.” Any statement given is considered along with other materials; the regulation imposes no penalty for silence.

In *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), this Court rejected a Fifth Amendment challenge to prison disciplinary proceedings in which an inmate’s silence could be considered. The Court held that the Constitution is not violated when a prisoner faces only the possibility that his silence may be weighed, provided he is not compelled to speak. *Id.* at 317–18. The same principle governs here: an inmate who elects the interview may invoke the privilege at any point; the State does not “compel [him] to be a witness against himself.” U.S. Const. amend. V.

Because the interview is wholly voluntary and carries no automatic sanction for silence, the Fifth Amendment is not implicated. The court of appeals’ remand for further proceedings on this claim was therefore unwarranted.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
