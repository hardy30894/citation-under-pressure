**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court’s Supplemental Instruction on “Shielding” Was Erroneous Because It Failed to Require a Specific Intent to Prevent Detection**

The term “shielding” in 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) is a statutory term of art that, like “concealing,” requires proof that the defendant acted with the specific purpose of preventing government detection of illegal aliens. *See United States v. Evans*, 333 U.S. 483, 495 (1948) (criminal statutes must be construed to give fair warning of prohibited conduct). The district court’s definition—that “shielding” means “to protect or hide from danger or harm”—impermissibly broadened the statute by omitting any requirement of an intent to evade immigration authorities. *Cf. Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 101 (1945) (where a statute uses a term with a settled meaning, the jury must be instructed on that meaning to avoid vagueness). This error was not harmless because the jury specifically requested clarification of “shielding,” indicating confusion. *United States v. Spatuzza*, 331 F.2d 214, 218 (7th Cir. 1964) (erroneous instruction on a critical element requires reversal unless harmless beyond a reasonable doubt).

The government’s proposed definition—and the one adopted by other circuits—improperly equates “shielding” with any act that incidentally makes detection more difficult, regardless of intent. But the statute’s structure, which pairs “shielding” with “concealing” and “harboring,” demonstrates that Congress intended to criminalize only conduct undertaken *for the purpose* of hiding aliens from authorities. *See United States v. Ragen*, 314 U.S. 513, 523 (1942) (words in a statute are to be read in context and given their ordinary meaning). A defendant who hires an illegal alien without taking affirmative steps to hide that alien from the government does not “shield” within the meaning of the statute. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952) (criminal intent is the rule, not the exception). The district court’s instruction allowed the jury to convict Ye for conduct that was merely negligent or passive, such as failing to maintain I-9 forms, which is separately punished under § 1324a. *United States v. Dotterweich*, 320 U.S. 277, 285 (1943) (when Congress creates separate offenses, courts must not conflate them).

Because the instruction omitted the essential element of specific intent to prevent detection, it was erroneous. *United States v. Crimmins*, 123 F.2d 271, 273 (2d Cir. 1941) (jury instructions must correctly state every element of the offense). The conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) must be reversed.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove That Ye Intended to Prevent the Government from Detecting His Illegal Alien Employees**

Even if the instruction were correct, the government failed to present evidence from which a rational jury could find beyond a reasonable doubt that Ye acted with the specific intent to shield the illegal aliens from detection. *United States v. Masiello*, 235 F.2d 279, 282 (2d Cir. 1956) (sufficiency review requires viewing evidence in light most favorable to government, but conviction must be supported by substantial evidence). Ye openly employed illegal aliens, paid them in cash, and did not require them to produce false documents until after government agents first visited the restaurant. He did not hide the workers; indeed, he told agents where the workers lived and admitted their illegal status. *Cf. United States v. Bufalino*, 285 F.2d 408, 415 (2d Cir. 1960) (affirmative acts of concealment, such as hiding aliens in a secret compartment, support an inference of intent to shield). Here, Ye’s conduct—hiring illegal aliens without paperwork—is consistent with a desire to avoid the costs of compliance, not with an intent to prevent detection. *United States v. Spatuzza*, 331 F.2d at 218 (evidence must exclude every reasonable hypothesis of innocence). The government’s evidence showed only that Ye knew the aliens were illegal and failed to comply with employment verification laws—conduct that supports the misdemeanor hiring count, but not the felony shielding charge. *United States v. Evans*, 333 U.S. at 495 (statutes must be strictly construed in favor of the accused). The district court therefore erred in denying Ye’s motion for judgment of acquittal on Count One.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse Ye’s conviction under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) and remand for entry of judgment of acquittal on that count, or in the alternative, for a new trial.
