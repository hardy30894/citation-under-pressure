# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF SECTION 355(f) ENCOMPASSES THE BOARD'S DENIAL OF REOPENING

Section 355(f) grants circuit courts jurisdiction to review "any final decision of the Board." The Board's order denying Salinas's request to reopen his 2006 claim was unquestionably a "decision" of the Board, and it was equally "final"—it concluded the administrative proceeding and left Salinas with no further recourse before the agency. Nothing in the statutory text distinguishes between decisions denying benefits in the first instance and decisions denying reopening of a prior benefits determination. Where Congress uses broad language such as "any final decision," courts should not import limitations absent from the statute itself.

This Court's decision in *SEC v. Louisiana Public Service Commission*, 353 U.S. 368 (1957), is instructive. There, the Louisiana Commission petitioned the Court of Appeals to review the Commission's order "denying its petition to reopen" a prior divestment proceeding. 353 U.S. at 369. Although this Court ultimately held that the particular statutory scheme at issue did not authorize review of that reopening denial, the Court's analysis proceeded from the premise that an order denying reopening is a "decision" capable of judicial review under the proper statutory authorization—the only question was whether the specific statute before it, by its terms, extended that far. Here, unlike the statute in *Louisiana Public Service Commission*, Section 355(f)'s "any final decision" language contains no such limitation.

## II. THIS COURT'S PRECEDENT CONFIRMS THAT ORDERS DENYING REOPENING ARE JUDICIALLY REVIEWABLE

The Fifth Circuit's categorical rule—that reopening denials are never reviewable—cannot be reconciled with this Court's decision in *Cheng Fan Kwok v. Immigration & Naturalization Service*, 392 U.S. 206 (1968). In *Cheng Fan Kwok*, this Court examined its own prior decision in *Giova v. Rosenberg*, 379 U. S. 18, where a petitioner had "moved before the Board of Immigration Appeals to reopen proceedings" that had terminated in a deportation order, and the Board denied relief. 392 U.S. at 212. The Court of Appeals had dismissed the petition for want of jurisdiction, concluding the Board's denial fell outside the reviewing statute. This Court's discussion of *Giova* demonstrates that whether a reopening denial is reviewable depends on the specific statutory grant at issue—not on a categorical exclusion of reopening orders from the concept of "final decision." The proper inquiry, this Court explained, turns on whether the order in question was among the "determinations made during and incident to the administrative proceeding." *Id.* at 216. A denial of reopening is no less a determination "incident to" the administrative proceeding than the original merits denial itself.

Similarly, in *International Union of Mine, Mill & Smelter Workers v. Eagle-Picher Mining & Smelting Co.*, 325 U.S. 335 (1945), this Court treated an agency's post-decree order altering back-pay computations as subject to the ordinary channels of review, without suggesting that the label "reopening" removed the order from judicial scrutiny. The Court's analysis proceeded on the assumption that such subsequent agency action was reviewable in the same manner as the original order.

## III. THE FIFTH CIRCUIT'S RELIANCE ON THE SOCIAL SECURITY FRAMEWORK IS MISPLACED

The courts adopting the majority rule—including the Fifth Circuit in *Roberts*—rely heavily on analogies to Social Security jurisprudence, particularly this Court's reasoning distinguishing reopening decisions from merits decisions. But that analogy is flawed because the Railroad Retirement Act's judicial-review provision, unlike the scheme this Court considered in *Dismuke v. United States*, 297 U.S. 167 (1936), reflects Congress's considered choice to commit railroad retirement claims to a specialized administrative framework with correspondingly broad judicial oversight. In *Dismuke*, this Court confronted a statute that committed "the adjudication of claims under it solely to administrative officers, to the exclusion of the courts," 297 U.S. at 168, and even there, this Court scrutinized closely whether Congress truly intended to foreclose judicial review, given "the public importance of the questions involved." *Id.* Section 355(f) reflects no such intent to foreclose review; to the contrary, its broad "any final decision" language demonstrates the opposite.

Moreover, this Court has consistently required clear congressial intent before foreclosing review of agency action affecting substantial rights. In *Robertson v. Chambers*, 341 U.S. 37 (1951), this Court carefully construed the scope of review afforded by a veterans' benefits statute according to its text, rather than presuming restrictions on review not found in the statutory language. The same interpretive approach should govern here: Section 355(f)'s text supplies no basis for the judicially created exception that has divided the circuits.

## IV. PRACTICAL AND EQUITABLE CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT REVIEWABILITY

The consequences of the Fifth Circuit's rule are severe. Claimants like Salinas—who may have meritorious grounds for reopening based on new evidence or administrative error, as recognized by the Board's own regulations—are left with no judicial recourse whatsoever, no matter how arbitrary or erroneous the Board's refusal to reopen might be. This result cannot be squared with the general principle, reflected in this Court's jurisprudence including *Pennsylvania Railroad v. Day*, 360 U.S. 548 (1959), that administrative determinations affecting substantial statutory entitlements are ordinarily subject to some form of judicial check to ensure the agency has not acted arbitrarily or contrary to law.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and hold that the Board's denial of Salinas's request to reopen constitutes a "final decision" reviewable under Section 355(f).
