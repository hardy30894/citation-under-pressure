The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court's failure to apply this heightened standard, known as the "reasoned choice" standard, violated Moran's due process rights.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a defendant's competency to stand trial is not the same as their competency to waive constitutional rights. In Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court held that a trial court must hold a hearing to determine a defendant's competency to stand trial if there is a good faith doubt about their competency. Similarly, in Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), the Court defined the standard for competency to stand trial as whether the defendant has "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him."

However, as the Ninth Circuit correctly noted, the standard for competency to waive constitutional rights is higher than that for competency to stand trial. In Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150 (1966), the Court held that a defendant's waiver of counsel must be knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. To be considered knowing and intelligent, a waiver must be made with a full understanding of the consequences, as required by Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938).

The trial court's failure to apply the "reasoned choice" standard to Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty plea was error. As the Ninth Circuit noted, Moran's behavior and statements at the time of his plea raised significant doubts about his competency to waive his constitutional rights. Moran had attempted suicide just a few months prior, was taking multiple medications, and gave monosyllabic responses to the court's questions. Under these circumstances, the trial court should have held a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights, as required by Massey v. Moore, 348 U.S. 105 (1954).

The state court's post-conviction hearing did not cure this error, as it applied the wrong legal standard to evaluate Moran's competency. The state court relied on psychiatric evaluations that were conducted to determine Moran's competency to stand trial, not his competency to waive constitutional rights. As the Ninth Circuit noted, these evaluations did not address the specific issue of Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights.

The "reasoned choice" standard requires a higher level of mental functioning than the standard for competency to stand trial. As the Court held in Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), a defendant's waiver of their constitutional rights must be made with a full understanding of the consequences. In Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), the Court emphasized that a waiver of counsel must be made with a full understanding of the charges and the potential consequences.

In conclusion, the trial court's failure to apply the "reasoned choice" standard to Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty plea was a due process violation. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court's post-conviction hearing did not cure this error, and that Moran's due process rights were violated. This Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit's decision.
