## ARGUMENT

### I. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment

The Sixth Amendment requires that any fact that increases the maximum penalty for a crime must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 602 (2002). In *Ring*, this Court held that Arizona’s scheme violated the Sixth Amendment because it allowed a judge, rather than a jury, to find the aggravating circumstances necessary to impose death. *Id.* at 609. Florida’s scheme is materially different.

Under Florida law, the jury renders an advisory recommendation of death or life imprisonment, but the judge makes the ultimate findings of fact regarding aggravating and mitigating circumstances. Fla. Stat. § 921.141 (2012). The judge must independently determine whether sufficient aggravating factors exist and whether they outweigh the mitigation. *See, e.g., State v. Steele*, 921 So. 2d 538, 546 (Fla. 2005) (en banc). This Court has long recognized that such advisory jury schemes are constitutionally permissible. In *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640-41 (1989) (per curiam), this Court held that the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to find aggravating factors when the judge makes the ultimate sentencing determination. Although *Ring* overruled *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), it did not disturb *Hildwin* or *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 465 (1984), which upheld Florida’s scheme against a Sixth Amendment challenge. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 597 n.4 (noting that *Hildwin* and *Spaziano* “are not overruled to the extent they allow a judge to impose a death sentence after a jury recommendation”).

The Florida Supreme Court has consistently held that *Ring* does not invalidate Florida’s scheme because the jury’s role is advisory and the judge makes the factual findings. *See Bottoson v. Moore*, 833 So. 2d 693, 695 (Fla. 2002) (per curiam). Hurst’s claim that the jury must unanimously find aggravators is likewise foreclosed. This Court has never required unanimity in capital sentencing recommendations. *See Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404, 411 (1972) (plurality opinion) (non-unanimous jury verdicts in criminal cases do not violate the Sixth Amendment). Florida’s requirement that a majority of the jury recommend death is constitutionally adequate. *See Coday v. State*, 946 So. 2d 988, 1023-24 (Fla. 2006) (Pariente, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part) (acknowledging that Florida precedent permits non-unanimous recommendations).

### II. Florida’s Scheme Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment

The Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of the mentally retarded. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 321 (2002). But *Atkins* expressly left to the States “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” *Id.* at 317. Florida has done so by enacting a statutory framework that requires the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence, with the judge making the ultimate determination. Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4) (2012). Nothing in *Atkins* mandates that a jury decide this issue. Indeed, this Court has upheld judge-made findings in capital sentencing when the jury’s role is advisory. *See Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 464-65.

Hurst’s claim that the lack of jury unanimity violates the Eighth Amendment is also meritless. The Eighth Amendment requires “heightened reliability” in capital sentencing, *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), but it does not prescribe a specific procedural mechanism. Florida’s scheme provides multiple layers of review: the jury’s advisory recommendation, the judge’s independent findings, and mandatory appellate review by the Florida Supreme Court. *See* Fla. R. App. P. 9.142. This Court has repeatedly declined to impose a unanimity requirement on capital sentencing. *See, e.g., Harris v. Alabama*, 513 U.S. 504, 512 (1995) (upholding Alabama’s scheme allowing judge to override jury’s life recommendation). Florida’s status as an outlier among death-penalty states does not render its scheme unconstitutional. The Eighth Amendment does not require conformity with the practices of other States. *See Kansas v. Marsh*, 548 U.S. 163, 179 (2006) (rejecting argument that “evolving standards of decency” require a particular procedural rule).

### CONCLUSION

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
