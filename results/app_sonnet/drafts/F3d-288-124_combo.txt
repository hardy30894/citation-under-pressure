# ARGUMENT

## I. Bryant Has Standing to Enforce the Arbitration Award Rendered in His Favor.

The district court erred in holding that Bryant, an individual bargaining-unit employee, lacked standing to enforce an arbitration award decided in his favor under the collective bargaining agreement between the CWA and Bell Atlantic. It is settled that Section 301 of the Labor Management Relations Act supplies federal courts with authority to enforce rights arising from labor contracts, and that "the substantive law to apply in suits under § 301(a) is federal law, which the courts must fashion from the policy of our national labor laws." *Textile Workers Union v. Lincoln Mills*, 353 U.S. 448, 456 (1957). That federal common law does not confine the right of enforcement to the union alone. In *Smith v. Evening News Ass'n*, 371 U.S. 195 (1962), the Supreme Court held that an individual employee may maintain an action against his employer for breach of a collective bargaining agreement, notwithstanding that the union was a signatory to the contract and could itself have sued. *Id.* at 200-01. Bryant's right to the fruits of an arbitration award entered in his favor is no less personal, and no less enforceable by him, than the underlying contractual right that gave rise to the grievance.

Nor may Bell Atlantic relitigate the merits of the award once rendered. The Steelworkers Trilogy makes plain that judicial review of labor arbitration awards is exceedingly narrow. "It is the arbitrator's construction which was bargained for; and so far as the arbitrator's decision concerns construction of the contract, the courts have no business overruling him because their interpretation of the contract is different from his." *United Steelworkers v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp.*, 363 U.S. 593, 599 (1960). Doubts as to the scope or effect of an arbitral disposition, like doubts about arbitrability itself, must be resolved in favor of the award, for "an order to arbitrate the particular grievance should not be denied unless it may be said with positive assurance that the arbitration clause is not susceptible of an interpretation that covers the asserted dispute." *United Steelworkers v. Warrior & Gulf Navigation Co.*, 363 U.S. 574, 582-83 (1960); *see also United Steelworkers v. American Mfg. Co.*, 363 U.S. 564, 567-68 (1960) (courts may not weigh the merits of a grievance committed to arbitration). Because Bryant won his award through the very grievance machinery the CBA created, he is entitled to sue to enforce it, and the district court's contrary ruling should be reversed.

## II. The District Court Applied an Erroneous Allocation of Proof to Bryant's Title VII and Section 1981 Claims.

Bryant's discrimination claims rest on statutes that this Court, and others, have long construed liberally to accomplish their remedial purpose. Section 1981's companion statute, 42 U.S.C. § 1982, has been read expansively to reach the substance, not merely the form, of racial exclusion, *Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*, 392 U.S. 409, 437-40 (1968), a principle equally applicable to Section 1981's guarantee of equal contractual rights. Title VII likewise was designed to remedy present and continuing discrimination, not merely to freeze prior discriminatory arrangements in place. As the court explained in rejecting an employer's reliance on a facially neutral seniority arrangement that perpetuated prior discrimination, "present discrimination cannot be justified by past discriminatory practices." *Quarles v. Philip Morris, Inc.*, 279 F. Supp. 505, 517 (E.D. Va. 1968). Once a plaintiff has shown that a facially neutral policy operates to his disadvantage because of race, color, or sex, the employer bears the burden of justifying the practice; the plaintiff is not required to prove that discriminatory animus was the sole or even primary motivation. *Local 189, United Papermakers & Paperworkers v. United States*, 416 F.2d 980, 987-88 (5th Cir. 1969). The district court's summary rejection of Bryant's claims, on the ground that Bell Atlantic articulated a facially neutral business justification for the Red Letter Day policy, ignored this settled allocation of the burden and failed to require Bell Atlantic to demonstrate that its policy, and its selective refusal of an accommodation the CWA itself proposed, was not a pretext for discrimination.

## III. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment.

Summary judgment is appropriate only where the record leaves no room for a reasonable dispute, and courts must be especially cautious where, as here, an employer's motive and intent are the central issues in controversy. In cases turning on questions of motive, "summary procedures should be used sparingly... where motive and intent play leading roles, the proof is largely in the hands of the alleged [wrongdoer], and hostile witnesses thicken the plot." *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting Sys., Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). So too, credibility determinations and the weight to be accorded conflicting evidence are matters for the trier of fact, not for resolution on a paper record. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627-28 (1944). Here, the record reveals disputed facts material to pretext: Bell Atlantic's refusal of the CWA's proposed swap accommodation; its selective enforcement of the Red Letter Day policy against Bryant; and the escalating discipline imposed on him despite his documented, ongoing efforts to comply. These disputes were for a jury, not the district court, to resolve.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
