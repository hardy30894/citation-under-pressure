**ARGUMENT**

Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act provides that the Act does not apply to “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies * * * exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The text requires an agency to have actually exercised authority over the particular working conditions at issue. See, e.g., *Southern Pac. Transp. Co. v. Usery*, 539 F.2d 386, 390 (5th Cir. 1976) (preemption turns on whether the other agency “has exercised” its authority). The Coast Guard has not done so here.

The Coast Guard’s statutory authority over uninspected vessels is narrowly circumscribed. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 authorizes regulations addressing only fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and certain alerting equipment. The regulations promulgated under that section, 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq., 26.01 et seq., likewise address only those discrete subjects. None governs the well-control, blowout-prevention, or hot-work procedures that gave rise to the explosion on Rig 52. Because the Coast Guard has not “exercise[d] statutory authority” over those working conditions, § 4(b)(1) leaves OSHA’s jurisdiction intact.

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary conclusion rested on its decisions in *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983). Those cases addressed inspected vessels or seamen’s general maritime duties and did not consider the limited regulatory scheme applicable to uninspected vessels. The broad language in those opinions cannot be divorced from the comprehensive Coast Guard regime that existed for the vessels there. Extending them to uninspected vessels would nullify the statutory distinction Congress drew between inspected and uninspected vessels and would produce the very “overlapping regulation” that § 4(b)(1) was designed to avoid only when another agency has acted.

The Act’s purpose confirms this result. Congress enacted the OSH Act to “assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions.” 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). Where another agency has left a regulatory gap, OSHA fills it. The Coast Guard’s investigation of the Rig 52 explosion produced no safety standards or enforcement action. OSHA’s citation therefore does not conflict with any exercised federal authority.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
