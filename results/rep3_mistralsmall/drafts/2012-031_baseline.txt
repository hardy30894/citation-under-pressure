**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY CERTIFYING A CLASS UNDER RULE 23(B)(3) WHERE PLAINTIFFS FAILED TO PRESENT ADMISSIBLE EVIDENCE THAT DAMAGES COULD BE MEASURED ON A CLASS-WIDE BASIS.**

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). To satisfy this standard, plaintiffs must show that they can prove both liability and damages through common evidence. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011). The Supreme Court has made clear that “[m]ere conjecture” is insufficient; plaintiffs must present “a method to measure and allocate damages on a class-wide basis.” *Id.* at 351 (citing *Tyson Foods, Inc. v. Bouaphakeo*, 136 S. Ct. 1036, 1048 (2016)).

Here, the district court erred by certifying a class despite plaintiffs’ failure to present admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. The Third Circuit’s decision in *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), requires that plaintiffs demonstrate a “common methodology” for calculating damages. *Id.* at 318. The district court’s reliance on Dr. McClave’s methodology was misplaced because it was not tied to a viable theory of damages. As this Court has recognized, “[a] damages model that is not tethered to the plaintiffs’ theory of liability cannot establish predominance.” *In re Nexium (Esomeprazole) Antitrust Litig.*, 777 F.3d 8, 20 (1st Cir. 2015) (citing *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 34 (2013)).

The district court’s certification order rested on the flawed assumption that plaintiffs’ damages model could be salvaged at trial. However, as this Court held in *Comcast*, “Rule 23 does not set forth a mere pleading standard.” 569 U.S. at 33. Instead, the court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” to ensure that plaintiffs can prove damages on a class-wide basis. *Id.* at 34. The district court’s failure to conduct this analysis was an abuse of discretion.

II. **THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED BY CERTIFYING A CLASS WHERE PLAINTIFFS’ DAMAGES MODEL WAS BASED ON SPECULATION RATHER THAN RELIABLE EVIDENCE.**

The district court’s certification decision was premised on Dr. McClave’s damages model, which the court found could be used to measure damages on a class-wide basis. However, Dr. McClave’s model was based on a flawed methodology that did not account for the individualized nature of damages. As this Court has recognized, “[a] model that assumes all class members were affected in the same way does not establish predominance where damages vary by class member.” *In re Rail Freight Fuel Surcharge Antitrust Litig.*, 725 F.3d 244, 252 (D.C. Cir. 2013).

The district court’s reliance on Dr. McClave’s model was also misplaced because it was not tied to a viable theory of damages. The Supreme Court has made clear that “[a] damages model that is not tethered to the plaintiffs’ theory of liability cannot establish predominance.” *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 34. Here, plaintiffs’ damages model was based on the assumption that Comcast’s clustering scheme caused all class members to pay higher prices for non-basic cable programming. However, this assumption was not supported by reliable evidence. As the district court acknowledged, the model did not account for the fact that some class members may have benefited from Comcast’s clustering scheme. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 191 (E.D. Pa. 2010).

The district court’s certification decision was therefore an abuse of discretion because it was based on a damages model that was speculative and not tethered to plaintiffs’ theory of liability. As this Court has recognized, “[a] class cannot be certified where the plaintiffs’ damages model is based on speculation rather than reliable evidence.” *In re Rail Freight Fuel Surcharge Antitrust Litig.*, 725 F.3d at 252.

III. **THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED BY CERTIFYING A CLASS WHERE PLAINTIFFS’ DAMAGES MODEL WAS NOT ADMISSIBLE UNDER DAUBERT.**

The district court’s certification decision was also flawed because it relied on Dr. McClave’s damages model, which was not admissible under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993). The Supreme Court has made clear that “[a] damages model that is not admissible under *Daubert* cannot establish predominance.” *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 34.

Here, Dr. McClave’s damages model was based on a flawed methodology that did not account for the individualized nature of damages. The model assumed that all class members were affected in the same way by Comcast’s clustering scheme, even though some class members may have benefited from the scheme. This assumption was not supported by reliable evidence and was therefore inadmissible under *Daubert*. As the district court acknowledged, the model did not account for the fact that some class members may have benefited from Comcast’s clustering scheme. *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 191.

The district court’s certification decision was therefore an abuse of discretion because it was based on a damages model that was not admissible under *Daubert*. As this Court has recognized, “[a] class cannot be certified where the plaintiffs’ damages model is not admissible under *Daubert*.” *In re Rail Freight Fuel Surcharge Antitrust Litig.*, 725 F.3d at 252.
