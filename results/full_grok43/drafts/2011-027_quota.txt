The equal footing doctrine entitles each State, upon admission to the Union, to “the ownership of the beds of navigable streams.” *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230 (1845). Title to those beds passes to the State “as an incident of sovereignty” if the stream was “navigable in fact” at the moment of statehood. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894); *see also Oregon ex rel. State Land Bd. v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co.*, 429 U.S. 363, 374 (1977).

Navigability for title is therefore fixed by the condition of the river “at the time of statehood.” *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). The inquiry examines “the natural and ordinary condition of the river” on that date, not later improvements or present-day use. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870). Evidence of post-statehood navigation is relevant only insofar as it corroborates the river’s capacity at the earlier time. *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9, 11-12 (1971).

The same temporal focus governs segment-by-segment analysis. A court must determine navigability “as to the particular portion of the river” that is claimed. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 554 (1981). Isolated stretches that were non-navigable at statehood remain outside state title even if other portions of the same river were navigable. *Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Mississippi*, 484 U.S. 469, 476-77 (1988).

The District Court applied precisely this standard. It required the State to prove, through contemporaneous historical sources, that the relevant reaches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were susceptible of commercial use in their natural condition in 1889. That evidence satisfied the constitutional test. Because the navigability determination was made at the constitutionally required time and for the specific segments at issue, the judgment awarding compensation for use of state-owned beds must be affirmed.
