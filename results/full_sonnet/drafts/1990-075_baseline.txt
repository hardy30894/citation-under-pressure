# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION SATISFIES THE INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TEST AND DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER

Article I vests "[a]ll legislative Powers" in Congress, U.S. Const. art. I, § 1, but this Court has long recognized that Congress may enlist the assistance of coordinate branches without offending the separation of powers, provided it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This Court reaffirmed that standard just two years before this case arose, upholding the delegation of guideline-making authority to the Sentencing Commission because Congress specified "the goals which the Sentencing Commission was meant to pursue," "the specific tool" for accomplishing them, and "detailed guidance" cabining the exercise of discretion. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 374-79 (1989). The Court explained that its application of the intelligible-principle test has been "driven by a practical understanding that in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives." *Id.* at 372.

Section 811(h) readily satisfies this standard. Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check; it specified that temporary scheduling is permitted only to "avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety," 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), and it required consideration of three enumerated statutory factors bearing on the substance's abuse history, scope of abuse, and risk to public health, *id.* § 811(h)(3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)). Congress further cabined the delegation temporally: any temporary scheduling order expires after one year, with a single six-month extension permitted only while permanent scheduling proceedings are pending. *Id.* § 811(h)(2). And Congress imposed procedural safeguards requiring notice in the Federal Register and consideration of the views of the Secretary of Health and Human Services. *Id.* § 811(h)(4).

These constraints are more exacting than delegations this Court has repeatedly sustained. In *Mistretta*, the Court upheld extensive guideline-making authority delegated to an independent commission; here, by contrast, Congress imposed a fixed temporal limit that automatically extinguishes the delegated power. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374-79. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 420, 423-24 (1944), the Court sustained a delegation to fix commodity prices that were "fair and equitable," a standard considerably less precise than the specific, itemized factors Congress provided here. And in *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946), the Court upheld delegated authority to prevent corporate structures from "unduly or unfairly" distributing voting power—again, language affording the agency far greater interpretive latitude than the concrete criteria of § 811(h)(3).

Petitioners' reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those decisions—the only two instances in which this Court has invalidated a delegation—involved statutes that, unlike § 811(h), supplied no meaningful standard at all. In *Panama Refining*, the statute "d[id] not declare any policy," "d[id] not qualify" the President's authority, and established no "criterion to govern the President's course." 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, the President was authorized to approve industry-drafted codes without any legislative articulation of the ends those codes were to serve. 295 U.S. at 541-42. Section 811(h) suffers from no comparable deficiency: it identifies the harm to be prevented (imminent hazard to public safety), the factors bearing on that determination, and a firm outer time limit on the exercise of the power.

That the temporary scheduling power touches criminal enforcement does not demand a more searching test. This Court sustained comparably consequential delegations touching individual liberty in *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952) (detention pending deportation), and *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (bank conservatorship), where "well-known and generally acceptable standards" in the regulated field permitted Congress to legislate with less granularity. The same is true of controlled substances, an area in which Congress had already, through the permanent scheduling provisions of the CSA, established the eight statutory factors and administrative framework from which § 811(h)'s three-factor subset is drawn.

## II. CONGRESS AUTHORIZED THE ATTORNEY GENERAL TO SUBDELEGATE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA

Congress expressly provided that the "Attorney General may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice." 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This general subdelegation provision applies with equal force to the temporary scheduling function added in 1984; nothing in § 811(h) withdraws that function from the scope of § 871(a)'s broad grant. Where Congress creates a general mechanism for subdelegation and later adds a new function to the same subchapter without excepting it, the natural reading is that the mechanism continues to apply. The Attorney General's delegation to the DEA Administrator, codified at 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), was thus authorized by the plain terms of the statute Congress enacted.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
