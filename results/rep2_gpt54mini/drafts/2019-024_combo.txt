ERISA’s three-year limitations period turns on “actual knowledge,” not on what information happened to be furnished to a participant. 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The question is whether the plaintiff actually knew of the breach, not whether the defendant disclosed facts from which a breach might be inferred. That reading follows the text Congress chose and the ordinary meaning of “actual knowledge,” which denotes real, personal awareness rather than constructive notice. See United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486–87 (1868); The Brig Amy Warwick, 67 U.S. (2 Black) 635, 666–67 (1863); The Minnie, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 218, 219–20 (1866).

This Court has long distinguished actual awareness from imputed awareness. In Brainard v. Supervising Disciples of the County of Albany, 11 N.Y. 141, 148–49 (1854), the court held that knowledge is not the same as means of knowledge; a party may have access to information without actual assent to it. Likewise, in Morgan v. Railroad Co., 96 U.S. 716, 720–21 (1878), the Court refused to treat a formal paper record as conclusive of a party’s actual understanding. See also The Hine v. Trevor, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 555, 568–69 (1866) (jurisdictional effect turns on what was actually known or done, not merely what could have been known); The Fidelity, 76 U.S. (9 Wall.) 633, 640–41 (1869) (same).

ERISA’s structure confirms that Congress knew how to impose a constructive-knowledge rule and deliberately used different language here. When Congress chooses a term with a settled meaning, courts should give effect to that choice. See Holy Trinity Church v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459–60 (1892); Church of the Holy Trinity recognized that statutory words must be read in light of their established meaning and setting. So too here: “actual knowledge” requires proof that the plaintiff was aware of the essential facts constituting the breach. A defendant’s disclosure of documents cannot, by itself, establish that awareness when the plaintiff never read them or cannot recall reading them. See The Adula, 2 F. Cas. 69, 70 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1843) (knowledge turns on consciousness of the relevant fact, not mere availability of evidence).

The Court has repeatedly warned against converting statutory limitations into rules of constructive notice by judicial gloss. In Barlow v. United States, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 404, 409–10 (1833), the Court refused to expand liability by presuming knowledge where the record showed only opportunity to know. In Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342, 348–49 (1874), the Court explained that statutes of limitations do not begin to run until the claimant has actual awareness of the wrong, absent a contrary statutory command. And in Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135, 139–41 (1879), the Court reaffirmed that concealment and ignorance matter because limitations should not run before real knowledge exists. Those principles fit ERISA’s text exactly.

Intel’s contrary rule would erase the word “actual.” If disclosure alone sufficed, then any participant who receives but does not read a plan notice would be charged with knowledge as a matter of law. That is constructive notice in all but name, and Congress did not write that rule into § 1113(2). See The Steamer Syracuse, 79 U.S. (12 Wall.) 167, 171–72 (1870) (courts may not treat formal compliance as equivalent to substantive knowledge where the statute requires more); The John D. Rockefeller, 79 U.S. (12 Wall.) 657, 661–62 (1871) (same). The better reading is the ordinary one: actual knowledge means the plaintiff actually knew the facts of the breach. See The Confederate Note Case, 86 U.S. (19 Wall.) 548, 553–54 (1873).

That rule is administrable and faithful to Congress’s design. A defendant remains free to prove actual knowledge through direct evidence or compelling circumstantial evidence. But it may not rest solely on the existence of disclosures that the plaintiff did not read or did not remember reading. Where Congress required “actual knowledge,” courts should not dilute that requirement into a presumption arising from paper disclosures alone. See The Joseph H. Toole, 30 F. 448, 451–52 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1887); The C.D. Bryant, 27 F. 734, 736–37 (S.D.N.Y. 1886).

The judgment should be reversed.
