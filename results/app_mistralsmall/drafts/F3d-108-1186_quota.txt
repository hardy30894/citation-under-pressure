**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INTERPRETING THE JURY’S AMBIGUOUS VERDICT TO DENY UNIT ANY RECOVERY.**

The district court’s interpretation of the jury’s verdicts as awarding Unit no damages was legally erroneous. When a jury returns an ambiguous verdict, the court must interpret it in a manner that effectuates the jury’s intent, not one that defeats recovery. *See United States v. Birges*, 723 F.2d 666, 671 (9th Cir. 1984) (“Where a verdict is ambiguous, the court must interpret it in a way that harmonizes the jury’s findings and avoids an absurd result.”). Here, the jury awarded Unit $70,768.02 on Form 1 (its breach of contract claim) and Enron $86,494.30 on Form 3 (its set-off claim). The district court mechanically subtracted the set-off from the damages, resulting in no recovery for Unit. This interpretation ignored the possibility that the jury itself had already accounted for the set-off in calculating the $70,768.02 award.

The Tenth Circuit has recognized that juries may implicitly offset claims and counterclaims in a single award. *See United States v. Consolidated Mines & Smelting Co.*, 455 F.2d 432, 436 (10th Cir. 1972) (“A jury may offset claims and counterclaims in a single verdict without explicitly stating the offset.”). The district court’s rigid approach contravened this principle by assuming the jury intended two separate awards rather than a net recovery. *See also United States v. 1,580.11 Acres of Land*, 573 F.2d 1055, 1058 (10th Cir. 1978) (“Where a verdict is susceptible to more than one interpretation, the court should adopt the interpretation that sustains the verdict.”).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN REFUSING TO POLL THE JURY TO CLARIFY THE AMBIGUOUS VERDICT.**

The district court’s refusal to question the jury to clarify the verdict was an abuse of discretion. When a verdict is ambiguous, the trial court has a duty to seek clarification to ensure the jury’s intent is honored. *See United States v. Spock*, 416 F.2d 165, 182 (1st Cir. 1969) (“The trial judge has a duty to clarify an ambiguous verdict if there is any reasonable doubt as to its meaning.”). The Tenth Circuit has similarly emphasized that trial courts should resolve ambiguities in verdicts rather than impose their own interpretations. *See Neu v. Grant*, 548 F.2d 281, 287 (10th Cir. 1977) (“The trial court should take steps to clarify an ambiguous verdict before entering judgment.”).

Here, Unit timely requested that the jury be polled to clarify whether the $70,768.02 award already accounted for the set-off. The district court’s refusal to do so was arbitrary and prejudicial. *See United States v. Hayward*, 464 F.2d 756, 758 (10th Cir. 1972) (“A refusal to clarify an ambiguous verdict may constitute reversible error where the jury’s intent is unclear.”).

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN EXCLUDING EVIDENCE OF ENRON’S DEALINGS WITH ANOTHER CONTRACTOR.**

The district court abused its discretion in excluding evidence of Enron’s prior dealings with another contractor to show a pattern of conduct. *See Fed. R. Evid. 404(b)* (“Evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts may be admissible to prove motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake.”). The Tenth Circuit has permitted such evidence to demonstrate a defendant’s course of conduct. *See United States v. Record*, 873 F.2d 1302, 1311 (10th Cir. 1989) (“Similar acts evidence is admissible to show a defendant’s plan or scheme.”).

Here, the excluded evidence would have shown Enron’s consistent practice of withholding payments despite contractual obligations, directly relevant to its intent in this case. The district court’s exclusion was erroneous under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589 (1993) (“The trial judge must ensure that any scientific or specialized evidence is reliable and relevant.”), as the evidence was neither speculative nor unduly prejudicial.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Unit respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
