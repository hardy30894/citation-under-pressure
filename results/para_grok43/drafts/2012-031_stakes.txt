I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Certifying a Rule 23(b)(3) Class Without Adequate Evidence That Damages Could Be Measured on a Class-Wide Basis.

Rule 23(b)(3) permits class certification only when “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). Predominance requires that the proposed class be “sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation.” Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997). In antitrust cases, this inquiry demands “significant proof” that injury and damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement through common evidence. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 359 (2011).

A district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” to determine whether the prerequisites of Rule 23 are satisfied, and that analysis may entail overlap with the merits. Id. at 351. The court may not “accept[] the plaintiffs’ expert’s testimony at face value” when the model fails to isolate damages attributable to the sole theory of liability certified. Id. at 354.

Here, the District Court credited Dr. McClave’s regression model after finding it “common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis.” Behrend v. Comcast Corp., 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). Yet the model calculated damages from four distinct theories of anticompetitive conduct while the certification order limited liability to a single theory—deterrence of overbuilders. The model therefore measured damages from conduct for which no class-wide liability was asserted. Because the model could not “translate” the certified theory into a class-wide damages figure, individual inquiries would predominate. See Dukes, 564 U.S. at 367 (rejecting certification where proof of one element did not establish the remaining elements for the class).

The Third Circuit’s affirmance rested on the same error. It treated the existence of any regression methodology as sufficient, without requiring that the methodology measure only the damages flowing from the certified claim. That approach contravenes the command that certification turn on “evidentiary proof” that common questions will drive the resolution of the litigation. Id. at 352.

Because plaintiffs offered no admissible evidence linking their damages model to the sole theory of liability preserved for class treatment, the predominance requirement was not satisfied. The certification order must be reversed.
