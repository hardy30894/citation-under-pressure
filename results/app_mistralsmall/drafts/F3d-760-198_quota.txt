**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON MOLL’S HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT CLAIM BY FAILING TO CONSIDER THE TOTALITY OF THE CIRCUMSTANCES**

A hostile work environment claim under Title VII requires proof that the workplace was “permeated” with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult that was “sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim’s employment.” *Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993) (quoting *Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986)). The district court erred by isolating Moll’s allegations and dismissing her hostile work environment claim because it found no “sexually offensive acts” within the limitations period. This was legal error.

The Second Circuit has repeatedly instructed that courts must evaluate hostile work environment claims by examining “all of the circumstances,” including facially sex-neutral incidents that, when viewed cumulatively, may evidence gender-based animus. *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002) (citing *Leopold v. Baccarat, Inc.*, 174 F.3d 261, 264 (2d Cir. 1999)). Facially neutral acts—such as exclusion from meetings, denial of flexible work arrangements, or differential promotion opportunities—may contribute to a hostile environment when motivated by gender bias. *See id.* at 376 (noting that “sexually offensive” acts need not be overtly sexual to support a hostile environment claim).

Here, Moll presented evidence that Irving, her supervisor, engaged in a pattern of conduct that, when viewed in totality, created a hostile environment: repeated inappropriate notes, intrusive phone calls, refusal to communicate electronically, denial of promotions despite a “promotion freeze,” and disparate treatment in scheduling and work-from-home privileges. These actions, taken together, plausibly altered the conditions of her employment. The district court’s piecemeal analysis contravened Second Circuit precedent requiring consideration of the entire record. *See id.*; *Leopold*, 174 F.3d at 264.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT IMPROPERLY DISMISSED MOLL’S RETALIATION CLAIM PREMATURELY**

To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under Title VII, a plaintiff must show: (1) participation in a protected activity; (2) adverse employment action; and (3) a causal connection between the two. *Gordon v. N.Y. Hosp. Med. Ctr. of Queens*, 538 F.3d 130, 136 (2d Cir. 2008). The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Moll’s retaliation claim based on the transfer to Syracuse, holding that it was not an adverse action.

An adverse employment action is one that “might have dissuaded a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination.” *Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). The Second Circuit has recognized that even a lateral transfer may constitute an adverse action if it involves significant changes in working conditions. *Galabya v. New York City Bd. of Educ.*, 202 F.3d 636, 640 (2d Cir. 2000). Here, Moll presented evidence that the transfer to Syracuse involved loss of flexible work arrangements, increased reporting requirements, and the elimination of work-from-home options—changes that could dissuade a reasonable employee from complaining. *See id.* at 640–41.

Moreover, Moll’s evidence of temporal proximity—her transfer occurred shortly after she filed her EEOC charge—created a triable issue of causation. *See Gordon*, 538 F.3d at 137 (noting that proximity, combined with other evidence, may suffice to show causation). The district court’s conclusion that the transfer was not adverse was therefore erroneous.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISREGARDING NON-PARTY TESTIMONY AS A “SHAM ISSUE OF FACT”**

Verizon argues that Moll’s evidence of discrimination should be disregarded because it contradicts her prior testimony. However, the “sham issue of fact” doctrine applies only to a party’s own contradictory statements, not to non-party witnesses. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 256 (1986) (noting that summary judgment is inappropriate where there is a genuine dispute of fact, even if based on conflicting evidence). The district court erred in applying this doctrine to non-party testimony, thereby improperly resolving credibility disputes at summary judgment.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be vacated in its entirety. The case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s opinion and controlling precedent.
