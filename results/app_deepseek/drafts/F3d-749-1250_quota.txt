## ARGUMENT

The district court plainly erred by admitting FBI Agent Jones’s expert testimony that directly opined on Mr. Hill’s credibility and guilt. Because Mr. Hill did not contemporaneously object, this Court reviews for plain error. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). To obtain reversal, Mr. Hill must show (1) error, (2) that is clear or obvious, (3) that affected his substantial rights, and (4) that seriously impairs the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *Id.* at 732-37. All four prongs are satisfied here.

### I. Agent Jones’s Testimony Was Impermissible Opinion on Credibility

It is well settled that an expert witness may not opine on the truthfulness of a defendant or the credibility of his statements. *United States v. Bedford*, 628 F.3d 1232, 1241 (10th Cir. 2010). Such testimony “invade[s] the province of the jury” by substituting the expert’s judgment for the jury’s own fact-finding role. *United States v. Adams*, 271 F.3d 1236, 1244 (10th Cir. 2001). Agent Jones repeatedly violated this prohibition. He testified that many of Mr. Hill’s answers “[did] not make sense” and were “not worthy of credence.” He characterized Mr. Hill’s evasive behaviors as “common among the criminal element.” He stated that Mr. Hill’s claim he would rather die than face charges was something he had “never seen … with an innocent person.” And he opined that invoking God to validate truthfulness is a sign of deception: “Those are deceptive statements.”

Each of these statements constitutes an explicit opinion on Mr. Hill’s veracity and, by extension, his guilt. Such testimony is inadmissible under Federal Rule of Evidence 702 because it is not “the product of reliable principles and methods” and does not “help the trier of fact to understand the evidence”—it usurps the jury’s role. *See United States v. Dee*, 912 F.2d 241, 242-43 (8th Cir. 1990) (expert testimony that defendant’s behavior was consistent with “someone trying to hide” guilt improperly vouched for the government’s theory). The Tenth Circuit has consistently held that no expert—including a law enforcement officer—may “offer direct opinion as to the defendant’s guilt or credibility.” *United States v. Charley*, 189 F.3d 1251, 1263-64 (10th Cir. 1999) (citing *United States v. Robinson*, 978 F.2d 1554, 1559-60 (10th Cir. 1992)). Agent Jones did precisely that.

### II. The Error Was Clear and Obvious

The prohibition against opinion testimony on credibility is well-established and “beyond dispute.” *Adams*, 271 F.3d at 1244. Indeed, this Court has repeatedly recognized that “expert testimony that a witness is truthful or untruthful is … inadmissible under Rule 702.” *United States v. Meling*, 47 F.3d 1546, 1557 (10th Cir. 1995). No reasonable district court could conclude that Agent Jones’s statements—characterizing Mr. Hill’s explanations as incredible, labeling his behaviors as criminal, and categorically denouncing his religious invocations—were proper expert testimony. The error is therefore “clear” and “obvious” under binding precedent. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734.

### III. The Error Affected Mr. Hill’s Substantial Rights

Agent Jones’s testimony directly undermined Mr. Hill’s defense that he was not a knowing participant in the robbery. The government’s case turned on credibility—the jury had to decide whether Mr. Hill’s exculpatory statements at trial and during his interview were truthful. By having an “expert” tell the jury that those very statements were deceptive, the government improperly bolstered its case with an aura of scientific authority. *See United States v. Solomon*, 399 F.3d 1231, 1241 (10th Cir. 2005) (plain error affected substantial rights where improper evidence “likely had a significant impact on the jury”). The jury was given no limiting instruction, and the prosecutor capitalized on the testimony in closing. Absent Agent Jones’s vouching, there is a reasonable probability the outcome would have been different. *See United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 13 (1985) (prosecutorial vouching taints the trial when it “may have induced the jury to trust the Government’s judgment rather than its own view of the evidence”).

### IV. The Error Seriously Impaired the Fairness of the Trial

Allowing a government expert to tell the jury that the defendant is lying undermines the fundamental role of the jury and erodes public confidence in the justice system. *See Bedford*, 628 F.3d at 1242 (reversal required where expert testimony “likely misled the jury”). This is not a case of harmless error; it is a case where “the integrity of the judicial process” demands correction. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. This Court should reverse and remand for a new trial.
