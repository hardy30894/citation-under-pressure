ARGUMENT

Section 1981a(b)(1) authorizes punitive damages only upon a showing that the employer acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This language does not eliminate the common-law requirement that the underlying conduct be sufficiently blameworthy to justify punishment. Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), confirms that punitive damages “are never awarded as of right, no matter how egregious the defendant’s conduct.” Id. at 52. The Court there rejected any notion that reckless indifference alone automatically warrants such relief; instead, the jury must still determine whether the defendant’s conduct crosses the threshold of culpability traditionally demanded for exemplary awards. Id. at 51-52.

Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), underscores the same principle in the Title VII context. The 1991 amendments effected “a seachange in employer liability,” id. at 286, precisely because they introduced damages that had previously been unavailable. That expansion was not intended to convert every finding of intentional discrimination into an occasion for punishment. Rather, the new remedy remains tethered to the historic limits on punitive awards, which require conduct that is “outrageous” or otherwise deserving of condemnation beyond the minimum showing of liability. See Smith, 461 U.S. at 46-47.

The legislative history of § 1981a reinforces this understanding by directing courts to the standards developed under 42 U.S.C. § 1981. Those standards, as applied by the circuits before 1991, consistently demanded proof of egregious misconduct in addition to the mental state necessary for liability. Because Kolstad presented no evidence that the Association’s decision rose to that level, the district court correctly refused to instruct the jury on punitive damages.
