**Argument**

The decision below should be reversed because the Equal Protection Clause does not create a freestanding cause of action for a plaintiff who alleges neither membership in a protected class nor discrimination directed at a class. This Court has repeatedly held that equal protection is concerned with laws and state action that classify persons, not with every instance in which government allegedly treats one person differently from another. See *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886); *Frost v. Corporation Commission of Oklahoma*, 278 U.S. 515 (1929); *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944).

This Court’s equal protection cases have always proceeded from the premise that “all persons similarly circumstanced shall be treated alike.” *F. S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia*, 253 U.S. 412, 415 (1920). But that principle has meaning only when the plaintiff identifies a constitutionally relevant class or a law that classifies. In *McCabe v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co.*, 235 U.S. 151 (1914), the Court explained that equal protection does not guarantee identical treatment in the abstract; rather, it forbids arbitrary classifications that deny a benefit to some while granting it to others similarly situated. Likewise, in *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27 (1885), the Court emphasized that the Amendment is aimed at state action that “make[s] an unjust and illegal discrimination between persons in similar circumstances.” Id. at 32.

The Seventh Circuit’s rule—that a plaintiff may prevail by alleging only that officials acted out of spite or ill will toward that individual—has no support in this Court’s precedents. To the contrary, *Missouri v. Lewis*, 101 U.S. 22 (1879), upheld state arrangements that treated different persons differently so long as the distinctions were not an invidious denial of equal protection. The Court did not suggest that a single person could transform every adverse governmental decision into an equal protection claim by alleging animus. Nor did *Hayes v. Missouri*, 120 U.S. 68 (1887), which invalidated discriminatory jury selection practices because they systematically excluded a class from service, not because one defendant was treated differently from another.

The same is true of *Gulf, Colorado & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. Ellis*, 165 U.S. 150 (1897), where the Court struck down a statute singling out railroads for attorney-fee liability. The constitutional injury there was legislative classification. *Southern Railway Co. v. Greene*, 216 U.S. 400 (1910), is similar: the Court invalidated a tax scheme that discriminated against foreign corporations as a class. Those decisions confirm that the Equal Protection Clause polices classifications, not isolated grievances.

The court below relied on an animus theory that this Court has never recognized as sufficient by itself. In *Standard Oil Co. v. Tennessee*, 217 U.S. 413 (1910), and *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), the Court addressed state action that burdened persons because of who they were or what group they represented. Again, the premise was class-based treatment. A plaintiff who alleges only that local officials demanded a larger easement from her than from some other owners has alleged, at most, a dispute over municipal administration or improper motive. That is not a constitutional classification.

This Court has been especially cautious about converting every alleged wrongful or arbitrary act by officials into a constitutional tort. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the Court held that unequal application of a law is not enough absent intentional discrimination in the constitutional sense. The Court stated that the Equal Protection Clause is not violated by “mere errors of state officers.” Id. at 11. That principle forecloses respondent’s claim here, where the complaint asserts only individualized mistreatment in a utility-easement negotiation.

Nor does *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), help respondent. There, the plaintiff showed that a facially neutral ordinance was administered with systematic discrimination against an identifiable class of Chinese laundry operators. Id. at 373-74. The case turned on discriminatory enforcement against a group, not a one-off grievance by a single landowner. Likewise, *Grosjean v. American Press Co.*, 297 U.S. 233 (1936), invalidated a targeted tax on newspapers because it singled out a class for burdensome treatment.

The better reading of the Equal Protection Clause is that it forbids invidious discrimination against persons similarly situated as members of a class, or discrimination that is otherwise tied to a constitutionally cognizable basis. A “class of one” theory untethered from any class or group would constitutionalize ordinary disputes over government discretion and invite federal courts to review every allegedly unfair municipal decision. Nothing in *Barbier*, *McCabe*, *Ellis*, *Greene*, *Yick Wo*, *Snowden*, *Royster Guano*, or *Frost* supports that expansion.

Because respondent did not allege membership in any class or discrimination directed at a class, her complaint fails to state an Equal Protection claim. The judgment below should be reversed.
