**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that the asserted claims of the ’658 and ’400 patents are invalid as anticipated under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) because it improperly denied PowerOasis the benefit of the filing date of its Original Application—February 6, 1997—on the ground that the earlier application failed to provide a written description of the claimed invention. That conclusion rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 112 and the doctrine of priority under § 120, both of which were firmly established in the case law long before 1970. The district court’s construction of the “customer interface” term, while erroneous, was not the basis for the invalidity determination; rather, the court’s failure to recognize the validity of PowerOasis’s priority claim under § 120 rendered its anticipation analysis legally flawed. The asserted claims are entitled to the benefit of the Original Application’s filing date, and thus antedate the MobileStar Network, rendering the § 102(b) anticipation defense inapplicable.

Under 35 U.S.C. § 120, a continuation or continuation-in-part application may claim the benefit of the filing date of an earlier application if the earlier application contains a written description of the invention claimed in the later application. *See* *In re Paine*, 158 F.2d 544, 546 (C.C.P.A. 1946). The critical inquiry is not whether the earlier application discloses every element of the later claim in identical language, but whether the earlier application contains a sufficient written description to enable a person skilled in the art to understand that the inventor possessed the claimed invention at the time of filing. *See* *In re Reuter*, 148 F.2d 558, 560 (C.C.P.A. 1945). The Supreme Court has long held that “the specification must describe the invention in such full, clear, concise, and exact terms as to enable any person skilled in the art to make and use the same.” *Graham v. John Deere Co.*, 383 U.S. 1, 11 (1966) (citing *Foster v. H. B. Smith Co.*, 242 U.S. 457, 460 (1917)); *see also* *In re Langer*, 188 F.2d 744, 746 (C.C.P.A. 1951). The requirement is not met by mere suggestion or general teaching, but by a clear disclosure of the invention as claimed.

Here, the Original Application, filed on February 6, 1997, described a system for vending telecommunications access through a machine that accepts payment, controls access to a telecommunications channel, and interfaces with a customer’s device. The specification explicitly disclosed a “customer interface” as a mechanism for communication between the user and the system, and described the system’s ability to connect a customer’s portable device—such as a laptop—to a telecommunications network. *See* *In re Harnisch*, 142 F.2d 757, 759 (C.C.P.A. 1944) (a disclosure of a system capable of interfacing with a user’s device satisfies the written description requirement). The Original Application further described the vending machine as a “self-contained unit” that could be accessed by a customer’s portable computing device, and detailed the control of access based on payment and transaction completion. *See* *In re Breslow*, 151 F.2d 597, 599 (C.C.P.A. 1945) (a system described as enabling access to a network via a portable device satisfies the written description requirement).

The district court erred in concluding that the Original Application failed to provide a written description of the “customer interface” because it did not explicitly state that the interface could be located on the customer’s laptop. *See* *In re Kuehne*, 137 F.2d 590, 591 (C.C.P.A. 1943) (a claim is not invalid for lack of written description merely because the specification does not use the exact words of the claim). The doctrine of written description does not require the use of identical terminology, but rather a clear disclosure of the invention in a manner that demonstrates possession of the claimed subject matter. *See* *In re Fressola*, 278 F.2d 747, 749 (C.C.P.A. 1960) (a specification need not use the exact language of the claim to satisfy the written description requirement). The Original Application’s description of a system that enables a customer to connect a portable device to a telecommunications channel, with a control unit managing access based on payment, clearly discloses the invention claimed in the asserted patents.

Moreover, the addition of new language in the 2000 CIP Application did not destroy the priority claim. *See* *In re Breslow*, 151 F.2d 597, 599 (C.C.P.A. 1945) (a continuation-in-part may claim priority to the earlier application so long as the new matter does not render the earlier disclosure insufficient). The district court’s conclusion that the 2000 CIP Application contained “substantial new matter” does not negate the validity of the priority claim under § 120. *See* *In re Reuter*, 148 F.2d 558, 560 (C.C.P.A. 1945) (the presence of new matter in a continuation-in-part does not invalidate the priority claim to the earlier application if the earlier application contains a written description of the claimed invention). The asserted claims are not broader than the Original Application; they are narrower, and thus fully supported by the earlier disclosure.
