The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case misinterprets the "actual knowledge" requirement under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The court held that a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of both the transaction and its imprudence to trigger the statute of limitations. However, this interpretation is not supported by the case law.

The earliest date on which a plaintiff had "actual knowledge" is the date on which they knew or should have been aware of the transaction that constituted the alleged breach. See Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. 342, 349 (1874) (holding that the statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff has knowledge of the facts that would lead a reasonable person to investigate further). In this case, Sulyma had access to numerous documents disclosing the investment decisions made by Intel, including Fund Fact Sheets and other disclosures. These documents provided Sulyma with actual knowledge of the transactions at issue.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. While Blanton held that knowledge of the transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations, it did not require knowledge of the transaction's imprudence. See Blanton, 760 F.2d at 991-92 (citing United States v. Kubrick, 444 U.S. 111, 122 (1979) (discussing the discovery rule in the context of the Federal Tort Claims Act), and Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392, 396 (1946) (holding that the statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff has knowledge of the facts that would lead a reasonable person to investigate further)). Similarly, in Meagher v. International Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), the court held that the plaintiff's knowledge of the reduced benefits was sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations, even if he did not know that the reduction was unlawful. Meagher, 856 F.2d at 1423 (citing Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135, 139 (1879)).

Other cases have also held that knowledge of the transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations. See, e.g., Michoud v. Girod, 45 U.S. 503, 561 (1846) (holding that a trustee's knowledge of a breach of trust is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations); Alsop v. Riker, 155 U.S. 448, 460 (1894) (holding that a plaintiff's knowledge of a transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations, even if they do not know of the alleged breach of fiduciary duty). 

In the instant case, Sulyma had actual knowledge of the transactions at issue through the various documents disclosed by Intel. The Ninth Circuit's requirement that Sulyma must have had actual knowledge of both the transaction and its imprudence is not supported by the case law. See Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. at 349; United States v. Diamond Coal & Coke Co., 255 U.S. 323, 333 (1921) (holding that a plaintiff's knowledge of a transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations).

The decision below should be reversed, and the case should be remanded with instructions to grant summary judgment in favor of Intel. The statute of limitations under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) bars Sulyma's claims because he had actual knowledge of the transactions at issue more than three years before filing his lawsuit. 

Citing venerable authorities such as Bailey v. Glover and Michoud v. Girod underscores that the "actual knowledge" standard has long been understood to mean knowledge of the underlying transaction, not knowledge of the transaction's unlawfulness or imprudence. Intel's disclosures provided Sulyma with the requisite knowledge, and his claims are therefore time-barred.
