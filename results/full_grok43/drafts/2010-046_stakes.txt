The three-judge court’s population-reduction order satisfies the Prison Litigation Reform Act. Under 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E), a prisoner-release order may issue only upon a finding that “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right” and that “no other relief will remedy the violation.” The court made both findings after a 14-day trial that included extensive expert testimony and review of prison conditions. Those findings are reviewable only for clear error. See Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985) (“If the district court’s account of the evidence is plausible in light of the record viewed in its entirety, the court of appeals may not reverse it even though convinced that had it been sitting as the trier of fact, it would have weighed the evidence differently.”).

The order also meets the PLRA’s additional prerequisites. It was preceded by prior orders for less intrusive relief that proved unavailing, satisfying § 3626(a)(3)(A)(i)–(ii). See Miller v. French, 530 U.S. 327, 333 (2000) (recognizing that the PLRA “establishes standards for the entry and termination of prospective relief in civil actions challenging prison conditions”). The court gave “substantial weight to any adverse impact on public safety,” § 3626(a)(3)(E)(ii), by incorporating evidence-based measures such as expanded good-time credits and parole for low-risk offenders—measures shown in the record to reduce recidivism.

Petitioners’ contrary reading would render the PLRA’s express authorization of release orders a nullity. That construction is incompatible with the statute’s text and with the principle that “a statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.” Corley v. United States, 556 U.S. 303, 314 (2009) (internal quotation marks omitted). Because the order rests on findings that crowding is the primary cause and that lesser remedies have failed, it does not violate the PLRA.
