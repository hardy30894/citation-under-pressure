ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Foley's Complaint, Which Alleged Sufficient Facts to State Each Claim.

A complaint may not be dismissed unless "it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can prove no set of facts in support of his claim which would entitle him to relief." Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S. 41, 45–46 (1957). Foley's complaint, construed in his favor, easily clears that threshold on every count.

II. Foley Adequately Pleaded Breach of Contract Because He Is an Intended Beneficiary of the Settlement Agreement.

Massachusetts has long permitted a stranger to a contract to enforce its terms when the contracting parties intended to confer a direct benefit upon him. In Brewer v. Dyer, 61 Mass. (7 Cush.) 337 (1851), the Supreme Judicial Court held that a party for whose benefit a promise is made, though not himself a party to the contract, may maintain an action upon it. The court reaffirmed that principle in Mellen v. Whipple, 67 Mass. (1 Gray) 317 (1854), recognizing that the promisee's intent to benefit the third party is the touchstone of enforceability.

Foley's allegations satisfy that standard. The settlement agreement expressly created "Settlement Class B," expressly required Wells Fargo to consider members of that class—of which Foley is one—for a HAMP modification and, failing that, for a MAP2R modification, and expressly imposed "servicing commitments" designed, in the agreement's own words, to "ensure that Borrowers are appropriately considered for a MAP2R Modification in a timely manner." Those provisions were not incidental; they were written for the direct benefit of borrowers like Foley. Under Brewer and Mellen, Foley may sue to enforce them.

Foley's complaint also alleges that Wells Fargo failed to perform the waterfall analysis, failed to provide the required written denial explanation, and failed to include the required foreclosure-related notice—each a breach of an express term. Even if some ambiguity existed about the precise scope of Wells Fargo's obligations, the New York Court of Appeals' teaching in Kirke La Shelle Co. v. Paul Armstrong Co., 263 N.Y. 79 (1933), controls: "In every contract there is an implied covenant that neither party shall do anything which will have the effect of destroying or injuring the right of the other party to receive the fruits of the contract." Id. at 87. Wells Fargo's seven-month delay in sending an application, its false statements that HAMP was the only available program, its failure to return calls, and its issuance of short-sale letters instead of modification decisions—all while scheduling foreclosure—plausibly allege conduct destroying Foley's right to receive the benefit the settlement promised him. Dismissal of the contract count was therefore error.

III. Foley's State-Law Claims Are Not Preempted by Federal Law.

Wells Fargo's preemption argument fails under settled principles limiting the displacement of state law. "[W]e start with the assumption that the historic police powers of the States were not to be superseded by the Federal Act unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress." Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). Preemption is appropriate only where state law "stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress." Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941).

Foley's claims impose no such obstacle. He does not ask the court to override any federal lending disclosure requirement or to impose terms inconsistent with federal regulation of national banks; he asks only that Wells Fargo be held to promises it voluntarily made in a settlement agreement it drafted. Where, as here, dual compliance with both state and federal obligations is possible, preemption is inappropriate. See Florida Lime & Avocado Growers, Inc. v. Paul, 373 U.S. 132, 142–43 (1963) (preemption unwarranted where a party can comply with both state and federal law). Wells Fargo can honor the settlement's waterfall, notice, and servicing terms without violating any federal mortgage-lending statute. Its preemption defense is therefore without merit, and the district court should not have accepted it as an alternative ground for dismissal.

IV. Foley Adequately Alleged Actionable Misrepresentation and Unfair Conduct.

Massachusetts recognizes liability for careless misstatements that cause pecuniary loss to those whom the defendant should reasonably expect to rely upon them. Craig v. Everett M. Brooks Co., 351 Mass. 497 (1967). That principle traces to Judge Cardozo's analysis in International Products Co. v. Erie R.R. Co., 244 N.Y. 331 (1927), which held that one who supplies false information for the guidance of others in their business dealings may be liable for the harm caused by justifiable reliance on that information.

Foley alleged exactly such reliance: for seven months Wells Fargo repeatedly and falsely told him that HAMP was the only modification for which he might qualify, concealing the MAP2R obligation the settlement had already created; it then ignored his completed application, denied receiving it, and responded to his inquiries with short-sale and deed-in-lieu letters rather than any modification decision—all while moving toward foreclosure. These allegations state a plausible claim of actionable misrepresentation causing Foley pecuniary and property harm, and the district court erred in holding otherwise.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of dismissal should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
