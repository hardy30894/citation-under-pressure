Argument for Petitioner, Humberto Fernandez-Vargas

The Immigration and Nationality Act’s reinstatement statute, § 241(a)(5), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date in 1997. The text of the statute speaks in the present tense: it applies when “the alien *re-enters* illegally.” This ordinary language indicates a prospective application. As this Court has long held, “statutes are to be construed as prospective in operation unless the contrary intention clearly appears.” *Union Pacific R.R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913). No such clear intention appears here. The statute neither defines “re-enters” to include past re-entries nor contains a retroactivity clause. The presumption against retroactivity is “a settled principle of law that a statute should not be given a retrospective operation unless such construction is required by clear and unequivocal language.” *United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T.R. Co.*, 270 U.S. 1, 5 (1926). Petitioner Humberto Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States in 1982—fifteen years before the statute’s enactment. He cannot “re-enter” anew under a law that did not exist at the time of his actual re-entry.

Even if the statute could be read to cover petitioner, applying it to his 1982 re-entry would be impermissibly retroactive. A statute is retroactive when “it attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Samuels v. McCurdy*, 267 U.S. 188, 195 (1925) (holding that a statute destroying a right previously acquired is retrospective). Before 1997, an alien who re-entered illegally could seek relief such as adjustment of status. Section 241(a)(5) retroactively strips that possibility, imposing a permanent bar on relief for a past act. This Court has consistently held that “a statute cannot be applied retroactively to impair vested rights or impose new duties on past transactions.” *Union Pacific*, 231 U.S. at 199; *cf. Cummings v. Missouri*, 71 U.S. 277, 325 (1866) (invalidating a law that imposed new disabilities on past acts as violative of the Ex Post Facto Clause, principles equally applicable in civil contexts).

The Tenth Circuit erred by focusing on whether petitioner had a “protectable expectation” of adjusting status. Retroactivity does not depend on such expectations; it depends on whether the law “attaches a new disability to past conduct.” *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. 333, 377 (1866). Petitioner’s 1982 re-entry was lawful in the sense that it did not then carry the consequence of forever foreclosing relief. To now apply the 1997 statute to that past act is to “change the legal effect of that act after it has been accomplished.” *Samuels*, 267 U.S. at 195. Congress did not clearly intend such a retroactive change, and the statute must be construed to apply only prospectively. The petition for review should be granted.
