**ARGUMENT**

**I. OSHA’s Statutory Authority Extends to Uninspected Vessels Operating as Workplaces in Territorial Waters.**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) applies to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The Act defines “workplace” broadly to include “any establishment, site, or location where work is being performed by an employee of an employer.” 29 U.S.C. § 652(10). The Fifth Circuit has recognized that this definition encompasses vessels engaged in industrial activity within territorial waters. *See Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825, 826 (5th Cir. 1983) (holding that OSHA jurisdiction turns on whether the vessel is a “workplace,” not its classification as a vessel).

Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52 was a stationary drilling barge engaged in oil and gas exploration in Louisiana’s territorial waters. It was not a vessel “in navigation” but a workplace where employees performed drilling operations. The explosion occurred during drilling activities, and the workers were engaged in industrial, not maritime, employment. Thus, Rig 52 was a “workplace” under the Act, and OSHA’s jurisdiction is proper.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction Over Uninspected Vessels.**

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that it does not apply to “working conditions with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard’s authority, however, is limited to vessels subject to its inspection regime. *See* 46 U.S.C. § 3301 (Coast Guard’s authority over inspected vessels). Uninspected vessels, such as Rig 52, are not subject to comprehensive Coast Guard safety regulations. *See* 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (Coast Guard’s limited authority over uninspected vessels).

The Fifth Circuit has held that OSHA’s jurisdiction is not preempted where the Coast Guard lacks authority over the vessel’s working conditions. *See Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120, 1122 (5th Cir. 1980) (OSHA applies to working conditions on drilling barges not subject to Coast Guard inspection). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is confined to specific safety equipment (e.g., life preservers, fire extinguishers) and does not extend to industrial safety standards governing drilling operations. *See* 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. Thus, there is no conflict preemption under § 4(b)(1).

**III. The Legislative History and Purpose of the Act Support OSHA’s Jurisdiction.**

Congress enacted the Act to “assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions.” 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). The Act’s coverage is broad, applying to “every employer” and “every employee” unless specifically exempted. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 652(5), (6). The legislative history reflects Congress’s intent to cover all workplaces, including those on navigable waters. *See* S. Rep. No. 91-1282, at 5 (1970) (OSHA’s coverage extends to “all places of employment under the jurisdiction of Federal agencies”).

The Coast Guard’s limited role in regulating uninspected vessels does not displace OSHA’s broader mandate. *See Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Assn.*, 505 U.S. 88, 107 (1992) (federal agency jurisdiction does not preempt OSHA unless Congress clearly intends otherwise). Here, Congress has not clearly divested OSHA of jurisdiction over uninspected vessels operating as workplaces.

**IV. The Fifth Circuit’s Decision in *Mallard Bay* Misapplies Precedent.**

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc. v. Herman*, 22 F.3d 145 (5th Cir. 2000), incorrectly extends *Clary* and *Donovan* to uninspected vessels. Those cases involved seamen on vessels in navigation, not industrial workplaces like Rig 52. *See Clary*, 609 F.2d at 1121 (plaintiff was a seaman injured on a drilling barge); *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826 (same). Rig 52’s workers were not seamen but industrial employees engaged in drilling operations. Thus, *Clary* and *Donovan* are inapposite.

**CONCLUSION**

OSHA’s jurisdiction over Rig 52 is proper. The Act applies to uninspected vessels operating as workplaces in territorial waters, and § 4(b)(1) does not preempt OSHA’s authority where the Coast Guard lacks jurisdiction. The Fifth Circuit’s decision should be reversed.
