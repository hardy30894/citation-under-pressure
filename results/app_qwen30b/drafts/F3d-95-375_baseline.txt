**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in striking the testimony of four expert witnesses and excluding two others under *Daubert*, and in granting summary judgment in favor of Defendants, thereby depriving Appellants of their right to a fair trial on their claims for personal injury and property damage. The court’s sanctions were disproportionate, based on an erroneous interpretation of discovery obligations, and applied without sufficient regard for the fundamental principles of due process and the right to present a defense.

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Striking the Testimony of Four Expert Witnesses**

The district court’s decision to strike the testimony of four expert witnesses—Dr. Connor, Dr. Sehroeder, Mr. Cooper, and Mr. Stock—was an abuse of discretion. While courts possess broad authority to enforce discovery rules, *see* *Henson v. City of Dallas*, 963 F.2d 788, 791 (5th Cir. 1992), such authority must be exercised with “careful regard for the interests of justice,” *see* *United States v. Hinkson*, 585 F.3d 229, 241 (5th Cir. 2009) (en banc). The court’s sanction here was both disproportionate and unsupported by the record.

The record reflects that Appellants’ failure to comply with the July 30, 1993, deposition deadline was not willful or in bad faith. The experts were not uncooperative; rather, they were attempting to fulfill their duties under the court’s own scheduling order. The court had previously ordered that depositions be completed by July 30, 1993, but the Plaintiffs’ counsel had filed a motion for a protective order on May 4, 1993, asserting that the experts had not yet had sufficient time to formulate their opinions. That motion was pending at the time of the June 3, 1993, hearing, and the court’s subsequent order—requiring completion of depositions by July 30—did not explicitly state that failure to comply would result in the automatic striking of expert testimony.

Moreover, the court’s June 3, 1993, warning—that experts who “have no knowledge,” “insufficient knowledge,” “will not appear at trial,” or “have not had enough time to examine all the documents” would be struck—was not a clear, unambiguous directive that such consequences would follow *automatically* upon missed deadlines. The court did not specify that failure to appear for deposition would result in exclusion of testimony, nor did it provide Appellants with an opportunity to cure the deficiency. *See* *In re Grand Jury Proceedings*, 875 F.2d 1177, 1180 (5th Cir. 1989) (sanctions must be proportional and tailored to the violation).

The Fifth Circuit has long held that “the striking of an expert’s testimony is a severe sanction that should be reserved for cases of willful or contumacious conduct.” *See* *Barnes v. City of Houston*, 119 F.3d 331, 337 (5th Cir. 1997). Here, there is no evidence of bad faith or deliberate obstruction. The experts were not unprepared; they were engaged in the process of forming opinions based on complex scientific data. The court’s failure to consider the circumstances—particularly the pending motion for a protective order and the lack of clear notice that noncompliance would result in exclusion—renders the sanction arbitrary and unjust.

**II. The District Court Erred in Excluding Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson Under *Daubert***

The district court’s exclusion of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under *Daubert* was legally erroneous. *Daubert* does not permit a court to exclude expert testimony based on the mere fact that the expert has not yet completed all analysis or has not yet formulated a final opinion. *See* *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 591, 113 S.Ct. at 2795 (“The trial judge must ensure that any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable.”). The focus is on the *methodology* and *principles* underlying the expert’s opinion, not on the completeness of the expert’s analysis at a particular stage.

Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson were in the process of reviewing voluminous environmental and medical data, including site-specific monitoring reports, exposure models, and epidemiological studies. Their testimony was intended to establish a causal link between chemical exposure at the Superfund sites and Appellants’ injuries. The court’s exclusion of their testimony based on the assertion that they had “not had enough time to examine all the documents” misapplies *Daubert*. The Supreme Court has made clear that “a party may not be precluded from presenting expert testimony simply because the expert has not yet completed all of his or her research.” *See* *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 152, 119 S.Ct. 1167, 1176 (1999) (holding that *Daubert* applies to all expert testimony, but not to exclude testimony based on incomplete analysis).

Furthermore, the court failed to conduct a proper *Daubert* hearing. *Daubert* requires a “rigorous analysis” of the expert’s methodology, not a cursory assessment of whether the expert has “had enough time.” *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 593, 113 S.Ct. at 2796. The court did not evaluate the reliability of the experts’ methods, nor did it consider whether their opinions were grounded in scientific principles. Instead, it relied on a procedural failure—failure to complete depositions—rather than on the substance of the testimony. This was a clear error.
