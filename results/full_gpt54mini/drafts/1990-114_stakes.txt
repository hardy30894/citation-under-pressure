Respondent’s judgment should be affirmed. Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for state judges does not violate either the ADEA or the Equal Protection Clause.

First, the ADEA does not reach these judges because Congress excluded “any appointee on the policymaking level” from the statute’s definition of “employee.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). In Gregory v. Ashcroft, 898 F.2d 598 (8th Cir. 1990), the court correctly held that appointed Missouri judges fall within that exclusion. That conclusion accords with the First Circuit’s reasoning in EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), which recognized that “policymaking” is part of judging because judges necessarily resolve legal gaps and create rules that guide future conduct. See also Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141 (1988); In re Stout, 521 Pa. 571, 559 A.2d 489 (1989). Judges do not merely apply fixed rules; they exercise discretion in ways that have systemic consequences for the law and for the administration of justice. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 603-04.

The structure of the Missouri judiciary confirms that point. Missouri judges participate in the supervision of the court system, and the Missouri Supreme Court exercises broad rulemaking and administrative authority over the judiciary and the bar. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 604-05. Congress drafted § 630(f) to exempt high-level governmental appointees with policymaking responsibilities, and the judiciary is not immune from that language merely because it is a separate branch. See Gregory, 898 F.2d at 605; EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d at 54-55. Nor does the legislative history compel a narrower reading. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 606. As the Eighth Circuit explained, the better reading of the statute is that appointed state judges are “appointee[s] on the policymaking level” and therefore outside the ADEA’s protected class. Id. at 603-06.

Second, Missouri’s age-70 retirement rule easily satisfies equal protection review. Age is not a suspect classification, and a mandatory retirement rule for judges is sustained if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 312-14 (1976) (per curiam). Gregory recognized that Missouri had ample rational bases for its rule, including preserving judicial competence, avoiding the difficult task of individualized competence hearings, promoting judicial efficiency, and ensuring the orderly turnover of the bench. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 607-08. Those reasons are more than enough under rational-basis review. See Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97-98 (1979); Dandridge v. Williams, 397 U.S. 471, 485 (1970).

The Supreme Court’s equal protection cases involving age-based employment limits point the same way. In Murgia, 427 U.S. at 314-17, the Court upheld mandatory retirement for police officers because the state could rationally conclude that physical ability declines with age and that public safety justified a bright-line rule. A state may likewise conclude that judicial performance, while not reducible to physical strength, can be affected by age-related decline and that a mandatory retirement age is a rational proxy for maintaining confidence in the judiciary. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 608; Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979); Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980). Missouri also could reasonably decide that mandatory retirement promotes administrative regularity and avoids the stigma and disruption of case-by-case competency proceedings. Apkin, 517 N.E.2d at 146. Rational-basis review does not permit a court to second-guess those legislative judgments simply because another policy might also be reasonable. Vance, 440 U.S. at 97; Minnesota v. Clover Leaf Creamery Co., 449 U.S. 456, 469 (1981).

The Constitution does not require Missouri to retain judges beyond age 70, and the ADEA does not forbid the State from drawing that line. The judgment should be affirmed.
