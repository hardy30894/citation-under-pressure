**Argument**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status does not constitute an “admission” for purposes of 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The better reading of the Immigration and Nationality Act is that TPS satisfies § 1255’s threshold requirement for an alien who is “inspected and admitted or paroled” because Congress directed that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for adjustment-of-status purposes. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). That command is naturally read to place the recipient in the legal position Congress associated with lawful nonimmigrant admission. See *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960–64 (9th Cir. 2017); *Flores v. U.S. Citizenship & Immigration Servs.*, 718 F.3d 548, 553–56 (6th Cir. 2013). At minimum, § 1254a(f)(4) should be construed to harmonize with § 1255, not to nullify the remedial path Congress created for TPS holders. See *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004); *Clark v. Martinez*, 543 U.S. 371, 380–81 (2005).

Section 1255(a) speaks of an alien who “was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). But Congress elsewhere made clear that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The statute does not merely excuse unlawful status; it deems the recipient to be in lawful nonimmigrant status. That matters because lawful nonimmigrant status is ordinarily tied to lawful entry. See *Reno v. Catholic Social Servs., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 43, 55 (1993); *Immigration & Naturalization Serv. v. Miranda*, 459 U.S. 14, 18 (1982) (per curiam). Courts have long recognized that Congress may define immigration consequences by reference to legal status rather than physical history. See *McGrath v. Kristensen*, 340 U.S. 162, 176–77 (1950); *Immigration & Naturalization Serv. v. Bagamasbad*, 429 U.S. 24, 25–26 (1976) (per curiam).

The Government and Third Circuit relied on the statutory definition of “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). But that definition does not resolve this case because Congress, in § 1254a(f)(4), supplied a special rule for TPS recipients. When Congress speaks specifically, that specific command controls over a more general definitional provision. See *Reid v. Immigration & Naturalization Serv.*, 420 U.S. 619, 623–24 (1975); *Clark*, 543 U.S. at 381. In *Serrano v. Att’y Gen.*, 655 F.3d 1260, 1265 (11th Cir. 2011) (per curiam), the court recognized that TPS gives an alien “lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for adjustment purposes. And in *Mejia Rodriguez v. U.S. Dep’t of Homeland Sec.*, 562 F.3d 1137, 1143–45 (11th Cir. 2009), the court explained that TPS is a lawful immigration status with concrete consequences under the Act.

The Third Circuit also erred by reading § 1254a(f)(4) to do nothing meaningful for adjustment of status. Statutes should be construed so that “effect is given to all [their] provisions.” *Hibbs*, 542 U.S. at 101. If TPS merely conferred a label with no bearing on § 1255, Congress would have provided a hollow benefit. That reading conflicts with § 1255(k), which treats “lawful admission” and “lawful status” as distinct but related concepts, and with § 1255(m), which addresses aliens “admitted into the United States (or otherwise provided nonimmigrant status).” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(1), (m)(1). Congress’s repeated focus on lawful status confirms that it expected status-based relief to matter in adjustment proceedings. See *Bagamasbad*, 429 U.S. at 25–26; *Jong Ha Wang*, 450 U.S. 139, 144–45 (1981) (per curiam).

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule also undermines the humanitarian purpose of TPS. Congress enacted TPS to protect vulnerable noncitizens from being forced to return to dangerous countries. See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1); *De Leon-Ochoa v. Att’y Gen.*, 622 F.3d 341, 353–54 (3d Cir. 2010). It is implausible that Congress would grant lawful nonimmigrant status and then deny TPS recipients access to adjustment even when they later become otherwise eligible. See *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 963–64; *Flores*, 718 F.3d at 555–56. The statute should not be construed to trap beneficiaries in a temporary status while foreclosing the permanent relief Congress made available under § 1255. See *Clark*, 543 U.S. at 380–81.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission for purposes of 8 U.S.C. § 1255, or at minimum that § 1254a(f)(4) supplies the equivalent legal predicate for adjustment of status.
