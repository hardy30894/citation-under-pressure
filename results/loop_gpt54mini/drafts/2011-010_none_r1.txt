**Argument**

The judgment should be affirmed because the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act does not extend Longshore Act coverage to every injury that can be traced in some attenuated way to offshore work. Congress limited OCSLA coverage to disability or death “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That text requires a real nexus to offshore operations, but it does not eliminate the statute’s geographic limitation or convert the provision into an all-purpose compensation scheme for shoreside accidents.

The starting point is the statutory text. “Where the words of a law are plain, and the meaning clear, we have no right to speculate upon the intentions of the legislature.” **Lake County v. Rollins, 130 U.S. 662, 670 (1889)**. Courts must enforce the words Congress chose. **Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917)**. And they may not enlarge a statute beyond its expressed limits. **United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820)**; **United States v. Goldenberg, 168 U.S. 95, 102-03 (1897)**. Section 1333(b) is not phrased as “any injury causally related to offshore operations”; it speaks instead of injury “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That language places an outer limit on coverage.

Longstanding interpretive principles reinforce that conclusion. In **Brown v. Duchesne, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 183, 194 (1856)**, the Court refused to extend statutory reach beyond the territorial limits Congress had set. In **Ex parte McNiel, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 236, 242 (1871)**, the Court treated statutory conditions as binding, not optional. And in **Morrill v. Jones, 106 U.S. 466, 467 (1883)**, the Court explained that “laws enacted for one purpose cannot be taken as extending to another purpose not expressed.” The same restraint applies here: OCSLA extends Longshore benefits only when the injury bears the kind of offshore connection Congress described, not merely when offshore employment is part of the background.

The compensation cases likewise show that coverage turns on the statutory boundaries Congress drew. In **Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 54-55 (1932)**, the Court emphasized that compensation jurisdiction depended on statutory facts fixed by Congress. In **South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 260-61 (1940)**, the Court again treated coverage as controlled by the statute’s own terms. Those decisions reject the notion that courts may redefine coverage based on general equitable concerns. A shoreside forklift accident at an oil-processing plant is not transformed into an offshore injury simply because the employee also worked on a platform.

Nor does the remedial nature of the statute authorize expansion beyond its text. The Court has long held that even beneficial statutes must be applied according to their terms. **United States v. Merriam, 263 U.S. 179, 187-88 (1923)**. And the Court has repeatedly recognized that compensation statutes may be broad but still remain bounded. **Baltimore & Philadelphia Steamboat Co. v. Norton, 284 U.S. 408, 416 (1932)**; **Ocean Accident & Guarantee Corp. v. Washington, 281 U.S. 574, 580-81 (1930)**. Congress could have written § 1333(b) to cover any worker whose job was connected to offshore production. It did not.

Respondent’s contrary theory would read the phrase “conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” out of the statute. It would also ignore the distinction between offshore operations and shoreside processing facilities. That distinction matters. The injury here occurred at La Conchita, an onshore facility where crude oil slurry was processed after being piped ashore. Whatever the facility’s relation to offshore production, it was not itself on the Shelf. Congress knows how to extend coverage expressly when it wishes to do so, and courts may not supply what Congress omitted. **Hebert v. Louisiana, 272 U.S. 312, 316-17 (1926)**; **Baltimore Mail S.S. Co. v. Fawcett, 269 U.S. 247, 251-52 (1925)**.

The Ninth Circuit therefore erred to the extent it suggested a sweeping causation test untethered to the statute’s geographic and operational limits. But the correct result is affirmance of the denial of OCSLA benefits, because respondent’s claim does not satisfy § 1333(b) as written. The employee was killed onshore, at an oil-processing facility, not in the course of injury on or as a direct part of outer Continental Shelf operations. Under the statute Congress enacted, that is not enough.

For those reasons, the Court should affirm the judgment below.
