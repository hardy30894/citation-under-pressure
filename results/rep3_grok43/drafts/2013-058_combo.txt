ARGUMENT

The Bankruptcy Code exempts only those assets that qualify as retirement funds in the debtor’s hands at the time of filing. Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181, 190 (1902). Property that has lost that character through transmission to another is reachable by creditors. Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294, 299 (1903).

An inherited IRA ceases to serve any retirement purpose for the new owner. The funds must be distributed within a fixed period after the original owner’s death and cannot be rolled over or augmented. This Court has long held that exemptions attach only to property devoted to the debtor’s own use at the commencement of the case. White v. Stump, 266 U.S. 310, 313 (1924). Once the asset is freed from that restriction, the exemption lapses. Burlingham v. Crouse, 228 U.S. 459, 473 (1913).

The same principle governs homestead and tools-of-trade exemptions. Chicago, Burlington & Quincy R.R. v. Hall, 229 U.S. 511, 515 (1913). A successor cannot claim the exemption merely because the decedent once enjoyed it. Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 236 U.S. 549, 554 (1915). The character of the property is measured by its function in the debtor’s possession, not by its prior history. Straton v. New, 283 U.S. 318, 322 (1931).

Congress did not alter this settled rule when it added the retirement-fund provision. Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). The statute still requires that the funds be “retirement funds” at the relevant time. Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622, 626 (1943). An inherited IRA supplies no such assurance; it is a time-limited tax deferral vehicle available for immediate consumption. Segal v. Rochelle, 382 U.S. 375, 379 (1966).

Allowing the exemption would convert a personal privilege into an inheritable shield, contrary to the policy that exemptions promote the debtor’s fresh start, not the enrichment of heirs. Hanover National Bank, 186 U.S. at 190. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
