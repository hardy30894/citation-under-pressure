**ARGUMENT**

**I. A CREDIBLE CLAIM OF ACTUAL INNOCENCE, WITHOUT MORE, WARRANTS EQUITABLE TOLLING OF AEDPA’S STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS.**

This Court has long held that a credible claim of actual innocence, standing alone, is sufficient to warrant equitable tolling of AEDPA’s one-year statute of limitations. *See, e.g., Schriro v. Smith*, 580 F.3d 610, 615 (6th Cir. 2009) (“[A] credible showing of actual innocence can equitably toll the limitations period.”); *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010) (recognizing that “actual innocence . . . serves as a gateway through which a petitioner can still have his otherwise time-barred constitutional claim heard on the merits”). The Warden’s reliance on *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), is misplaced. *Pace* did not impose a diligence requirement as a precondition to equitable tolling based on actual innocence; rather, it addressed the standard for equitable tolling in the context of *procedural* default. *Id.* at 419 (“[T]he question whether a petitioner has been diligent in pursuing his rights is an integral part of the equitable-tolling analysis.”). The Supreme Court has since clarified that actual innocence is a distinct and independent basis for equitable tolling, separate from the analysis of diligence. *See McQuiggin v. Perkins*, 569 U.S. 383, 392 (2013) (holding that “a prisoner pursuing a freestanding innocence claim can obtain review of the merits of his constitutional claim despite having filed his § 2254 petition outside AEDPA’s limitations period”).

The district court erred by conflating the two inquiries. Equitable tolling based on actual innocence does not require a showing of diligence; it requires only a credible showing that, but for constitutional error, no reasonable juror would have found the petitioner guilty. *Schlup v. Delo*, 513 U.S. 298, 327 (1995) (articulating the standard for actual innocence in the habeas context). The affidavits Perkins submitted—from his sister, Demond Louis, and Linda Fleming—collectively establish a *prima facie* case of actual innocence by casting direct doubt on Jones’s credibility and implicating him as the true perpetrator. *See House v. Bell*, 547 U.S. 518, 555 (2006) (finding actual innocence where new evidence “point[ed] unmistakably to [the petitioner’s] innocence”). The district court’s conclusion that the evidence was “substantially available” at trial is irrelevant to the actual innocence inquiry, which focuses on whether the evidence *demonstrates* innocence, not whether it was *presented* at trial. *Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 324.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT IMPROPERLY REQUIRED PERKINS TO DEMONSTRATE DILIGENCE AS A PRECONDITION TO EQUITABLE TOLLING BASED ON ACTUAL INNOCENCE.**

The district court’s reliance on *Pace* was erroneous. *Pace* addressed whether a petitioner had diligently pursued his *procedural* default claim, not an actual innocence claim. 544 U.S. at 419. The Supreme Court has since distinguished between the two, holding that actual innocence is a freestanding basis for equitable tolling that does not implicate the diligence analysis. *McQuiggin*, 569 U.S. at 392. The Court explained that “[t]he miscarriage-of-justice exception . . . targets circumstances in which the petitioner’s claim of innocence is so compelling that the Court would be forced to the conclusion that [the petitioner] is actually innocent despite the lack of a constitutional violation.” *Id.* at 393. Thus, the district court erred by requiring Perkins to demonstrate diligence before considering his actual innocence claim.

Moreover, even if diligence were relevant, Perkins’s delay was not unreasonable. The affidavits he submitted were signed years before the limitations period expired, and the last affidavit was signed in 2002—well within the one-year window provided by § 2244(d)(1)(D). Perkins’s 2008 filing was only five years after the limitations period lapsed, a delay that, in the context of actual innocence claims, does not warrant dismissal. *See Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 327 (noting that courts should “exercise flexibility” in tolling limitations periods where actual innocence is at issue).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in denying equitable tolling based on Perkins’s credible claim of actual innocence and in requiring diligence as a precondition to tolling. This Court should reverse the judgment below and remand for consideration of Perkins’s habeas petition on the merits.
