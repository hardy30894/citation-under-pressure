# ARGUMENT

## I. THE WAIVERS SIGNED BY CLASS MEMBERS DO NOT BAR THEIR WARN ACT CLAIMS

### A. WARN Act Rights May Not Be Waived Without Compliance With Statutory Notice Requirements

The WARN Act requires covered employers to provide sixty days' advance written notice before ordering a plant closing or mass layoff. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). This notice requirement serves a public purpose extending beyond the individual employee: it is designed to give workers "transition time to adjust to the prospective loss of employment, to seek and obtain alternative jobs and, if necessary, to enter skill training or retraining that will allow these workers to successfully compete in the job market." 20 C.F.R. § 639.1(a).

Because the WARN Act protects this broader public interest, courts have recognized that WARN rights cannot be waived through ordinary severance negotiations without regard to the statute's specific requirements. In *United Steelworkers of America v. North Star Steel Co.*, 5 F.3d 39 (3d Cir. 1993), the Third Circuit held that a union may waive its members' WARN Act rights only through "clear and unmistakable" language in a collective bargaining agreement. *Id.* at 42. The waiver here fails this standard for two independent reasons.

### B. The Waivers Were Not Bargained-For CBA Provisions but Post-Hoc Individual Releases Signed Under Economic Duress

The releases at issue were not negotiated modifications to the collective bargaining agreements themselves. The underlying CBAs "did not provide for severance benefits" at all. Instead, DHL extracted individual waivers from employees as a condition of receiving severance payments to which they had no other entitlement—payments the release itself acknowledged were "beyond anything to which I am already or otherwise entitled."

This structure inverts the protection *North Star Steel* demands. A union's waiver of statutory rights in exchange for a negotiated benefit is fundamentally different from an employer's presentation of a take-it-or-leave-it release to individual workers already reeling from the announced closure of their worksite. The drivers, in particular, were given mere days—until December 11, 2008—to decide simultaneously whether to accept the ten-week package and how to complete unrelated job-rebid paperworok, against the backdrop of a public announcement that five of six Chicagoland facilities were shutting down. This is not the considered, arm's-length union bargaining that *North Star Steel* contemplated as sufficient to extinguish statutory notice rights.

### C. The Release Language Does Not Satisfy the "Knowing and Voluntary" Standard Applied to Statutory Employment Waivers

Even where individual waivers of federal employment-law claims are permitted, courts require that such waivers be "knowing and voluntary" under a totality-of-the-circumstances test. See *Oberg v. Allied Van Lines, Inc.*, 11 F.3d 679, 681 (7th Cir. 1993) (applying knowing-and-voluntary standard to release of federal statutory employment claims). Relevant factors include the clarity of the waiver's language, the amount of time given for consideration, and whether the employee had the opportunity to consult counsel. *Id.*

The release here recited boilerplate protections—seven days to consider, seven days to revoke, advice to consult an attorney—borrowed wholesale from the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act's amendments to the ADEA. See 29 U.S.C. § 626(f)(1). But the OWBPA's twenty-one-day minimum consideration period, *id.* § 626(f)(1)(F)(i), reflects Congress's judgment about what workers facing job loss actually need to make an informed decision. DHL's seven-day window, imposed on workers who had just learned their facility was closing entirely, does not approximate the protection Congress required even for the analogous ADEA context, let alone satisfy the heightened protection WARN's public-notice purpose demands.

### D. Any Ambiguity in the Release Must Be Construed Against DHL as Drafter

To the extent the release's broad, boilerplate language—covering "any and all actions, causes of action, demands, claims or liabilities (whether known or unknown)"—is read to encompass WARN claims that arose from DHL's simultaneous failure to give sixty days' notice of the very closure that prompted the severance negotiations, that language must be construed against DHL as the drafting party. Contra proferentem is a bedrock principle of contract interpretation. *See Mastrobuono v. Shearson Lehman Hutton, Inc.*, 514 U.S. 52, 62 (1995) (ambiguities in a contract "should be resolved against the drafter"). DHL drafted the release; DHL controlled its timing; and DHL cannot now invoke general release language to erase notice obligations it never mentioned in the agreements it presented to unrepresented individual signatories.

### E. Conclusion

The releases signed by the plaintiff class do not satisfy the clear-and-unmistakable standard of *North Star Steel*, nor the knowing-and-voluntary standard this Circuit applies to waivers of federal statutory employment rights under *Oberg*. This Court should hold that the releases do not bar the class's WARN Act claims and should remand for further proceedings on the merits of DHL's compliance with the Act's sixty-day notice requirement.
