# ARGUMENT

## THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE DOES NOT BAR RETRIAL OF A PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION IN A NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDING

### A. Sentencing Proceedings Have Never Been Treated as Trials for Double Jeopardy Purposes

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant against "a second prosecution for the same offence after acquittal." *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). By its own terms, the Clause applies to jeopardy for an "offence," not to sentencing enhancements based on recidivism. This Court has long recognized that traditional trial protections do not automatically extend to sentencing determinations, even when those determinations turn on disputed factual questions. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), this Court upheld a death sentence imposed after the judge relied on information about uncharged burglaries that the defendant had never been convicted of and had no opportunity to confront. The Court explained that "[t]he due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure." *Id.* at 251. This Court reaffirmed that principle as recently as *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), and in *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389 (1995), where it declined to require that "courts throughout the Nation abandon their age-old practice of seeking information from out-of-court sources to guide their judgment toward a more enlightened and just sentence." *Id.* at 398.

Because a State is not constitutionally required to provide any adversarial proceeding to determine the existence of a prior conviction, it follows that a State which chooses to provide such a proceeding is not thereby required to graft onto it the full panoply of trial-type double jeopardy protections. Just as this Court has held that a defendant has no federal constitutional right to a jury determination of sentencing facts, *see Williams*, 337 U.S. at 245-46, the corollary is that the State may permit retrial of a sentencing allegation without offending the Fifth Amendment.

### B. *Bullington* and *Rumsey* Are Confined to the Unique Context of Capital Sentencing

Respondent recognizes that this Court has applied double jeopardy principles to bar retrial of certain sentencing determinations in capital cases. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that where a capital sentencing proceeding has the "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence," *id.* at 439, and results in an acquittal-like determination that death is not warranted, retrial of the death penalty question is barred. The Court extended this reasoning in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), asking whether the proceeding possesses "characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause." *Id.* at 209-10.

But *Bullington*'s reasoning was expressly rooted in the singular features of a capital sentencing hearing—a bifurcated proceeding in which the sentencer, guided by standards akin to the reasonable-doubt standard, must weigh aggravating and mitigating evidence to decide whether the defendant lives or dies. This Court itself has cautioned against extending that unique analysis to noncapital sentencing. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), the Court observed that "[b]oth *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases, and our reasoning in those cases was based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding." *Id.* at 392. The Court further noted that "a reasonable jurist reviewing our precedents ... would not have considered the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause to a noncapital sentencing proceeding to be dictated by our precedents." *Id.* at 393. Similarly, in *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28 (1985) (per curiam), the Court permitted resentencing on noncapital convictions notwithstanding the expiration of one sentence, signaling that ordinary noncapital sentencing does not carry the same double jeopardy consequences as the capital context.

The capital sentencing determination in *Bullington* was functionally equivalent to a trial on a discrete "offense"—whether the defendant should live or die—supported by a burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt and bounded by statutorily enumerated aggravating factors that operate much like elements of a capital "offense." A noncapital recidivism enhancement bears no such resemblance. It does not adjudicate guilt or innocence of any offense; it is administrative in character, addressing only the defendant's status by virtue of a conviction that has already become final through a separate proceeding. There is no risk that the State is using multiple prosecutions to wear down a defendant on the question of guilt, because the fact of the prior conviction is not relitigated in the sense of a criminal charge—it is merely evidentiary proof of status.

### C. California's Statutory Procedural Protections Do Not Convert Sentencing Into a Trial for Double Jeopardy Purposes

Petitioner points to California's statutory procedures—including the requirement that the prosecution prove a prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt, *see* Cal. Penal Code § 1025; *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993)—as evidence that the proceeding functions as a trial. But this Court has already rejected the notion that a State's voluntary provision of procedural protections beyond constitutional minimums transforms a proceeding into one entitled to full double jeopardy protection. In *Williams*, this Court made clear that sentencing procedures adopted by a State as a matter of policy do not thereby acquire independent constitutional double jeopardy significance. The privilege of receiving heightened statutory protections at sentencing is a matter of state law, not a signal that the state legislature intended to convert a sentencing hearing into a criminal trial on a separate "offense."

Moreover, permitting retrial here promotes accuracy without offending the values underlying the Double Jeopardy Clause—protection against governmental oppression through repeated attempts to convict, and protection of the finality of acquittals. See *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717. Here, the Court of Appeal reversed the trial court's true finding solely for insufficiency of proof that Monge personally used a deadly weapon—a technical evidentiary gap, not a determination that he lacks the qualifying prior conviction. Barring retrial would permanently insulate an admittedly recidivist offender from the enhancement the legislature intended for the "Three Strikes" scheme, based on nothing more than a correctable proof deficiency untethered to any of the finality or vindictiveness concerns the Clause exists to address.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of the prior conviction allegation.
