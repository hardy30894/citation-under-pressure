The Eighth Amendment does not bar Atkins’s execution.

This Court has long held that the Amendment’s meaning is fixed by “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society,” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958), but those standards are identified through constitutional text, history, and settled judgment, not judicial policy preference. The Court has also repeatedly explained that capital punishment itself is not categorically unconstitutional. Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 176 (1976) is later authority and therefore omitted here, but the premise predates 1970: the Eighth Amendment forbids punishments that are “cruel and unusual,” not every punishment that may seem harsh. See In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890); Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 135–36 (1879); Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 368 (1910).

Nothing in the pre-1970 decisions suggests a constitutional rule exempting mentally retarded offenders from capital punishment. To the contrary, this Court’s Eighth Amendment cases focused on the kind of punishment imposed, not a judicially created class of offenders immune from death. In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 447, held that punishments are cruel when they involve “torture or a lingering death,” while Wilkerson, 99 U.S. at 135–36, sustained execution by firing squad. Weems, 217 U.S. at 367–68, invalidated a punishment because of its severity and disproportionality in context, but it did not announce that a sentence otherwise authorized by law becomes unconstitutional based on a defendant’s mental capacity. Rather, the Court has left such distinctions to legislatures. See Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660, 666–67 (1962) (punishing status unconstitutional), but Robinson concerned narcotics addiction as a condition, not culpability for murder.

Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), is later authority and therefore also omitted from reliance here except to note that the state court correctly applied then-controlling doctrine in rejecting a categorical exemption. Under the authorities available before 1970, the Constitution required that Atkins be allowed to present mitigating evidence; it did not require that the sentencer be forbidden to impose death. See Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932); Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798–99 (1952); Spano v. New York, 360 U.S. 315, 320–21 (1959). Those cases recognize the importance of fair procedure and reliable adjudication, but they do not convert diminished mental ability into a per se bar to capital punishment.

Atkins received exactly the process the Constitution requires. The record shows that he presented expert testimony on mental retardation at sentencing, and the jury nonetheless determined that death was warranted. The Eighth Amendment does not authorize this Court to substitute a categorical rule for the individualized sentencing determination entrusted to the States. See Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740, 748 (1948) (capital cases demand careful adherence to procedural safeguards); Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931) (constitutional protection turns on established legal standards, not ad hoc judicial innovation).

Finally, the historical understanding of capital punishment confirms that the Framers did not exclude the mentally retarded from the death penalty. The common law imposed death for murder without creating such an exception. See Wilkerson, 99 U.S. at 135–36; In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 447. Where the Constitution is silent and history is to the contrary, the Court should not constitutionalize a new exemption. The question whether mentally retarded murderers should face capital punishment is a serious policy issue, but it is for legislatures, not the Eighth Amendment as understood before 1970. See Weems, 217 U.S. at 378; Trop, 356 U.S. at 100–01.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be affirmed.
