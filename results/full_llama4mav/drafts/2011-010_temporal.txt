ARGUMENT

I. The OCSLA Extends Longshore Act Coverage Only to Workers Injured on the Outer Continental Shelf Itself

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") to employees injured "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). A plain reading of this provision indicates that Congress intended to limit coverage to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf.

The Supreme Court has observed that the OCSLA's coverage is "determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed." Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, is not directly on point, but the Court's statement that "Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA's coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale" is instructive. 477 U.S. at 219. Although the Court's footnote regarding § 1333(b) is dictum, it suggests that a situs requirement applies to OCSLA claims. Id. at 219 n.2.

Furthermore, the Fifth Circuit has held that an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above the outer continental shelf. See Pure Oil Co. v. Snipes, 293 F.2d 60 (5th Cir. 1961). The court in Snipes interpreted the OCSLA's workers' compensation provision to require a direct connection between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf. Id. at 65.

In this case, Juan Valladolid was killed on the grounds of Pacific Operations's onshore oil-processing facility, La Conchita, which is not located on the outer Continental Shelf. Therefore, his injury does not satisfy the situs requirement under the OCSLA, and his widow is not entitled to benefits under the Act.

II. The LHWCA Claim Fails Because La Conchita is Not a Maritime Situs

To be eligible for benefits under the LHWCA, an employee must be injured on a maritime situs. The LHWCA defines a maritime situs as "navigable waters of the United States" or "any adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel." 33 U.S.C. § 903(a).

La Conchita is not a maritime situs because it is not used for maritime activities. Although it is located near the coast, it is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks, and has no direct access to any pier, dock, or other loading facility. See Motoviloff v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, is not available, but the principle that a facility must be "used as an integral part of longshoring operations" to be considered a maritime situs is supported by earlier case law, such as Johnston v. Marshall, 128 F.2d 13 (2d Cir. 1942).

The primary purpose of La Conchita is to receive and process crude oil slurry extracted by the offshore platforms, a non-maritime activity. Its occasional use as a dumping ground for scrap metal from the platforms does not convert it into a maritime situs.

Therefore, the BRB correctly denied benefits under the LHWCA, as Valladolid's injury did not occur on a maritime situs.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that the OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to workers injured on the outer Continental Shelf itself.
