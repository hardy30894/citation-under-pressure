**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General, nor does it permit an impermissible delegation to the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA). The Toubys’ challenge rests on a misreading of the non-delegation doctrine, a doctrine this Court has repeatedly reaffirmed as both alive and enforceable. The delegation at issue here satisfies the constitutional requirement of an “intelligible principle” under *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928), and is fully consistent with this Court’s precedent, including *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), which upheld a broad delegation of legislative authority to the Federal Sentencing Commission.

Congress did not abdicate its legislative function when it empowered the Attorney General to temporarily schedule substances under 21 U.S.C. § 811(h). Instead, Congress established a clear, legislatively defined standard: the Attorney General may temporarily schedule a substance only if it poses an “imminent hazard to the public safety,” and only after considering the eight factors set forth in § 811(c), including the substance’s potential for abuse, its history and pattern of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. These criteria are not vague or amorphous; they are specific, objective, and judicially reviewable. As this Court held in *Mistretta*, “Congress may delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to exercise the delegated authority is directed to conform.” 488 U.S. at 654 (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co.*, 276 U.S. at 409). The factors in § 811(c) constitute precisely such an intelligible principle.

The Toubys argue that the delegation is impermissible because it allows the creation of new crimes. But this Court has long rejected the notion that the mere power to define criminal conduct renders a delegation unconstitutional. In *United States v. Doremus*, 249 U.S. 86 (1919), this Court upheld the Harrison Anti-Narcotic Act—legislation that criminalized the non-tax-compliant distribution of narcotics—despite the fact that it delegated authority to the Secretary of the Treasury to regulate the sale and dispensing of controlled substances. The Court held that such regulation was a valid exercise of Congress’s power to tax and regulate under the Commerce Clause. Id. at 94. The same principle applies here: Congress may delegate authority to enforce criminal prohibitions so long as it provides a clear standard for the delegate to follow. The temporary scheduling provision does exactly that.

Moreover, the Attorney General’s authority is not unbounded. The statute requires notice and comment under the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 553, and mandates that the Secretary of Health and Human Services provide a binding evaluation. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b), (h)(4). These procedural safeguards ensure that the Attorney General’s decision is not arbitrary and that the public has an opportunity to participate in the process. The existence of such checks further demonstrates that Congress did not abdicate its responsibility.

The Toubys also contend that the sub-delegation of authority to the DEA Administrator is invalid. But this Court has consistently upheld such sub-delegations when Congress has authorized them. In *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357 (1942), this Court upheld the delegation of investigative authority to regional directors, even though the subpoena power was not explicitly delegated, because Congress had not intended to withhold that power. Id. at 364. Here, Congress explicitly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the CSA to the DEA Administrator through 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), a regulation that has been in place since 1973. The Attorney General’s amendment of that regulation in 1987 to include temporary scheduling authority was a lawful exercise of his delegated power. The DEA Administrator’s actions in temporarily scheduling 4-methylaminorex were thus fully authorized.

The Third Circuit correctly held that the temporary scheduling provision is not insulated from constitutional review. The statute’s provision that orders under § 811(h) “are not subject to judicial review” does not bar constitutional challenges. As this Court has made clear, “the availability of judicial review is presumed and [courts] will not read a statutory scheme to take the ‘extraordinary’ step of foreclosing jurisdiction unless Congress' intent to do so is manifested by ‘clear and convincing’ evidence.” *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). No such intent appears here. Indeed, every court to consider the issue has concluded that constitutional challenges to the delegation remain reviewable. *United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1429 (10th Cir. 1987).

Finally, the Toubys’ argument that the delegation is unconstitutional because it allows for the criminalization of conduct without prior notice is belied by the record. The DEA published notice of its intent to temporarily schedule 4-methylaminorex in the Federal Register, 52 Fed. Reg. 30,174 (1987), and provided a detailed explanation of the substance’s dangers. The Toubys had ample opportunity to challenge the scheduling before their indictment. The fact that the substance was later permanently scheduled further confirms that the temporary designation was not arbitrary but based on a careful, fact-driven process.

In sum, the delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General, and its sub-delegation to the DEA, is fully consistent with the Constitution. Congress provided an intelligible principle, procedural safeguards, and meaningful checks on the exercise of power. The Toubys’ conviction stands on firm legal ground. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
