**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Text of § 522, Read According to Its Terms, Exempts Only Funds That Retain the Objective Characteristics of Retirement Savings**

The starting point in construing 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is the statutory text itself. This Court has long held that "where the language of an act is plain," the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 522 exempts "retirement funds"—a phrase Congress chose deliberately, and one that describes a *function*, not merely a *pedigree*. Money is not rendered "retirement funds" simply because it once resided in an account bearing that label; it must actually be held for, and usable only in connection with, the owner's own retirement.

An inherited IRA fails that description. By operation of the Internal Revenue Code, the beneficiary of a non-spousal inherited IRA may not make new contributions, may withdraw the entire balance at any time without penalty, and must withdraw the entire balance on an accelerated schedule that has nothing to do with the beneficiary's age or retirement date. These are not incidental features; they are the defining legal characteristics of the asset petitioners ask this Court to exempt. Congress's use of the word "retirement" in the popular shorthand "inherited IRA" cannot substitute for the substantive requirement that the funds serve a retirement purpose in the hands of the debtor who claims the exemption.

**II. The Purpose of the Bankruptcy Exemption for Retirement Funds Confirms That the Funds Must Be the Debtor's Own Retirement Savings**

This Court has explained that bankruptcy exemptions exist to give "the honest but unfortunate debtor . . . a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). That rationale—protecting a debtor's capacity to work, save, and eventually retire—has no application to a pool of money that, by law, must be drained out for present consumption long before the debtor's own retirement. Exempting such funds would not further the "fresh start" policy identified in *Local Loan Co.*; it would instead shield a windfall inheritance available for immediate spending, at the direct expense of creditors who are entitled to look to the debtor's non-exempt assets in bankruptcy.

This Court's functional approach to bankruptcy-estate questions supports the same conclusion. In *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court looked past formal characterization of an asset to its practical economic attributes in deciding whether it belonged to the estate, recognizing that bankruptcy law is concerned with the real character of property rights, not labels attached to them. The same functional inquiry governs here: an inherited IRA's substance—mandatory, near-term, penalty-free distribution—is the substance of a general asset available for current use, not of a retirement account.

**III. Congress's Specific Statutory Design Controls Over Generalized Policy Arguments for a Broader Exemption**

Petitioners' argument that a broad reading of "retirement funds" would encourage savings and generational transfers is unavailing. This Court has repeatedly declined to expand statutory protections beyond their terms based on such policy appeals, holding instead that the specific terms Congress enacted—not extratextual purposes—control the disposition of a claimed benefit. *See Wissner v. Wissner*, 338 U.S. 655, 658 (1950) (enforcing the precise terms of a federal insurance statute over sympathetic equitable claims to the contrary). If Congress wished to exempt all funds that were ever anyone's retirement savings, regardless of their current character, it could have said so. It did not. Instead, Congress enumerated a specific, limited category of exempt "retirement funds," just as it enumerated other specific and limited exemptions elsewhere in § 522—for homesteads occupied by the debtor, tools of the debtor's trade, and the like. Those provisions have consistently been construed according to their own terms, not enlarged to encompass property that no longer performs the function for which the exemption was created. *Cf. Holden v. Stratton*, 198 U.S. 202 (1905) (exemption available under the Bankruptcy Act determined by reference to the specific statutory conditions governing the claimed exemption, not by generalized notions of debtor protection).

Finally, the fact that an inherited IRA continues to defer taxation does not convert it into "retirement funds." As this Court recognized in analogous contexts involving federally created exempt funds, an asset's exempt character depends on whether it continues to serve the specific statutory purpose for which the exemption was granted—not on ancillary tax treatment. *Cf. Porter v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 370 U.S. 159 (1962) (exempt status of federal benefit funds turns on whether the funds retain the character Congress intended to protect). Tax deferral without retirement-consumption purpose is not the equivalent Congress intended when it exempted "retirement funds" from the bankruptcy estate.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Seventh Circuit and hold that funds in a non-spousal inherited IRA are not exempt "retirement funds" under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12).
