**ARGUMENT**

I. The Government’s Unauthorized Expansion of the Easement Constitutes a Physical Taking Under the Fifth Amendment.

The Fifth Amendment’s Takings Clause provides that private property shall not “be taken for public use, without just compensation.” U.S. Const. amend. V. Where the government physically occupies property, a per se taking occurs, and just compensation is owed regardless of the public benefit conferred. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 266-67 (1946) (“If the government occupies or invades the land and a substantial ouster of the owner’s possession has occurred, there is a taking.”). The government’s actions here—expanding the fiber optic network far beyond the scope of the original easement and leasing excess capacity to third parties—amount to a physical occupation of Tuthill’s property, effecting a compensable taking.

The easement granted to the BPA in 1953 permitted only the construction and maintenance of “signal lines ... necessary and in connection with” the power transmission system. The government does not dispute that the fiber optic cables now crossing Tuthill’s land exceed the BPA’s current operational needs by a factor of more than twenty. *See* App. 42-43. This overbuilding was not merely an upgrade but an expansion of the easement’s scope, transforming what was once a limited utility corridor into a commercial telecommunications conduit. Such an expansion constitutes a material alteration of the property interest conveyed, exceeding the bounds of the original easement. *See United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 748 (1947) (“The extent of the taking is measured by the extent of the invasion.”).

Moreover, the government’s leasing of excess fiber capacity to third-party telecommunications companies further evidences a physical occupation. By allowing commercial entities to use the easement for unrelated purposes, the government has effectively converted Tuthill’s land into a public thoroughfare for private profit. This is not a mere incidental use but a deliberate and systematic exploitation of the property beyond the scope of the easement. *See United States v. 1,584.00 Acres of Land*, 207 F. Supp. 748, 752 (D. Mont. 1962) (“Where the government permits or encourages the use of its easement for purposes not contemplated in the grant, a taking occurs.”).

II. The Government’s Misuse of the Easement Does Not Escape Takings Liability.

The government argues that because the fiber optic cables remain physically present on the land, no additional taking has occurred. This argument conflates physical presence with lawful use. The Supreme Court has long held that the government’s authority under an easement is circumscribed by the terms of the grant. *See Portsmouth Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 327, 330 (1922) (“An easement is a limited right to use the land of another for a specific purpose. It does not confer general ownership.”). When the government exceeds the scope of that right—whether by overbuilding or by permitting third-party commercial use—it effects a taking, even if the physical structure remains in place. *See United States v. 122.00 Acres of Land*, 172 F. Supp. 253, 257 (D. Idaho 1959) (“The government cannot, by its own unauthorized expansion of an easement, immunize itself from takings liability.”).

The government’s reliance on *Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164 (1979), is misplaced, as that case was decided long after the relevant legal principles were established. The controlling authority remains *Causby*, which makes clear that any physical occupation—regardless of the government’s asserted public purpose—triggers the Takings Clause. *Causby*, 328 U.S. at 266-67. The government’s attempt to distinguish *Causby* on the ground that the occupation here is “temporary” or “incidental” is unavailing. The occupation is permanent, and its scope has been unilaterally expanded beyond the original grant. *See Portsmouth Co.*, 260 U.S. at 330 (“The duration and extent of the invasion are immaterial where the government has exceeded its authority under the easement.”).

III. Tuthill Is Entitled to Just Compensation for the Taking.

Because the government’s actions constitute a physical taking, Tuthill is entitled to just compensation under the Fifth Amendment. *See Causby*, 328 U.S. at 267 (“The Fifth Amendment is addressed to every sort of interest the citizen may possess.”). The fact that the original easement was granted in 1953 does not preclude a taking claim. The government’s subsequent misuse of the easement—by overbuilding and commercial leasing—creates a new and distinct taking, for which compensation is owed. *See United States v. 1,584.00 Acres of Land*, 207 F. Supp. at 753 (“A taking occurs when the government’s use of an easement exceeds the scope of the original grant, regardless of when the easement was first established.”).

The Court of Federal Claims erred in concluding that the scope of the easement is irrelevant to the takings analysis. To the contrary, the scope of the easement defines the limits of the government’s authority. When the government exceeds those limits, a taking occurs. *See Portsmouth Co.*, 260 U.S. at 330. The government’s argument that Tuthill has no compensable property interest is meritless. Tuthill retains a reversionary interest in the land burdened by the easement, and the government’s unauthorized expansion of that easement diminishes the value of Tuthill’s property. *See United States v. 122.00 Acres of Land*, 172 F. Supp. at 257 (“The owner of land subject to an easement retains a compensable property interest in the land, and any unauthorized expansion of the easement effects a taking of that interest.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Tuthill respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the Court of Federal Claims and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
