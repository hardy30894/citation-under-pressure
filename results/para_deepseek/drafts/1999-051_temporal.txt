# ARGUMENT

The Court of Appeals correctly held that Carpenter’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. Under this Court’s settled habeas corpus jurisprudence, a state procedural default does not bar federal review unless the petitioner “deliberately by-passed the orderly procedure of the state courts.” *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963). Where, as here, the default resulted from constitutionally ineffective counsel rather than a knowing and intelligent decision by the petitioner, no deliberate bypass occurred, and the federal court may examine the merits of the underlying claim.

## I. Ineffective Assistance of Counsel Constitutes Cause to Excuse a Procedural Default

In *Fay v. Noia*, this Court held that a federal habeas court may entertain a state prisoner’s constitutional claim even if the petitioner failed to comply with a state procedural rule, so long as that failure was not a “deliberate bypass” of state procedures. 372 U.S. at 438–39. The deliberate bypass standard requires “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). A default caused by ineffective assistance of counsel is not a “deliberate, bypassing” by the petitioner himself. *Fay*, 372 U.S. at 439. Rather, it is a circumstance external to the petitioner that prevents compliance with state procedural rules. This Court has long recognized that the right to effective assistance of counsel “is as much a part of the due process of law as the right to be heard by counsel at the trial.” *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 69 (1932). When counsel’s constitutionally deficient performance causes a procedural default, the petitioner cannot be charged with forfeiting his claim. The same principle applies to appellate counsel. See *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353, 357–58 (1963) (holding that due process requires effective assistance on direct appeal).

Accordingly, Carpenter’s claim that his appellate counsel was ineffective in failing to raise the sufficiency claim on direct appeal provides “cause” for the default of that claim. The state procedural ruling barring the sufficiency claim cannot preclude federal habeas review because the default was not Carpenter’s own deliberate choice.

## II. A Procedurally Defaulted Ineffective Assistance Claim May Itself Be Considered as Cause

The State may argue that the ineffective assistance claim is itself procedurally defaulted and therefore cannot serve as cause. But under pre-1970 habeas law, the deliberate bypass standard applies to all claims, including the ineffective assistance claim. Carpenter did not deliberately bypass state procedures for presenting his ineffective assistance claim. He filed an application to reopen his direct appeal, but the Ohio courts dismissed it as untimely. That untimeliness resulted from his appellate counsel’s failure to file within the prescribed period—again, not from a knowing decision by Carpenter. *Fay* teaches that when a petitioner has not deliberately bypassed state remedies, the federal court may review the merits of any constitutional claim, even if the state court declined to reach it on procedural grounds. 372 U.S. at 398–99.

Moreover, this Court has held that a state court’s procedural ruling does not create an adequate and independent state ground barring federal review when the petitioner’s failure to comply with the state rule was due to “ignorance, inadvertence, or mistake” rather than a deliberate bypass. *See, e.g., Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 486–87 (1953) (remanding for consideration of whether petitioner had waived his claim). Here, Carpenter had no actual knowledge of the filing deadline or the need to raise ineffective assistance separately; his counsel controlled the appellate process. *Fay* specifically disapproved of using state procedural defaults to foreclose federal habeas review absent a showing that the petitioner “understandingly and knowingly forewent the privilege.” 372 U.S. at 439.

The Sixth Circuit correctly concluded that the ineffective assistance claim, even if procedurally defaulted, may nonetheless establish cause for the default of the sufficiency claim. To hold otherwise would create an endless loop: a state prisoner could never use ineffective assistance to excuse a default if the ineffective assistance claim itself were defaulted, yet the very nature of ineffective assistance often leads to procedural defaults. This Court’s decision in *Fay* rejected such a rigid approach, emphasizing that federal courts must guard against “the miscarriage of justice” resulting from a procedural technicality. 372 U.S. at 438.

## III. The District Court and Court of Appeals Properly Found Prejudice

Because Carpenter’s appellate counsel failed to raise a meritorious sufficiency claim, Carpenter was prejudiced. The sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge, had it been presented, would have required reversal. The lower courts’ prejudice findings rest on the record and should be affirmed.

## Conclusion

Under this Court’s pre-1970 habeas jurisprudence, a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another claim when the petitioner did not deliberately bypass state procedures. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
