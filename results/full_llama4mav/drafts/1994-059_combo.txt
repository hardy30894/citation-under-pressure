The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution, Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress. The Constitution sets forth specific requirements for age, citizenship, and residency that candidates for Congress must meet. The States are not empowered to add to or alter these qualifications.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are a limitation on the power of the States to regulate the qualifications of congressional candidates. In Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that the Constitution sets forth the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress, and that Congress may not add to or alter these qualifications. Similarly, in Ex parte Dailey, 93 Tex. Crim. 68, 246 S.W. 91 (1922), the court held that a state statute that attempted to impose additional qualifications on congressional candidates was unconstitutional.

The framers of the Constitution intentionally limited the power of the States to regulate the qualifications of congressional candidates. As the Court noted in McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. 316 (1819), the Constitution is a grant of power to the federal government, and a limitation on the power of the States. The Qualifications Clauses are a key aspect of this limitation, ensuring that the States do not interfere with the federal government's authority to determine the qualifications of its members.

The history of the Constitutional Convention and the debates surrounding the Qualifications Clauses demonstrate that the framers intentionally rejected proposals to allow the States to set additional qualifications for congressional candidates. As the Court observed in Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. 1 (1824), the Constitution was designed to create a uniform system of government, with the federal government possessing the authority to regulate matters of national concern.

The attempt by Arkansas to impose term limits on congressional candidates through Amendment 73 is a clear infringement on the exclusive qualifications established by the Constitution. By limiting the ability of certain individuals to appear on the ballot, Amendment 73 effectively adds a new qualification for congressional office that is not authorized by the Constitution.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that state laws that conflict with the Constitution are void. In Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137 (1803), the Court established the principle of judicial review, holding that the Constitution is the supreme law of the land, and that state laws that conflict with it are invalid. Similarly, in Fletcher v. Peck, 10 U.S. 87 (1810), the Court held that a state law that impaired the obligation of contracts was unconstitutional.

The severability clause in Amendment 73 cannot save the provision limiting congressional terms. As this Court held in Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964), the test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion. In this case, it is clear that the voters of Arkansas considered the term limits for state and federal officeholders as a single package, and it is unlikely that the measure would have passed without the provision limiting congressional terms.
