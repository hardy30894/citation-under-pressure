**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether Congress, in enacting the reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), may apply that provision retroactively to reinstate a deportation order issued before the statute’s enactment, thereby stripping an alien of all avenues of relief—including adjustment of status—based on conduct occurring prior to the law’s effective date. The answer is no. The application of Section 241(a)(5) of the INA to Petitioner Humberto Fernandez-Vargas, whose prior deportation order was issued in 1981 and whose re-entry occurred in 1982, violates the constitutional prohibition against retroactive legislation. This Court has long held that laws which impair vested rights or impose new burdens for past conduct are impermissible under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. *United States v. Brown*, 338 U.S. 486, 492 (1950); *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 29 (1953). The reinstatement statute, as applied here, does precisely that.

The principle that a law cannot be applied retroactively to deprive a person of a right that had already accrued is deeply rooted in American jurisprudence. In *United States v. Pizzuto*, 335 U.S. 410, 414 (1948), this Court emphasized that “a statute which imposes a new disability upon a person for an act done before its passage is not a law but a punishment.” The same principle applies when a statute strips an individual of a legal right that had become available through the operation of prior law. Here, Petitioner’s re-entry in 1982 occurred under a legal regime that permitted adjustment of status for certain aliens, including those who had been previously removed. The fact that Congress later enacted a law that denies such relief to those who re-enter after deportation does not justify applying that law to individuals whose conduct predated the law’s existence.

The Court has consistently held that the Due Process Clause bars the application of laws that “retroactively alter the legal consequences of acts completed before the enactment of the law.” *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922). In *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 29 (1953), the Court reiterated that “a law which imposes a new disability upon a person for an act done before its passage is not a law but a punishment.” The reinstatement statute, by denying Petitioner any form of relief—including adjustment of status—based on conduct that occurred before the statute’s enactment, imposes a new disability for past conduct. It is not a mere procedural change; it is a substantive alteration of the legal consequences of re-entry, which had previously been subject to discretionary relief under the INA.

Moreover, the Court has recognized that “a person may have a legitimate claim of entitlement to a benefit based on prior conduct or existing circumstances.” *United States v. Brown*, 338 U.S. 486, 492 (1950). Petitioner’s marriage to a U.S. citizen in 2001, and his subsequent application for adjustment of status, constituted a legitimate claim of entitlement under the INA as it existed at the time. The fact that Congress later enacted a law that bars such relief for those who re-enter after deportation does not extinguish that claim. To hold otherwise would be to allow Congress to nullify a person’s lawful expectations based on conduct that occurred before the law was passed.

The Court has also held that “a law which takes away a right that has already accrued is not a law but a punishment.” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 335 U.S. 410, 414 (1948). The right to apply for adjustment of status, which Petitioner exercised in good faith, was a right that had accrued under the law in effect at the time of his application. The reinstatement statute, by denying that right to Petitioner based on a prior deportation order issued before the statute’s enactment, effectively takes away a right that had already accrued. This is precisely the kind of retroactive legislation that the Due Process Clause forbids.

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that Petitioner had no “protectable expectation” of adjusting his status is legally unsound. The Court has long recognized that “a person may have a legitimate claim of entitlement to a benefit based on prior conduct or existing circumstances.” *United States v. Brown*, 338 U.S. 486, 492 (1950). Petitioner’s marriage to a U.S. citizen, his long residence in the United States, and his application for adjustment of status were all acts that occurred under a legal regime that permitted such relief. To say that he had no protectable expectation is to deny the very concept of legal entitlement, which the Court has repeatedly upheld.

Finally, the Court has held that “a law which imposes a new disability upon a person for an act done before its passage is not a law but a punishment.” *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 29 (1953). The reinstatement statute, as applied to Petitioner, imposes a new disability—denial of all relief—based on conduct that occurred before the law was enacted. This is not a mere procedural change; it is a substantive deprivation of a right that had already accrued. Such a law violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

For these reasons, the application of the reinstatement statute to Petitioner is impermissibly retroactive and violates the Constitution. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit must be reversed.
