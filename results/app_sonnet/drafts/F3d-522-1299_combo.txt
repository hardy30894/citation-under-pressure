**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Asserted Claims Are Entitled to the Filing Date of the 1997 Original Application, Which Antedates the MobileStar Network.**

It has long been settled that when a later application discloses the same invention as an earlier application in the same chain, the later claims take the benefit of the earlier filing date. *Godfrey v. Eames*, 68 U.S. (1 Wall.) 317, 326 (1864) (an application withdrawn and refiled, or continued, is treated as part of a continuous proceeding where the invention disclosed is the same). This Court's predecessor confirmed that a second application "so far as it embraces the same invention" as the first is entitled to relate back to the original filing. *Topliff v. Topliff*, 145 U.S. 156, 171 (1892). The Supreme Court reaffirmed the same principle in the priority-of-invention context, holding that an applicant is entitled to the benefit of an earlier constructive reduction to practice reflected in a prior application disclosing the invention. *Milburn Co. v. Davis-Bournonville Co.*, 270 U.S. 390, 401 (1926).

The district court's refusal to extend priority to the 1997 Original Application rested on the mistaken premise that the Original Application must use the identical words "customer interface located on the customer's device" that later appeared in the 2000 CIP Application. That is not the test. A description is adequate if, read as a whole by a person skilled in the art, it conveys the invention, even though it does not employ the precise verbal formula later adopted in the claims. *Christie v. Seybold*, 55 F. 69, 76 (6th Cir. 1893). The Original Application's disclosure of a system in which the customer's own device receives status and payment information necessarily discloses a "customer interface" that may reside off the vending machine itself; nothing in the later CIP added subject matter absent from that disclosure—it merely made explicit what was already inherent. The proper inquiry, later articulated in the same line of authority relied upon by the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals, is whether the earlier specification reasonably conveys to one skilled in the art that the inventor had possession of the claimed invention as of the earlier date. *In re Ruschig*, 379 F.2d 990, 996 (C.C.P.A. 1967). Because the Original Application conveys possession of a customer interface not confined to the vending machine housing, the asserted claims are entitled under this line of authority to the 1997 priority date, which predates the MobileStar Network by more than two years. Summary judgment of anticipation was therefore improper as a matter of law.

**II. Even Measured From the 2000 CIP Filing Date, the MobileStar Network Does Not Anticipate Because Anticipation Requires Identity of Every Claimed Element.**

Anticipation is a demanding standard: a single prior art reference must disclose each and every element of the claimed invention, arranged as in the claim. *Continental Paper Bag Co. v. Eastern Paper Bag Co.*, 210 U.S. 405, 419 (1908) (prior device must embody "the whole" of the claimed combination to anticipate). The Supreme Court has likewise cautioned that a prior structure does not anticipate merely because it performs a similar function; it must contain the same means, combined in the same way. *Permutit Co. v. Graver Corp.*, 284 U.S. 52, 60 (1931). Nor does a prior public use bar a patent unless it was a use of the patented invention itself, not merely of a device that later inspired or resembled it. *Muncie Gear Works v. Outboard Marine & Mfg. Co.*, 315 U.S. 759, 768 (1942).

The district court's anticipation ruling assumed, without record support, that the MobileStar Network's use of a customer's laptop for status display was structurally identical to the claimed "customer interface . . . for indicating the status of said vending machine." That assumption conflates similarity of function with identity of structure and combination, precisely the error condemned in *Permutit* and *Continental Paper Bag*. At minimum, whether the MobileStar Network's laptop-side display was integrated into the claimed "control unit" and "electronic circuit" combination in the manner required by claim 1 and its dependent claims presents a genuine dispute that cannot be resolved on summary judgment.

**III. The District Court's Claim Construction Improperly Expanded the Claims Beyond What the Specification Discloses.**

Claims must be construed in light of, and limited by, what the specification actually discloses; a court may not read into the claims a scope broader than the invention the specification describes. *General Elec. Co. v. Wabash Appliance Corp.*, 304 U.S. 364, 369 (1938). Similarly, where the specification consistently describes an invention in a particular form, the claims cannot be stretched beyond that disclosed embodiment merely because broader language might otherwise be read into isolated claim terms. *Schriber-Schroth Co. v. Cleveland Trust Co.*, 311 U.S. 211, 220 (1940). Here, the district court used the CIP's later-added language to construe "customer interface" broadly enough to include a laptop interface, yet turned around and held that this same construction found no support in the earlier specification for priority purposes. That is inconsistent: if the broad construction is correct, as the district court held for infringement purposes, it must be measured against the invention actually disclosed in the Original Application, which supports it under *Christie* and *Milburn*, supra.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the district court's grant of summary judgment of invalidity should be reversed.
