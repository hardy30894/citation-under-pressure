**Argument**

The Equal Protection Clause does not create a freestanding cause of action for a plaintiff who alleges no membership in any class or group and complains only that government treated her differently from one neighbor. The Constitution’s equal protection guarantee is aimed at invidious and arbitrary class legislation, not every allegedly unfair decision by local officials. *See* *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373-74 (1886); *Gulf, Colorado & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. Ellis*, 165 U.S. 150, 155-56 (1897).

This Court has repeatedly held that the Fourteenth Amendment forbids states from selecting persons for disfavored treatment without a rational basis, but only in the context of legislation or administration that operates as class discrimination. In *Yick Wo*, the Court invalidated a licensing scheme applied “with an evil eye and an unequal hand,” 118 U.S. at 373-74, because the ordinance had been administered so as to burden one racial group. That case does not support a claim by a lone property owner who alleges no membership in any protected or identifiable class. Rather, it illustrates the settled principle that equal protection is concerned with discriminatory treatment of persons because of their place in a class, not simply with individualized annoyance.

Likewise, *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949), explained that the Equal Protection Clause does not require government to strike at every evil “at the same time and in the same way.” The Constitution permits line drawing and imperfect enforcement so long as the state acts on a rational basis. *Id.* at 109-10. A “class of one” theory would convert that deferential standard into constitutional review of any alleged departure from prior treatment. *See also* *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78-79 (1911) (classification is presumed valid if any state of facts reasonably may be conceived to justify it).

This Court’s cases also confirm that the Equal Protection Clause is not a general federal remedy for government wrongdoing. In *Hayes v. Missouri*, 120 U.S. 68, 71-72 (1887), the Court upheld a state practice against an equal protection challenge because the Constitution does not forbid all differences in treatment, only those lacking a lawful basis. And in *Missouri v. Lewis*, 101 U.S. 22, 31 (1880), the Court stated that the Fourteenth Amendment was not designed to require uniformity in all governmental administration, but to prevent hostile discrimination against persons or classes. Those decisions foreclose petitioner's claim that a municipal demand for an easement, standing alone, is itself an equal protection violation.

The Seventh Circuit’s theory that a plaintiff may recover whenever government acts from “spite” untethered to a legitimate public purpose finds no support in this Court’s pre-1970 precedent. Equal protection doctrine has never turned on the subjective motive of officials divorced from class-based discrimination. *See* *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425-26 (1961) (a classification “must be sustained if any state of facts reasonably may be conceived to justify it”); *Borden’s Farm Products Co. v. Baldwin*, 293 U.S. 194, 209 (1934) (equal protection is not violated unless the classification is “wholly arbitrary”). Even where unequal treatment is shown, the constitutional question remains whether the challenged action rests upon a permissible distinction, not whether officials were annoyed by a particular citizen. *Lindsley*, 220 U.S. at 78-79.

Nor does petitioner’s allegation that the Village imposed a larger easement than it imposed on others state a cognizable equal protection claim absent an allegation that she was singled out because of class membership. The Court has recognized that the Equal Protection Clause protects against arbitrary distinctions among similarly situated persons, but always in the context of a classification. *See* *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8-10 (1944) (unequal application of a law is not a denial of equal protection absent intentional or purposeful discrimination). Petitioner alleges at most a single, individualized dispute over a municipal condition for water service. That is not the sort of class discrimination the Fourteenth Amendment was designed to address.

If every person who believed a local official acted harshly or unfairly could sue under the Equal Protection Clause, federal courts would be transformed into super-review boards for routine municipal decisionmaking. The pre-1970 cases reject that result. The Constitution requires equal laws and equal administration, not a general guarantee against arbitrary treatment in one-off disputes. *Railway Express Agency*, 336 U.S. at 110; *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373-74.

Because petitioner alleged no membership in any class or group, and because the Constitution does not recognize a standalone “class of one” equal protection claim on these facts, the judgment below should be reversed.
