The Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one." The Seventh Circuit's holding that a plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective" is an unwarranted extension of equal protection jurisprudence.

The Equal Protection Clause is traditionally understood to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group. As the Court held in Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), the clause prohibits state action that discriminates against individuals based on their membership in a particular group. The Court has consistently applied this principle in cases such as Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303 (1880), and Truax v. Raich, 239 U.S. 33 (1915), where it struck down laws that discriminated against particular racial or ethnic groups.

In contrast, the Seventh Circuit's holding in this case would allow individuals to bring equal protection claims based solely on their status as a "class of one." This approach is inconsistent with the traditional understanding of the Equal Protection Clause. As the Court held in Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the clause is intended to protect against intentional and systematic discrimination, not against individualized or arbitrary government action.

Furthermore, allowing "class of one" claims would undermine the requirement that plaintiffs demonstrate membership in a protected class. As the Court held in McLaughlin v. Florida, 379 U.S. 184 (1964), and Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448 (1962), a plaintiff must show that they are a member of a particular class or group that has been subjected to discriminatory treatment. The Seventh Circuit's holding would eliminate this requirement and allow plaintiffs to bring equal protection claims based on nothing more than a showing of individualized animus.

The Court has also recognized that the Equal Protection Clause is not a general prohibition on arbitrary or irrational government action. As the Court held in Williamson v. Lee Optical Co., 348 U.S. 483 (1955), and Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm'rs, 330 U.S. 552 (1947), the clause is not intended to proscribe all forms of unequal treatment, but rather only those that are based on impermissible factors such as race or ethnicity.

In addition, the Court has long recognized that government officials must be given broad discretion to make decisions, even if those decisions are not always fair or rational. As the Court held in Railroad Co. v. Richmond, 96 U.S. 521 (1878), and Sinking-Fund Cases, 99 U.S. 700 (1879), government officials are not required to treat all individuals equally in all circumstances.

Other cases, such as Barbier v. Connolly, 113 U.S. 27 (1885), and Soon Hing v. Crowley, 113 U.S. 703 (1885), have also established that the Equal Protection Clause is not a means to challenge individualized or arbitrary government action. Allowing "class of one" claims would be inconsistent with these precedents.

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit's holding that a plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective" is an unwarranted extension of equal protection jurisprudence. The Equal Protection Clause is intended to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group, not against individualized or arbitrary government action. The Court should reverse the Seventh Circuit's holding and reaffirm the traditional understanding of the Equal Protection Clause.
