**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 1981a's Text, Structure, and History Require a Showing Independent of, and More Demanding Than, the State of Mind Sufficient to Establish Liability.**

Title VII liability requires only that a plaintiff prove intentional discrimination through the familiar burden-shifting framework of *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). Once a plaintiff establishes a prima facie case and discredits the employer's proffered explanation, a jury "may infer discrimination" without more. *St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993) (explaining that "no additional proof of discrimination is required"). That minimal threshold is appropriate for liability, but Congress did not intend it to double as the threshold for punitive damages. When Congress enacted the Civil Rights Act of 1991, it deliberately created a separate, additional remedy—capped, discretionary, and reserved for a narrower category of conduct. See *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994) (describing the 1991 Act's expansion of remedies available "in addition to" equitable relief previously available under Title VII). If punitive damages followed automatically from proof of intentional discrimination, the caps and the "malice or reckless indifference" language of § 1981a(b)(1) would be superfluous, because every successful Title VII plaintiff could obtain them.

The legislative history confirms this reading. Members of Congress who spoke to the punitive-damages standard understood it to demand more than ordinary liability. The House Report accompanying the bill stated that plaintiffs seeking punitive damages must satisfy "an even higher standard" than that required to prove intentional discrimination. Senator Dole's interpretive memorandum likewise explained that punitive damages should be available only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). These statements are not idiosyncratic; they reflect the ordinary understanding that punitive damages are an extraordinary remedy layered atop liability, not a routine consequence of it.

**II. This Court's Punitive-Damages Jurisprudence Confirms that Egregiousness, Not Merely State of Mind, Governs the Availability of Punitive Relief.**

This Court has consistently treated punitive damages as reserved for conduct that is qualitatively more blameworthy than the conduct sufficient to establish liability. In *BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559 (1996), the Court held that "the most important indicium of the reasonableness of a punitive damages award" is "the degree of reprehensibility of the [defendant's] conduct," id. at 575, and cautioned that punitive awards must be reserved for conduct that is truly blameworthy. Similarly, in *TXO Production Corp. v. Alliance Resources Corp.*, 509 U.S. 443 (1993), the Court examined the "reprehensibility" of the defendant's conduct as the touchstone for whether punitive damages were properly imposed, id. at 462. And in *Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Haslip*, 499 U.S. 1 (1991), the Court emphasized that punitive damages serve to punish and deter conduct that exceeds the ordinary wrong giving rise to liability, id. at 19-20. These cases teach a consistent lesson: punitive damages track the reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct, not merely its intent. A rule permitting punitive damages upon nothing more than the state of mind necessary to prove discrimination collapses this distinction and disregards the constitutional and common-law tradition this Court has repeatedly invoked.

Nothing in the text of § 1981a suggests that Congress meant to depart from that tradition. To the contrary, Congress borrowed the "malice or reckless indifference" formulation from the common law of punitive damages, which has long required that a defendant's conduct be egregious in a way that goes beyond the minimal showing needed to establish the underlying wrong. Courts construing analogous statutes have recognized as much. In *Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co.*, 817 F.2d 1290 (7th Cir. 1987), the court held that punitive damages under a related civil rights statute required proof "so clear at the time of the act that reasonably competent people would have agreed on its application," id. at 1296—a standard that necessarily demands something beyond bare intentional discrimination. And in *Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co.*, 95 F.3d 627 (7th Cir. 1996), the court confirmed that an award of punitive damages must surmount a "higher hurdle" than mere proof of intentional discrimination, id. at 636. These decisions reflect the sound and widely shared understanding that punitive damages are exceptional, not automatic.

**III. Sound Statutory Construction Counsels Against Reading § 1981a to Make Punitive Damages the Norm in Title VII Litigation.**

This Court has repeatedly cautioned against expansive readings of remedial civil rights statutes that were not clearly intended by Congress. See *Patterson v. McLean Credit Union*, 491 U.S. 164, 171 (1989) (declining to extend statutory remedies beyond what Congress plainly authorized). Because liability under Title VII may rest on circumstantial inferences alone, see *Hicks*, 509 U.S. at 511, collapsing the punitive-damages inquiry into the liability inquiry would expose employers to punitive exposure in virtually every case, contrary to Congress's evident intent to reserve that remedy for egregious misconduct. The caps Congress imposed on damages, see 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3), make sense only if punitive damages are the exception, not the rule.

For these reasons, this Court should hold that an employer's conduct must be egregious, independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII.
