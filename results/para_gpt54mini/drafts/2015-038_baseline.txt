**Argument**

ANILCA does not strip the National Park Service of authority to regulate conduct on all nonfederal land and water within Alaska park-system boundaries. The statute’s text, structure, and history confirm that Congress adopted a limited carveout for “lands owned by the State of Alaska, or any Native Corporation, or any private party” only when those lands are “within the boundaries of any conservation system unit,” and only to the extent a regulation would apply “solely to public lands within such units.” Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act, Pub. L. No. 96-487, § 103(c), 94 Stat. 2371, 2378 (1980) (codified at 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c)). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the hovercraft ban is not such a regulation. Sturgeon v. Frost, 768 F.3d 1066, 1071-78 (9th Cir. 2014).

1. **Section 103(c) does not categorically withdraw NPS authority over nonfederal lands and waters.**

This Court has long instructed that statutory interpretation begins with the text. Robinson v. Shell Oil Co., 519 U.S. 337, 340 (1997). Section 103(c) states that “only those lands which are within the boundaries of any conservation system unit, National Recreation Area, National Conservation Area, national monument, national preserve, or any other unit of the National Park System which are privately owned lands or lands conveyed to the State of Alaska or to any Native Corporation shall be deemed excluded from the purposes of this Act,” and that “no regulation shall apply to non-Federal land within the boundaries of any conservation system unit except as may be necessary and appropriate to control activities which are subject to the jurisdiction of the United States.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The key phrase is “apply to non-Federal land within the boundaries” only when a regulation is otherwise directed “solely to public lands within such units.” Id. The statute thus preserves federal regulation when the challenged rule is not limited to federal land alone. Sturgeon, 768 F.3d at 1075-76.

The hovercraft regulation, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), is a generally applicable park regulation that bans hovercraft use in National Park System areas. It is not a rule “solely” targeting public lands in Alaska. The Ninth Circuit properly held that such a regulation is outside § 103(c)’s carveout. Sturgeon, 768 F.3d at 1077-78.

2. **The National Park System’s jurisdiction is unit-wide, even where title to submerged lands or other nonfederal lands may be state-owned.**

This Court has recognized that federal authority can coexist with state title or interests in lands and waters. Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529, 543-45 (1976); Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 525-26 (1897). In Alaska specifically, the Court has repeatedly acknowledged that navigable waters and submerged lands may be owned by the State while the United States retains regulatory authority for federal purposes. PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana, 565 U.S. 576, 604-05 (2012). Nothing in ANILCA requires the extraordinary inference that Congress meant to disable the Park Service from enforcing general park rules whenever a regulated activity occurs on state-owned waters that happen to lie within park boundaries. Sturgeon, 768 F.3d at 1076-77.

Indeed, Congress built ANILCA around the premise that Alaska’s conservation units would remain part of the National Park System, subject to federal management. Alaska Nat. Interest Lands Conservation Act, Pub. L. No. 96-487, § 101, 94 Stat. 2371, 2372-74 (1980). Section 103(c) is an exception, not a wholesale jurisdictional transfer. See Sturgeon v. Masica, 768 F.3d at 1074.

3. **The statutory history confirms that § 103(c) was not designed to create a broad immunity for conduct on nonfederal lands and waters.**

Legislative history may not override plain text, but it may confirm it. Milner v. Department of the Navy, 562 U.S. 562, 572-73 (2011). Here, the history shows Congress’s concern that Alaska landowners not have their title altered by inclusion within park boundaries, not that the NPS be barred from regulating conduct affecting park resources or park administration. See S. Rep. No. 96-413, at 150-51 (1979); H.R. Rep. No. 96-97, pt. 2, at 158-60 (1979). That is consistent with the Ninth Circuit’s reading that § 103(c) removes nonfederal lands from being “deemed” federal park lands, but does not eliminate federal regulation that operates across the park as a whole. Sturgeon, 768 F.3d at 1074-75.

4. **Petitioner’s contrary reading would create absurd and administratively unworkable results.**

Statutes should not be read to produce absurd consequences. United States v. Granderson, 511 U.S. 39, 47-48 (1994). Under petitioner’s theory, the Park Service could not enforce any generally applicable park regulation on state-owned inholdings, easements, or navigable waters within a preserve—even rules governing fires, hunting access, sanitation, wildlife disturbance, or emergency response—so long as a nonfederal property interest was implicated. That sweeping immunity finds no support in ANILCA’s text or this Court’s federal-public-lands precedents. See Kleppe, 426 U.S. at 539-41.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
