The Board’s order should not be enforced because its polling rule is not “rational and consistent” with the Act, and because the Board’s own factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The Act nowhere prescribes a special polling standard. Instead, the Board must administer the statute in a manner that “is reasonable and defensible under the statute.” NLRB v. Seven-Up Bottling Co. of Miami, Inc., 344 U.S. 344, 346 (1953); see also NLRB v. Pittsburgh S.S. Co., 337 U.S. 656, 659-60 (1949). Here, the Board’s rule perversely allows an employer to do the more intrusive thing only after a showing sufficient to justify the less intrusive thing. That inversion is inconsistent with the Act’s structure and with the Board’s duty to apply “reasoned decisionmaking.” SEC v. Chenery Corp., 332 U.S. 194, 203 (1947).

First, the Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” polling standard is irrational because it collapses the distinction between polling and withdrawal of recognition. A union may be presumed to enjoy continuing majority support, but that presumption has always been rebuttable by objective circumstances showing loss of support. NLRB v. Bradford Dyeing Ass’n, 310 U.S. 318, 339-40 (1940); Brooks v. NLRB, 348 U.S. 96, 98-100 (1954). The Board cannot sensibly insist that an employer already possess enough evidence to justify a unilateral refusal to bargain before it may ask employees whether the union still enjoys support. That rule strips polling of any independent function and conflicts with the Act’s preference for reliable determinations of majority status through employee choice rather than administrative fiat. See NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 45-46 (1937); Labor Bd. v. Curtin Matheson Sci., Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 789 (1990) (the Board must act consistently with the Act’s policies).

Second, the Board’s evidentiary ruling against Allentown Mack is unsupported by substantial evidence. Under Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951), reviewing courts must consider “the record in its entirety,” including evidence that detracts from the agency’s conclusion. The Board discounted concrete, contemporaneous statements from multiple employees indicating loss of support, and it disregarded probative testimony from employees familiar with unit sentiment. That approach conflicts with Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229-30 (1938), which requires substantial evidence, not speculation or selective crediting of the record. It also ignores the long-settled rule that administrative findings cannot rest on “mere suspicion” or inference untethered to the evidence. NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co., 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939).

The Board compounded that error by treating ordinary employee expressions of dissatisfaction as legally irrelevant while crediting the union’s presumed support. But employee statements bearing on representation must be weighed in context, not dismissed by categorical rule. See Pittsburgh S.S. Co., 337 U.S. at 660; Consolidated Edison, 305 U.S. at 229-30. Where, as here, the employer had multiple objective indications of erosion in support, the Board could not lawfully conclude that a reasonable doubt was absent without substantial record support. Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 488. To uphold this order would be to permit the Board to substitute presumption for proof, contrary to the Act and to the substantial-evidence standard. NLRB v. Walton Mfg. Co., 369 U.S. 404, 405-06 (1962).

Finally, deference does not save the Board. The Court defers to agency judgment only when the Board’s construction is reasonable and faithful to statutory policy. NLRB v. Hearst Publ’ns, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 130-31 (1944); NLRB v. Seven-Up Bottling, 344 U.S. at 346. A rule that makes polling practically unavailable unless the employer could already lawfully withdraw recognition is not faithful to the Act’s text or purposes. The Court should therefore reject the Board’s polling standard and hold that, on this record, Allentown Mack had an objectively reasonable basis to doubt the union’s majority support. At minimum, the Board’s contrary factual finding should be set aside for want of substantial evidence. Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 488; Columbian Enameling, 306 U.S. at 300.
