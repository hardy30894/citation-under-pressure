The statute’s text, structure, and pre-1970 authority all confirm that the Government may obtain the ten-year collection period only by assessing the particular taxpayer from whom it seeks collection. Section 6502’s ten-year extension runs only when “the assessment of any tax” has been made within the limitations period, 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a), and an “assessment” is the formal recording of liability against a specific taxpayer. As this Court explained in Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247 (1935), an assessment is the administrative act that “fixes the liability” and starts collection remedies. See also Dixie Pine Products Co. v. Commissioner, 320 U.S. 516 (1944) (assessment and collection proceed against the taxpayer whose liability has been administratively determined). That rule cannot be stretched to permit collection from an individual who was never assessed merely because an entity related to him was.

The Ninth Circuit’s pre-1970 cases point in the same direction. In Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), the Court recognized that a partner could be personally liable for partnership taxes as a matter of state law, but the case did not hold that an assessment against the partnership alone authorized collection from the partner without more. To the contrary, the district court opinion affirmed in Young emphasized that naming individual partners in the assessment was unnecessary only for purposes of establishing substantive liability where the tax had already been paid; it did not decide that the Government could later enforce that assessment against the partner’s assets absent an individual assessment or judgment. Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831 (S.D. Cal. 1959), aff’d, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960).

United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), is even clearer. There, the Court held that a federal tax lien could not attach to a particular individual’s property unless the underlying tax obligation had been assessed against him under the Code’s assessment provisions. Id. at 458, 462-63. Coson recognized that assessment is taxpayer-specific and that the Government cannot bootstrap a proceeding against one taxpayer from an assessment made only against another. Id. That reasoning defeats the Government’s position here.

The Government’s contrary theory also conflicts with general principles of partnership law recognized before 1970. Although partners may be jointly liable for partnership debts, a creditor ordinarily must proceed against the partner in a manner that satisfies the requirements of personal liability. See, e.g., Merchants’ Nat’l Bank v. Wehrmann, 202 U.S. 295 (1906) (partners’ liability depends on the legal basis for proceeding against them personally). State-law liability does not itself create a federal assessment against the partner. And federal collection remedies, especially the extraordinary ten-year levy-and-suit period, turn on compliance with the tax statutes, not on the mere existence of some underlying obligation.

The Government relies on the notion that because the partnership was assessed, the partners were too. But the Code treats “person” and “taxpayer” as distinct and includes both individuals and partnerships within those definitions. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). When Congress authorizes assessment against “the taxpayer,” it means the particular person whose liability is being recorded. Bull, 295 U.S. 247; Coson, 286 F.2d 453. If the IRS could extend the limitations period against a partner without ever assessing that partner, then the three-year assessment period in § 6501(a) would be rendered meaningless as to individuals who happened to be associated with an assessed entity.

That is especially so here, where the Government seeks to collect from the Gallettis and the Briguglios after the ordinary limitations period expired without any individual assessment. The tax assessment against the partnership extended collection time only as to the assessed taxpayer. Because no assessment was made against respondents, the ten-year period in § 6502 never began to run against them. Under Bull, Dixie Pine, Young, and Coson, the Government cannot convert an entity assessment into an individual one by indirection.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
