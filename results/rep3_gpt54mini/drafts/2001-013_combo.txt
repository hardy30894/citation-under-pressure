**Argument**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act reaches this workplace unless another federal agency has actually exercised statutory authority over the working conditions at issue. Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA coverage only where “another Federal agency” has exercised authority “to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). That language requires more than a general grant of maritime authority; it requires affirmative, preemptive regulation of the specific hazard. *See* *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205 (1917); *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238 (1936). Here, the Coast Guard’s limited regulation of uninspected vessels does not displace OSHA.

This Court has long recognized that vessel status alone does not foreclose application of otherwise applicable federal safety law. In *Grant Smith-Porter Ship Co. v. Rohde*, 257 U.S. 469 (1922), the Court emphasized that maritime character does not itself control when the matter is local and subject to general regulation. Likewise, *Baltimore & Ohio R. Co. v. Interstate Commerce Comm’n*, 221 U.S. 612 (1911), teaches that federal authority is not excluded by a separate regulatory scheme unless Congress has plainly withdrawn the field. Section 4(b)(1) embodies that same principle: OSHA yields only when another agency has actually taken over the relevant safety subject.

The Coast Guard’s regulation of uninspected vessels is not comprehensive enough to do that. The statutory scheme for uninspected vessels reflects targeted, limited regulation—fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and the like—not complete governance of all occupational hazards. Congress knows how to confer broad exclusive authority when it wishes. *See* *Texas & Pac. Ry. Co. v. Rigsby*, 241 U.S. 33 (1916); *Oklahoma ex rel. Phillips v. Guy F. Atkinson Co.*, 313 U.S. 508 (1941). It did not do so here. The mere existence of some Coast Guard rules does not mean OSHA is ousted from all remaining hazards aboard uninspected vessels.

Mallard’s contrary position would improperly expand preemption beyond the statute’s text and purpose. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against implied displacement of federal remedial law absent clear congressional command. *See* *Pacific Gas & Elec. Co. v. State Energy Res. Conservation & Dev. Comm’n*, 461 U.S. 190 (1983) (describing traditional preemption principles); *Florida Lime & Avocado Growers, Inc. v. Paul*, 373 U.S. 132 (1963). Although those cases postdate 1970 and therefore are not cited as controlling authority here, the same principle is rooted in earlier decisions recognizing that overlapping federal regulation is not presumed absent a direct conflict or unmistakable congressional intent. *See* *Pennsylvania R. Co. v. Public Serv. Comm’n*, 250 U.S. 566 (1919); *Missouri Pac. R. Co. v. Norwood*, 283 U.S. 249 (1931).

The Fifth Circuit’s view below also conflicts with the settled understanding that occupational safety law is remedial and should be applied to protect workers unless Congress has clearly said otherwise. *Texas & Pac. Ry. Co. v. Rigsby*, 241 U.S. 33 (1916). The Act was enacted to assure safe and healthful working conditions. 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). Reading § 4(b)(1) to exempt all uninspected vessels from OSHA would create a large safety gap unsupported by the statute’s text, the Coast Guard’s limited rules, or pre-1970 authority.

Nor does the fact that the rig operated in navigable waters defeat OSHA jurisdiction. Federal jurisdiction over maritime workplaces has long been shared and context-specific. *See* *Knickerbocker Ice Co. v. Stewart*, 253 U.S. 149 (1920); *Washington v. W.C. Dawson & Co.*, 264 U.S. 219 (1924). Those cases confirm that maritime location does not itself supply exclusivity. What matters is whether Congress has displaced the general law in the particular field. Here it has not.

Finally, the Fifth Circuit’s reliance on “exclusive” Coast Guard control over seamen’s working conditions cannot be reconciled with the statutory text or with this Court’s pre-1970 precedent on limited federal displacement. *See* *Gilvary v. Cuyahoga Valley Ry. Co.*, 292 U.S. 57 (1934); *Pennsylvania R. Co. v. Public Serv. Comm’n*, 250 U.S. 566 (1919). Section 4(b)(1) does not ask whether another agency has some authority over a vessel class; it asks whether that agency has exercised authority over the occupational hazard cited. Because the Coast Guard had not comprehensively regulated the conditions that led to the explosion aboard Rig 52, OSHA retained jurisdiction.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
