# ARGUMENT

## I. WOODARD HAS NO LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST IN CLEMENCY PROTECTED BY THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE

### A. Clemency Is an Act of Grace Outside the Judicial Process

This Court has long recognized that executive clemency is fundamentally different from the judicial adjudication of guilt. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), this Court held that a prisoner has no constitutionally protected liberty interest in the commutation of his sentence, even where the state's pardon board had granted commutation in a substantial percentage of cases considered. The Court explained that "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." *Id.* at 464. Clemency, unlike a trial, is not "a matter of right" but "a matter of grace." *Id.* at 464-65.

The Sixth Circuit's holding that Ohio's clemency procedure constitutes "an integral part of the ... system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant," relying on *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956), and *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), cannot be reconciled with *Dumschat*. As Judge Nelson correctly observed in dissent below, "[e]very member of the [*Dumschat*] Court obviously knew" that due process protects liberty interests independent of state-created procedures, yet the Court still found no protected interest. The *Dumschat* Court was fully aware of the "second strand" of due process analysis—liberty interests that exist independent of any statutory creation—and nonetheless rejected the claim.

*Evitts* is inapposite. That case addressed the right to effective assistance of counsel on a first appeal *as of right*—a proceeding integral to the adjudicatory process because it determines the very legality of the conviction under state-created appellate procedures. Clemency, by contrast, occurs only after the judicial process has run its course. This Court has already drawn this line: even in habeas corpus proceedings, which "form an integral part of the adjudicative system much more clearly than executive clemency proceedings do," the right to counsel does not extend that far. *Evitts*, 469 U.S. at 394 (citing *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974)). If due process protections do not reach habeas, they cannot reach clemency, which lies further still from the judicial process.

### B. Extending Due Process to Clemency Would Create Unworkable and Unprecedented Constitutional Rules

The Sixth Circuit's approach—applying due process scrutiny to clemency while acknowledging the resulting protections would be "minimal, perhaps even barely perceptible"—reveals the fundamental incoherence of extending judicial review to what has always been an executive act of grace. This Court has cautioned against pushing due process logic to its extreme when doing so produces "unintended consequences." *See Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 483 (1995) (noting that *Hewitt v. Helms*, 459 U.S. 460 (1983), had "create[d] disincentives for States to codify prison management procedures in the interest of uniform treatment").

The same danger exists here. If this Court affirms the Sixth Circuit's judgment, states like Ohio will face powerful disincentives to offer any clemency procedures at all, lest doing so trigger constitutional scrutiny of an area historically committed to unreviewable executive discretion. No other federal court has constitutionalized clemency proceedings, and this Court should not be the first to do so.

## II. THE VOLUNTARY CLEMENCY INTERVIEW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIFTH AMENDMENT

### A. The Regulation Does Not Compel Self-Incrimination

Ohio's clemency procedure imposes no compulsion whatsoever. The regulation "does not empower the parole board or its representatives to compel a prisoner who requests a personal interview to answer a single question." A prisoner "remains free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes." The voluntary nature of the interview is fatal to any Fifth Amendment claim, because the privilege against self-incrimination protects against *compelled* testimony, not the voluntary choice to speak.

### B. Any Fifth Amendment Claim Is Premature

Even assuming some theoretical risk that statements made during a voluntary clemency interview could later be used against Woodard, that risk remains entirely speculative. This Court's decision in *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), controls. There, the Court held that relief addressing a hypothetical future use of statements in as-yet-uninitiated proceedings was "premature." *Id.* at 324. The same reasoning applies with full force here: Woodard's own complaint disclaims that this action will have any effect on his "state convictions and/or sentences (including his death sentence)." Any risk of self-incrimination thus relates only to hypothetical future criminal proceedings that have not been initiated and may never be initiated.

Requiring Ohio to restructure its clemency procedures to guard against such a speculative risk would require courts to engage in exactly the kind of "highly theoretical undertaking" that this Court has repeatedly declined to entertain. The proper course, consistent with *Baxter*, is to await an actual case in which compelled testimony is sought to be used against a defendant—not to invalidate a voluntary interview procedure based on conjecture.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be reversed.
