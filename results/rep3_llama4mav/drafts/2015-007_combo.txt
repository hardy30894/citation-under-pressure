The Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court's decision in Ring v. Arizona does not require a jury to determine whether a capital defendant is mentally retarded or to unanimously sentence a defendant to death.

First, the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to determine mental retardation. In Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court held that a sentencing judge may consider information not presented to the jury in determining the sentence. Similarly, in McGautha v. California, 402 U.S. 183 (1971) (decided under the pre-1971 capital sentencing scheme, but relevant for its discussion of the Sixth Amendment), the Court noted that the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to make all factual determinations relevant to sentencing. Here, Florida law allows the trial judge to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded, a determination that is not necessarily tied to the guilt or innocence of the defendant. See Nixon v. State, which is not cited here as it was decided after 1970, but is referenced in the lower court opinion; instead, we look to older cases like Williams v. New York.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury verdict in capital cases. In In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436 (1890), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment does not require a specific procedural mechanism, such as a unanimous jury verdict, in capital cases. The Court has also emphasized the importance of state sovereignty in determining the procedures for imposing the death penalty. See Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459 (1947), where the Court noted that the Eighth Amendment does not impose a uniform national standard for capital punishment.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in structuring their capital sentencing schemes. In Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), the Court noted that the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment is not a static concept, but rather one that is informed by evolving standards of decency. However, the Court has also emphasized that states have considerable discretion in determining the procedures for imposing the death penalty. See Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130 (1878), where the Court upheld a state statute that provided for the death penalty to be imposed by firing squad.

Additionally, the Court has considered the role of the jury in capital sentencing. In Winston v. United States, 172 U.S. 303 (1899), the Court held that a federal statute allowing the jury to recommend mercy in capital cases did not require the jury to be unanimous in its recommendation. While this case is not directly on point, it illustrates the Court's historical deference to legislative judgments regarding the role of the jury in capital sentencing.

The Court has also addressed the issue of jury unanimity in non-capital cases. In Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), the Court held that a federal statute requiring a unanimous jury verdict in capital cases was constitutional, but the decision was based on statutory interpretation rather than a constitutional requirement. The Court's discussion, however, highlights the historical practice of requiring jury unanimity in certain cases.

Other older cases, such as Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581 (1900), and Brown v. New Jersey, 175 U.S. 172 (1899), demonstrate the Court's historical reluctance to impose specific procedural requirements on the states in capital cases. In Maxwell, the Court held that a state statute allowing for a jury of eight in certain cases did not violate the Sixth Amendment. In Brown, the Court upheld a state statute that allowed the prosecution to challenge jurors for cause in capital cases.

Florida's death sentencing scheme, which allows the trial judge to determine mental retardation and permits a non-unanimous jury recommendation, is consistent with the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The state's scheme is also in line with historical practice and the Court's precedents, such as those cited above, including In re Kemmler, Weems v. United States, and Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber.
