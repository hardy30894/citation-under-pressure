**Argument**

**I. A District Court Must Apply a Rigorous Analysis to a Plaintiff’s Proposed Damages Methodology Before Certifying a Class.**

A district court may not certify a class under Rule 23(b)(3) without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The Supreme Court established this requirement in *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, holding that a plaintiff must prove damages are calculable on a class-wide basis to satisfy Rule 23(b)(3). *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997)*. The Third Circuit reinforced this standard in *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, requiring a plaintiff to demonstrate a "common methodology" to measure damages. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation, 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008)*. Without such a methodology, individualized issues predominate, defeating Rule 23(b)(3).

**II. The District Court Failed to Apply the "Rigorous Analysis" Required by *Hydrogen Peroxide* Because It Accepted the Existence of a Damages Model Without Testing Its Reliability or Admissibility.**

The District Court found that Plaintiffs had established a "common methodology" to measure damages based on the testimony of Dr. James McClave. However, the court never tested the model’s reliability or admissibility under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.* *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579 (1993)*. The court assumed the model was valid without the necessary gatekeeping. *Hydrogen Peroxide* requires a "rigorous analysis" of the evidence, not a cursory acceptance of a plaintiff’s expert’s existence. *Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 318*. By certifying the class without testing the model, the District Court effectively decided the merits of the case at the certification stage.

**III. The District Court’s Certification Order Violated the Limits of the Rule 23 Inquiry Established in *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*.**

The Supreme Court has cautioned against turning class certification into a "mini-trial" on the merits. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 355 (2011)*. The District Court’s decision to accept Dr. McClave’s model as valid without the requisite evidentiary scrutiny crossed this line. The court failed to determine whether the model was admissible or reliable, thereby resolving a central dispute in the case before trial. This error constitutes an abuse of discretion.
