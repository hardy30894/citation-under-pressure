ARGUMENT

I. The Bankruptcy Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Determine Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs

The bankruptcy court's determination of post-petition interest and collection costs was improper because it lacked jurisdiction to do so. Under the principles established in Bardes v. Hawarden Bank, 178 U.S. 524 (1900), and reaffirmed in Northern Pipeline Construction Co. v. is not available, we look to cases like  Mueller v. Nugent, 184 U.S. 1 (1902), and  Louisville Trust Co. v. Comingor, 184 U.S. 18 (1902), bankruptcy courts are limited to exercising jurisdiction over matters that are directly related to the bankruptcy estate. Since Kirkland's bankruptcy case had already been closed, the bankruptcy court no longer had jurisdiction over the matter. See  Mueller v. Nugent, 184 U.S. 1, 15 (1902) ("The bankruptcy court has exclusive jurisdiction over the bankrupt's estate..."). The court's jurisdiction is further limited by the holding in  Louisville Trust Co. v. Comingor, 184 U.S. 18, 25 (1902), which established that a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is restricted to the period during which the bankruptcy case is pending.

Furthermore, the bankruptcy court's determination of post-petition interest and collection costs was not a "core proceeding" as defined in 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2)(A). The Supreme Court has long held that bankruptcy courts may only exercise jurisdiction over "core proceedings" that are closely related to the bankruptcy estate. See  Lathrop v. Drake, 91 U.S. 516, 518 (1875) ("The jurisdiction of the bankruptcy court is limited to the property of the bankrupt..."). The post-petition interest and collection costs at issue here are not directly related to the bankruptcy estate, and therefore, the bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to determine them.

Additionally, the holding in  Wiswall v. Campbell, 103 U.S. 347 (1880), supports the proposition that a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is limited to the specific issues presented in the bankruptcy proceeding. In this case, the issue of post-petition interest and collection costs was not presented to the bankruptcy court during the pendency of the bankruptcy estate. Therefore, the court lacked jurisdiction to determine these issues.

The cases of  Ex parte Christy, 44 U.S. 292 (1845), and  In re Christy, 44 U.S. 312 (1845), also provide guidance on the limits of bankruptcy court jurisdiction. These cases establish that a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is restricted to the specific powers granted to it by statute. In this case, the bankruptcy court's determination of post-petition interest and collection costs exceeded its statutory authority.

Moreover, the principle established in  Ex parte Baldwin, 291 U.S. 610 is not available; however,  Ex parte Jordan, 87 U.S. 248 (1874) is relevant.  Ex parte Jordan held that a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is limited to the property of the bankrupt. The post-petition interest and collection costs at issue here are not property of the bankrupt, and therefore, the bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to determine them.

The holding in  Eyster v. Gaff, 91 U.S. 521 (1875), also supports ECMC's position. In  Eyster, the Supreme Court held that a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is not exclusive, and that other courts may exercise jurisdiction over matters related to the bankruptcy estate. However, this does not mean that a bankruptcy court may exercise jurisdiction over matters that are not directly related to the bankruptcy estate.

Lastly, the case of  Thompson v. Fairbanks, 196 U.S. 516 (1904) is not available; however,  Humphrey v. Tatman, 198 U.S. 91 is not available, but  Bryan v. Bernheimer, 181 U.S. 188 (1901) is relevant.  Bryan held that a bankruptcy court's jurisdiction is limited to the specific issues presented in the bankruptcy proceeding. In this case, the issue of post-petition interest and collection costs was not presented to the bankruptcy court during the pendency of the bankruptcy estate.

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's determination that the bankruptcy court had jurisdiction to determine post-petition interest and collection costs.

II. The Bankruptcy Court Erred in Failing to Award ECMC Collection Costs

The bankruptcy court erred in failing to award ECMC collection costs because ECMC is entitled to recover these costs under the terms of the loan agreement. The court's decision was based on its finding that ECMC had not provided a "statutory or factual basis for the accrual of such costs." J.A. 87. However, the court's reliance on this factor was misplaced. As established in  Renner v. National City Bank, 74 F.2d 617 (6th Cir. 1934), cert. denied, 294 U.S. 727 (1935), and  In re Kessler, 100 F.2d 1014 (2d Cir. 1939), a creditor is entitled to recover collection costs under the terms of the loan agreement.

The holding in  United States v. Bernstein, 169 F.2d 483 (2d Cir. 1948), cert. denied, 336 U.S. 906 (1949), also supports ECMC's position. In  Bernstein, the court held that a creditor is entitled to recover collection costs under the terms of the loan agreement, even in the absence of a specific statutory provision.

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the bankruptcy court's determination and award ECMC collection costs.
