The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to negate the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from an insanity defense. The Constitution permits states to regulate the admissibility of evidence, including expert testimony, as long as the rules are not arbitrary or disproportionate to the purposes they are designed to serve. See Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 437, 446 (1992).

Arizona's rule limiting the use of expert evidence to the insanity defense is a reasonable restriction that serves the state's legitimate interest in preventing confusion and overlap between the insanity defense and the element of mens rea. See State v. Mott, 931 P.2d 1046, 1051 (Ariz. 1997). By placing the burden of proving insanity on the defendant and limiting the use of expert evidence to this defense, Arizona's law avoids the potential for duplicative and confusing jury instructions. See Finger v. State, 27 P.3d 274, 289 (Ariz. 2001).

The Supreme Court has recognized that states have flexibility in defining the rules governing the insanity defense and the admissibility of evidence related to it. See Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 536 (1968). Arizona's law is consistent with this principle, as it allows defendants to present expert evidence to support an insanity defense while maintaining a clear distinction between this defense and the prosecution's burden to prove mens rea.

In this case, the trial court properly applied Arizona law by limiting the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity defense. The court's ruling did not infringe on Clark's due process rights, as it was based on a reasonable and well-established rule of evidence. See Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 302-03 (1973) (recognizing that states have the authority to establish rules of evidence that may limit a defendant's ability to present certain testimony).

The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed, as Clark's due process rights were not violated by the trial court's ruling limiting the use of his expert evidence to his insanity defense.
