**ARGUMENT**

**I. An Inherited IRA Is Not Composed of “Retirement Funds” Within the Meaning of 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12).**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts only “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. The phrase “retirement funds” is undefined, but its ordinary meaning requires assets “set aside for retirement.” *Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320, 326 (2005). An inherited IRA fails this test because the Bankruptcy Code looks to the character of the funds in the debtor’s hands, not their prior status in another person’s estate. *See Schwab v. Reilly*, 560 U.S. 770, 782 (2010) (exemptions turn on “the debtor’s interest” at the commencement of the case).

The Internal Revenue Code itself confirms the distinction. Once an IRA passes to a non-spouse beneficiary, “no new contributions can be made,” the account “cannot be rolled over,” and distributions must begin within one year of the original owner’s death. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); *see also* 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B). These mandatory withdrawals are available for “current consumption,” not for the beneficiary’s retirement. The funds therefore lose their character as retirement savings upon inheritance.

This conclusion follows from the principle that “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987). Allowing an inherited IRA to remain exempt would extend tax-deferred treatment across generations, converting a limited deferral into permanent exemption—a result Congress did not enact. The same logic that prevents a debtor from claiming an inherited homestead as exempt, *see* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A), precludes treating inherited retirement assets as the debtor’s own retirement funds.

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary view in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and the Eighth Circuit BAP’s decision in *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), overlook that the statutory text requires the funds to retain their retirement character in the debtor’s possession. Because the Clarks’ inherited IRA does not, it is not exempt.
