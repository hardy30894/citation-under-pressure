**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE EXEMPT AS “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL”

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. § 621–634, does not extend to appointed state court judges because they fall within the statutory exemption for “appointees on the policymaking level.” Congress enacted this exception to preserve the independence of the executive and legislative branches from age-based employment restrictions, and the judiciary—particularly in its role as a co-equal branch of government—must be understood as equally entitled to the protection of this exemption. The Supreme Court has long recognized that judges exercise substantial policymaking authority in the performance of their duties, and that the function of judging inherently involves the creation and interpretation of law, which is a quintessential exercise of policymaking.

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person ... chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The phrase “policymaking level” is not limited to executive or legislative appointees; rather, it applies to any individual whose responsibilities involve the formulation or implementation of policy in a manner that affects the operation of government. As the First Circuit held in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), “policymaking” is “indisputably a part of the function of judging” because judicial decisions often resolve unsettled legal questions and establish binding precedent that governs future conduct by individuals, businesses, and government entities. This is not mere interpretation—it is lawmaking in the common law tradition.

The role of a state judge is not limited to mechanically applying settled rules to fact patterns. As the Eighth Circuit correctly observed, “the reality of judging is that it is not always—perhaps not even usually—merely a matter of mechanically applying settled, unambiguous principles of law.” *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 604 (8th Cir. 1990). Judges regularly confront legal gaps, interpret ambiguous statutes, and develop new doctrines—such as the Missouri Supreme Court’s adoption of comparative negligence in *Gustafson v. Benda*, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo.1983) (en banc)—that have profound societal consequences. These decisions are not mere adjudication; they are the creation of legal policy. The Court has recognized that “the judiciary is not a passive instrument of the law but an active participant in the development of legal principles.” *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 703 (1974).

Moreover, Missouri’s appointed judges exercise significant supervisory and administrative authority over the state’s court system. The Missouri Constitution vests the Supreme Court with the power to adopt rules governing the conduct of the judiciary and the bar, to resolve internal disputes among judicial officers, and to oversee the administration of justice across the state. See Mo. Const. art. V, § 4. The Chief Justice, Chief Judges of appellate divisions, and Presiding Judges in circuit courts all serve in policymaking roles, setting standards for court operations, managing judicial resources, and ensuring the efficient delivery of justice. These responsibilities are no less policymaking in nature than those of a cabinet secretary or a legislative aide.

The petitioners’ argument that judges are not “on the policymaking level” because they are not “elected” or “political” is unavailing. The ADEA’s exemption is not based on electoral status but on functional role. The legislative history confirms that Congress intended the “policymaking level” exception to apply broadly to individuals who exercise discretion in shaping government policy, regardless of branch. See *Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746*, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180 (“It is the intention of the conferees to exempt ... persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments”). The judiciary is a “department” of state government, and its appointed members are no less policymaking than their counterparts in the executive or legislative branches.

To hold otherwise would create an absurd result: that judges, who shape the law and govern the administration of justice, are subject to the ADEA while cabinet secretaries and legislative aides are not. Such a reading would undermine the structural integrity of the federal system and contradict the clear intent of Congress to protect the policymaking functions of government. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “in traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved.” *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). Here, Congress did not clearly intend to subject state judges to the ADEA. The exemption for “appointees on the policymaking level” must be interpreted to include judges, who are among the most consequential policymakers in state government.

II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT OF STATE JUDGES AT AGE SEVENTY IS RATIONALLY RELATED TO A LEGITIMATE STATE INTEREST

Even if the ADEA applied, Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for judges does not violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The standard of review is rational basis: a law is constitutional if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest. *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985).
