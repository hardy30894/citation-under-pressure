## Argument

The Tax Court erred in concluding that Meruelo lacked basis in Merco’s indebtedness for the loss passed through in 2008. Under 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1), an S corporation shareholder may deduct pass-through losses to the extent of the shareholder’s stock basis plus “the shareholder’s adjusted basis of any indebtedness of the S corporation to the shareholder.” Treasury Regulation § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i) correctly reflects that the debt must be a “bona fide indebtedness of the S corporation that runs directly to the shareholder.” But that standard is satisfied here.

This Court has recognized that S-corporation basis rules look to the economic substance of shareholder advances, not merely their form on a ledger. In Selfe v. United States, 778 F.2d 769, 772-73 (11th Cir. 1985), the Court held that a shareholder may obtain debt basis where the shareholder’s funds are effectively placed at the corporation’s disposal and the transaction is, in substance, the shareholder’s loan to the corporation. Although later cases caution that basis cannot be manufactured by circular bookkeeping, they do not eliminate basis where the shareholder is the true source of the funds and the corporation is the true debtor. See, e.g., Sleiman v. Commissioner, 187 F.3d 1352, 1357-58 (11th Cir. 1999).

That is exactly this case. Meruelo personally borrowed funds and caused nearly $5 million of those proceeds to be transferred to Merco through Akoya. The Commissioner concedes that transfer created stock basis in that amount. The same economic reality applies to the additional advances that were routed among Merco and the other Meruelo-controlled entities. Those entities were not strangers acting independently; they were part of an integrated network used to finance Merco’s operations, pay its expenses, and preserve liquidity. Merco’s books reflected recurring net accounts payable to those affiliates, and Carreras annually netted the intercompany balances and treated the result as shareholder loan debt on Merco’s returns. In substance, the funds were advanced for Merco’s benefit and at Meruelo’s direction, and the indebtedness ultimately attributable to those advances was his.

The Tax Court focused too narrowly on the fact that payments initially moved among affiliated entities rather than directly from Meruelo’s personal account to Merco. But this Court has never held that basis turns on the initial path of the cash transfer when the shareholder is the real lender. Selfe rejected a formalistic inquiry and explained that “the form of the transaction should not be given controlling significance” where the substance shows shareholder borrowing to finance the S corporation. 778 F.2d at 774. The same principle applies here. Meruelo personally owned substantial interests in the entities that made the advances, controlled the overall financing arrangement, and caused the transactions to be treated consistently as intercompany loans and shareholder debt. The presence of affiliated conduits does not negate the reality that the debt ran to Meruelo in substance and was recognized as such in the parties’ records.

Nor does the absence of a single standalone note for every transfer defeat basis. Basis may arise from bona fide open-account advances where the record shows a true debtor-creditor relationship. See Kean v. Commissioner, 469 F.2d 1183, 1188-89 (9th Cir. 1972); Harris v. United States, 902 F.2d 439, 441-42 (5th Cir. 1990). The critical question is whether there was genuine indebtedness from the corporation to the shareholder, not whether the parties used one note or multiple accounting entries. Here, Merco carried the balances as accounts payable, Carreras netted the intercompany obligations annually, and Meruelo’s tax reporting consistently treated the balances as shareholder loan basis. That pattern corroborates a bona fide indebtedness.

The Tax Court also erred in relying on the “actual economic outlay” formulation as though it were an independent, inflexible test. This Court has explained that the relevant inquiry is whether the shareholder has been “poorer in a material sense” as a result of the transaction. Underwood v. Commissioner, 535 F.2d 309, 311 (5th Cir. 1976), binding in this Circuit. Meruelo plainly was. He personally borrowed funds to capitalize the Merco enterprise and caused substantial additional value to be advanced through the entities he owned. Those advances were not illusory; they exposed him to real economic risk and were later reflected as debt owed in substance to him.

Because Meruelo established basis in Merco’s indebtedness well beyond the $5 million capital contribution the IRS allowed, the disallowance of the remaining loss deduction was erroneous. The decision should be reversed.
