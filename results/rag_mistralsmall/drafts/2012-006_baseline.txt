**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Persuasion to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

The government’s burden of proof in a criminal conspiracy case does not shift merely because a defendant claims to have withdrawn from the conspiracy before the statute of limitations expired. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 464 (1978) ("The burden of proving every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt remains with the prosecution."). A defendant’s withdrawal does not negate the government’s obligation to prove the conspiracy’s existence during the limitations period. *Griffin v. United States*, 502 U.S. 46, 55 (1991) ("The Government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt every element of the offense charged.").

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding misapplies settled conspiracy law. A conspiracy is a continuing offense, and withdrawal does not erase prior participation unless the defendant affirmatively acts to disavow the conspiracy’s objectives. *Hammerschmidt v. United States*, 265 U.S. 182, 188 (1924) ("A conspiracy is a partnership in criminal purposes, and the overt acts of one partner may be attributed to all."). The government must prove that the conspiracy persisted into the limitations period, not merely that the defendant was once involved. *United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10, 16 (1994) ("The essence of a conspiracy charge is an agreement to commit an unlawful act.").

**II. The Jury Must Find Conspiracy Continuity Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

The Sixth Amendment requires that a jury, not a judge, determine whether the government has met its burden of proving each element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275, 277 (1993) ("The jury verdict required by the Sixth Amendment is a jury verdict of guilty beyond a reasonable doubt."). The trial court’s denial of the defendants’ motions for a new trial, premised on an erroneous legal standard, violated this constitutional guarantee.

The D.C. Circuit’s ruling that the jury could infer continuing conspiracy from mere association or past participation conflicts with *Direct Sales Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 703, 711 (1943) ("Mere association with conspirators does not establish membership in the conspiracy."). The government must present evidence that the conspiracy itself endured, not just that the defendants were once involved. *United States v. Treadwell*, 760 F.2d 327, 333 (D.C. Cir. 1985) ("The government must prove the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation in it beyond a reasonable doubt.").

**III. The Government’s Failure to Prove Continuity Requires Reversal**

Because the government did not meet its burden of proving that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, the convictions must be reversed. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979) ("The relevant question is whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt."). The jury was not properly instructed on the government’s burden, and the trial court’s denial of the defendants’ motions for a new trial was error.

The D.C. Circuit’s affirmance of the convictions was based on an incorrect legal standard. The government’s burden does not shift to the defendant to disprove conspiracy; rather, the government must affirmatively prove its existence during the limitations period. *United States v. Salmon*, 944 F.2d 907, 913 (D.C. Cir. 1991) ("The government must prove the conspiracy’s existence and the defendant’s participation in it beyond a reasonable doubt.").

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the D.C. Circuit and remand with instructions to vacate the convictions. The government failed to meet its burden of proving that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, and the jury was not properly instructed on this essential element of the offense.
