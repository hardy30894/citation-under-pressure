**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN LIFE AND LIBERTY THAT TRIGGERS DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that Ohio’s clemency process does not implicate a constitutionally protected liberty interest. This Court has long recognized that clemency proceedings, while discretionary, are not beyond the reach of the Due Process Clause when they directly affect a prisoner’s life or liberty. In *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal & Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 12 (1979), the Court held that a state-created right to parole does not automatically create a protected liberty interest, but it did not foreclose the possibility that clemency—particularly in capital cases—may implicate due process concerns. More recently, in *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 484 (1995), the Court clarified that liberty interests may arise from state law or practice, provided they impose "atypical and significant hardship" on the prisoner. Clemency in Ohio, particularly for a death-sentenced inmate, plainly meets this threshold.

The Ohio Supreme Court has acknowledged that clemency is the "final safeguard" in the criminal justice system, *State v. Combs*, 67 Ohio St.3d 471, 476 (1993), and this Court has recognized that clemency proceedings may be "an integral part of the ... system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant," *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956). The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Connecticut Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981)—which involved a non-capital case and no allegation of a protected interest—is misplaced. As Justice Stevens noted in dissent, *Dumschat* did not address whether clemency in a capital case, where life itself is at stake, implicates due process. *Id.* at 469 (Stevens, J., dissenting). The Court has since reaffirmed that due process protections attach where state action "deprives an individual of life, liberty, or property." *Zinermon v. Burch*, 494 U.S. 113, 125 (1990). Ohio’s clemency process, by determining whether a death sentence will be carried out, plainly implicates these interests.

**II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS OHIO FROM IMPOSING A "HOBSON’S CHOICE" BETWEEN PARTICIPATING IN A CLEMENCY INTERVIEW AND ASSERTING THE RIGHT AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION.**

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that Ohio’s clemency process does not violate the Fifth Amendment is erroneous. The Fifth Amendment provides that no person "shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." U.S. Const. amend. V. This right is not limited to formal criminal proceedings; it applies wherever the state seeks to compel testimonial self-incrimination. *Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial Dist. Court of Nev., Humboldt Cty.*, 542 U.S. 177, 189 (2004).

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), is misplaced. In *Baxter*, the Court upheld a prison disciplinary proceeding where the inmate’s silence could be used against him, but it did not address whether the state could condition clemency on participation in an interview that could later be used in criminal proceedings. *Id.* at 319. Here, Ohio’s clemency process explicitly permits the use of statements made in an interview against the inmate in future proceedings. Ohio Admin. Code § 5120:1-1-12(C)(2). This creates an unconstitutional "Hobson’s choice": the inmate must either forego clemency or risk self-incrimination.

This Court has repeatedly condemned state actions that coerce waiver of constitutional rights. In *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972), the Court held that due process prohibits conditioning public employment on waiver of First Amendment rights. Similarly, in *Shelton v. Tucker*, 364 U.S. 479, 485 (1960), the Court struck down a law requiring teachers to disclose all organizational affiliations, noting that such compelled disclosures chill constitutional rights. The same principle applies here: Ohio cannot condition clemency on the waiver of Fifth Amendment rights.

**III. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN CONCLUDING THAT NO DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS ATTACH TO CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS.**

The Sixth Circuit’s analysis conflates two distinct strands of due process jurisprudence. First, it correctly held that Ohio’s clemency process does not create a protected liberty interest *per se*. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 465. However, it erred in concluding that no due process protections attach at all. As this Court has recognized, due process protections may arise where state action "imposes an atypical and significant hardship" on the prisoner. *Sandin*, 515 U.S. at 484. Clemency in a capital case plainly meets this standard.

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215 (1976)—which involved prison transfers—is inapposite. Unlike prison transfers, clemency directly determines whether a death sentence will be carried out. The Court has distinguished such "final" state actions from mere administrative decisions. *See Zinermon*, 494 U.S. at 136 (noting that due process protections attach where state action "deprives an individual of life, liberty, or property"). Ohio’s clemency process, by determining whether Woodard will live or die, plainly implicates these interests.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s judgment and hold that Ohio’s clemency process violates the Due Process and Fifth Amendments.
