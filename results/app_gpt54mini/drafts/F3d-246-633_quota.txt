**ARGUMENT**

I. The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Smoot’s Railway Labor Act conspiracy claim.

The Railway Labor Act imposes a duty on the union to represent employees without hostility, discrimination, or bad faith, and courts may review conduct that amounts to a union’s arbitrary or bad-faith sabotage of a member’s rights. *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co.*, 323 U.S. 192 (1944); *Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171 (1967). A union breaches that duty when it acts in a manner that is “arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith.” *Air Line Pilots Ass’n, Int’l v. O’Neill*, 499 U.S. 65 (1991). Summary judgment was improper because the record, viewed for Smoot, permits a finding that UTU, through Earley and other officials, abandoned Smoot’s grievance and aligned with CSX against him.

Smoot presented evidence that UTU’s internal decision-makers initially concluded he was entitled to the CSX stock, yet the union representative who handled the arbitration later participated in a closed executive session with CSX’s representative and the neutral arbitrator, after which Smoot lost his claim. A union’s duty of fair representation is not satisfied by nominal participation if the union’s conduct effectively deprives the employee of an honest presentation. *Vaca*, 386 U.S. at 190-93. Nor may summary judgment stand where circumstantial evidence supports a reasonable inference of collusion or hostile treatment. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242 (1986).

The district court also erred to the extent it treated the alleged wrongdoing as insulated by arbitral finality. Although finality is important in labor arbitration, it does not bar relief where the proceeding is tainted by fraud, deceit, or serious misconduct. *Union Pac. R.R. v. Sheehan*, 439 U.S. 89 (1978); *Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers v. Louisville & Nashville R.R.*, 373 U.S. 33 (1963). Smoot’s theory is precisely that the union and carrier conspired to ensure an adverse outcome by misuse of the executive session and by manipulating the grievance process. That is enough to require trial.

II. The district court improperly resolved disputed issues of fact and credibility at summary judgment.

Summary judgment is appropriate only when there is no genuine dispute of material fact. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317 (1986); *Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574 (1986). Courts must draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmovant. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255. Here, the court impermissibly credited Defendants’ version of the executive session and accepted their contention that Earley’s conduct was merely advocacy. But Smoot’s proof, including the taped discussion and the sequence of internal union actions, permits a contrary inference: that UTU and CSX coordinated against him, and that Earley’s conduct was materially adverse to Smoot’s interests.

The Sixth Circuit has repeatedly recognized that a plaintiff may prove conspiracy through circumstantial evidence and that motive and intent are often questions for the factfinder. *Browning v. Levy*, 283 F.3d 761 (6th Cir. 2002); *Hooks v. Hooks*, 771 F.2d 935 (6th Cir. 1985). The district court’s entry of summary judgment usurped that role.

III. The statutory wiretap damages award must be vacated or substantially reduced.

Even if the Act was violated, the district court’s damages award exceeds what the statute and precedent allow on this record. The Act provides statutory damages measured by the greater of actual damages or $100 a day for each day of violation, subject to a $10,000 minimum. 18 U.S.C. § 2520(c)(2). The Supreme Court has held that fee and damages awards under the Act are not automatic and must be grounded in the statute’s text and purposes. *Doe v. Chao*, 540 U.S. 614 (2004). Punitive damages may be awarded only where the court finds the violation was willful or intentional, and they must be supported by specific findings. *Smoot v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 30 F.3d 818 (7th Cir. 1994); *Reynolds v. Spears*, 93 F.3d 428 (8th Cir. 1996).

Here, the award of $250,000 in statutory damages and $100,000 in punitive damages is untethered to the record and duplicative in effect. The district court failed to explain how its figures related to any actual loss, the duration of any interception, or the statutory scheme. That is reversible error. *Doe*, 540 U.S. at 620-24.

IV. The attorneys’ fee award should also fall if the damages judgment is reversed.

Because the fee award rested on Defendants’ counterclaims and the district court’s prevailing-party determination, reversal or substantial reduction of the underlying judgment requires reconsideration of fees and costs. Fee awards are dependent on success on the merits and must be reasonable. *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983); *Perdue v. Kenny A. ex rel. Winn*, 559 U.S. 542 (2010). If the statutory recovery is vacated or reduced, the fee award cannot stand as entered.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the summary judgment on Smoot’s RLA conspiracy claim, vacate the damages and fee awards, and remand for trial.
