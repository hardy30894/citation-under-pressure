**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional principle: the government may not imprison an individual without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. The Fifth Circuit’s initial conclusion that indefinite detention under § 1231(a)(6) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) is lawful so long as the government makes “good faith efforts” to remove the alien is irreconcilable with the Constitution and this Court’s holding in *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S.Ct. 2491, 150 L.Ed.2d 653 (2001). That decision, which the Fifth Circuit now acknowledges is controlling, establishes that post-removal-period detention is not authorized beyond a “period reasonably necessary to secure the alien’s removal.” Id. at 2498. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates due process.

The Court in *Zadvydas* made clear that the statute does not permit indefinite confinement. It held that “the statute, read in light of the Constitution’s demands, limits an alien’s post-removal-period detention to a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien’s removal. It does not permit indefinite detention.” Id. at 2498. This is not a mere interpretive suggestion—it is a constitutional imperative. The Court emphasized that “we construe the statute to contain an implicit ‘reasonable time’ limitation, the application of which is subject to federal court review.” Id. at 2495. The Fifth Circuit’s prior standard—requiring an alien to prove that deportation is “impossible”—demands more than the Constitution requires and contradicts this Court’s directive. As the Supreme Court observed, “this standard would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable—which demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” Id. at 2505.

Here, the record demonstrates that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. Zadvydas has been in custody for over three years beyond the 90-day removal period. Germany and Lithuania have both refused to accept him. His reapplication for Lithuanian citizenship remains pending, but no country has committed to repatriate him. The INS has made no meaningful progress toward removal, and no country has expressed willingness to receive him. The Supreme Court recognized that “as the period of prior post-removal confinement grows, what counts as the ‘reasonably foreseeable future’ conversely would have to shrink.” Id. at 2505. After six months, the burden shifts to the government to demonstrate a significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. That burden has not been met here.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 73 S.Ct. 625, 97 L.Ed. 956 (1953), is misplaced. *Mezei* involved an alien denied entry at the border, whose detention was justified by national security concerns and the government’s authority to exclude. Id. at 212. The Court in *Zadvydas* explicitly rejected *Mezei* as controlling, noting that it “dealt with aliens who had been stopped at the border, and had not effected entry into the United States.” 121 S.Ct. at 2500. Zadvydas is not an excludable alien; he is a long-term resident who has been ordered removed. His detention is not for exclusionary purposes but for enforcement of a removal order. The constitutional concerns are far greater when the government detains a resident alien who has lived in the United States for decades, contributed to society, and poses no threat to public safety.

The government’s argument that Zadvydas remains a risk to the community is unavailing. The Supreme Court made clear that “the choice, however, is not between imprisonment and the alien ‘living at large’... It is between imprisonment and supervision under release conditions that may not be violated.” Id. at 2502. The district court’s conditions—regular reporting, employment, residence with family, bond posting, and health insurance—provide a robust framework for ensuring compliance. The INS has not challenged these conditions, nor has it shown that Zadvydas has violated them. The Court in *Zadvydas* affirmed that “the alien may no doubt be returned to custody upon a violation of those conditions.” Id. at 2504. Thus, the government retains the ability to enforce the removal order without indefinite incarceration.

Finally, the Court’s recognition of a “presumptively reasonable period of detention” of six months is not a rigid deadline but a benchmark for judicial inquiry. Id. at 2505. After that point, the government must justify continued detention with evidence of a realistic prospect of removal. Here, no such evidence exists. The government has not shown that any country will accept Zadvydas in the foreseeable future. The burden is on the government to demonstrate that removal is reasonably likely. It has not done so.

For these reasons, the Fifth Circuit’s prior opinion, which upheld indefinite detention based on a “good faith efforts” standard, is inconsistent with *Zadvydas*. The district court’s order releasing Zadvydas, conditioned on compliance with strict supervision, is not only lawful but constitutionally required. The judgment should be affirmed.
