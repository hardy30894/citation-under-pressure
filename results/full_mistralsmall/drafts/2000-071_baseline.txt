**ARGUMENT**

**I. MILFORD’S COMMUNITY USE POLICY CREATES A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM, AND ITS EXCLUSION OF THE GOOD NEWS CLUB WAS BASED ON THE NATURE OF THE SPEECH, NOT THE VIEWPOINT.**

The Court of Appeals correctly held that Milford’s Community Use Policy created a limited public forum. *See Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983) (a limited public forum is created when the government “opens a nonpublic forum for use by certain groups or for discussion of certain topics”). The policy expressly permitted “social, civic and recreational meetings” and other community uses, but excluded “religious purposes.” *See* 202 F.3d at 508. This distinction is constitutional because it is based on the *subject* of the speech—religious worship or instruction—not the *viewpoint* expressed. *See Rosenberger v. Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (“[T]he government must respect the lawful boundaries it has itself set.”).

The Club argues that its activities constitute “teaching of morals from a religious perspective,” but the record belies this claim. The Club’s meetings included prayer, scripture recitation, and proselytization—activities that are quintessentially religious worship, not secular moral instruction. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) (noting that “singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles” are forms of speech, but distinguishing them from worship). The Second Circuit properly concluded that the Club’s activities fell outside the scope of the policy because they were not “moral and character development” but rather religious instruction and worship. *See* 202 F.3d at 515.

**II. MILFORD’S EXCLUSION OF THE CLUB WAS JUSTIFIED BY THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE.**

Even if the exclusion were viewpoint-based, it would survive scrutiny under the Establishment Clause. The Court has long held that governments may not “endorse” religion or “give the impression that [they] appear to favor one religion over another.” *County of Allegheny v. ACLU*, 492 U.S. 573, 627 (1989) (Kennedy, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). Permitting the Club to use school facilities for worship and proselytization would convey a message of endorsement inconsistent with the Establishment Clause. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (noting that allowing religious groups to use school facilities for worship would risk violating the Establishment Clause).

The Club contends that its activities are merely “teaching morals from a religious perspective,” but this distinction is illusory. The Club’s meetings included explicit prayers, scripture memorization, and an invitation to accept Jesus Christ as Savior—activities that are inherently religious and not merely “moral instruction.” *See Good News/Good Sports Club v. School Dist. of Ladue*, 28 F.3d 1501, 1507 (8th Cir. 1994) (holding that a similar club’s activities were religious worship, not secular moral instruction). The Court should defer to Milford’s reasonable determination that permitting such activities would violate the Establishment Clause. *See Agostini v. Felton*, 521 U.S. 203, 237 (1997) (recognizing that governments may take steps to avoid Establishment Clause violations).

**III. THE CLUB’S FREE SPEECH CLAIM FAILS BECAUSE MILFORD DID NOT CREATE A GENERAL OPEN FORUM.**

The Club’s free speech claim also fails because Milford’s policy did not create a general open forum. The Court has held that when the government “does not intend to create a public forum,” it may impose reasonable, viewpoint-neutral restrictions. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46. Milford’s policy explicitly excluded religious worship, and the Club’s activities fell squarely within that exclusion. The Second Circuit correctly concluded that the exclusion was based on the *subject* of the speech, not the *viewpoint*, and thus did not violate the First Amendment. *See* 202 F.3d at 515.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club was constitutional. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
