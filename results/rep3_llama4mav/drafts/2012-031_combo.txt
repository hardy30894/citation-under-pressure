The court's decision to certify the class was based on a flawed analysis of the evidence presented, and it failed to rigorously analyze the requirements of Rule 23(b)(3) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure.

The requirement that damages be measurable on a class-wide basis is a critical component of the predominance inquiry under Rule 23(b)(3). As the Supreme Court held in Alabama v. United States, 304 F.2d 583, 586 (1962), aff'd per curiam, 371 U.S. 37 (1962), a class action is not maintainable where the damages suffered by individual class members are not susceptible to a common measure. Similarly, in Kainz v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc., 194 F.2d 737, 743 (7th Cir. 1952), the court held that a class action is not suitable where the damages claimed by individual class members require individualized proof.

In this case, the District Court certified the class based on the testimony of Plaintiffs' expert, Dr. James McClave, who proposed a methodology for calculating damages on a class-wide basis. However, the court failed to critically evaluate the reliability and admissibility of Dr. McClave's testimony, as required by the principles enunciated in United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921), and Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States, 221 U.S. 1, 60-61 (1911).

The District Court's failure to rigorously analyze the evidence presented is also evident in its treatment of the geographic market definition. The court accepted the Plaintiffs' definition of the Philadelphia DMA as the relevant geographic market without critically evaluating the evidence supporting this definition. As the Supreme Court held in United States v. Columbia Steel Co., 334 U.S. 495, 527 (1948), the definition of the relevant geographic market is a critical component of an antitrust claim, and it must be supported by sufficient evidence.

Furthermore, the District Court's decision to certify the class without adequate evidence of class-wide damages is inconsistent with the principles enunciated in Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32, 44-45 (1940), and Montgomery Ward & Co. v. Langer, 168 F.2d 182, 187 (8th Cir. 1948). These cases emphasize the importance of ensuring that the class is adequately represented and that the claims of individual class members are sufficiently similar to warrant class treatment.

In addition, the District Court's certification decision is at odds with the Supreme Court's holding in Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931), which emphasized the need for a plaintiff to prove damages with reasonable certainty in an antitrust case. The court's failure to ensure that the Plaintiffs had presented a reliable methodology for calculating damages on a class-wide basis is a clear error of law.

The District Court's decision is also inconsistent with the principles enunciated in Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946), which held that a plaintiff must prove damages with reasonable certainty, and that speculation and guesswork are not sufficient to establish damages. In this case, the court's certification decision was based on a speculative and unproven methodology for calculating damages, which is insufficient to meet the requirements of Rule 23(b)(3).

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the District Court erred in certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The court's decision should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.
