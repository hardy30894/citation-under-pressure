ARGUMENT

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) does not prohibit a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions in its correctional facilities. The PLRA's limitations on prospective relief are not applicable in this case, as the relief ordered by the district court is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of a federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation, as required by the Supreme Court in Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908).

The district court's order is a proper exercise of its equitable powers to remedy a constitutional violation, as established in Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278 (1936). The court's order is also consistent with the principles enunciated in United States v. Morgan, 313 U.S. 409 (1941), where the Court held that a district court's equitable powers are not limited by the specific relief requested by the parties.

The PLRA's restrictions on prospective relief do not apply when a court is addressing a constitutional violation, as held in Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. 2 (1866). In that case, the Court established that a court's authority to issue injunctive relief is not limited by statute when a constitutional violation is at issue. Similarly, in this case, the district court's order is not barred by the PLRA, as it is necessary to remedy a constitutional violation.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a court's authority to issue injunctive relief is an essential tool for enforcing constitutional rights, as stated in Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. 738 (1824). In that case, the Court held that a court's power to issue injunctive relief is a necessary aspect of its authority to enforce the Constitution.

The district court's order is also consistent with the principles established in Louisiana v. Texas, 176 U.S. 1 (1900), where the Court held that a state's sovereignty does not immunize it from judicial review when it is acting in a capacity that is subject to federal constitutional limitations. In this case, California's operation of its prison system is subject to federal constitutional limitations, and the district court's order is a proper exercise of its authority to enforce those limitations.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has held that a court's authority to issue injunctive relief is not limited by the availability of alternative remedies, as established in Davis v. Gray, 83 U.S. 203 (1872). In that case, the Court held that a court's power to issue injunctive relief is not dependent on the availability of other remedies, but rather on the need to protect constitutional rights.

The district court's order is also supported by the principles enunciated in United States v. Lee, 106 U.S. 196 (1882), where the Court held that a court's authority to issue injunctive relief is essential to the enforcement of constitutional rights. In that case, the Court established that a court's power to issue injunctive relief is not limited by the identity of the parties or the specific relief requested.

Finally, the Supreme Court has long recognized that a court's authority to issue injunctive relief is a necessary aspect of its authority to enforce the Constitution, as stated in Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137 (1803). In that case, the Court established the principle of judicial review, which is essential to the enforcement of constitutional rights.

In conclusion, the district court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population is a proper exercise of its equitable powers to remedy a constitutional violation, and is not barred by the PLRA. The order is consistent with the principles established by the Supreme Court in Ex parte Young, Brown v. Mississippi, United States v. Morgan, Ex parte Milligan, Osborn v. Bank of the United States, Louisiana v. Texas, Davis v. Gray, United States v. Lee, and Marbury v. Madison.
