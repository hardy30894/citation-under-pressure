**ARGUMENT**

**I. The State Court’s Rejection of the IAC Claim as Untimely Constitutes an Independent and Adequate State Ground, Barred Federal Review of the Sufficiency Claim.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in excusing the procedural default of Carpenter’s sufficiency claim based on his ineffective assistance of counsel (IAC) claim. The Ohio Supreme Court’s decision to affirm the dismissal of the IAC claim as untimely constitutes an independent and adequate state ground of law, which bars federal habeas review of the sufficiency claim. Under the doctrine established in *Barrett v. Whitley*, 501 U.S. 776 (1991), a state court’s decision resting on an independent and adequate state ground is conclusive in federal court, regardless of whether the federal court would have reached a different conclusion on the merits.

In *Barrett*, the Supreme Court held that a state court’s decision to dismiss a claim on an independent and adequate state law ground is not subject to federal habeas review, even if the federal court would have found the state court’s application of its procedural rules erroneous. 501 U.S. at 781. Here, the Ohio Supreme Court affirmed the Ohio Court of Appeals’ determination that Carpenter’s application to reopen his direct appeal was untimely. This decision was based on Ohio’s procedural rules, which are an independent and adequate state ground. Consequently, the federal courts lack jurisdiction to review the merits of the sufficiency claim, which was also dismissed as untimely. The Sixth Circuit’s attempt to circumvent this bar by excusing the default of the sufficiency claim with the IAC claim is foreclosed by *Barrett*.

**II. Even If Not an Independent Ground, the IAC Claim Cannot Serve as “Cause” to Excuse the Procedural Default of the Sufficiency Claim.**

Even assuming the state court’s decision was not an independent ground, the Sixth Circuit erred in holding that the IAC claim could serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of the sufficiency claim. Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(b)(1), a petitioner may excuse a procedural default by showing “cause” for the default and actual prejudice resulting from the alleged violation of federal law. The “cause” exception is limited to extraordinary circumstances that make it impracticable to raise the claim in state court. *Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes*, 504 U.S. 1, 9 (1992).

The state courts had a full and fair opportunity to consider the IAC claim. The Ohio Supreme Court rejected the claim on the merits, finding that Carpenter failed to demonstrate that his appellate counsel’s performance was deficient or that the deficiency prejudiced him. Because the IAC claim was adjudicated and rejected by the state courts, it cannot serve as the “cause” to excuse the default of the sufficiency claim. *See* *Keeney*, 504 U.S. at 9 (holding that the “cause” exception is limited to “extraordinary circumstances” that make it impracticable to raise the claim in state court). The mere fact that Carpenter was represented by new counsel on the state post-conviction petition does not constitute an extraordinary circumstance that would excuse the default of the sufficiency claim, which was also raised and rejected in state court.

**III. Carpenter Cannot Show Prejudice Regarding the Sufficiency Claim.**

Finally, even if the procedural default were excused, Carpenter cannot demonstrate the requisite prejudice to obtain habeas relief. Under *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), a petitioner must show that the alleged constitutional violation resulted in the denial of a fundamental right. Here, Carpenter pleaded guilty to aggravated murder and aggravated robbery. He admits the facts underlying the conviction. A claim of insufficient evidence is meritless when a defendant admits the facts of the crime. *See* *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218, 236 (1967). Because Carpenter admits the facts, he cannot show that the evidence was insufficient to support his conviction. Therefore, he cannot demonstrate the prejudice necessary to survive a procedural default analysis.
