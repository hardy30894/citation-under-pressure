I. The Constitutional Rule Invalidating Vague Criminal Provisions Applies Retroactively.

A statute that fails to give fair notice of the conduct it punishes is void from its inception. Norton v. Shelby County, 118 U.S. 425, 442 (1886) (“An unconstitutional act is not a law; it confers no rights; it imposes no duties; it affords no protection; it creates no office; it is, in legal contemplation, as inoperative as though it had never been passed.”). The same principle governs federal criminal statutes. Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371, 376 (1880) (“An unconstitutional law is void, and is as no law.”). When a court later declares such a provision invalid, the declaration operates retroactively. United States v. Schooner Peggy, 1 Cranch 103, 110 (1801) (“if subsequent to the judgment and before the decision of the appellate court, a law intervenes and positively changes the rule which governs, the law must be obeyed”).

This Court has consistently applied new constitutional rules to cases already final when the rule was announced. Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 20 (1956) (new rule concerning transcripts on appeal applied to prior convictions); Eskridge v. Washington State Bd. of Prison Terms & Paroles, 357 U.S. 214, 215 (1958) (same). Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618, 628 (1965), confirmed that retroactive effect turns on whether the new rule affects the integrity of the underlying conviction. The Court there held that the exclusionary rule announced in Mapp would not be applied retroactively on collateral review, but only after weighing the purpose of the rule, reliance interests, and the effect on the administration of justice. Id. at 636.

The same factors require retroactive application here. Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott, 382 U.S. 406, 416 (1966), explained that rules going to the fairness of the trial itself must be given full retroactive effect. Stovall v. Denno, 388 U.S. 293, 297 (1967), reiterated that a rule “affecting the integrity of the fact-finding process” is retroactive. A vagueness holding directly implicates that integrity: it means the statute supplied no ascertainable standard and therefore could not lawfully support a conviction or sentence. Desist v. United States, 394 U.S. 244, 249 (1969), summarized the inquiry as whether the new rule is a “clear break” with the past; when it is, retroactivity follows unless countervailing considerations of reliance and administration are overwhelming. Chicot County Drainage Dist. v. Baxter State Bank, 308 U.S. 371, 374 (1940), likewise recognized that a later declaration of invalidity restores the parties to the position they would have occupied had the void provision never existed.

Because the constitutional prohibition on vague criminal statutes is fundamental to due process and directly determines whether a conviction or enhanced sentence may stand, the rule must be applied to cases on collateral review.
