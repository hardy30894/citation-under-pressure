**ARGUMENT**

The text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only where a plaintiff demonstrates that the employer “engaged in a discriminatory practice . . . with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” Congress did not intend that simple proof of intentional discrimination—even knowing discrimination—automatically satisfies this standard. Instead, the statute demands a showing that the employer’s conduct was “egregious” or “outrageous” in the sense required at common law.

**I. The Plain Language of § 1981a Requires More Than Intentional Discrimination**

The terms “malice” and “reckless indifference” carry settled meanings under the common law of punitive damages. In *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 46 (1983), this Court explained that punitive damages in federal civil rights actions require “more than the commission of a tort” and demand a “reckless or callous disregard for the plaintiff’s rights.” The Court there contrasted simple negligence or even intentional misconduct with the “evil motive” or “recklessness” needed to justify punishment. Id. at 39-40.

Congress incorporated that common-law threshold into § 1981a. The House Report accompanying the Civil Rights Act of 1991 stated that punitive damages are available only where “the employer acted with ‘malice or reckless indifference’—an even higher standard than simple intentional discrimination.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(II), at 30 (1991), *reprinted in* 1991 U.S.C.C.A.N. 694, 724. If mere intentional discrimination were enough, the statutory phrase “malice or with reckless indifference” would be superfluous. See *Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Communities for a Great Oregon*, 515 U.S. 687, 698 (1995) (courts should give effect to every word of a statute). Congress thus meant to require conduct “in the nature of an outrage”—that is, conduct far exceeding the minimum needed to prove liability.

**II. Legislative History Confirms Congress’s Intent to Require Egregious Conduct**

Senator Dole, a key sponsor, explained that punitive damages under § 1981a are available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15,473 (daily ed. Oct. 30, 1991) (Interpretive Memorandum). Seven other senators joined that statement. Id. The Dole memorandum expressly rejected the idea that “any intentional discrimination” gives rise to punitive damages. Id.

The alternative view—that § 1981a punishes any “knowing” violation—was never enacted. The House bill that would have allowed punitive damages for “callous indifference” was replaced by the stricter “malice or reckless indifference” standard. See 137 Cong. Rec. H9527 (daily ed. Nov. 7, 1991) (Edwards memorandum). This history shows Congress deliberately chose a heightened standard akin to the common law’s requirement of “outrageous” conduct.

**III. The Circuits Requiring Egregious Conduct Correctly Interpret the Statute**

Every court of appeals that had addressed the issue before the D.C. Circuit in this case held that § 1981a demands egregious or outrageous conduct. See *Karcher v. Emerson Elec. Co.*, 94 F.3d 502, 508-09 (8th Cir. 1996); *Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co.*, 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996); *McKinnos v. Kwang Wah Restaurant*, 83 F.3d 498, 507-08 (D.C. Cir. 1996).. The Sixth Circuit in *Spuler v. Kroger Co.*, 120 F.3d 620, 627 (6th Cir. 1997) described the standard as “a high hurdle” requiring evidence of “egregious or reckless conduct.”

These decisions correctly recognize that the statute links punitive damages to the employer’s *state of mind*—not merely the fact of discrimination. The very concept of “reckless indifference” implies a conscious disregard of known rights, which necessarily involves a high degree of fault. *Farragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775, 789 (1998) (holding that employer liability for punitive damages requires “a deliberate indifference to [Title VII rights]”). That standard cannot be satisfied by proof that the emplooyer merely acted intentionally; it requires proof that the employer acted with a mental state of “culpable indiference” to the plaintiff’s rights—a standard that typically requires extreme conduct.

**IV. Applying the Correct Standard here**

The district court properly found insufficient evidence of egregious conduct. The record shows that ADA followed a considered process: its director consulted with the executive director, interviewed both candidates, and offered the promotion to Spangler based on his superior legislative experience. Even if ADA’s proffred reasons were pretextual, that alone does not show malice or regurging indifference. *Hartford Fire Ins. Co. v. Thessi Property & Casualty Co.*, 506 U.S. 511, 531 (1993) (discussing “recklessness” standard). Kolstad presented no evidence that ADA knew it was violating her rights or that it acted with the required mental state.

Because Congress intended § 1981a to punish only “extraordinarly egregious” discrimination, and because Kolstad’s evidence fell far short of that threshold, the judgment below should be reversed.
