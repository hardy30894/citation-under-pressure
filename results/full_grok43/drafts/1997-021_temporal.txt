The Board's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for employer polling is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act. An incumbent union enjoys only a rebuttable presumption of continued majority support. Brooks v. NLRB, 348 U.S. 96, 104 (1954). That presumption does not preclude an employer from testing employee sentiment when objective indications suggest loss of support. The Act protects the right of employees to choose or reject representation free from employer coercion, yet it also permits noncoercive inquiry. Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 797-798 (1945). Equating the evidentiary threshold for polling with that for withdrawal of recognition or an RM election renders polling a nullity, contrary to the statutory balance struck in NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575, 609 (1969), which recognized that employers may lawfully ascertain employee views through properly conducted polls.

The Board's insistence on the same quantum of proof for all three options cannot be reconciled with the distinct purposes and risks of each. Polling, when conducted under procedural safeguards, supplies precise information without the permanent consequences of withdrawal. Treating it as equivalent to withdrawal therefore imposes an unwarranted restriction unsupported by the Act's text or structure.

The Board's factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked objective indications of reasonable doubt is likewise unsupported by substantial evidence. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Multiple employees expressed dissatisfaction with the union's representation and indicated that a majority no longer desired continued representation. These statements, made before the poll was announced, supplied objective grounds for doubt. The Board erred in discounting them on grounds that the Act does not require. When the record as a whole demonstrates such indications, the employer satisfies its burden. Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 490.

The poll itself, supervised by a neutral third party and yielding a clear 19-13 result against the union, confirmed the doubt. The Board's refusal to credit this evidence and its order compelling continued recognition rest on an erroneous legal standard and lack substantial evidentiary support.
