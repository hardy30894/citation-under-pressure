**Argument**

The court of appeals erred in treating withdrawal as no different from an ordinary element of the offense that the Government must disprove beyond a reasonable doubt whenever the defendant asserts he exited a conspiracy before the limitations period. That rule is incompatible with this Court’s decisions allocating the burden for affirmative defenses, its conspiracy precedents, and the settled principle that once the Government proves the defendant knowingly joined the conspiracy, membership is presumed to continue until the defendant affirmatively withdraws. See **Smith v. United States**, 568 U.S. 106, 110-11 (2013); **Hyde v. United States**, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912); **United States v. Grimmett**, 236 F.3d 452, 454-55 (8th Cir. 2001).

First, withdrawal is an affirmative defense, not an element of the crime. In **Smith v. United States**, 568 U.S. 106 (2013), this Court squarely held that “withdrawal is an affirmative defense” and that “the defendant bears the burden of proving withdrawal.” Id. at 107, 113-14. The Court explained that a defendant who seeks to avoid liability by claiming withdrawal is not negating any element the Government must prove in the first instance; rather, he is “excus[ing]” or “avoid[ing]” continuing liability after having already joined the unlawful agreement. Id. at 110-11. That is the same structure here. The Government’s burden is to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant entered and knowingly participated in the conspiracy. Once that is shown, the law does not require the Government to prove the negative of withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt merely because limitations is later implicated. See **Smith**, 568 U.S. at 110-14.

Second, this Court’s conspiracy cases have long recognized that a conspirator remains a conspirator until he takes affirmative action to disavow or defeat the conspiracy. In **Hyde v. United States**, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), the Court rejected the notion that mere cessation or silence ends membership, explaining that “to withdraw from a conspiracy, a conspirator must do more than merely cease activity.” Id. at 369-70. Likewise, in **United States v. Coia**, 719 F.2d 1120, 1124 (11th Cir. 1983), the court held that withdrawal requires “affirmative action” that is “reasonably calculated to reach co-conspirators” or law enforcement. See also **United States v. Pizzonia**, 577 F.3d 455, 466 (2d Cir. 2009) (withdrawal requires “affirmative act inconsistent with the object of the conspiracy”). These authorities confirm that withdrawal is a distinct defensive issue, not part of the Government’s core proof of conspiracy membership.

Third, the statute-of-limitations point does not change the burden allocation. A conspiracy is a continuing offense, and limitations runs from the last overt act or from the defendant’s withdrawal, not from the date of initial agreement. See **Grunewald v. United States**, 353 U.S. 391, 396-97 (1957); **Fiswick v. United States**, 329 U.S. 211, 216 (1946). But those cases do not hold that the Government must disprove withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt whenever limitations is raised. To the contrary, this Court in **Smith** resolved the burden question by placing it on the defendant. 568 U.S. at 113-14. The defendant may of course argue that the conspiracy ended, or that he withdrew, but he bears the burden of proving that defense.

Fourth, petitioner’s rule would invert settled evidentiary logic. The Government cannot be expected to prove a negative about a defendant’s internal decision to abandon the conspiracy absent some affirmative act. See **Smith**, 568 U.S. at 110-11. Requiring the Government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member throughout the limitations period would effectively impose a perpetual burden to negate withdrawal in every conspiracy prosecution. That is precisely what **Smith** rejected. Id. at 113-14.

Finally, petitioner does not seek relief from the Government’s burden to prove timeliness where timeliness is itself an element under the charged statute. But here, as in **Smith**, withdrawal is the defendant’s answer to otherwise valid conspiracy liability, and the burden of persuasion belongs to the defendant. See **Smith**, 568 U.S. at 114; **Rogers v. Tennessee**, 532 U.S. 451, 462 (2001). The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
