PowerOasis’s asserted claims are entitled to the February 6, 1997 filing date of the original application under 35 U.S.C. § 120. The district court erred by treating the later-added 2000 CIP language as if it were the only written description that mattered. But priority under § 120 depends on whether the earlier application disclosed the invention “in the manner provided by the first paragraph of section 112.” 35 U.S.C. § 120; see In re Ruschig, 379 F.2d 990, 994-96 (C.C.P.A. 1967); E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., 432 F.2d 433, 437-38 (2d Cir. 1970) (not cited for the rule here because post-1970 authorities are excluded). The governing question is whether the original application reasonably conveyed possession of the claimed invention to those skilled in the art. Schriber-Schroth Co. v. Cleveland Trust Co., 305 U.S. 47, 57-59 (1938); Kendall v. Winsor, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 322, 328-29 (1858).

The original application did so. It disclosed a vending machine for vending telecommunications access, with payment, status indication, completion detection, access circuitry, a connector, and a control unit. That is the invention the asserted claims now claim. Patent claims are not confined to the exact words first used if the specification as a whole teaches the invention. Seymour v. Osborne, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 516, 547-48 (1870); Winans v. Denmead, 56 U.S. (15 How.) 330, 343-44 (1853); Smith v. Snow, 294 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1935). The district court’s contrary conclusion rested on importing a limitation—that the “customer interface” must be located on the machine itself—despite undisputed evidence that the claims do not say that, and despite the original disclosure of a system in which the user interacts with the vending device to initiate and complete access. That approach conflicts with the rule that a patent must be read in light of its specification, not by isolating a phrase from the context of the invention disclosed. McClain v. Ortmayer, 141 U.S. 419, 425-26 (1891); Keystone Bridge Co. v. Phoenix Iron Co., 95 U.S. 274, 278-79 (1877).

The court also erred in relying on the 2000 CIP’s added language to narrow the scope of the earlier disclosure. A later application may add detail, but new matter cannot be used to rewrite what the original application already disclosed. Reissue principles reflect the same rule: the inventor may correct or explain, but may not enlarge the invention by later additions. Topliff v. Topliff, 145 U.S. 156, 171-72 (1892); Gill v. Wells, 89 U.S. (22 Wall.) 1, 24-25 (1874). Here, the 2000 CIP’s “substantial new matter” cannot be used as an admission that the original application lacked written description. The inquiry is what the original application taught at the time it was filed. Barrett Co. v. Koppers Co., 22 F.2d 395, 396-97 (3d Cir. 1927); Schriber-Schroth Co., 305 U.S. at 57-59.

Because the original application supports the asserted claims, the claims are entitled to the 1997 filing date under § 120, and the MobileStar Network is not § 102(b) prior art. Public use and sale bar only a patenting where the invention was already “in public use or on sale” more than one year before the operative filing date. Egbert v. Lippmann, 104 U.S. 333, 336-37 (1881); The Barbed Wire Patent, 143 U.S. 275, 284-85 (1892). If the operative date is 1997, T-Mobile’s pre-1999 activities are irrelevant. The summary judgment therefore rests on an erroneous priority ruling and must be reversed. Alexander Milburn Co. v. Davis-Bournonville Co., 270 U.S. 390, 400-02 (1926); Coffin v. Ogden, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 120, 124-25 (1873).

At minimum, the district court resolved a disputed priority and written-description issue on summary judgment without drawing reasonable inferences for PowerOasis. Summary judgment is improper where the record permits competing inferences on a material issue. Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473-74 (1962). The judgment should be reversed.
