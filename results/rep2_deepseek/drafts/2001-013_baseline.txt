**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE COURT OF APPEALS IMPROPERLY EXTENDED THE CLARY AND DONOVAN “SEAMEN” EXEMPTION TO UNINSPECTED VESSELS SUBJECT TO MINIMAL COAST GUARD REGULATION.**

Under the plain text of § 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act, OSHA jurisdiction is displaced only where another federal agency exercises statutory authority “to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The burden rests on the party invoking the exemption to demonstrate that the other agency in fact exercises such authority over the specific working conditions at issue. *See Southern Pacific Transp. Co. v. Usery*, 539 F.2d 386, 390 (5th Cir. 1976). Because the Coast Guard does not exercise significant regulatory authority over occupational safety and health on uninspected vessels like Rig 52, § 4(b)(1) does not preclude OSHA’s jurisdiction.

**A. The Coast Guard’s Authority Over Uninspected Vessels Is Narrow and Does Not Extend to Occupational Safety or Health.**

The Coast Guard’s authority to regulate uninspected vessels is strictly limited by statute. Under 46 U.S.C. § 4102, the Coast Guard may prescribe regulations only for: (1) fire extinguishers; (2) life preservers; (3) flame arrestors; (4) ventilation of engine compartments; and (5) emergency locating equipment. Critically, Congress did not authorize the Coast Guard to regulate general occupational safety and health matters such as the well-drilling operations, electrical hazards, or industrial conditions that caused the explosion on Rig 52. This limited delegation is categorically different from the comprehensive authority the Coast Guard exercises over inspected vessels. *Cf. Kelley v. Southern Pac. Co.*, 419 U.S. 318, 326 (1974) (statute must demonstrate “clearly” that an agency has authority over a given condition to trigger preemption under § 4(b)(1)).

The Coast Guard itself has recognized the limits of its jurisdiction. In its investigation of the Rig 52 explosion, the Coast Guard’s report expressly noted that Rig 52 was an uninspected vessel and took no enforcement action against Mallard. A federal agency that disclaims regulatory authority over a workplace condition cannot be said to “exercise statutory authority” sufficient to oust OSHA jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1). *See United States v. Figueroa*, 165 F.3d 111, 117 (2d Cir. 1998) (interpreting “exercise of authority” under analogous statutory scheme).

**B. Clary and Donovan Do Not Control This Case Because They Addressed Inspected Vessels and Established a “Seamen” Exception That Does Not Apply to Uninspected Drilling Barges.**

In *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120, 1122 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825, 826 (5th Cir. 1983), the Fifth Circuit held that OSHA regulations do not apply to “seamen on vessels in navigation.” Those decisions, however, were premised on the Coast Guard’s “comprehensive regulation and supervision of seamen’s working conditions.” *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826. Yet neither *Clary* nor *Donovan* involved an uninspected vessel, and neither decision considered whether the same rule applies where Coast Guard regulation is minimal and incomplete.

Indeed, the *Donovan* court itself recognized that “anomalies” arise when vessels pass into and out of OSHA coverage. 720 F.2d at 829. That concern is magnified here. Rig 52 is a stationary drilling barge that works entirely in state territorial waters. It has no crew quarters, is not self-propelled, and its workers are not “seamen” in the traditional sense. *See Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis*, 515 U.S. 347, 368 (1995) (defining “seaman” as a worker with a substantial connection to a vessel in navigation). Applying the *Clary* exception to every uninspected barge that happens to float on navigable waters would create the very “confusion, governmental proliferation, and stultification of enterprise” that § 4(b)(1) was designed to avoid. *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 827.

**C. The Text and Purpose of the OSH Act Require OSHA Jurisdiction Over Uninspected Vessels.**

Congress enacted the OSH Act to “assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions.” 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). That remedial purpose is frustrated if an uninspected vessel, subject to only five narrow Coast Guard safety regulations, escapes OSHA coverage entirely. Rig 52’s employees performed industrial work in a floating workplace indistinguishable from a land-based drilling rig. The OSH Act defines “workplace” broadly to include “any place where employment is performed.” 29 U.S.C. § 652(8). There is no statutory exception for uninspected vessels. *See Secretary of Labor v. Triton Indus., Inc.*, 14 O.S.H. Cas. (BNA) 1809, 1811 (O.S.H.R.C. 1990) (uninspected vessel is a “workplace” under § 4(a)).

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
