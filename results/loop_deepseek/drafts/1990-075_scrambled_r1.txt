**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision is a lawful delegation because Congress supplied an intelligible principle. This Court has long held that Congress may obtain assistance from coordinate branches when it lays “down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The temporary scheduling provision satisfies that test.

**I. The Statute Channels the Attorney General’s Discretion**

The provision permits temporary scheduling only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” and only after consideration of defined factors: the drug’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4)-(6). This is a far more precise standard than delegations this Court has sustained. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 420 (1944), this Court upheld a delegation to the Price Administrator to fix prices, and in *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), it upheld broad authority to preserve “orderly and efficient” operation of financial institutions. In *Bowles v. Willingham*, 321 U.S. 503, 512 (1944), this Court upheld an Administrator’s power to set maximum rents under a general standard of fairness. The temporary scheduling power, requiring a finding of imminent public danger and weighing specified criteria, is more confined, not less.

Even broader delegations have been approved. In *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692-93 (1892), this Court upheld the President’s authority to suspend tariff provisions upon finding that other nations had imposed unequal duties. In *Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), it upheld the Secretary of the Treasury’s authority to decide what tea was of “inferior quality.” And in *In re Kollock*, 165 U.S. 526, 533 (1897), it upheld a delegation to the Secretary of the Treasury to define the classification of oleomargarine. If those open-ended delegations passed constitutional muster, the temporary scheduling provision’s tight standards necessarily do as well. See also *Cargo of the Brig Aurora*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 382 (1813) (recognizing Congress may condition a law on executive findings).

The Act further restricts the Attorney General by limiting temporary scheduling to one year, with a possible extension of no more than six months. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). This temporal limit stands in stark contrast to the unconditional grants invalidated in the 1930s, where the President received authority with virtually no guiding criteria. Here, Congress supplied both a substantive standard and a sunset.

**II. The Criminal Penalty Does Not Change the Analysis**

The Toubys assert that a stricter nondelegation test applies because temporary scheduling defines criminal conduct. But this Court has never so held. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911), this Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to issue regulations for the use of national forests, even though violating those regulations was a crime, because Congress had sufficiently indicated the policy and the standards to guide the Secretary. The temporary scheduling provision gives the Attorney General far more specific instructions than did the statute in *Grimaud*. The Attorney General cannot create a crime out of whole cloth; he may act only upon finding an imminent hazard and considering three enumerated factors.

Nor does the solicitude for liberty underlying the nondelegation doctrine require more. The provision still places the ultimate policy choice in Congress: Congress decided that drugs posing an imminent threat to public safety should be temporarily controlled. The Attorney General merely applies that legislative policy to particular substances. That is the sort of interstitial rulemaking the Constitution permits.

**III. The Attorney General Properly Subdelegated to the DEA**

The Toubys also argue that the Attorney General could not assign temporary scheduling authority to the Drug Enforcement Administration. But Congress vested the power in the “Attorney General” generally, not in a particular subordinate. This Court has long recognized that an officer charged with executing statutory duties may delegate them to subordinates unless Congress forbids it. *Runkle v. United States*, 122 U.S. 543, 557 (1887). Nothing in the Controlled Substances Act forbids subdelegation; to the contrary, 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) authorizes the Attorney General to issue rules and regulations necessary to carry out the Act. The regulation at issue, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), is a routine administrative assignment of functions within the Department of Justice.

Requiring the Attorney General personally to exercise every discretionary power would paralyze enforcement of federal law and finds no support in precedent. The Toubys’ contrary position would invalidate countless reasonable subdelegations throughout the executive branch. Nothing in the Constitution demands such rigidity.

**Conclusion**

The temporary scheduling provision supplies an intelligible principle: an imminent hazard to the public safety, evaluated under specified factors and subject to a strict time limit. That standard comfortably satisfies the nondelegation doctrine as applied in *J.W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409; *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 420; *Fahey*, 332 U.S. at 250; *Bowles*, 321 U.S. at 512; *Field*, 143 U.S. at 692-93; *Buttfield*, 192 U.S. at 496; *Kollock*, 165 U.S. at 533; and *Grimaud*, 220 U.S. at 517. The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA was authorized under *Runkle*, 122 U.S. at 557. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
