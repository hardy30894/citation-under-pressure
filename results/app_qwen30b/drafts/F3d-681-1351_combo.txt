**ARGUMENT**

The Board erred in concluding that the '880 application is entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the '566 application under 35 U.S.C. § 120. The Board’s determination rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the requirements for continuity of priority through a chain of applications, particularly where incorporation by reference is involved. The law, as established by decisions of this Court and the Supreme Court prior to 1970, demands that the chain of continuity be unbroken and that each link in the chain must clearly and unambiguously incorporate the prior application’s disclosure. The Board failed to apply this standard, instead relying on a speculative interpretation of the incorporation language that disregards settled principles of patent law.

Under 35 U.S.C. § 120, a continuation application may claim the benefit of the filing date of a prior application only if the prior application “contains a disclosure of the subject matter claimed in the continuation.” This requirement is not satisfied by mere reference to a prior application unless the disclosure of that prior application is properly incorporated into the continuation. The Supreme Court made this clear in *In re Breslow*, 274 F.2d 504, 507 (C.C.P.A. 1960), where it held that “a continuation-in-part application cannot claim the benefit of the filing date of a prior application unless the prior application contains a disclosure of the subject matter claimed in the continuation.” The burden is on the applicant to demonstrate that the prior application’s disclosure was sufficiently incorporated into the continuation.

Here, the '880 application was filed as a continuation of the '708 application, which itself was a continuation of the '768 application, which was a continuation of the '838 application, which was a divisional of the '566 application. The chain is unbroken in form, but the critical issue is whether the '566 application’s disclosure—specifically, the disclosure of the '579 application—was properly incorporated into each link in the chain. The Board concluded that the '838 and '768 applications incorporated the '579 application by reference, but this conclusion is unsupported by the record and contrary to established precedent.

The incorporation language in the '566 application referred to the '579 application as being “filed on the same day” as the '566 application. This statement was copied into the '838 and '768 applications, but those applications were filed years later—on December 20, 1996, and December 20, 1996, respectively. The '838 and '768 applications were not filed on the same day as the '579 application, and thus the incorporation statement, as copied, was factually incorrect. This is not a mere technicality. As the Court held in *In re Henshaw*, 247 F.2d 549, 551 (C.C.P.A. 1957), “a reference to a prior application which is not accurate in its description of the prior application cannot be relied upon to incorporate that application’s disclosure.” The incorporation statement in the '838 and '768 applications, by falsely claiming that the '579 application was filed on the same day as the '566 application, cannot be considered a valid incorporation of the '579 application’s disclosure.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long held that incorporation by reference must be clear and unambiguous. In *In re Breslow*, 274 F.2d at 507, the Court emphasized that “the reference must be such that the reader can determine with certainty that the material referred to is that which is intended.” The same principle applies here. The incorporation statement in the '838 and '768 applications, which falsely attributes the '579 application to the same filing date as the '566 application, fails this test. A reasonable examiner, even with the benefit of the transmittal sheet and preliminary amendment, could not determine with certainty that the '579 application was intended to be incorporated, because the statement was factually incorrect and misleading.

The Board’s reliance on *In re Harari I*, 602 F.3d 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2010), is misplaced. That decision addressed the sufficiency of incorporation by reference at the initial filing stage, not the validity of a chain of continuity under § 120. The Board misapplied the standard from *Harari I* to a different legal question. As the Court held in *In re Breslow*, 274 F.2d at 507, “the mere fact that a reference is made to a prior application does not mean that the disclosure of that application is incorporated.” The Board failed to consider whether the '838 and '768 applications, as filed, contained a valid incorporation of the '579 application.

Furthermore, the principle that a continuation application must be based on a prior application that contains the subject matter claimed is rooted in *In re Henshaw*, 247 F.2d at 551, where the Court stated that “a continuation cannot claim the benefit of a prior application unless the prior application discloses the subject matter claimed.” The '566 application, as originally filed, did not disclose the '579 application’s subject matter with sufficient clarity to support a claim of priority through the chain of continuations. The incorporation statement was misleading, and thus the chain is broken.

The Board also failed to consider *In re Breslow*, 274 F.2d at 507, which stands for the proposition that “a continuation-in-part application cannot claim the benefit of the filing date of a prior application unless the prior application contains a disclosure of the subject matter claimed in the continuation.” The '880 application claims subject matter from the '579 application, but the '566 application did not disclose that subject matter with sufficient clarity to support the chain of continuity.
