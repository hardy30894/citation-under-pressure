**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling employees is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll only when it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status is contrary to the Act’s text and purpose. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 42 (1937) (the Act’s objective is “the effectuation of the policies of the Act”); *NLRB v. Pennsylvania Greyhound Lines*, 303 U.S. 261, 267 (1938) (the Board’s authority is “not to be enlarged by implication”). The Board’s standard frustrates the Act’s goal of enabling employers and unions to determine employee preferences through reliable means. *NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 309 U.S. 206, 226 (1940) (the Act contemplates “the right of self-organization and collective bargaining”).

The Board’s insistence on the same standard for polling as for withdrawal of recognition is arbitrary. *NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Tel. Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 346 (1938) (the Board may not “ignore the policy of the Act”). Polling is a less intrusive and more precise tool than withdrawal of recognition. *NLRB v. Bradford Dyeing Ass’n*, 310 U.S. 318, 339 (1940) (the Board must consider “the practicability of the Board’s suggested alternatives”). The Board’s rule renders polling virtually useless, as employers can already withdraw recognition if they have sufficient doubt. *NLRB v. Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. 469, 478 (1941) (the Board’s authority is “not absolute”).

II. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence.

The Board erred in discounting employee statements that evidenced a loss of union support. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) (the Board’s findings must be supported by “substantial evidence”). Seven employees expressed opposition to the union, including statements that the union was not worth the dues and that employees did not need representation. *NLRB v. Link-Belt Co.*, 311 U.S. 584, 597 (1941) (the Board may not “disregard uncontradicted testimony”). The Board’s refusal to credit these statements was arbitrary.

The Board also erred in excluding employees hired or terminated after January 25, 1991. *NLRB v. Hearst Publications*, 322 U.S. 111, 131 (1944) (the Board must consider “the realities of the working environment”). The Board’s rigid cutoff date ignored the fluid nature of the workforce. *NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 309 U.S. at 226 (the Board may not “ignore the facts of industrial life”).

The Board’s reliance on the presumption of continuing majority support was misplaced. *NLRB v. Nevada Consolidated Copper Corp.*, 316 U.S. 105, 110 (1942) (the presumption is rebuttable). The bargaining unit at Allentown Mack was a subset of the prior unit, and the presumption was weak. *NLRB v. Streamway Div. of Scott & Fetzer Co.*, 309 F.2d 105, 110 (6th Cir. 1962) (the presumption is “not conclusive”). The Board’s refusal to consider the poll results as evidence of the union’s loss of support was irrational. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. at 300.

III. The Board’s standard and its application in this case violate the Act.

The Board’s standard chills employers from seeking reliable information about employee preferences. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. at 42 (the Act protects “the right of self-organization”). The Board’s factual determination is unsupported by substantial evidence. *NLRB v. Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. at 478. The Court should reject the Board’s standard and enforce the poll results.
