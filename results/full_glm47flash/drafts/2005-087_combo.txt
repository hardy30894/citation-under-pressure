**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Trial Court Properly Excluded Irrelevant Evidence**

The State respectfully submits that the trial court’s ruling was a valid application of state law and did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment. The defendant’s proposed expert testimony was irrelevant to the specific intent required for the crime of murder and thus properly excluded.

First, the State has broad authority to regulate the admission of evidence. *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43 (1906). The exclusion of evidence is an error only if it violates a fundamental right. *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605 (1967). However, the right to present evidence is not absolute. *Id.* The right to be heard is a fundamental principle, but it does not extend to presenting evidence that is irrelevant to the elements of the crime. *Hale*, 201 U.S. at 43.

Second, the evidence was irrelevant because the crime of murder requires specific intent. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895). The law presumes that every man intends the ordinary consequences of his voluntary acts. *Id.* The defense of insanity is not a defense to a charge of a crime which does not require a specific intent. *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479 (1951). *Maddox v. United States*, 365 U.S. 310 (1961) reiterates that the defense of insanity is not a defense to a crime which does not require a specific intent. Since the crime of murder generally requires specific intent, the defendant's mental state regarding his delusions is irrelevant to the *mens rea* element.

**II. The Defendant’s Due Process Claim Fails**

The defendant argues a Fourteenth Amendment right to present evidence to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963). However, the right to present evidence is a fundamental element of due process only when the evidence is relevant to the elements of the offense. *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605 (1967). The Supreme Court has held that the defense of insanity is not a defense to a crime which does not require a specific intent. *Dixon v. United States*, 308 U.S. 164 (1939). Therefore, the trial court did not violate due process by excluding the evidence.

**III. The Insanity Defense is Separate from the Intent Defense**

The defendant conflates the insanity defense with the lack of intent. *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843). The M'Naghten rule defines the insanity defense. It does not negate the specific intent required for a general intent crime. *Maddox v. United States*, 365 U.S. 310 (1961). Because the defendant failed to prove he was legally insane, the trial court correctly ruled that his expert testimony was inadmissible to negate the specific intent required for the crime.
