**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE DOES NOT APPLY TO ALIENS WHO RE-ENTERED BEFORE ITS EFFECTIVE DATE**

The plain text of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) (the “reinstatement statute”) authorizes the Attorney General to “reinstate” a prior removal order only where the alien “has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed.” The statute does not specify whether it applies to re-entries occurring before its 1997 effective date. However, this Court has repeatedly held that “[s]tatutes are not to be applied retroactively absent clear congressional intent.” *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 260 (1994). Here, Congress expressed no such intent. The statute’s silence on retroactive application, coupled with its focus on future conduct (re-entry *after* removal), compels the conclusion that it does not apply to pre-enactment re-entries.

This Court has consistently refused to apply immigration statutes retroactively where Congress has not clearly indicated such an intent. *See INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001) (holding that the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act’s elimination of discretionary relief was not retroactive because Congress did not clearly state so); *Vartelas v. Holder*, 566 U.S. 257, 266 (2012) (applying the same principle to the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act). The reinstatement statute, like the provisions at issue in *St. Cyr* and *Vartelas*, contains no express retroactivity provision. Thus, it cannot be applied to Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 re-entry.

The government’s contrary interpretation would render the statute impermissibly retroactive. As this Court has explained, “[a] statute is retroactive if it ‘takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability in respect to transactions or considerations already past.’” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269 (quoting *Sonzinsky v. United States*, 300 U.S. 506, 513 (1937)). Here, Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 re-entry occurred under a legal regime that did not subject him to automatic removal without a hearing. Applying the reinstatement statute to that re-entry would impose a new disability—deportation without relief—based on past conduct, in violation of due process. *See INS v. Lopez-Mendoza*, 468 U.S. 1032, 1040 (1984) (recognizing that due process protections apply in deportation proceedings).

**II. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, AS APPLIED TO PRE-ENFORCEMENT RE-ENTRIES, IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE**

Even if the reinstatement statute could be construed to apply to pre-enactment re-entries, such an application would violate the Constitution’s prohibition on retroactive legislation. The Due Process Clause forbids statutes that “attach[] legal significance to an event occurring before [the statute’s] enactment.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. Here, the reinstatement statute imposes consequences—deportation without a hearing—based on Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 re-entry, an event that occurred 15 years before the statute’s enactment.

This Court has invalidated retroactive immigration laws where they impaired settled expectations. In *St. Cyr*, the Court held that the elimination of discretionary relief for pre-enactment conduct violated due process because aliens had relied on the possibility of such relief when entering guilty pleas. 533 U.S. at 322-23. Similarly, Fernandez-Vargas reasonably expected that his 1982 re-entry would not subject him to automatic removal without a hearing. The reinstatement statute upsets that expectation by attaching new legal consequences to his past conduct.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Security Industrial Bank*, 459 U.S. 70 (1982), is misplaced. In *Security Industrial Bank*, the Court upheld a retroactive application of a bankruptcy statute because it did not impair vested rights. Id. at 79-80. Here, by contrast, Fernandez-Vargas had a vested right to a hearing before deportation, which the reinstatement statute eliminates. *See Reno v. Flores*, 507 U.S. 292, 306 (1993) (recognizing that due process requires a hearing before deportation).

**III. THE TENTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION CONTRADICTS THIS COURT’S RETROACTIVITY PRECEDENTS**

The Tenth Circuit erred by holding that Fernandez-Vargas had no “protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status.” *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 428 F.3d 1198, 1205 (10th Cir. 2005). This reasoning conflates the question of retroactivity with the merits of Fernandez-Vargas’s adjustment application. The retroactivity inquiry focuses on whether the statute impairs settled expectations regarding past conduct, not future applications for relief. *See Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. The Tenth Circuit’s approach would eviscerate retroactivity protections in immigration cases, as every alien subject to removal could be deemed to have no protectable expectation of relief.

This Court has rejected similar reasoning in *St. Cyr*, where it held that aliens’ reliance on discretionary relief when entering guilty pleas created a protectable expectation. 533 U.S. at 322-23. Fernandez-Vargas’s reliance on settled expectations—his belief that his 1982 re-entry would not subject him to automatic removal without a hearing—is no less deserving of protection.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Tenth Circuit’s decision and hold that the reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date. Even if the statute could be so construed, its retroactive application would violate due process. The judgment below should be reversed.
