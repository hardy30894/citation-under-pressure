The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The non-delegation doctrine, as reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), permits Congress to delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform." Id. at 372 (quoting J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The CSA provides sufficient guidance to the Attorney General, listing specific factors to consider when scheduling substances, including the substance's potential for abuse, its pharmacological effect, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). The Attorney General must also obtain a scientific and medical evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services before scheduling a substance. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b). These statutory constraints ensure that the Attorney General's authority is not unfettered.

The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), is similarly constitutional. Although it grants the Attorney General the power to temporarily schedule substances without following the usual notice and hearing procedures, it requires the Attorney General to consider specific factors, including the history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). The provision also limits the duration of temporary scheduling to one year, with a possible six-month extension. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). These limitations and guidelines provide an intelligible principle to guide the Attorney General's exercise of authority.

The Attorney General's delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA) is also authorized. The CSA explicitly permits the Attorney General to delegate his functions to other officials. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General has done so through a regulation, which assigns to the DEA Administrator the functions vested in the Attorney General by the CSA. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). This sub-delegation is consistent with the statutory text and is a reasonable exercise of the Attorney General's authority.

The Third Circuit's decision in this case is consistent with the Supreme Court's jurisprudence on the non-delegation doctrine. The court correctly applied the intelligible principle test and found that the CSA's delegation to the Attorney General was constitutional. Other courts have reached similar conclusions when considering the constitutionality of the CSA's delegation provisions. See, e.g., United States v. Emerson, 846 F.2d 541 (9th Cir. 1988); United States v. Spain, 825 F.2d 1426 (10th Cir. 1987). The Toubys' challenge to the CSA's delegation provisions should be rejected, and their convictions should be affirmed.
