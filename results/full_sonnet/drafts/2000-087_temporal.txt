# ARGUMENT

## I. The Post-Removal-Period Statute Must Be Construed to Avoid the Serious Constitutional Question That Indefinite Detention Would Present.

It is settled that "the cardinal principle of statutory construction" requires courts, when a statute is "susceptible of two constructions[,] by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise[,] ... to adopt that [construction] which will avoid" the constitutional difficulty. *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). This canon has particular force in the immigration context, where the Court has repeatedly refused to read deportation and detention statutes to reach further than their text plainly requires when a broader reading would work severe consequences on individual liberty. *See Delgadillo v. Carmichael*, 332 U.S. 388, 391 (1947) (declining to extend a deportation statute "beyond its evident scope" because "the consequences of the construction ... are so drastic").

This Court applied precisely that canon to the predecessor of the very detention scheme at issue here. In *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194 (1957), the Court confronted a statute authorizing the Attorney General to demand information from aliens held under supervision after a final order of deportation could not be executed. Rather than read the statute to give the Attorney General unbounded authority over such aliens, the Court limited it to inquiries "reasonably calculated to keep the Attorney General advised regarding the [alien's] whereabouts and conduct," *id.* at 202, precisely because a broader reading would raise "the spectre of variant and wide-ranging application" that Congress could not have intended, *id.* at 199. The Court there refused to assume that Congress meant to subject aliens whose deportation had stalled to open-ended government control. The same interpretive restraint compels a limiting construction of the current post-removal-period detention provision.

## II. Detention Without a Foreseeable End Point Is the Kind of Deprivation of Liberty from Which Resident Aliens Are Constitutionally Protected.

This Court has long recognized that once an alien has been lawfully admitted to residence in the United States, he is a "person" entitled to the protections of the Due Process Clause, not merely an applicant for admission subject to the plenary discretion of the political branches. In *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), the Court held that aliens present in the United States "are entitled to the protection guaranteed by [the] amendments" of the Constitution, and that Congress may not impose punishment — including confinement — on an alien "without a judicial trial." *Id.* at 237. Detention that has no discernible relationship to an achievable removal, and that persists indefinitely, is functionally indistinguishable from the punitive confinement condemned in *Wong Wing*.

That principle applies with special force to resident aliens like petitioner. In *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276 (1922), the Court held that persons residing in the United States under color of law could not be deprived of their liberty through summary administrative process, explaining that such a deprivation "might result in an unwarranted deprivation of liberty," *id.* at 284, and requiring that any deportation-related confinement of a resident be tested against due process norms, *id.* at 284-85. Likewise, in *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590 (1953), the Court treated a lawfully admitted resident alien as entitled to the same due process protection against arbitrary deprivation of liberty enjoyed by citizens, emphasizing that such an alien's continuing presence in the country materially changes the constitutional calculus. *Id.* at 596 n.5.

The government's reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. *Mezei* upheld detention of an alien who had never effected entry into the United States and who, under the "entry fiction," was treated "as if stopped at the border." *Id.* at 215. The Court's toleration of prolonged confinement in that case rested squarely on the alien's status as an excludable entrant seeking admission — not as someone, like petitioner, who was lawfully admitted to permanent residence years earlier. *Mezei* does not extend to, much less control, the detention of a resident alien whose removal has become impossible to accomplish within any foreseeable period.

## III. Because Removal Was Not Reasonably Foreseeable, Continued Detention Exceeded Any Authority the Statute Could Be Read to Confer.

Read against this background, the statute cannot be construed to authorize detention untethered to any prospect of removal. Detention serves the statutory purpose of "assuring the alien's presence" for removal; once removal is not reasonably foreseeable, further confinement no longer serves that purpose and instead becomes the kind of open-ended civil detention this Court's due process precedents forbid. *See Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 237; *Ng Fung Ho*, 259 U.S. at 284-85. As in *Witkovich*, where the Court refused to assume that Congress authorized indefinite governmental control over aliens awaiting deportation, 353 U.S. at 202, this Court should hold that the post-removal-period statute contains an implicit temporal limitation and does not authorize the prolonged, indefinite detention petitioner has endured.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
