The Sixth Amendment does not bar the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply to tribal court proceedings because Indian tribes are not subject to the constraints of the U.S. Constitution. See Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). The Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) governs tribal court proceedings, and it does not provide a right to counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment. 

The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Bryant, 769 F.3d 671 (9th Cir. 2014), which held that an uncounseled tribal court conviction cannot be used as a predicate offense under § 117(a), is contrary to Supreme Court precedent. In Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), the Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction can be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, as long as the conviction did not violate the Sixth Amendment. The Court's reasoning in Nichols applies with equal force to the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a).

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is misplaced. Ant predates Nichols and is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's holding that an uncounseled conviction is valid if it does not result in a sentence of imprisonment. See Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367 (1979). Moreover, the use of prior convictions under § 117(a) is a matter of congressional prerogative, and Congress has chosen to permit the use of tribal court convictions as predicate offenses. See United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978) (recognizing tribal sovereignty and Congress's authority to legislate with respect to Indian tribes).

The Ninth Circuit's decision also undermines the principles of comity and respect for tribal court judgments. See Wilson v. Marchington, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997) (extending comity to tribal courts). By holding that tribal court convictions are "constitutionally infirm" because they do not afford a right to counsel under the Sixth Amendment, the Ninth Circuit disregarded the ICRA and the sovereignty of tribal courts.

In conclusion, the Sixth Amendment does not prohibit the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The Ninth Circuit's decision in Bryant is contrary to Supreme Court precedent and undermines the principles of comity and respect for tribal court judgments. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that uncounseled tribal court convictions can be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a).
