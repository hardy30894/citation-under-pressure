The command of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) is unambiguous: each action transferred for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings “shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” The word “shall” is the language of command. Anderson v. Yungkau, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947). Once pretrial proceedings end, therefore, the statute leaves the transferee court no discretion to retain the case for trial.

Section 1404(a), by contrast, is a general venue statute enacted earlier and addressed to ordinary transfer motions. It cannot override the later, specific mandate of § 1407(a). Where Congress has enacted a particular provision addressed to a defined class of cases, that provision controls over a more general statute. See Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58-61 (1949) (holding § 1404(a) inapplicable where a more specific venue statute governed). Allowing a transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to assign the action to itself would nullify the remand command and convert a limited pretrial assignment into a permanent change of forum.

The text and structure of § 1407(a) confirm that the Judicial Panel alone possesses authority to effect any further transfer, and that authority is exhausted once pretrial ends. A district court’s self-assignment under the general transfer statute would therefore exceed the limited powers conferred by the multidistrict statute. Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335, 342-44 (1960) (emphasizing that § 1404(a) may not be used to enlarge or evade statutory venue limitations). Plaintiffs’ ancient right to choose the trial forum, preserved by the remand requirement, would be defeated. See Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 508 (1947).

The court of appeals’ contrary conclusion rests on an impermissible subordination of the specific statutory command to a general transfer provision. Because § 1407(a) requires remand, the district court lacked authority to retain the case for trial under § 1404(a).
