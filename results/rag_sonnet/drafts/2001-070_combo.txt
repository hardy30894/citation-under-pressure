# ARGUMENT

## I. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Categorically Forbid the Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders Who Have Been Found, Through Individualized Proceedings, to Deserve Death.

This Court's Eighth Amendment jurisprudence has never treated capital punishment itself as inherently unconstitutional, nor has it fashioned categorical exemptions from that punishment absent a settled historical consensus. In *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130 (1879), this Court confronted a sentence directing that the prisoner "be taken from your place of confinement to some place within this district, and that you there be publicly shot until you are dead," and found no constitutional infirmity in that mode of execution. The inquiry this Court has traditionally applied looks to whether a challenged practice comports with settled usage, not whether individual jurors, employing their own moral judgment in a particular case, might have reached a different result.

Consistent with that historical approach, this Court has repeatedly entertained challenges to capital sentences without suggesting that the punishment of death is subject to categorical exemption for classes of defendants based on characteristics presented as mitigation. In *Kelley v. Oregon*, 273 U.S. 589 (1927), this Court reviewed a capital sentence imposed after the jury returned "a verdict of guilty as charged in the indictment, without recommendation," and found "there is really no federal question in the case." Similarly, in *McElvaine v. Brush*, 142 U.S. 155 (1891), the Court recounted the procedural history of a capital sentence affirmed after retrial, without hint that any class-based Eighth Amendment infirmity attended the punishment. The consistent teaching of these decisions is that the Eighth Amendment inquiry does not displace the ordinary processes by which juries weigh a defendant's individual circumstances, including his mental condition, against the gravity of his crime.

## II. Virginia's Capital Sentencing Scheme Properly Commits the Assessment of Mitigating Mental Condition to the Individualized Judgment of the Jury.

This Court has long recognized that the jury, not a categorical constitutional rule, is the proper body to weigh a defendant's diminished capacity against his culpability for a capital offense. In *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), the Court examined a statute under which "the jury may qualify their verdict by adding thereto 'without capital punishment'" and, absent such qualification, "the death penalty is mandatory." That statutory structure — permitting the jury, informed by all the evidence including evidence of the defendant's mental state, to determine whether death is the appropriate punishment — reflects precisely the individualized process Virginia employed here, where Atkins's own expert was permitted to testify at length regarding his intellectual functioning.

This Court's decisions construing analogous federal capital statutes confirm that the jury's qualification of its verdict is the mechanism through which considerations such as diminished mental capacity are properly weighed, not evidence that the underlying punishment is constitutionally suspect. In *Rakes v. United States*, 212 U.S. 55 (1909), the Court explained that a defendant convicted of murder in the second degree, "for which the sentence of death could not be imposed," presents a different case from one "where the penalty of death was escaped by qualification of the verdict." And in *Good Shot v. United States*, 179 U.S. 87 (1900), the Court likewise treated the jury's verdict "guilty as charged in the indictment, without capital punishment" as the operative means of tailoring punishment to the individual defendant. Nothing in these decisions suggests that a defendant's mental condition removes the case from the jury's competence; rather, it is precisely the kind of individualized fact the jury is equipped, and constitutionally entitled, to weigh.

That individualized approach also finds support in *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605 (1967), where this Court required "notice and full hearing" before a defendant could be subjected to an enhanced sentence based on facts beyond the elements of the offense. Virginia satisfied precisely that requirement: Atkins received a full penalty-phase hearing, complete with expert testimony concerning his mental functioning, before two separate juries. The remedy for an inadequate individualized hearing is a better hearing, not a categorical constitutional rule removing the class of claim from jury consideration altogether.

## III. This Court Has Been Unwilling to Fashion Categorical Rules of Incompetency from Generalized Claims of Mental Deficiency, and Should Decline to Do So Here.

Even where a defendant has invoked mental deficiency as a basis for relief, this Court has demanded a rigorous, individualized showing rather than presuming incapacity from a diagnostic label. In *Palmer v. Ashe*, 342 U.S. 134 (1951), the petitioner's "only allegation concerning mental incompetency" was a bare recital that he was "mentally abnormal," and this Court found no "special circumstances" sufficient to disturb the judgment on due process grounds. That precedent counsels against converting a contested clinical diagnosis — here, one the Commonwealth's evidence at resentencing directly rebutted — into an automatic bar to a sentence the jury twice imposed after weighing all the evidence, including psychological testimony, at a fully adversarial proceeding.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
