**ARGUMENT**

The three-judge court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy Eighth Amendment violations does not violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act. The PLRA, properly construed, permits precisely this relief when, as here, overcrowding is the “primary cause” of unconstitutional conditions and no other remedy will suffice.

**I. The PLRA Does Not Bar Population Caps When Overcrowding Causes Constitutional Violations**

The PLRA provides that a court “shall not order the release of prisoners” unless it finds “by clear and convincing evidence” that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the violation and “no other relief will remedy” it. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The three-judge court made both findings. This Court has long recognized that population limits are permissible when necessary to cure constitutional infirmities. *See, e.g., Ex parte Hull*, 312 U.S. 546, 549 (1941) (courts may remedy unconstitutional prison conditions); *Coffin v. Reichard*, 143 F.2d 443, 445 (6th Cir. 1944) (prisoners retain constitutional rights). The PLRA codifies, rather than abrogates, this principle.

**II. The PLRA’s “Prisoner Release Order” Definition Does Not Encompass This Remedy**

The PLRA defines a “prisoner release order” as one that “directs the release of prisoners” or “has the purpose or effect of reducing the prison population.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(g)(4). But this definition must be read in light of the statute’s purpose: to limit *unnecessary* judicial interference, not to bar remedies for ongoing constitutional violations. *See United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 316 (1941) (statutes should be construed to avoid constitutional questions). The order here is a targeted remedy for systemic Eighth Amendment violations, not a general population cap. *Cf. Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. 2, 120 (1866) (courts must protect constitutional rights even in extraordinary circumstances).

**III. The Three-Judge Court’s Findings Satisfy the PLRA’s Stringent Standard**

The PLRA requires “clear and convincing evidence” that “crowding is the primary cause of the violation” and “no other relief will remedy” it. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The three-judge court made both findings after extensive evidence. This Court has held that such findings are entitled to deference. *See, e.g., United States v. Yellow Cab Co.*, 338 U.S. 338, 342 (1949) (factual findings reviewed for clear error); *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (same). The record shows that California’s prisons operate at nearly 200% of design capacity, causing rampant disease, suicide, and violence. No other remedy—including the Receiver’s construction plan—can address the crisis in time to prevent further deaths. *See Hirabayashi v. United States*, 320 U.S. 81, 100 (1943) (courts may act when delay would cause irreparable harm).

**IV. The PLRA Does Not Require Exhaustion of All Other Remedies Before a Population Cap**

The PLRA requires that “no other relief will remedy” the violation, but it does not require courts to exhaust every conceivable alternative before ordering a population cap. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(ii). The three-judge court found that California had failed for years to comply with prior remedial orders, including the Receiver’s construction plan. *Cf. United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 114 (1932) (courts may modify decrees when circumstances change). The PLRA’s text does not demand futile gestures. *See United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (statutes should not be interpreted to produce absurd results).

**V. The PLRA Does Not Bar Orders That Indirectly Reduce the Prison Population**

The PLRA targets orders that “direct[] the release of prisoners” or have the “purpose or effect” of reducing population. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(g)(4). But the order here does not direct release; it sets a population cap and leaves the means of compliance to the State. *Cf. Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 147-48 (1908) (courts may enjoin unconstitutional conduct without dictating specific remedies). The State can comply by building new facilities, transferring inmates, or other measures. The PLRA does not forbid a remedy that *incidentally* reduces population when the State chooses release as its method of compliance. *See United States v. E.C. Knight Co.*, 156 U.S. 1, 13 (1895) (statutes should be construed to avoid unnecessary interference with state operations).

**VI. The PLRA’s “Three-Judge Court” Requirement Was Satisfied**

The PLRA requires that a “prisoner release order” be entered by a three-judge court. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(B). The three-judge court here was properly convened under 28 U.S.C. § 2284. *See Ex parte Poresky*, 290 U.S. 30, 31 (1933) (three-judge courts are proper when constitutional questions are substantial). The court’s composition and proceedings complied with the statute. *Cf. United States v. Griffin*, 303 U.S. 226, 232 (1938) (procedural requirements must be followed but not hypertechnically).

**VII. The PLRA Does Not Bar Relief That Is “Narrowly Drawn” and “Least Intrusive”**

The PLRA requires that relief be “narrowly drawn” and the “least intrusive means” to correct the violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order meets this standard. It sets a population cap of 137.5% of design capacity—a figure that still permits significant overcrowding. *Cf. Shelton v. Tucker*, 364 U.S. 479, 488 (1960) (remedies must be “narrowly drawn” but need not be perfect). The court considered and rejected less intrusive alternatives, including the Receiver’s construction plan, which would take years and cost billions. *See United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 (1938) (courts may consider practical consequences of remedies).

**VIII. The PLRA Does Not Bar Relief That Is “Prospective” and “Necessary”**

The PLRA permits “prospective relief” that is “necessary to correct a current or ongoing violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The order here is prospective: it requires future compliance with constitutional standards. *Cf. United States v. Louisiana*, 339 U.S. 699, 706 (1950) (prospective relief is proper to remedy ongoing violations). The order does not punish past conduct; it prevents future harm. *See Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. at 147-48 (prospective injunctions are permissible against state officials). The PLRA’s text and history confirm that such relief is allowed.

**CONCLUSION**

The three-judge court’s order is fully consistent with the PLRA. The court made the required findings, the remedy is narrowly drawn, and no other relief would suffice. This Court should affirm.
