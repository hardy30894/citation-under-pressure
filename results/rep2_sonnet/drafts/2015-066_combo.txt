# ARGUMENT

**I. THE SIXTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT APPLY TO TRIBAL COURT PROCEEDINGS, AND THEREFORE BRYANT'S TRIBAL CONVICTIONS WERE NOT CONSTITUTIONALLY DEFECTIVE.**

The predicate offense here—a final conviction rendered by the Northern Cheyenne Tribal Court—was not subject to the Sixth Amendment at all. Indian tribes retain "original natural rights" as governments that predate the Constitution and were never surrendered to it. *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832). Because tribal sovereignty is not derivative of the federal Constitution, this Court has long held that the Bill of Rights simply does not constrain the internal governmental and judicial functions of tribes. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896) (holding that the Fifth Amendment grand jury requirement did not govern proceedings of the Cherokee Nation because the powers exercised by tribal government "existed prior to" and independent of the Constitution). The same logic that exempted the Cherokee Nation's criminal process from the Fifth Amendment in *Talton* exempts the Northern Cheyenne Tribal Court's process from the Sixth Amendment here.

This principle is reinforced by the settled understanding that Congress, not the judiciary, defines the relationship between tribal self-governance and federal criminal law. *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 379-80 (1886) (recognizing Congress's plenary authority over crimes committed in Indian country as an incident of the unique relationship between the tribes and the federal government). Tribal courts historically exercised independent criminal jurisdiction over their own members, *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556, 571-72 (1883), and Congress has never required, as a condition of that jurisdiction's validity, that tribal proceedings mirror federal constitutional procedure. A conviction lawfully rendered under that separate sovereign authority is not rendered "uncounseled" in any constitutionally cognizable sense—it was never subject to the Sixth Amendment's counsel guarantee in the first place.

By contrast, this Court's right-to-counsel jurisprudence has always been framed as a limitation on the *federal and state* governments. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 463 (1938) (holding that the Sixth Amendment "withholds from federal courts, in all criminal proceedings, the power and authority to deprive an accused of his life or liberty unless he has or waives the assistance of counsel"); *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963) (holding that "lawyers in criminal courts are necessities, not luxuries" and requiring appointed counsel in state prosecutions). Both *Zerbst* and *Gideon* speak to obligations imposed on federal and state sovereigns exercising Article III or Fourteenth Amendment-bound authority. Neither purports to extend that obligation to tribal governments acting under their own retained sovereignty. The Ninth Circuit's rule effectively imports the Sixth Amendment into tribal adjudication through the back door of a federal recidivist statute—a result inconsistent with a century of precedent holding that the Bill of Rights simply does not reach tribal courts.

**II. USING A VALID TRIBAL CONVICTION AS THE PREDICATE FOR A FEDERAL RECIDIVIST OFFENSE DOES NOT OFFEND THE SIXTH AMENDMENT, BECAUSE RECIDIVIST STATUTES PUNISH ONLY THE STATUS OF REPEATED OFFENDING.**

Even apart from tribal sovereignty, this Court has long recognized that habitual-offender statutes do not reopen or relitigate the validity of prior convictions; they simply recognize the historical fact that the defendant has offended before. In *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616, 623-24 (1912), the Court upheld the use of prior convictions to establish habitual-criminal status, explaining that such statutes punish the new offense more severely in light of the defendant's persistence in unlawful conduct, not the prior offenses themselves. Likewise, in *McDonald v. Massachusetts*, 180 U.S. 311, 313 (1901), this Court sustained a habitual-offender enhancement, holding that the additional punishment "is for the [new] crime only, but is heavier if he is a habitual criminal." Section 117(a) operates on precisely this recidivist logic: it punishes the *current* domestic assault, using prior convictions—federal, state, or tribal—only as evidence of the defendant's established pattern of abuse. Because the statute does not ask a jury to relitigate the fairness of the tribal proceeding, but only whether it occurred, no Sixth Amendment concern regarding the *predicate* offense arises.

**III. PRINCIPLES OF COMITY COUNSEL AGAINST INVALIDATING TRIBAL JUDGMENTS THAT WERE ENTERED IN FULL COMPLIANCE WITH TRIBAL LAW.**

Finally, longstanding comity principles support recognizing tribal judgments as valid for federal statutory purposes, even though tribal procedure differs from federal or state procedure. This Court has explained that comity is owed to judgments of another sovereign "having due regard both to international duty and convenience, and to the rights of its own citizens." *Hilton v. Guyot*, 159 U.S. 113, 163-64 (1895). The Northern Cheyenne Tribal Court's judgments, rendered under procedures Congress itself has sanctioned through the Indian Civil Rights Act, are entitled to no less respect. To hold otherwise—as the Ninth Circuit did—treats tribal adjudications as inherently suspect merely because they do not replicate federal constitutional procedure, contrary to the deference this Court's precedents demand for the judgments of sovereigns operating under their own valid legal orders.

For these reasons, Bryant's uncounseled tribal-court convictions were valid when entered and may properly serve as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) without violating the Sixth Amendment.
