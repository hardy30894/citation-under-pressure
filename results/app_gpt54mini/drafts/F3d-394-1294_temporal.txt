## Argument

The Tax Court erred in holding that the amended Davis trust fails to qualify for the marital deduction under I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7). The controlling question is whether, at Mr. Davis’s death, Mrs. Davis held a “qualifying income interest for life,” meaning she was entitled to all income from the property for life and no one could divert any part of that property to another beneficiary. I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). The Amendment satisfies that standard on its face.

A marital deduction turns on the interest passing from the decedent, not on labels or later speculation. See Henslee v. Union Planters Nat’l Bank & Tr. Co., 335 U.S. 595, 597-98 (1949). Here, the Amendment provides that, during Mrs. Davis’s lifetime, “the trustee shall pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse . . . all of the net income from the trust estate.” That is an express direction that she receive the trust’s entire income stream. Courts have long treated mandatory income provisions as creating a present beneficial right, not a mere expectancy. See Helvering v. Stuart, 317 U.S. 154, 163-64 (1942).

The Commissioner and the Tax Court placed undue weight on the trustee’s discretion to determine the manner of payment and to consider outside resources. Those provisions do not defeat the spouse’s entitlement to income. The critical inquiry is whether the beneficiary is entitled to all income, not whether the trustee may administer distributions prudently. See United States v. Merchants Nat’l Bank of Mobile, 261 F.2d 640, 642-43 (5th Cir. 1958), aff’d, 363 U.S. 555 (1960). A fiduciary’s authority to choose administrative details does not erase the underlying duty to pay income to the income beneficiary. See Old Colony Tr. Co. v. Commissioner, 87 F.2d 131, 133 (1st Cir. 1936). The Amendment says the trustee “shall pay” all net income; that language is mandatory, not permissive.

Nor does the principal invasion clause defeat the marital deduction. Section 2056(b)(7) expressly tolerates principal protection and even principal invasion; the disqualifying feature is a power to appoint property away from the spouse. I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). The Amendment permits invasion of principal only if income and the surviving spouse’s own trust assets are insufficient for her health, support, and maintenance, and then only in the trustee’s reasonable discretion. That is a limited support standard, not a power to reallocate the property for the benefit of others. Support-trust language has long been recognized as compatible with a beneficial life interest. See Restatement (First) of Trusts § 128 cmt. e (1935); see also Commissioner v. Estate of Bosch, 387 U.S. 456, 465 (1967) (state-law property interests govern federal tax consequences).

The Tax Court’s contrary approach effectively treated the trustee’s discretion as if it created a terminable interest. But federal estate-tax law has never required that the surviving spouse possess absolute, unfettered dominion over trust property to qualify for marital treatment. In interpreting the marital deduction, the Supreme Court has emphasized that the statutory purpose is to prevent taxation where the surviving spouse receives the equivalent of outright ownership, subject to the statutory exceptions. See Estate of Field v. Commissioner, 324 U.S. 113, 115-16 (1945). Mrs. Davis’s right to all net income for life is exactly such an interest.

The trust instrument also satisfies the statutory requirement that no one have power to appoint any part of the property to anyone other than the surviving spouse. I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). The daughters’ contingent succession rights arise only after Mrs. Davis’s death, and the trustee’s power to invade principal exists solely for her benefit during life. Nothing in the Amendment authorizes diversion of trust property away from her during her lifetime. See Helvering v. Hallock, 309 U.S. 106, 112-13 (1940) (federal tax consequences depend on present interests and retained control, not remote contingencies).

In short, the Amendment gives Mrs. Davis an enforceable right to all net income for life, with only limited administrative and support-based provisions that do not negate that right. Because the trust satisfies the requirements of I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7), the estate is entitled to the marital deduction. The judgment should be reversed.
