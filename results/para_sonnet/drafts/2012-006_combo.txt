**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Proving Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That a Defendant Remained a Member of the Conspiracy Within the Limitations Period Once the Defendant Has Come Forward With Evidence of Withdrawal.**

A conspiracy is, in the words of this Court, "a partnership in criminal purposes." *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 608 (1910). Because that partnership is ordinarily understood to persist until its objects are either accomplished or abandoned, this Court has long recognized a rebuttable presumption that a conspirator's membership continues until the conspiracy itself ends. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) (a conspirator who has joined an unlawful combination continues in it "unless he can show affirmatively that he withdrew" from the enterprise). But the very fact that the presumption is rebuttable means that it disappears once evidence of withdrawal is introduced; it cannot be transformed into a fixed burden that the accused must carry beyond persuasion of production. *Id.*

This Court applied precisely that principle in *Fiswick v. United States*, 329 U.S. 211 (1946). There, the government sought to hold members of an organization liable for conspiracy within the limitations period notwithstanding evidence that the organization's American branch had been dissolved years earlier. This Court held that the government could not rely on presumed continuity once the defendants had shown that the enterprise's purpose had been abandoned; the prosecution was required to establish that each defendant's participation extended into the limitations period. *Id.* at 216–17. The presumption of continuing membership, in other words, yields to proof of termination, and the ultimate burden of persuasion as to continued participation remains with the government throughout.

The Second Circuit confirmed this rule in *United States v. Borelli*, 336 F.2d 376 (2d Cir. 1964), a decision by Judge Friendly directly addressing the question presented here. The court held that "once a conspiracy is shown to have existed, the burden of showing withdrawal from it rests upon the defendant," but that this burden is merely a burden of coming forward with evidence; when a defendant produces such evidence, "the burden of proving that the conspiracy continued to exist, and that the defendants continued to be members of it, rests upon the Government." *Id.* at 388. This allocation follows naturally from the settled principle that the government must prove every element of the charged offense — including the defendant's participation within the statutory period — beyond a reasonable doubt. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 493 (1895) (the government must establish guilt "beyond a reasonable doubt" as to every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged).

**II. Requiring the Government to Prove Continued Membership Beyond a Reasonable Doubt Is Consistent With This Court's Conspiracy Jurisprudence, Which Treats Membership as an Element the Prosecution Must Establish for Each Defendant.**

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that conspiracy liability is personal and must be proved defendant by defendant; membership cannot be assumed from association with wrongdoers generally. *United States v. Falcone*, 311 U.S. 205, 210 (1940) ("those who buy and sell to a peddler in the ordinary course of business... do not thereby become sponsors" of his unlawful venture merely by continued dealing). Likewise, in *Direct Sales Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 703 (1943), the Court explained that participation in a conspiracy requires proof of a defendant's own continuing intent to further its purposes, not merely his historical association with it. *Id.* at 711–13. These cases demonstrate that this Court has never permitted the government to satisfy its burden by resting on the defendant's earlier participation alone once that participation is called into question by evidence of termination.

*Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440 (1949), reinforces the same principle from another angle. There the Court refused to admit a co-conspirator's statement made after the conspiracy's central objective had already been accomplished, reasoning that "the central aim of the alleged conspiracy had already either failed or been achieved" by that time. *Id.* at 443. Implicit in that holding is the recognition that a conspiracy does not extend indefinitely by fiction; once its purpose is achieved or abandoned, the conspiracy — and membership in it — ends as a matter of proof, not presumption.

The same logic informed Judge Learned Hand's opinion in *United States v. Compagna*, 146 F.2d 524 (2d Cir. 1944), which observed that a conspirator's liability for acts of the conspiracy cannot be extended past the point at which his participation has ceased, for "the theory of the crime forbids" treating a former conspirator as bound by a venture he has left. *Id.* at 528. And in *Blumenthal v. United States*, 332 U.S. 539 (1947), this Court cautioned against permitting broad inferences of continuing common purpose to substitute for actual proof of each defendant's ongoing participation. *Id.* at 556–57.

**III. Conclusion**

These authorities converge on a single, settled rule: the presumption that conspiratorial membership continues is rebuttable, and once rebutted by evidence of withdrawal, the government — not the accused — must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy within the statute-of-limitations period. *Fiswick*, 329 U.S. 211; *Borelli*, 336 F.2d 376; *Hyde*, 225 U.S. 347. The judgment below, which placed that burden instead on petitioners, should be reversed.
