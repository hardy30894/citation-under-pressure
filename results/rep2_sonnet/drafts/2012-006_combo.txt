**ARGUMENT**

**The Government Must Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That a Defendant Remained a Member of the Conspiracy During the Limitations Period, Even After the Defendant Has Produced Evidence of Withdrawal**

**I. The presumption of innocence places on the government the burden of proving every element of the charged offense, including that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy within the statutory period.**

This Court has long recognized that "the presumption of innocence is a conclusion drawn by the law in favor of the citizen, by virtue whereof, when brought to trial upon a criminal charge, he must be acquitted, unless he is proven to be guilty." *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895). That presumption does not evaporate simply because a defendant introduces evidence bearing on an element of the crime. In *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), this Court held that once evidence of insanity is introduced, the government retains the burden to establish sanity, and hence guilt, beyond a reasonable doubt; the burden of persuasion on an issue central to criminal liability does not shift to the accused merely because he has come forward with proof. *Id.* at 486–88. The same logic controls here. Continued membership in the conspiracy during the limitations period is not a collateral matter—it is the very fact that makes prosecution timely and that establishes the defendant's guilt of the charged, ongoing offense. Requiring the defendant to disprove that fact by a preponderance of the evidence inverts the constitutional allocation of risk that *Coffin* and *Davis* establish.

**II. Conspiracy is a continuing partnership in crime, and the government—not the defendant—bears the burden of proving that the partnership persisted into the limitations period.**

This Court has consistently described conspiracy as a continuing offense that persists "until it either accomplishes its purpose or is abandoned." *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 608 (1910). Because the crime is continuous, the statute of limitations runs not from the moment the conspiracy is formed, but from the last act performed in furtherance of it during the defendant's membership. *Id.* It follows that the government must affirmatively establish that the defendant remained part of that continuing partnership within the limitations window; it cannot rest on proof of membership at some earlier point and then shift to the defendant the burden of showing when, if ever, that membership ceased.

This Court applied precisely that principle in *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), holding that a conspirator "continues to be a party to it until by some affirmative act he shows that he has withdrawn," *id.* at 369, but nowhere suggesting that the defendant bears the burden of proving that withdrawal to negate an element the government must otherwise establish. Similarly, in *Fiswick v. United States*, 329 U.S. 211 (1946), this Court examined evidence that the petitioner had ceased his membership in the German-American Bund before the limitations period and found the proof of continued participation within that period insufficient to sustain conviction—treating the timing of membership as part of the prosecution's burden of proof, not as an affirmative defense the accused had to establish. *Id.* at 216–17.

Likewise, in *Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440 (1949), the Court cautioned against permitting the prosecution to extend a conspiracy's life artificially through post-conspiracy statements, precisely because doing so risks eroding the requirement that guilt for the continuing offense be proven, not presumed, for the relevant period. *Id.* at 443–44. And in *Lutwak v. United States*, 344 U.S. 604 (1953), the Court reaffirmed that the substantive scope of a conspiracy—including when it ends—is a matter for the trier of fact to determine from the government's proof, not from a burden imposed upon the accused. *Id.* at 616–17.

**III. Allocating the burden of persuasion to the defendant on this issue is inconsistent with this Court's settled approach to presumptions and burdens in criminal cases.**

This Court has repeatedly refused to permit legislatures or courts to ease the government's burden of proof through presumptions or burden-shifting devices that bear on guilt. In *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463 (1943), the Court struck down a statutory presumption because it lacked a rational connection to the fact presumed, explaining that due process forbids inference mechanisms that relieve the government of its obligation to prove guilt. *Id.* at 467–68. And in *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), the Court refused to presume criminal intent from an act alone, insisting that the government prove each element essential to culpability. *Id.* at 274–75. Treating withdrawal as an affirmative defense that the defendant must establish—rather than treating evidence of withdrawal as raising a doubt that the government must dispel—runs directly counter to this unbroken line of authority. As this Court observed in *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513 (1958), where fundamental interests are at stake, "the risk of an erroneous determination" must fall on the party best able to bear it, and due process ordinarily assigns that risk to the government in criminal prosecutions. *Id.* at 525–26.

Because continued membership in the conspiracy during the limitations period is an element of the continuing offense, and because this Court's precedents uniformly forbid shifting the burden of persuasion on offense elements to the accused, the government—not petitioner—must bear the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that petitioner remained a member of the conspiracy within the statute of limitations period.
