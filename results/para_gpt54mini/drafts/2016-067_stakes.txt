**Argument**

The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander is not a “debt collector” under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act when it collects debts it owns for its own account. The statutory text, structure, and precedent all point the same way: the FDCPA generally reaches third parties collecting for others, not creditors collecting their own debts. See 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6); Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 294 (1995); Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 135-37 (4th Cir. 2016).

First, the FDCPA’s core definition turns on whether the defendant “regularly collects or attempts to collect . . . debts owed or due . . . another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). That language naturally covers debt-collection agents, not entities pursuing debts they own. As the Supreme Court has explained, the Act’s definition “includes a person ‘who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] . . . another.’” Heintz, 514 U.S. at 293 (alteration in original). Congress’s stated purpose confirms that reading: the FDCPA was enacted “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors,” not to regulate ordinary creditors collecting their own accounts. 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e).

Second, the statutory exclusions do not convert every purchaser of defaulted debt into a debt collector. Petitioners rely heavily on the “not in default” language in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), but that exclusion applies only to a person collecting “any debt owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another” and only when the debt “was not in default at the time it was obtained.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). That is an exclusion from an already-applicable debt-collector definition; it does not itself define debt collectors. The Eleventh Circuit has correctly recognized that “where a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a ‘debt collector’ as a matter of course.” Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1314 (11th Cir. 2015). The threshold question remains whether the defendant is collecting debts “owed . . . another” in the first place. Id. at 1315-16.

Third, the better reading of § 1692a(6) is that status depends on the defendant’s role at the time of the challenged conduct. A company collecting debts it owns is acting as a creditor, not as a debt collector. See Henson, 817 F.3d at 136-37. The Fourth Circuit properly rejected petitioners’ attempt to freeze Santander’s status based on the fact that the debts were in default when purchased. As that court explained, “the default status of a debt has no bearing on whether a person qualifies as a debt collector” under the principal definition in § 1692a(6). Id. at 136. The Ninth Circuit has likewise held that the statute is “not susceptible” to the theory that “‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another.’” Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013).

Fourth, petitioners’ proposed rule would collapse the FDCPA’s creditor/debt-collector distinction and sweep ordinary creditors into the Act whenever they buy defaulted receivables. That is inconsistent with the statute’s structure and purpose. Courts have repeatedly recognized that the FDCPA “generally does not regulate creditors” collecting on their own behalf. See, e.g., Henson, 817 F.3d at 136; Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB, 681 F.3d 355, 359 (6th Cir. 2012); FTC v. Check Investors, Inc., 502 F.3d 159, 173 (3d Cir. 2007). Congress drew a line between those who collect for others and those who collect for themselves, and the Court should not erase that line by equating ownership of defaulted debt with third-party collection.

Finally, petitioners’ reliance on Santander’s alleged prior role as a servicer fares no better. The FDCPA does not impose permanent “debt collector” status on an entity simply because it once collected for another. Liability under § 1692e and related provisions requires that the defendant be acting as a debt collector “in connection with the collection of any debt” at the time of the challenged communication. 15 U.S.C. § 1692e; see Heintz, 514 U.S. at 294. Once Santander purchased the loans, it collected for itself, not “another,” and fell outside the Act’s ordinary coverage.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
