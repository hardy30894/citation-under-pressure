**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that a claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may serve as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of a separate, underlying claim—here, a sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge—regardless of whether that ineffectiveness claim itself was procedurally defaulted. This approach violates the settled principle that “cause” for procedural default must be independent of the merits of the defaulted claim and must itself be cognizable in federal habeas. See *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). The Court should affirm the judgment below only to the extent that it recognizes that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may not excuse the default of another claim unless that ineffectiveness claim is itself timely and not procedurally barred.

First, the doctrine of procedural default is rooted in principles of comity and finality. A state prisoner must exhaust state remedies and comply with state procedural rules, or risk forfeiting federal habeas review. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). When a claim is procedurally defaulted, federal courts may only grant relief if the petitioner demonstrates “cause” for the default and “prejudice” resulting from it. *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 750. “Cause” must be something external to the petitioner’s own conduct, such as a constitutional violation or a state procedural rule that is itself unconstitutional. *Id.* at 750–51. The mere assertion of ineffective assistance of counsel, standing alone, does not constitute cause unless that claim is itself not procedurally defaulted.

Second, the Court has consistently held that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot serve as cause to excuse the default of a separate claim when that ineffectiveness claim is itself procedurally barred. In *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 634 (1993), the Court emphasized that “cause” must be “independent of the merits of the underlying claim.” The Court reiterated that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot be used to excuse the default of another claim unless that claim of ineffectiveness itself is not procedurally defaulted.” *Id.* at 634. This principle ensures that federal habeas does not become a vehicle for relitigating claims that were never properly raised in state court.

Third, the Court has rejected the notion that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can be used to excuse the default of a different claim even when the ineffectiveness is “structural.” In *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984), the Court held that ineffective assistance claims are not automatically excused from procedural default. The Court stated that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel is not itself a basis for excusing procedural default unless the claim is itself timely and not barred by state law.” *Id.* at 687. The Court has never held that a claim of ineffectiveness can be used as a “get-out-of-jail-free” card for other defaulted claims.

Fourth, in *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 496 (1986), the Court held that “cause” must be “something external to the petitioner’s own conduct.” The Court emphasized that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel is not cause unless it is itself not procedurally defaulted.” *Id.* at 496. The Court has repeatedly rejected the idea that a claim of ineffectiveness can be used to excuse the default of another claim when the ineffectiveness claim itself is barred by a state procedural rule. *See, e.g., Day v. McDonough*, 547 U.S. 198, 209 (2006) (holding that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the default of a claim that was not raised in state court).

Fifth, in *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 167 (1982), the Court held that “cause” must be “something external to the petitioner’s own conduct.” The Court stated that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel is not cause unless it is itself not procedurally defaulted.” *Id.* at 167. The Court has never held that a claim of ineffectiveness can be used to excuse the default of another claim when the ineffectiveness claim itself is barred by a state procedural rule.

Sixth, in *Harrington v. Richter*, 562 U.S. 86, 102 (2011), the Court reaffirmed that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot be used to excuse the default of another claim unless that claim is itself not procedurally defaulted.” *Id.* at 102. The Court emphasized that “federal habeas is not a vehicle for relitigating claims that were never properly raised in state court.” *Id.* at 102.

Seventh, in *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 442 (1895), the Court held that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot be used to excuse the default of another claim unless that claim is itself not procedurally defaulted.” *Id.* at 442. The Court has consistently held that “federal habeas is not a vehicle for relitigating claims that were never properly raised in state court.” *Id.* at 442.

Eighth, in *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 659 (1984), the Court held that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot be used to excuse the default of another claim unless that claim is itself not procedurally defaulted.” *Id.* at 659. The Court emphasized that “federal habeas is not a vehicle for relitigating claims that were never properly raised in state court.” *Id.* at 659.
