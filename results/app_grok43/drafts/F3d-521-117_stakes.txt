ARGUMENT

The district court erred in denying the motion to suppress. A traffic stop constitutes a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. Delaware v. Prouse, 440 U.S. 648, 653 (1979). Officers may order a passenger from the vehicle during such a stop. Maryland v. Wilson, 519 U.S. 408, 414-15 (1997). A subsequent pat-frisk, however, requires reasonable suspicion, based on specific and articulable facts, that the passenger is armed and dangerous. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968); see also United States v. Romain, 393 F.3d 63, 71 (1st Cir. 2004).

The officers observed the Lexus in a high-crime area at night with its headlights off. These circumstances alone do not justify a frisk. Brown v. Texas, 443 U.S. 47, 52 (1979). The passengers’ movements toward the console and door likewise fall short. United States v. McKoy, 428 F.3d 38, 41 (1st Cir. 2005) (holding that furtive movements during a traffic stop, without more, do not establish reasonable suspicion of danger). Soares’s verbal agitation, rocking, and hand movements occurred after the stop was underway and consisted of profanity and accusations of racial profiling. Such conduct, while disrespectful, does not supply the requisite suspicion that the individual is armed. See United States v. Brown, 510 F.3d 57, 68 (1st Cir. 2007) (distinguishing verbal non-compliance from conduct indicating a weapon).

No officer reported observing a bulge, reaching toward a weapon, or any other affirmative indication of danger. The arrival of backup officers and the driver’s consent to a frisk do not retroactively validate the decision to frisk Soares. See Florida v. J.L., 529 U.S. 266, 271 (2000) (rejecting reliance on generalized concerns rather than individualized suspicion). Because the pat-frisk rested on an insufficient factual predicate, the handgun must be suppressed. Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 484-85 (1963).
