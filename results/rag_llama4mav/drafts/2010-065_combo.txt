The respondent respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on grounds that the statute is beyond the federal government's enumerated powers and inconsistent with the Tenth Amendment.

The Tenth Amendment challenge is foreclosed by the Supreme Court's decision in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), where the Court held that private parties lack standing to raise a Tenth Amendment claim. The Court stated that "absent the states or their officers, [the utility companies] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]." Id. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366. This holding has been followed by several circuit courts, and it controls the outcome here.

The Necessary and Proper Clause, as interpreted by the Supreme Court in McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. 316 (1819), grants Congress the authority to enact laws necessary to effectuate its treaty obligations. The Treaty Power, as established in Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), permits the federal government to enact legislation implementing treaties, even if such legislation would otherwise be beyond Congress's enumerated powers. See id. at 432-433, 40 S.Ct. 382. The Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act of 1998 and its associated penal provision, 18 U.S.C. § 229, are valid exercises of this authority.

The Supreme Court has long recognized the importance of the Treaty Power in Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), and United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp., 299 U.S. 304 (1936). In Curtiss-Wright, the Court noted that the federal government's authority to make treaties is not limited by the Tenth Amendment. See id. at 318, 57 S.Ct. 216. Similarly, in Holland, the Court held that a treaty and its implementing legislation can be valid even if they regulate matters that are typically considered local in nature. See 252 U.S. at 433, 40 S.Ct. 382.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has established that the federal government's authority to enact legislation under the Necessary and Proper Clause is not limited to laws that are "absolutely necessary" to carry out its enumerated powers. See McCulloch, 17 U.S. at 414-415. Rather, Congress has the authority to enact laws that are "appropriate" to achieve its constitutional objectives. See id. at 421.

Other Supreme Court decisions support the proposition that the federal government's authority to enact legislation under the Treaty Power is not limited by the Tenth Amendment. See, e.g., De Geofroy v. Riggs, 133 U.S. 258 (1890); Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52 (1941); and Asakura v. City of Seattle, 265 U.S. 332 (1924).

In light of these authorities, it is clear that Congress had the authority to enact 18 U.S.C. § 229 under the Necessary and Proper Clause, and that the statute is a valid exercise of the Treaty Power. Bond's Tenth Amendment challenge is therefore without merit, and her conviction should be affirmed.

For the foregoing reasons, the respondent respectfully submits that the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
