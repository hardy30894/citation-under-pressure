Petitioner’s answer to the Question Presented is no. The Due Process Clause does not compel a trial court to apply a special “reasoned choice” competency standard before accepting a guilty plea or waiver of counsel. This Court’s decisions distinguish between competence to stand trial and competence to plead guilty or waive rights, but they do not announce a constitutional “heightened” test framed in those terms. See Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162 (1975); Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993).

A defendant is competent to stand trial if he has “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him” and can consult with counsel with a reasonable degree of rational understanding. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). But this Court has never constitutionalized a second, more demanding “reasoned choice” standard for plea or waiver decisions. To the contrary, the governing question under the Due Process Clause is whether the defendant is competent—i.e., whether he possesses the mental capacity required by the Constitution—and whether the waiver itself is knowing and voluntary. See Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993); Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938); Brookhart v. Janis, 384 U.S. 1 (1966).

Godinez is controlling. There, the Court rejected the notion that competency to waive counsel or plead guilty requires a “higher” level of mental functioning than competency to stand trial. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389, 399-400 (1993). The Court explained that “the competency standard for pleading guilty or waiving the right to counsel is the same as the competency standard for standing trial.” Id. at 398. What is different is the separate requirement that the plea or waiver be knowing and voluntary. Id. at 400-01. That is exactly the framework the Constitution requires here.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” formulation cannot be squared with this Court’s precedents. Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and its progeny treated the decision to plead guilty as demanding a greater cognitive capacity than trial competence. But Godinez repudiated that approach. 509 U.S. at 397-400. The Court rejected the premise that a defendant must be able to make a “rational choice” among alternatives as a distinct constitutional test for competence to plead guilty. Id. at 398-99. Due process does not require a trial judge to conduct a separate inquiry into whether the defendant’s decision is especially prudent, wise, or “reasoned” in the Ninth Circuit’s sense. Id. at 399.

That does not mean a court may ignore signs of incompetence. Where the evidence raises a bona fide doubt as to competence, due process requires a hearing. Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 180-81 (1975). But that obligation arises from evidence of incompetence, not from a heightened substantive standard for waivers. Indeed, this Court has repeatedly recognized that competency inquiries concern the defendant’s present capacity, while voluntariness and knowledge govern the validity of the waiver itself. Henderson v. Morgan, 426 U.S. 637, 645-46 (1976); North Carolina v. Alford, 400 U.S. 25, 31 (1970); Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 242-44 (1969).

The Ninth Circuit’s rule also threatens the autonomy interests that underlie Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975). If a defendant is competent under Dusky, the Constitution permits him to waive counsel and plead guilty even when those choices appear irrational or unwise. Godinez, 509 U.S. at 400. The Due Process Clause does not authorize courts to substitute their own assessment of whether the defendant has chosen well for the separate constitutional inquiries of competence and valid waiver. Id. at 401-02.

Here, the state court found Moran competent after psychiatric examination, and the federal habeas court properly rejected his claim for a heightened competency test. The Ninth Circuit reversed only because it believed the Constitution demanded a “reasoned choice” standard and that the record did not satisfy it. That was error. Under Godinez, once Moran was competent under Dusky and his guilty plea and waiver of counsel were knowing and voluntary, due process was satisfied. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993).

The judgment of the court of appeals should therefore be reversed.
