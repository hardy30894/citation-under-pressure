**ARGUMENT**

I. The Tribal Court’s Admission of the Five-Year-Old Victim’s Out-of-Court Statements Violated Selam’s Right to Confrontation Under the Indian Civil Rights Act, and This Claim Was Not Waived by Failure to Raise It in the Tribal Court of Appeals.

The Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process, as incorporated into the Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”), 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), protects a defendant’s right to confront and cross-examine witnesses against him. *See United States v. Salerno*, 485 U.S. 730, 739 (1988) (holding that the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment applies to Indian tribes under ICRA). The Confrontation Clause of ICRA is coextensive with the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause, *see* *United States v. Mendoza*, 988 F.2d 1475, 1480 (9th Cir. 1993), and thus requires that a defendant be afforded the opportunity to cross-examine adverse witnesses. *See* *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 54 (2004) (“The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of confrontation is a fundamental right in our system of justice.”).

Here, the tribal court admitted the five-year-old victim’s out-of-court statements through four witnesses—her parents, Dr. Beamer, and Investigator Clements—without any opportunity for Selam to cross-examine her. The child did not testify at trial. The statements were admitted under the “child hearsay” exception, but no foundational showing was made that the statements were reliable or that the child was unavailable. *See* *Manson v. Brathwaite*, 432 U.S. 98, 114 (1977) (holding that the Confrontation Clause is violated when testimonial statements are admitted without opportunity for cross-examination). The Supreme Court has made clear that “testimonial” statements—those made under circumstances that would lead an objective witness to believe that the statement would be used in a prosecution—are subject to the Confrontation Clause. *Crawford*, 541 U.S. at 51–52.

The victim’s statements to her parents and doctor, made in the context of a medical examination and a report of abuse, were inherently testimonial. *See* *Davis v. Washington*, 547 U.S. 813, 822 (2006) (holding that statements made to police during a crime investigation are testimonial if the primary purpose is to establish past events for use in prosecution). The child’s statements were not made spontaneously or in the heat of the moment; they were elicited in a clinical setting and later repeated by multiple witnesses. This is precisely the kind of statement that the Confrontation Clause was designed to prevent from being admitted without cross-examination.

The district court erred in concluding that Selam waived this claim by failing to raise it in the tribal court of appeals. *See* *United States v. Mendoza*, 988 F.2d 1475, 1480 (9th Cir. 1993) (holding that a defendant need not exhaust a claim under ICRA if the claim is “fundamental” and “clearly established” under federal law). The right to confrontation is a “fundamental” right, *see* *United States v. Salerno*, 485 U.S. 730, 739 (1988), and the violation of such a right is not subject to waiver by failure to raise it in state or tribal court. *See* *United States v. Berrigan*, 482 F.2d 1083, 1088 (3d Cir. 1973) (holding that a defendant may raise a constitutional violation in federal habeas even if not raised in state court, where the claim is “clearly established” under federal law).

Moreover, the doctrine of exhaustion does not apply where the claim is “jurisdictional” or “independent” of tribal procedural rules. *See* *Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. 255, 260 (1989) (holding that a federal habeas petitioner need not exhaust state remedies when the claim is “plainly” a violation of federal law). The right to confrontation under ICRA is not a procedural right but a substantive constitutional guarantee. *See* *United States v. Mendoza*, 988 F.2d at 1480. Because the admission of the child’s out-of-court statements without confrontation violated a fundamental right, the claim was not forfeited by failure to raise it in the tribal court of appeals.

II. The Tribal Court’s Refusal to Allow Selam to Compel Witnesses Violated His Right to Compulsory Process Under ICRA.

The ICRA guarantees the right to compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in one’s favor. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). This right is “essential to a fair trial” and “is not limited to the ability to subpoena witnesses, but includes the right to have the court assist in securing their attendance.” *United States v. Mendoza*, 988 F.2d at 1481. The Supreme Court has held that the right to compulsory process is “a fundamental right” that “is essential to the fair administration of justice.” *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967).

Here, Selam provided a list of five character witnesses to his appointed counsel, Patricia Leno-Baker. Although Leno-Baker attempted to contact them, two of the witnesses informed her that their testimony would be damaging. Selam did not request that the tribal judge issue subpoenas, but he did not waive his right to do so. The tribal judge and Leno-Baker both advised Selam of his right to compel witnesses, but the record shows no indication that Selam was informed of the consequences of failing to act. *See* *United States v. Mendoza*, 988 F.2d at 1481 (holding that a defendant must be given a meaningful opportunity to exercise his right to compulsory process).
