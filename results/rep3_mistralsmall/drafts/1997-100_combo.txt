**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The California Supreme Court erred in concluding otherwise. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against repeated prosecutions for the same offense, *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 173 (1873), and this protection extends to sentencing proceedings where the State seeks to enhance punishment based on prior convictions. *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919); *United States v. Benz*, 282 U.S. 304, 307 (1931).

The Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal or conviction. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187 (1957). This principle applies with equal force to sentencing proceedings where the State seeks to relitigate a prior conviction allegation. In *Stroud*, the Supreme Court held that a defendant could not be retried on a prior conviction allegation after the trial court had found the allegation not proven. 251 U.S. at 18. The Court reasoned that the Double Jeopardy Clause barred retrial because the prior conviction allegation was an "offense" within the meaning of the Clause. *Id.* at 18-19.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. *Williams* held that a sentencing court could consider untested hearsay evidence in imposing a sentence, but it did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. *Id.* at 246-47. The Supreme Court has since clarified that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing proceedings where the State seeks to enhance punishment based on prior convictions. *Benz*, 282 U.S. at 307.

The California Supreme Court’s conclusion that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s precedents. In *Benz*, the Supreme Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause barred retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding. 282 U.S. at 307. The Court reasoned that the prior conviction allegation was an "offense" within the meaning of the Clause, and that retrial would violate the Clause’s prohibition against double jeopardy. *Id.*

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605 (1967), is also misplaced. *Specht* held that the Due Process Clause did not require a formal sentencing hearing, but it did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. *Id.* at 608-09. The Supreme Court has since held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing proceedings where the State seeks to enhance punishment based on prior convictions. *Benz*, 282 U.S. at 307.

The California Supreme Court’s conclusion that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s precedents and should be reversed. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings, and the California Court of Appeal correctly so held.
