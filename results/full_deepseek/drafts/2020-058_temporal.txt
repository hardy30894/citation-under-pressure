## Argument

The decision below errs by divorcing the plain text of the Temporary Protected Status statute from its statutory context. Congress expressly provided that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). Because a nonimmigrant is, by definition, an alien who has been “admitted” into the United States, 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15), the legal fiction mandated by § 1254a(f)(4) necessarily carries with it the element of admission. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding violates the plain meaning of the statute and cannot be squared with settled canons of construction.

### I. The Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Requires That TPS Recipients Be Treated as Admitted

The INA defines “admission” and “admitted” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). A “nonimmigrant” is defined as “an alien who is admitted into the United States” for a temporary period. 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15). When Congress uses a defined term, it “must be taken to have used the words in the sense in which they are defined.” *United States v. Dickerson*, 310 U.S. 554, 559 (1940). By deeming a TPS recipient to be “in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” § 1254a(f)(4) necessarily deems that alien to have been admitted. The Third Circuit’s attempt to separate “status” from “admission” ignores that the two concepts are conjoined in the definition of nonimmigrant. A nonimmigrant is not merely an alien with a certain status; he is an alien who has been admitted. The statute’s “considered as” language creates a conclusive legal fiction, and courts must give full effect to that fiction. *See, e.g., United States v. Allen*, 317 U.S. 602, 606 (1943) (deeming clause “creates a legal fiction” that must be applied “for all purposes” of the statute). The Third Circuit effectively read the word “admitted” out of the definition of nonimmigrant when applied to TPS recipients, a result Congress could not have intended.

### II. The Third Circuit’s Interpretation Renders § 1254a(f)(4) Superfluous

If, as the Third Circuit held, a grant of TPS does not constitute an admission, then § 1254a(f)(4) is meaningless for the very aliens it was designed to benefit: those who entered the United States without inspection. Such aliens, like petitioners, are ineligible for adjustment of status under § 1255(a) unless they can satisfy the threshold requirement of being “inspected and admitted or paroled.” After the Third Circuit’s decision, a TPS recipient who entered without inspection can never satisfy that requirement, no matter how long he has maintained lawful status. The statutory provision that he “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” would then be wholly inoperative in the adjustment-of-status context. This violates the canon that a statute should be construed so that “no clause, sentence, or word shall be superfluous, void, or insignificant.” *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955) (internal quotation marks omitted). Congress plainly intended § 1254a(f)(4) to have operative effect; the only way to achieve that is to treat the legal fiction of nonimmigrant status as including the element of admission.

### III. The Legislative Purpose Supports the Petitioner’s Reading

The TPS statute was enacted to provide temporary protection to aliens from countries experiencing armed conflict or environmental disasters. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). By its terms, it is a humanitarian measure. Congress also provided a path to adjustment of status for TPS recipients in § 1254a(f)(4). The Third Circuit’s interpretation closes that path for the very aliens who most need it—those who entered illegally and thus cannot return to their home countries to obtain a visa. This Court has long held that “the terms of a statute should be liberally construed to effectuate the purposes of the Congress.” *E.g., United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). More specifically, in the immigration context, “ambiguity in a deportation statute should be resolved in favor of the alien.” *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948). That principle applies with equal force here, where the statute governs eligibility for a benefit rather than deportation. The Third Circuit’s narrow construction undermines the humanitarian purpose of TPS and creates an anomalous result: TPS recipients who entered lawfully may adjust status, but those who entered illegally—and who are most likely to have fled dangerous conditions without documentation—are permanently barred. Congress could not have intended such a perverse outcome.

### IV. The Third Circuit’s Structural Argument Is Unpersuasive

The court below relied on the fact that Congress created exceptions to the admission requirement in other subsections of § 1255, but not for TPS recipients. See 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i). That argument proves too much. Congress did not need to create an express exception for TPS recipients because it accomplished the same result through the legal fiction in § 1254a(f)(4). The canon *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* “has force only when the items expressed are members of an associated group or series.” *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 65 (2002) (citing pre-1970 cases). Here, the group of aliens excepted from the admission requirement by § 1255(h) and (i) are “special immigrants” and those with certain visa petitions. TPS recipients are not part of that group; they are addressed by a separate, cross-referencing statute. The fact that Congress did not repeat the exception in § 1255 is irrelevant because § 1254a(f)(4) itself provides the necessary equivalence.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
