# ARGUMENT

## I. THE FEDERAL PATENT ISSUE EMBEDDED IN MINTON'S MALPRACTICE CLAIM DOES NOT SATISFY THE *GRABLE* TEST FOR "ARISING UNDER" JURISDICTION

Federal courts possess jurisdiction over civil actions "arising under" federal patent law. 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). This Court has long recognized, however, that such jurisdiction extends beyond cases where federal law creates the cause of action to certain state-law claims that "necessarily raise a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." *Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Manufacturing*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). This four-part test—necessity, actual dispute, substantiality, and preservation of the federal-state balance—must all be satisfied. *Id.* Minton's malpractice claim fails at least the substantiality and balance-of-responsibilities prongs, and the Federal Circuit's contrary approach cannot be squared with this Court's precedent.

### A. This Court's Precedent Requires a Case-Specific Assessment of Substantiality, Not a Categorical Rule for Malpractice Claims Involving Patents

This Court has consistently rejected bright-line jurisdictional rules in favor of a "selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936). A federal issue is not substantial merely because it must be resolved to decide the case; rather, courts must assess whether the issue is significant "to the federal system as a whole." *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313. In *Grable*, the dispositive federal issue—the proper interpretation of an IRS notice statute—was important to the federal tax system generally, not merely to the parties before the court. *Id.* at 315.

By contrast, the "substantiality" inquiry in *Grable* asks whether "the Government has a direct interest in the availability of a federal forum," *id.* at 315, and whether resolution of the issue would "be controlling in numerous other cases," *id.* Minton's case presents nothing of the sort. The application of the experimental-use exception to the on-sale bar, 35 U.S.C. § 102(b), turns entirely on case-specific historical facts—what Minton subjectively intended when leasing TEXCEN to Stark in 1995. This retrospective, fact-bound inquiry into a single, long-since-expired patent has no bearing on the "validity, construction, or effect" of federal patent law prospectively. *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). No future litigant's rights, no PTO practice, and no other patent's validity depends on how a Texas jury resolves whether Gunn's malpractice caused Minton's loss.

### B. Exercising Jurisdiction Here Would Disturb the Congressionally Approved Balance Between State and Federal Courts

*Grable* itself cautioned that federal jurisdiction over state-law claims with embedded federal issues must not disrupt the "welcome mat" states traditionally extend to state-law causes of action, particularly where allowing federal jurisdiction would trigger "a potentially enormous number of cases." 545 U.S. at 318–19. Legal malpractice is a quintessential state-law tort, traditionally and exclusively the province of state courts, which possess particular competence in regulating the state bar and adjudicating professional negligence. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Associates*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). Unlike *Grable*, where "it will be the rare state title case that raises a contested matter of federal law," 545 U.S. at 319, patent-related malpractice claims are not rare—patent litigation is common, and malpractice claims inevitably follow adverse outcomes. Sweeping every such case into federal court would work exactly the disruption *Grable* warned against: converting a garden-variety state tort into a federal case based solely on the happenstance that the underlying representation involved a patent.

### C. The Federal Circuit's *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept* Line of Cases Cannot Be Reconciled With *Grable*

The Federal Circuit's decisions in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), held that whenever "establishing patent infringement is a necessary element of a malpractice claim stemming from alleged mishandling of ... earlier patent litigation, the issue is substantial and contested, and federal resolution of the issue was intended by Congress." *Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1273. This formulation impermissibly collapses *Grable*'s four independent requirements into the single "necessity" element, treating any necessary patent question as automatically substantial. Later Federal Circuit decisions abandoned even the pretense of a *Grable* analysis. *See Davis v. Brouse McDowell, L.P.A.*, 596 F.3d 1355 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (no citation to *Grable*); *Warrior Sports, Inc. v. Dickinson Wright, P.L.L.C.*, 631 F.3d 1367 (Fed. Cir. 2011) (same).

This Court's decision in *Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800 (1988), does not support the Federal Circuit's categorical approach. *Christianson* held that § 1338(a) jurisdiction attaches where "the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law," *id.* at 809 (emphasis added), expressly requiring substantiality as a distinct inquiry from necessity. The Federal Circuit's malpractice cases read the substantiality requirement out of *Christianson* and *Grable* alike.

Numerous courts confronting this question directly have rejected the Federal Circuit's reasoning. *See, e.g., New Tek Manufacturing, Inc. v. Beehner*, 702 N.W.2d 336 (Neb. 2005) ("[w]hen patent issues are merely implicated incidentally in a cause of action ... federal courts do not have jurisdiction"); *Roof Technical Services, Inc. v. Hill*, 679 F. Supp. 2d 749, 753–54 (N.D. Tex. 2010) (holding that resolution of embedded patent issues in malpractice suit "will not create or destroy any patent rights such that uniformity in the way patents are issued or enforced will be threatened").

## II. THE PATENT ISSUE IN THIS MALPRACTICE ACTION IS HYPOTHETICAL AND BACKWARD-LOOKING, FURTHER UNDERMINING ANY CLAIM OF SUBSTANTIALITY

The federal patent question here is doubly attenuated: Minton must show not merely that the experimental-use exception applies, but that it *would have* applied in hypothetical litigation that never fully occurred, in a case already conclusively resolved on other grounds by the Federal Circuit. *See Minton v. National Association of Securities Dealers, Inc.*, 336 F.3d 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2003). No patent right is at stake; the '643 patent's invalidity is already final. This purely retrospective, hypothetical application of patent doctrine to establish attorney causation lacks the prospective significance to the patent system that *Grable* requires for exclusive federal jurisdiction. 545 U.S. at 315.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that federal courts do not possess exclusive jurisdiction over state legal malpractice claims merely because they require application of patent law principles, and should affirm that such jurisdiction depends on the case-specific *Grable* factors, none of which are satisfied here.
