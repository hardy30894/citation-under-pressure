The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case, holding that an uncounseled conviction in tribal court cannot be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), is erroneous. The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, and the use of prior uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) does not violate the Constitution.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir.1989), is misplaced. Ant was decided before the Supreme Court's decision in Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), which held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction can be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, as long as the conviction did not violate the Sixth Amendment. Nichols, 511 U.S. at 746-47. The Court in Nichols reasoned that the sentencing scheme "accommodated" its reliability concerns because a defendant could challenge the reliability of the prior conviction at sentencing. Id. at 747-48.

The same logic applies here. Although § 117(a) uses prior convictions as an element of the offense, rather than as a sentencing enhancement, the reliability concerns are still mitigated by the fact that the prior convictions were validly obtained under tribal law. See Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (noting that the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes). The Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) provides some protections for defendants in tribal court, and Congress has taken steps to expand the right to counsel in tribal courts. See 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)-(c).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently held that recidivist statutes, like § 117(a), "penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant." Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747; see also United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008). The punishment is for the current offense, not the prior convictions. Alabama v. Shelton, 535 U.S. 654, 661-62 (2002), is instructive, as it held that a suspended sentence that was not actually imposed did not render the underlying conviction invalid. Similarly, Bryant's prior uncounseled tribal court convictions are valid, even if they were not accompanied by counsel.

The Ninth Circuit's decision creates a circuit split, as the Eighth and Tenth Circuits have held that prior uncounseled tribal court convictions can be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a). See United States v. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir.2011); United States v. Shavanaux, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir.2011). The decision also undermines the purpose of § 117(a), which is to provide felony-level punishment for serial domestic violence offenders. See United States v. Lara, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004) (describing Congress's plenary power to pass legislation affecting Indian tribes).

In Wilson v. Marchington, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir.1997), this Court extended comity to tribal court judgments, noting that "federal courts must also be careful to respect tribal jurisprudence along with the special customs and practical limitations of tribal court systems." The Ninth Circuit's decision disregards this principle of comity and intrudes on tribal self-governance. See Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978).

The use of prior uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) is consistent with Supreme Court precedent and does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
