Ohio’s clemency process creates no protected life or liberty interest cognizable under the Due Process Clause, and the voluntary interview option does not compel self-incrimination within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment. Before 1970, this Court had already made clear that clemency is an act of grace, not a judicially enforceable entitlement, and that the privilege against self-incrimination is violated only by compelled testimony.

First, there is no protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings themselves. The Constitution does not create a substantive entitlement to executive mercy. In *United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy*, 347 U.S. 260 (1954), the Court recognized that due process may be implicated where an agency fails to follow rules governing adjudication, but that principle rests on the existence of enforceable procedures attached to a legal status. Clemency is different. Long before 1970, the Court repeatedly treated pardon and commutation as matters committed to executive discretion rather than judicially protected rights. See *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380-81 (1866) (describing the pardon power as extending to “every offence known to the law” and restoring civil rights as an executive act); *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927) (“A pardon in our days is not a private act of grace from an individual happening to possess power.”). Those decisions confirm that clemency does not vest the inmate with a protected claim to favorable action, much less a constitutionally protected liberty interest in the process itself.

The same conclusion follows from the foundational due process cases. Due process protects persons against deprivation of life or liberty by law, but it does not transform every governmental consideration of mercy into an adjudication of rights. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 370 (1886), and *Truax v. Corrigan*, 257 U.S. 312, 332 (1921), involved governmental action affecting settled rights and status; clemency is not of that character. Nor does the Constitution require due process protections for every executive judgment affecting punishment. See *Biddle*, 274 U.S. at 486; *Ex parte Grossman*, 267 U.S. 87, 120-21 (1925) (recognizing the breadth of the presidential pardon power). A clemency proceeding is therefore not an additional adjudicative stage in which a prisoner acquires a new liberty interest.

Second, the option to participate voluntarily in a clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment. The privilege against self-incrimination protects against compulsion. *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892), held that the privilege prevents the government from forcing a person to give testimony that may incriminate him. But where the person may remain silent, there is no compulsion. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 92-93 (1908), recognized that the privilege is a protection against being “compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” And in *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 597-98 (1896), the Court explained that the constitutional objection lies in compulsion, not in the mere existence of an opportunity to speak.

Ohio’s procedure presents no compulsion. The prisoner may request an interview, but he need not do so. If he declines, he suffers no constitutional penalty. That is the opposite of the coercive conditions condemned in cases where the government forced a choice between self-incrimination and punishment. See *Garrity v. New Jersey*, 385 U.S. 493, 497-98 (1967) (invalidating compelled statements under threat of job loss); *Spevack v. Klein*, 385 U.S. 511, 516 (1967) (disbarring a lawyer for invoking the privilege). Ohio does not require the inmate to answer questions, and a voluntary opportunity to seek mercy is not the kind of coercion those cases prohibit.

Nor does the possibility that a prisoner might choose to speak, and later regret what he said, create a Fifth Amendment violation. The privilege bars compulsion, not all strategic choices that may have consequences. In *Heike v. United States*, 227 U.S. 131, 144-45 (1913), the Court emphasized that the Amendment is concerned with “compulsion of testimony” and not with every adverse result that may follow from voluntary disclosures. Likewise, *Wilson v. United States*, 221 U.S. 361, 382-84 (1911), distinguished compelled testimony from voluntary action. A voluntary clemency interview is just that: voluntary.

Finally, petitioners’ due process and Fifth Amendment theories cannot be bootstrapped from the mere gravity of a death sentence. The constitutional question is not whether clemency is important, but whether Ohio has converted mercy into a right or coerced testimony from the inmate. Pre-1970 precedent says no on both counts. Ohio may offer a forum for mercy without creating a protected entitlement to clemency, and it may invite a prisoner to speak without compelling him to do so. Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed.
