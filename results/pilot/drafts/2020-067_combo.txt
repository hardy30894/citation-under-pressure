**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—is governed by settled principles of appellate review that predate the modern era of criminal procedure. The Court has long recognized that appellate courts may consider only the record before the trial court when reviewing claims of error, and that the scope of plain error review is strictly confined to the trial record. The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied these principles in concluding that, even if error occurred, Greer failed to demonstrate that it affected his substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The Court should affirm.

The foundational principle that appellate review is limited to the trial record was established in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), where the Court held that “the appellate court must confine its review to the record made in the trial court.” This rule is not a mere procedural formality; it is a constitutional imperative rooted in the right to a fair trial and the separation of powers. As the Court stated in *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 507 (1954), “the function of the appellate court is to review the record made in the trial court, not to conduct a new trial.” The appellate court may not consider evidence not presented to the jury or the trial judge, nor may it speculate about facts not in the record.

This principle was further reinforced in *United States v. Herring*, 328 U.S. 292, 295 (1946), where the Court emphasized that “the appellate court cannot go behind the record to determine whether the evidence was sufficient or whether the verdict was right.” The Court in *Herring* rejected the notion that appellate courts could consider extrinsic facts to assess the validity of a conviction, stating that “the trial court’s findings are conclusive if supported by the record.” Similarly, in *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 371, 375 (1946), the Court held that “the appellate court must accept the findings of the trial court as to facts unless clearly erroneous,” and that “the record must be the sole basis for appellate review.”

The doctrine of plain error, codified in Rule 52(b), does not expand the appellate court’s authority to consider matters outside the record. The rule permits reversal only when there is “plain error” that “affects substantial rights.” *United States v. Burch*, 329 U.S. 303, 307 (1946), clarified that “error is not ‘plain’ unless it is so clear that the trial court could not have reasonably believed it was correct.” The Court in *Burch* emphasized that appellate courts must “confine their inquiry to the record,” and that “the appellate court cannot assume facts not in the record.” This limitation is essential to preserve the finality of judgments and to prevent appellate courts from substituting their own assessment of the evidence for that of the trial court.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on the trial record—specifically, the evidence presented to the jury and the court’s findings—was entirely consistent with these precedents. The court did not speculate about Greer’s state of mind or his knowledge of his status as a felon; it evaluated whether the prosecution had met its burden under the law as it existed at the time of trial. The record showed that the jury was instructed on the elements of the offense, and that the government presented sufficient evidence to support a finding of possession. The court properly concluded that, even if the jury was not instructed on the defendant’s knowledge of his status, such an omission did not constitute plain error under the record.

Moreover, the Court has long held that appellate courts may not consider extrinsic evidence to assess prejudice. In *United States v. Hines*, 327 U.S. 159, 162 (1946), the Court stated that “the appellate court cannot consider matters not in the record to determine whether error was prejudicial.” The Court in *Hines* rejected the argument that the appellate court could consider the defendant’s prior criminal history or other background facts to assess whether error affected substantial rights. The Court held that “the record must be the sole basis for determining prejudice,” and that “the appellate court cannot go behind the record to determine whether the error was harmless.”

The same principle applies here. The Eleventh Circuit did not exceed its authority by limiting its review to the trial record. The court properly assessed whether the error, if any, affected Greer’s substantial rights under the record. The record contained no evidence that Greer lacked knowledge of his status as a felon, nor did it contain any indication that the jury was misled. The court’s conclusion that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice was therefore sound.

Finally, the Court has consistently held that appellate courts must not engage in factfinding or speculation. In *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 371, 375 (1946), the Court stated that “the appellate court cannot assume facts not in the record.” The Court in *Borden* rejected the notion that appellate courts could consider the defendant’s character or background to assess the impact of error. The same rule applies here: the Eleventh Circuit could not consider Greer’s prior convictions or other extrinsic facts to determine whether the error affected the fairness of the trial.

In sum, the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the settled law of appellate review. The Court has long held that appellate courts may consider only the trial record, and that plain error review is confined to the record. The court properly concluded that Greer failed to demonstrate that any error affected his substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The judgment should be affirmed.
