**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Language of § 523(a)(2)(A) Limits Nondischargeability to the Value “Obtained by” Fraud**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt . . . for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by . . . actual fraud.” The critical phrase—“to the extent obtained by”—unambiguously ties nondischargeability to the value the debtor actually received through fraud. This Court has long instructed that exceptions to discharge must be strictly construed against the creditor and in favor of the debtor. *Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915). The statutory text does not sweep in punitive or treble damages, attorney’s fees, or other penalties that are not “obtained by” the debtor from the victim.

The Third Circuit erred by conflating the “debt” with the “extent obtained.” The debt here includes treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, but those additional amounts were never “obtained by” Cohen from his tenants. He obtained only the overcharged rent—$31,382.50. The treble damages are a statutory penalty imposed by state law, not property or money that Cohen fraudulently induced the tenants to part with. *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir. 1991) (holding that punitive damages are dischargeable because they are not “obtained by” fraud). The plain language of § 523(a)(2)(A) therefore renders only the actual overcharges nondischargeable.

**II. Treble Damages and Attorney’s Fees Are Not “Obtained by” Fraud**

The Third Circuit adopted the reasoning of *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), which held that the term “debt” is broad enough to encompass punitive damages. But *St. Laurent* ignored the limiting phrase “to the extent obtained by.” The debt is nondischargeable only to the extent it was obtained by fraud. A punitive award is not obtained by the debtor from the creditor; it is imposed by a court to punish misconduct. *Levy*, 951 F.2d at 198. The distinction is critical: the debtor’s fraud causes the creditor to part with money or property, but the punitive award is a separate legal consequence that does not represent anything the debtor “obtained.”

This Court has recognized that the Bankruptcy Code’s “fresh start” policy is reserved for the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991). But that policy does not justify expanding exceptions beyond their statutory text. Congress carefully limited the fraud exception to the value obtained, and courts must respect that limitation. *Brown v. Felsen*, 442 U.S. 127, 136 (1979) (exceptions to discharge should be confined to those plainly expressed).

**III. The “Fresh Start” Policy Requires Narrow Construction**

The Bankruptcy Code’s central purpose is to give debtors a fresh start, unencumbered by pre-petition debts. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). Exceptions to discharge are to be narrowly construed to effectuate that purpose. *Gleason*, 236 U.S. at 562. The Third Circuit’s decision undermines this policy by rendering nondischargeable amounts far exceeding what the debtor actually took. A debtor who fraudulently obtains $30,000 should not face a nondischargeable debt of $90,000 in treble damages plus fees. That result punishes the debtor beyond the scope of the fraud exception Congress enacted.

The *St. Laurent* court’s reliance on the debtor’s “dishonesty” to justify expanding the exception is misplaced. The statute already denies discharge to the full value obtained by fraud. Adding penalties does not serve the statutory purpose; it transforms an exception to discharge into a punitive device. This Court should reject that approach.

**IV. The Third Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with the Statutory Scheme**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) is part of a comprehensive scheme that lists specific exceptions. If Congress intended to except punitive damages from discharge, it could have said so explicitly, as it did for other categories. *See, e.g.*, § 523(a)(5) (domestic support obligations); § 523(a)(9) (death or injury caused by drunk driving). The absence of any mention of punitive damages confirms that they are dischargeable. *Levy*, 951 F.2d at 199.

The judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to limit nondischargeability to the actual overcharges obtained by fraud.
