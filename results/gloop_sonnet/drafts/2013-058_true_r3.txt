ARGUMENT

I. The Exemption for "Retirement Funds" Turns on the Present Character of the Account, Not Its Origin

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code exempt "retirement funds" from the claims of creditors. That exemption, like every exemption Congress has written into the Code, protects a defined category of property because of what the property presently is and does—not because of what it once was in someone else's hands. This Court has consistently held that entitlement to a bankruptcy exemption depends on the debtor's own relationship to the property at the time the statutory conditions must be satisfied. In Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622 (1943), the Court examined whether a debtor's spouse could claim a homestead exemption, focusing on when the homestead declaration was filed and whether the property functioned as the claimant's home at the relevant time—not merely on the property's historical use by the couple.

The inherited IRA here fails that test. By the time the Clarks filed their petition, the account no longer functioned as retirement savings for anyone. The Seventh Circuit correctly recognized below that an inherited IRA of this kind represents an opportunity for current consumption, not a fund of retirement savings, because federal tax law compels distribution of the entire balance within a fixed, short period that bears no relation to the beneficiary's retirement.

Separately, this Court's decision in Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294 (1903), confirms that exemption analysis must attend to the actual character of the property claimed. There the Court explained that erroneously treating property as part of the exempt category is especially harmful because "the inconvenience would be irremediable, since it would compel the administration of the exempt property as part of the estate in bankruptcy." The inverse harm is equally real here: erroneously exempting an inherited IRA—an account that must be drained within five years and that generates ordinary taxable income upon withdrawal rather than deferred retirement growth—would shield a pool of freely spendable cash from creditors who are otherwise entitled to reach it.

II. Bankruptcy Exemptions and Related Statutory Categories Are Construed According to Substance and Purpose, Not Labels

This Court has repeatedly refused to let a statutory label control where the underlying economic reality diverges from the purpose the label is meant to signal. In Joint Industry Board of Electrical Industry v. United States, 391 U.S. 224 (1968), the Court held, discussing United States v. Embassy Restaurant, Inc., that a claim to a "welfare fund" "was not entitled to § 64a (2) priority because payments to such a welfare fund did not satisfy the manifest purpose of the priority." The fund in that case carried the label and trappings of an employee-benefit account, yet the Court looked past the label to ask whether the arrangement actually served the statute's purpose. The same logic controls here. The inherited IRA carries the label "IRA," but the label does not satisfy the purpose behind §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)—shielding funds set aside for retirement, not funds that federal tax law affirmatively requires be distributed and consumed within a fixed, short period regardless of the recipient's age or proximity to retirement.

This Court's decision in Kaufman v. Tredway, 195 U.S. 271 (1904), likewise instructs that Congress conditions favorable bankruptcy treatment on the genuine presence of the purpose the statute is designed to serve, not on the formal characteristics of a transaction. There, in construing the preference provisions, the Court explained that "the purpose was that the property which passed from the creditor should in fact become a part of the debtor's estate, and that the credit should be only for such property," rejecting arrangements that satisfied the letter of the statute while defeating its object. So too here: an inherited IRA satisfies the letter of the Internal Revenue Code's IRA provisions, but it does not serve the object of the Bankruptcy Code's retirement exemption, because the beneficiary cannot hold the funds until retirement even if she wished to.

Similarly, in McKee v. Paradise, 299 U.S. 119 (1936), this Court recognized that a fund's claim to special treatment in bankruptcy depends on whether it can still be traced and identified as bearing the character entitling it to that treatment, not merely on its remote origin in a trust relationship. Once Ruth Heffron's IRA passed to her daughter, it lost the identifying attributes of a retirement account—no further contributions, no discretion to defer withdrawal past five years, no connection to the beneficiary's own working life—just as surely as commingled funds may lose a traceable trust character.

This Court's decisions in Bruning v. United States, 376 U.S. 358 (1964), and Boteler v. Ingels, 308 U.S. 57 (1939), confirm that provisions relieving debtors of ordinary obligations are not stretched beyond their text merely because a broader reading would further the general policy of debtor relief. Bruning declined to extend the discharge to post-petition tax interest despite the Bankruptcy Act's general solicitude for debtors, and Boteler likewise construed statutory relief according to its terms rather than an asserted underlying purpose. Whatever savings incentive might be served by exempting inherited IRAs, that policy does not license an exemption for assets that are not, in the debtor's own hands, retirement funds at all.

Finally, in Bronx Brass Foundry, Inc. v. Irving Trust Co., 297 U.S. 230 (1936), this Court confirmed that a bankruptcy court's classification of a claim must rest on the actual facts developed in the proceeding rather than on the parties' own characterization of the transaction. The same principle governs here: whether an account qualifies as "retirement funds" depends on the account's actual attributes at the time of bankruptcy, not on the fact that it was once labeled a retirement account in a predecessor's hands.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
