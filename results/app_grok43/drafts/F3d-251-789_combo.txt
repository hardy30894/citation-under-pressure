The district court erred in concluding that appellant consented to the permanent removal and retention of her child. Consent to the surrender of parental custody must be established by clear and unequivocal evidence. May v. Anderson, 345 U.S. 528, 536 (1953). The record shows only a conditional arrangement under which appellee was to assist appellant’s immigration before custody would shift.

Parental rights are fundamental and may not be terminated by informal or ambiguous agreements. Meyer v. Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390, 399 (1923); Pierce v. Society of Sisters, 268 U.S. 510, 534-535 (1925). Appellant’s delivery of travel documents and accompaniment to the airport reflected temporary permission, not relinquishment. Such acts do not constitute the voluntary and intelligent waiver required to divest a parent of custody. Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158, 166 (1944).

Full faith and credit principles governing custody decrees further require that any purported consent be free from duress or misunderstanding. New York ex rel. Halvey v. Halvey, 330 U.S. 610, 614 (1947); Estin v. Estin, 334 U.S. 541, 546 (1948). The evidence demonstrates that appellant repeatedly sought the child’s return once appellee failed to fulfill the immigration condition, negating any inference of permanent consent.

Modification of custody arrangements demands a substantial change in circumstances shown by competent proof. Kovacs v. Brewer, 356 U.S. 604, 607 (1958). No such showing appears here; the district court instead credited conflicting accounts without requiring appellee to meet his burden. Ford v. Ford, 371 U.S. 187, 192 (1962). Likewise, a parent’s temporary entrustment of a child to the other parent for a limited purpose does not effect a change in legal custody. Williams v. North Carolina, 325 U.S. 226, 229 (1945).

Because the findings rest on an erroneous application of these settled principles to the undisputed facts, the judgment must be reversed.
