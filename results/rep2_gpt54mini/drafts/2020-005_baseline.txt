Ford’s proposed rule would collapse specific jurisdiction into a strict causation requirement that this Court has repeatedly rejected. The Due Process Clause permits a state to exercise specific jurisdiction when the defendant has deliberately created forum contacts and the litigation “arise[s] out of or relate[s] to” those contacts. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316-17 (1945); *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472-76 (1985). The Constitution does not require the plaintiff’s claim to be caused by the defendant’s forum contacts in the but-for sense. To the contrary, “arise out of or relate to” is disjunctive, and the “relate to” language preserves jurisdiction where the defendant’s forum conduct and the plaintiff’s claim have a substantial connection, even if the specific product at issue was designed, manufactured, or first sold elsewhere. *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court*, 141 S. Ct. 1017, 1026-30 (2021).

That is exactly this case. Ford “systematically served” the Montana market by advertising, selling, and supporting the same model of vehicle in the State. *Ford Motor Co.*, 141 S. Ct. at 1028-30. Gullett was a Montana resident; the crash and death occurred in Montana; and Ford had extensively cultivated that market for the very vehicle involved. Id. at 1022-23, 1028-30. On those facts, Montana courts may exercise jurisdiction because Ford’s Montana conduct “relate[s] to” the claims and makes litigation there fundamentally fair. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297-98 (1980); *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 476-77.

Ford’s contrary argument is foreclosed by *Ford Motor Co.* itself. There, the Court rejected Ford’s contention that specific jurisdiction exists only if the forum contact “caused” the injury-producing product to enter the forum. 141 S. Ct. at 1026-30. The Court explained that a strict causal rule would “relocate” the “relate to” clause out of the Due Process analysis. Id. at 1026-27. It held instead that where the defendant has “purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State,” and the suit has a sufficient connection to those activities, jurisdiction is proper. *Id.* at 1024-25 (quoting *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958)); *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 774-75 (1984).

The Court’s earlier precedent points the same way. In *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, the Court rejected jurisdiction because the nonresident plaintiffs’ claims lacked a forum connection; the defendant’s California conduct did not relate to those plaintiffs’ injuries. 582 U.S. 255, 264-67 (2017). But the Court emphasized that specific jurisdiction exists where there is “an affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy.” *Id.* at 262 (quoting *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011)). Here, unlike in *Bristol-Myers*, the forum is the place of the accident, the place of injury, and the place where Ford deliberately served the market for the same product line. *Ford Motor Co.*, 141 S. Ct. at 1028-30.

Ford’s due process objection also ignores the settled fairness factors. Once purposeful availment and forum-relatedness are shown, the defendant must present a “compelling case” that jurisdiction is unreasonable. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 477-78. Ford cannot do so. It is a global manufacturer with nationwide operations, including extensive Montana sales and marketing. Litigating in Montana imposes no constitutionally cognizable burden beyond ordinary interstate defense burdens, which are routinely insufficient to defeat jurisdiction. *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 292, 294; *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 478-79.

In short, the Constitution does not condition specific jurisdiction on a strict causal chain. Where, as here, the defendant has deliberately exploited the forum market for the very product involved and the plaintiff’s claims are closely tied to that forum activity and in-forum injury, due process is satisfied. *Ford Motor Co.*, 141 S. Ct. at 1026-30. The judgment should be affirmed.
