# ARGUMENT

**The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act Adopts State Law Only to Fill Gaps in Federal Law, Not Whenever State Law Touches the Same Subject Matter**

## I. The Text and Structure of OCSLA Establish Federal Law as the Governing Body of Law, With State Law Serving Only a Gap-Filling Function.

Congress enacted OCSLA to resolve a jurisdictional vacuum over the outer Continental Shelf, declaring that "the Constitution and laws and civil and political jurisdiction of the United States" extend to fixed drilling platforms "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). This Court has recognized that "[t]he purpose of the [OCSLA] was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf." *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). That purpose—to *define* a governing body of law—confirms that federal law was intended to be the primary and comprehensive source of substantive rules, with state law borrowed only where federal law is silent.

The "exclusive Federal jurisdiction" language of § 1333(a)(1) is not incidental; it deliberately mirrors the treatment of federal enclaves, where state law is assimilated only through mechanisms like the Assimilative Crimes Act, 18 U.S.C. § 13(a), which applies state law only when the underlying conduct "would be punishable" under state law because no federal enactment addresses it. This Court's decision in *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), upheld that gap-filling model of assimilation precisely because it does not supplant existing federal law but supplements its absence. OCSLA's choice-of-law clause, adopting "the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State" only "[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), imports the same enclave-based logic: state law is not concurrent law but surrogate federal law, triggered only by necessity.

*Rodrigue* itself confirms this gap-dependent structure. There, the Court held that a federal maritime wrongful-death remedy did not apply to deaths on fixed platforms because the platforms were not vessels and the deaths did not arise from "traditional maritime activity." 395 U.S. at 360. Only because no federal remedy existed did the Court conclude that recourse to state wrongful-death law removed "any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]." *Id.* at 366. The necessary premise of that holding is that, had a federal remedy existed, it would have displaced the state analog. State law filled the gap; it did not operate concurrently alongside an existing federal scheme.

This Court's later decision in *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), reinforces the same principle. The Court adopted Louisiana's statute of limitations as OCSLA surrogate law for a personal-injury claim because federal law supplied no analogous limitations period—again, a pure gap-filling exercise, not an inquiry into whether state and federal law were merely "not inconsistent" with one another in the abstract.

## II. The "Applicable and Not Inconsistent" Standard Requires a Significant Void in Federal Law, as the Fifth Circuit Correctly Held in *Continental Oil*.

The Fifth Circuit—which has adjudicated the vast majority of OCSLA disputes—has long recognized that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law only where there is a "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" left open by federal law. *Cont'l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). That formulation gives content to the word "applicable" in § 1333(a)(2)(A): state law is "applicable" only when federal law does not already occupy the relevant field. A contrary reading—permitting state law whenever it is merely "not inconsistent" with federal law—would collapse the two-part statutory test into a single inquiry and render the necessity requirement embedded in OCSLA's history and structure a nullity.

## III. The FLSA Comprehensively Regulates Wage-and-Hour Matters on the OCS, Leaving No Gap for California Law to Fill.

The Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. § 201 *et seq.*, together with its implementing regulations, provides a complete federal scheme governing minimum wages, overtime, and recordkeeping for covered employees, including those on fixed OCS platforms. The FLSA's savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), permits *more* protective state law to operate concurrently with the FLSA in the ordinary domestic context—where the Supremacy Clause otherwise would not displace state police-power regulation. But that clause presupposes a jurisdiction in which state law independently applies; it does not itself supply the affirmative predicate for adopting state law as *federal* law under OCSLA's distinct, gap-dependent choice-of-law provision. Respondent's position effectively imports ordinary preemption analysis into a statute expressly designed to function differently—by borrowing state law only interstitially, as federal enclave law does. Because the FLSA directly and comprehensively addresses the meal-break and overtime subject matter respondent invokes, no "significant void or gap," *Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036, exists for California law to fill.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
