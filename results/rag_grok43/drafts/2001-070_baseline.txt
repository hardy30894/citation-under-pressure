**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of mentally retarded offenders. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), this Court rejected the claim that such executions constitute cruel and unusual punishment. The Court explained that “mental retardation” does not place an offender “beyond the reach of the death penalty” because “retarded persons” are capable of “distinguishing between right and wrong” and “acting on that distinction.” *Id.* at 333, 338. The same conclusion follows here.

Petitioner’s reliance on “evolving standards of decency” does not overcome *Penry*. At the time of that decision, only two States barred execution of the mentally retarded, a number insufficient to demonstrate national consensus. *Id.* at 334. The Court further noted that “mental retardation is a factor that may well lessen a defendant’s culpability,” yet it remains “for the jury to consider” in determining whether death is appropriate. *Id.* at 340. Virginia’s sentencing procedures permit precisely that consideration.

Subsequent decisions confirm that individualized assessment, rather than categorical exemption, satisfies the Eighth Amendment. In *Taylor v. United States*, 511 U.S. 1097 (1994), the Court declined to treat mental retardation as a per se bar. Likewise, *McCollum v. North Carolina*, 512 U.S. 1254 (1994), and *Smith v. Francis*, 474 U.S. 925 (1985), reflect the principle that mental condition is mitigating evidence to be weighed by the sentencer, not a ground for exempting an entire class from capital punishment.

The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Penry* when it affirmed petitioner’s sentence. Because no sufficient change in legislative consensus or constitutional understanding has occurred to warrant departure from that precedent, the judgment should be affirmed.
