ARGUMENT

I. Bryant possesses standing to enforce the arbitration award. Collective bargaining agreements create enforceable rights in favor of individual employees. Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R. Co., 323 U.S. 192, 198 (1944) (recognizing that the union’s statutory authority “is not without limit” and that individuals may vindicate personal rights arising under the contract). An employee may sue to enforce an award rendered pursuant to such an agreement. Smith v. Evening News Ass’n, 371 U.S. 195, 200 (1962). Federal courts possess jurisdiction to compel performance of promises to arbitrate contained in collective agreements. Textile Workers Union v. Lincoln Mills, 353 U.S. 448, 451 (1957). Once an award issues, it is final and binding on the parties. United Steelworkers v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp., 363 U.S. 593, 596 (1960). Bryant, as the grievant and intended beneficiary, therefore has standing to seek judicial enforcement.

II. The district court misapplied the governing standards for summary judgment in actions alleging racial discrimination. Summary judgment may be granted only when the moving party demonstrates the absence of any genuine issue of material fact. Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). All reasonable inferences must be drawn in favor of the nonmoving party. Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). Evidence that an employer singled out an employee on account of race raises a triable question. Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954). The same principle applies when an employer imposes adverse conditions that fall with disproportionate force upon members of a protected class. Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303, 308 (1880). Retaliation for protesting racially disparate treatment likewise states a claim for relief. See Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S. 41, 45-46 (1957) (affording liberal construction to pleadings asserting denial of equal rights).

III. Genuine issues of material fact remain concerning both the arbitration claim and the statutory discrimination and retaliation claims. The record shows that Bryant, an African-American employee with long seniority, received progressively harsher discipline after objecting to the Red Letter Day policy on grounds that it conflicted with his child-care responsibilities, while similarly situated white employees were treated more leniently. These facts, if credited, support the inference of race-based animus and retaliatory motive. Because the evidence permits conflicting inferences, summary judgment was improper. Poller, 368 U.S. at 473; Sartor, 321 U.S. at 627.

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
