California’s challenge fails because the PLRA expressly authorizes a population-reduction order when overcrowding is the primary cause of an Eighth Amendment violation and no narrower remedy will suffice. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)–(ii). The three-judge court found precisely that: serious overcrowding was the “primary cause” of unconstitutional medical and mental-health conditions in California’s prisons, and no other relief would cure the violations. That conclusion fits squarely within the Supreme Court’s recognition that overcrowding can itself be remedial target of equitable relief when it produces unconstitutional conditions. In Rhodes v. Chapman, 452 U.S. 337, 347–49 (1981), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits conditions that deny “the minimal civilized measure of life’s necessities,” and in Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 685–88 (1978), it upheld a comprehensive remedial order addressing overcrowding as part of a prison-conditions violation. Likewise, in Costello v. Wainwright, 430 U.S. 325, 326–27 (1977), the Court recognized that overcrowding litigation may require relief that reduces inmate population or increases capacity.

The PLRA does not forbid that remedy; it channels it. Congress imposed strict findings and procedural safeguards before a federal court may order population reduction, but it did not eliminate the power to do so. See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(A), (E). The statutory standard demands a “clear and convincing evidence” finding that crowding is the primary cause of the violation and that no other relief will remedy it. Id. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)–(ii). Once those findings are made, the statute authorizes a “prisoner release order.” Id. § 3626(a)(3)(A). California’s position would rewrite the statute to ban the very remedy Congress preserved.

That reading is reinforced by Eighth Amendment doctrine. Prison officials must provide humane conditions of confinement, including adequate medical care and reasonable safety. Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825, 832–34 (1994); Wilson v. Seiter, 501 U.S. 294, 298–300 (1991). Where those constitutional minima are violated by systemic overcrowding, a court has ample equitable authority to order structural relief, including population limits. Hutto, 437 U.S. at 687–88. The Court has repeatedly recognized that prison conditions may be so interdependent that only a comprehensive remedy will work. Id. at 687. California’s own prison system exemplifies that principle: the record showed overcrowding driving delays in medical access, inadequate screening, and unsafe housing.

California’s contrary reading would also be inconsistent with the PLRA’s remedial structure. The statute requires that relief be “narrowly drawn,” extend “no further than necessary,” and be the “least intrusive means” to correct the federal right. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). But the three-judge court found, on a full record, that lesser measures had failed for years. When a state has been given repeated opportunities to comply and has not done so, federal courts may enter more comprehensive relief. Hutto, 437 U.S. at 687. The point of § 3626(a)(3) is to permit exactly that extraordinary remedy when the ordinary tools have proved inadequate.

Nor does the requested order offend federalism. Prison administration is ordinarily a state function, but when state prison conditions violate the Constitution, federal courts must enforce the Constitution’s commands. Rhodes, 452 U.S. at 354–55; Farmer, 511 U.S. at 832. The PLRA reflects Congress’s effort to preserve that authority while limiting unnecessary intrusion. It does not strip courts of power to require California to reduce overcrowding where overcrowding itself is the constitutional violation and no lesser remedy will suffice.

Finally, California’s reliance on the magnitude of the order is beside the point. The question under the PLRA is not whether a population-reduction order is drastic; it is whether the statutory findings have been made. See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). Here they were. The order therefore falls within the remedy Congress authorized and the Constitution permits. The judgment should be affirmed.
