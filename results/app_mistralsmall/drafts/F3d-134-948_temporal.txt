**ARGUMENT**

**I. SELAM WAS DENIED HIS RIGHT TO COMPULSORY PROCESS UNDER THE INDIAN CIVIL RIGHTS ACT.**

The Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”) guarantees to all tribal defendants “the right . . . to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6) (1994). This right is coextensive with the Sixth Amendment’s Compulsory Process Clause, *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), and thus must be interpreted consistently with pre-1970 Sixth Amendment jurisprudence. *See* *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 65 (1978) (applying Sixth Amendment standards to ICRA claims).

The Supreme Court has long held that the compulsory process right is violated when a defendant is “denied the opportunity to present witnesses in his own defense.” *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 23 (1967). This right is not absolute, however, and a defendant must take reasonable steps to secure witnesses. *See* *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919) (“The right to compulsory process is not a right to compel the attendance of witnesses at all events, but a right to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses within reasonable limitations.”).

Here, Selam’s counsel requested a list of potential witnesses but failed to assist in securing their appearance. The tribal judge advised Selam of his right to subpoena witnesses, yet Selam never requested one. Under these circumstances, the tribal court did not deny compulsory process; rather, Selam waived his right by failing to take the necessary procedural steps. *See* *People v. Elliott*, 270 P.2d 1021, 1024 (Cal. 1954) (“Where a defendant fails to request a subpoena or otherwise invoke the court’s process, he cannot later complain of a denial of compulsory process.”).

**II. SELAM DID NOT WAIVE HIS CONFRONTATION CLAIM BY FAILING TO RAISE IT IN TRIBAL APPELLATE PROCEEDINGS.**

The district court erred in holding that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by not raising it in tribal appellate proceedings. The ICRA’s confrontation guarantee, like the Sixth Amendment, ensures that a defendant may not be convicted on hearsay evidence without an opportunity to cross-examine adverse witnesses. *Pointer v. Texas*, 380 U.S. 400, 406 (1965).

However, the Supreme Court has recognized that confrontation claims may be forfeited if not properly preserved. *See* *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934) (“A constitutional right may be forfeited in criminal proceedings by the failure to make timely assertion of it.”). But this rule applies only where the defendant had a fair opportunity to raise the claim. *See* *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884) (“A defendant’s failure to object does not waive a constitutional right where the error is fundamental and affects substantial rights.”).

Here, Selam’s confrontation claim was not raised in tribal appellate proceedings because he was unaware of the claim at the time. The record shows that Selam’s appellate counsel did not raise the issue, and there is no indication that Selam was informed of his confrontation rights. Under these circumstances, Selam did not waive his claim. *See* *Moore v. Michigan*, 355 U.S. 155, 161 (1957) (“A defendant cannot be held to have waived a constitutional right where he was not apprised of its existence.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Selam’s compulsory process claim fails because he did not take reasonable steps to secure witnesses, and his confrontation claim was not waived. The district court’s judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
