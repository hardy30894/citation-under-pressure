Argument

I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Erdman's FMLA Claim

The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) entitles eligible employees to take up to 12 weeks of unpaid leave per year for certain family and medical reasons. 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1). To be eligible, an employee must have worked at least 1,250 hours during the 12-month period preceding the start of the leave. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(ii). The District Court granted summary judgment in favor of Nationwide, finding that Erdman did not meet this hours requirement. However, the Court failed to properly account for Erdman's extra hours worked outside the office, which were previously allowed as "comp" time.

The FMLA defines "hours of service" as including "work performed for the employer." 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c). Courts have consistently held that this definition encompasses not only regular working hours but also overtime and other extra hours worked. See, e.g., Plumley v. Southern Container, Inc., 303 F.3d 364, 372 (1st Cir. 2002) (holding that "hours of service" includes "overtime hours actually worked"); see also Harrell v. U.S. Postal Serv., 445 F.3d 913, 919 (7th Cir. 2006) (noting that "hours worked" under the FMLA includes "overtime and other extra hours").

The record shows that Erdman regularly worked extra hours outside the office, which were previously authorized by her supervisors, including Sarno and Getgen. Although Getgen later claimed that she was unaware of Erdman's "comp" time, her failure to respond to Erdman's September 2002 email requesting clarification on this issue, as well as her concession in January 2003 that Erdman's vacation time calculation was correct, suggest that she was, in fact, aware of and acquiesced to Erdman's practice. See, e.g., Kosakow v. New Rochelle Radiology Assoc., 274 F.3d 706, 724 (2d Cir. 2001) (holding that an employer's knowledge of an employee's work hours is relevant to determining whether the employee has met the FMLA's hours requirement).

Furthermore, Nationwide's sudden change in policy regarding "comp" time, as evidenced by Getgen's February 10, 2003 email, raises a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Erdman was discouraged from working extra hours. See, e.g., Stoops v. One Call Commc'ns, Inc., 141 F.3d 309, 312 (7th Cir. 1998) (noting that an employer's actions can be considered "interference" with an employee's FMLA rights if they discourage the employee from taking leave). The District Court's failure to consider this evidence in the light most favorable to Erdman was error. See, e.g., McTernan v. City of York, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009) (noting that summary judgment is only appropriate if there are no genuine issues of material fact).

In light of the disputed facts regarding Erdman's work hours and Nationwide's change in policy, summary judgment was improper. See, e.g., Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) (noting that summary judgment is only appropriate if "the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could not return a verdict for the nonmoving party"). The District Court's grant of summary judgment on Erdman's FMLA claim should be reversed.

II. Erdman's FMLA Retaliation Claim is Supported by the Record

Erdman's FMLA retaliation claim is also supported by the record. To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, Erdman must show that (1) she engaged in protected activity, (2) she suffered an adverse employment action, and (3) there is a causal link between the two. See, e.g., Conoshenti v. Public Serv. Elec. & Gas Co., 364 F.3d 135, 146 (3d Cir. 2004). Erdman's request for FMLA leave on April 21, 2003, constitutes protected activity, and her termination on May 9, 2003, is an adverse employment action. The close temporal proximity between these two events is sufficient to establish a causal link. See, e.g., Farrell v. Planters Lifesavers Co., 206 F.3d 271, 280 (3d Cir. 2000) (noting that "temporal proximity" can be used to establish causation).

Moreover, Nationwide's proffered reasons for terminating Erdman are pretextual. The record shows that Erdman's behavior was not as egregious as Nationwide claims, and that other employees had used similar language without being terminated. See, e.g., Fuentes v. Perskie, 32 F.3d 759, 765 (3d Cir. 1994) (noting that a plaintiff can establish pretext by showing that the employer's proffered reasons are "weak, implausible, inconsistent, incoherent, or contradictory"). The District Court's grant of summary judgment on Erdman's FMLA retaliation claim should be reversed.

In sum, the District Court erred in granting summary judgment on Erdman's FMLA claim. The record raises genuine issues of material fact regarding Erdman's work hours and Nationwide's motives for terminating her. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
