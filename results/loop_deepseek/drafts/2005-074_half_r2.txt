## ARGUMENT

### I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered Before Its Effective Date

The Immigration and Nationality Act’s reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), became effective on April 1, 1997. Petitioner Fernandez-Vargas reentered the United States illegally in 1982—fifteen years before the statute existed. This Court has long held that “the presumption is against retroactive legislation, and a statute is not to be given a retrospective operation unless its words are so clear and positive as to leave no doubt of the intention.” *Union Pacific R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913). Nothing in Section 1231(a)(5)’s text manifests such a clear intent to reach prior reentries. The statute speaks in the present tense—“if the Attorney General finds that an alien has reentered”—and does not specify that it applies to reentries completed before its enactment. This ambiguity must be resolved in favor of the alien, for “deportation is a drastic measure.” *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284 (1922). See also *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698, 730 (1893) (deportation statutes must be strictly construed). Congress easily could have written “who reentered at any time” had it intended retroactive application. It did not. The statute therefore applies only to reentries occurring after its effective date.

### II. Applying the Reinstatement Statute Retroactively Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statute could be read to encompass pre-1997 reentries, such application would attach new legal consequences to past conduct, violating the bedrock presumption against retroactive legislation. A statute is not to be given retroactive effect unless that purpose is clearly expressed. *United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926). Here, no clear expression exists.

When petitioner reentered in 1982, the law permitted him to later apply for adjustment of status if he married a United States citizen. See *Barber v. Gonzales*, 347 U.S. 637, 642 (1954) (recognizing that the law accords the alien a full and fair hearing on his application for suspension of deportation). The reinstatement statute eliminated that possibility entirely, barring any form of relief. This new bar alters the legal consequences of a reentry that was completed long before the statute’s enactment. Such a change is impermissibly retroactive because it impairs the rights a party possessed when he acted. *Union Pacific*, 231 U.S. at 199. Petitioner’s reentry in 1982 carried with it the prospect of eventual adjustment. The 1997 statute stripped that prospect away.

This Court has consistently held that deportation statutes must not be applied retroactively to attach new penalties to past conduct. In *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284 (1922), the Court refused to apply a deportation ground to an alien who had returned to the United States before the ground’s enactment, stating that “deportation is a drastic measure” and that statutes imposing it must be strictly construed. The same principle governs here. The reinstatement statute imposes a new, harsh consequence—foreclosing all relief—on petitioner’s pre-1997 reentry. As *Union Pacific* teaches, the presumption is against retroactive legislation. 231 U.S. at 199.

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that petitioner lacked a protectable expectation of adjusting his status. An alien who reenters illegally does not forfeit all future claims to relief. *See Fong Yue Ting*, 149 U.S. at 730 (the power to deport is a grave one, and statutes must be construed in favor of the alien). The expectation of applying for adjustment under then-existing law was a legitimate right that the government could not retroactively extinguish without clear congressional command. *St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. at 3.

Retroactive application also offends due process by upsetting settled reliance interests. When petitioner returned in 1982, the legal landscape permitted him to later seek adjustment if he married a citizen. The 1997 statute retroactively destroyed that avenue, making his earlier conduct the basis for permanent exclusion from relief. This is precisely the kind of retrospective change the presumption against retroactivity is designed to prevent. *Union Pacific*, 231 U.S. at 199.

For these reasons, Section 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date. Even if it did, applying it retroactively would be impermissible. The petition for review should be granted.
