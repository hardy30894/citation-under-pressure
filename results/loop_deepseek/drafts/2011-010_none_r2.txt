**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision disregards the plain terms of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That provision extends the Longshore Act’s compensation scheme to “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The modifying clause describes where the injury must “occur.” A worker killed in a forklift accident at an onshore processing facility did not sustain an injury “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” because the injury did not occur on the shelf. The situs-of-injury test is therefore compelled by the statutory text.

**I. The plain language of § 1333(b) requires that the injury occur on the shelf.**

This Court has long instructed that where the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, courts must enforce it according to its terms. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). “[I]f the words be plain, and the meaning certain,” the judicial inquiry ends. *United States v. Fisher*, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 396 (1805). The statute’s operative clause modifies “injury” with the phrase “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” That phrase limits the *location* of the injury; the verb “occurring” refers to the place where the injury happens. An injury that happens on land does not occur on the shelf, even if shelf-related operations caused it.

The Legislature’s choice of words is not accidental. OCSLA elsewhere speaks of activities “conducted on” the shelf, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a), or “artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon,” *id.* § 1333(a)(2)(A). When Congress intends to reach beyond a geographic location, it says so explicitly. *Cf. FTC v. Bunte Bros., Inc.*, 312 U.S. 349, 354 (1941) (a statute’s jurisdictional language confines its reach). Section 1333(b), by contrast, repeatedly ties coverage to the shelf itself: the injury results from shelf operations, and the operations must be “conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The most natural reading is that both the operations and the injury must be situated on the shelf.

**II. OCSLA’s territorial scope confirms the situs requirement.**

OCSLA was enacted to assert “paramount rights” of the United States in the submerged lands beyond state limits. *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 29 (1947); *United States v. Texas*, 339 U.S. 707, 718 (1950). The Act is fundamentally a territorial statute, governing rights and liabilities on the shelf. Its workers’ compensation provision is an adjunct to that territorial scheme, extending the Longshore Act to the shelf—not to every facility on shore that happens to support shelf operations. A statute is to be read in light of its evident purpose, *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), and nothing in OCSLA’s purpose supports nationwide coverage for onshore injuries.

Moreover, the LHWCA itself—whose benefits OCSLA incorporates—has always had a situs requirement. *Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 4 (1946) (stating that the LHWCA covers injuries occurring on navigable waters or adjoining areas). OCSLA operates as a situs substitute: for employees injured on the shelf, the shelf itself satisfies the situs element. If the employee is injured on land, he must look to state law, not to OCSLA. *See Skiriotes v. Florida*, 313 U.S. 69, 75 (1941) (recognizing the limits of statutory jurisdiction over offshore areas). Reading OCSLA to cover any injury anywhere would make the Longshore Act’s situs requirement meaningless for offshore workers.

**III. The “substantial nexus” test rewrites the statute.**

The Ninth Circuit adopted a test asking whether there is a “substantial nexus” between the injury and shelf operations. That standard bears no resemblance to the text. The statute does not ask whether an injury is *related* to shelf operations; it asks whether the injury *occurred* as a result of those operations. A “nexus” test severs the connection between the place of injury and the place of operations, reading the word “occurring” out of the statute. That violates the canon that every word is to be given effect. *Packard v. Banton*, 264 U.S. 140, 145 (1924) (courts must construe statutes “according to the import of their language” and not add or subtract words). Congress could have written a status-based provision, but it did not; it wrote a location-based one. *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485.

**IV. Valladolid’s onshore death is outside the statute.**

Valladolid was killed at La Conchita, a land-based facility separated from the shore by a highway and railroad tracks. He was not injured on the Outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it. Accordingly, his death did not “occur” as the result of operations conducted on the shelf, within the meaning of § 1333(b). The court of appeals erred in remanding for an open-ended “substantial nexus” inquiry. The judgment should be reversed.
