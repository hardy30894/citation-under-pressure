The rule governing recovery of an infringer’s profits in design-patent cases has long required apportionment when the patent covers only a component of the article sold. In Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), the Court held that “the burden of proof is upon the complainant” to “separate or apportion the defendant’s profits and the patentee’s damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features.” Id. at 121. Absent such proof, “the entire profit made in the manufacture or sale of the article” cannot be awarded. Ibid.

That principle was applied to design patents in Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885). There the Court reversed an award of the defendant’s entire profits on infringing carpets because the patent covered only the design of the carpet, not the fabric or the process of manufacture. The Court explained that “the plaintiff must show what part of the defendant’s profits was derived from the use of the design,” and that without such evidence “only nominal damages” could be recovered. Id. at 444-45. The same rule was reiterated in Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), where the Court again refused to permit recovery of total profits on an article containing both patented and unpatented elements.

These decisions rest on the foundational requirement, stated in Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. 480 (1853), that patent damages must be limited to the actual injury caused by the infringement. The Court there observed that “the measure of damages” is “the value of the use of the invention,” not the value of the entire machine or article in which the invention is embodied. Id. at 489. When a design patent reaches only a portion of a product, the same limitation applies: the patentee may recover only those profits fairly attributable to the patented design.

The Federal Circuit’s refusal to require any apportionment therefore conflicts with the settled rule that “the patentee must prove what portion of the infringer’s profits is attributable to the patented invention.” Garretson, 111 U.S. at 121. Because Apple offered no evidence separating the profits derived from the patented design elements from those derived from the unpatented components of Samsung’s smartphones, the award of Samsung’s entire profits cannot stand.
