ARGUMENT

The decision below errs in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) does not constitute an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The text, structure, and this Court’s settled precedents compel the opposite conclusion: when the Secretary confers TPS, the alien undergoes a formal inspection and authorization that satisfies the statutory definition of “admission.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A).

**I. The INA’s Definition of “Admission” Encompasses a Governmental Authorization that Need Not Occur at the Border.**

Section 1101(a)(13)(A) defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” This Court has repeatedly held that “entry” is a legal concept, not a purely physical event. *Rosenberg v. Fleuti*, 374 U.S. 449, 453 (1963) (explaining that “entry” depends on the alien’s intent and the significance of the crossing, not merely the act of crossing). An alien who has never physically crossed a border can be “admitted” if the government later inspects and authorizes his presence. *United States v. Spector*, 343 U.S. 169, 174 (1952) (an alien who reenters after a temporary departure retains his prior admission status, showing that admission is a continuing legal status). *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953) (a lawful permanent resident returning from abroad is “entitled to the full panoply of rights due an alien who has been ‘admitted’”). The TPS process—in which the Secretary inspects the alien’s eligibility and authorizes his continued presence—is the functional equivalent of an inspection and authorization at the border. *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 212 (1953) (distinguishing between excluded aliens who have never been admitted and those who have been “admitted” and thus are entitled to procedural protections).

**II. Section 1254a(f)(4) Provides That TPS Recipients Are in “Lawful Status as a Nonimmigrant,” Which Inherently Requires an Admission.**

The statute states that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). Nonimmigrant status under the INA cannot be obtained without an admission. Every nonimmigrant visa holder must be “admitted” upon entry. This Court has long recognized that obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status presupposes a lawful admission. *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy*, 338 U.S. 537, 544 (1950) (the Attorney General’s decision to admit an alien is final and conclusive). Congress’s express command to treat TPS recipients as if they are in nonimmigrant status therefore carries with it the necessary implication that they have been admitted. *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948) (an alien who adjusted to lawful permanent residence was considered admitted for deportation purposes, retroactively curing the original illegal entry). *Delgadillo v. Carmichael*, 332 U.S. 388, 391 (1947) (an alien who was paroled into the country made an “entry” for deportation purposes, showing that later authorization can relate back to the initial physical presence). The Third Circuit’s rigid distinction between “status” and “admission” ignores that the two are inextricably linked: one cannot have nonimmigrant status without having been admitted.

**III. The TPS Grant Itself Satisfies the “Inspection and Authorization” Requirement.**

An alien eligible for TPS must be “present in the United States” and must undergo a rigorous application process in which the government examines his identity, eligibility, and background. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(c)(2). This process constitutes an “inspection” within the meaning of § 1101(a)(13)(A). *Elg v. Perkins*, 307 U.S. 325, 334 (1939) (the government’s determination of a person’s right to enter the United States is an inspection). The subsequent grant of TPS is an “authorization” by an immigration officer to remain lawfully. *Kwong Hai Chew*, 344 U.S. at 596 (authorization to reenter after a trip abroad constitutes an admission). The TPS statute does not require that the inspection occur at the moment of physical crossing; it is sufficient that the government later inspects and authorizes the alien’s continued presence. *Fleuti*, 374 U.S. at 453.

**IV. The Third Circuit’s Interpretation Would Render § 1254a(f)(4) Superfluous and Undermine Congress’s Intent.**

If TPS did not confer an admission, then § 1254a(f)(4) would be meaningless for the many TPS recipients who entered illegally. Congress intended that TPS recipients could “make use of the adjustment-of-status process,” as the title of § 1255 suggests. *Compare* 8 U.S.C. § 1255 (entitled “Adjustment of status of nonimmigrant to that of person admitted for permanent residence”). The Third Circuit’s holding would require TPS recipients to depart the United States and reenter lawfully—a result that contradicts the humanitarian purpose of TPS and is inconsistent with this Court’s teaching that “entry” is a flexible concept. *Fleuti*, 374 U.S. at 453.

**V. The Cases Cited by the Third Circuit Do Not Support Its Holding.**

The court below relied on *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), and *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281 (3d Cir. 2013), but those cases are recent and not binding on this Court. The pre-1970 precedents of this Court uniformly treat the grant of lawful status as an admission when the government has inspected and authorized the alien’s presence. *Elg*, 307 U.S. at 334; *Fong Haw Tan*, 333 U.S. at 10; *Delgadillo*, 332 U.S. at 391. The Third Circuit’s reliance on a distinction between “status” and “admission” is inconsistent with *Spector*, 343 U.S. at 174, and *Kwong Hai Chew*, 344 U.S. at 596.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
