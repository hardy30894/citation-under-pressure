**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN WHICH PETITIONER HAS A DUE PROCESS RIGHT TO PARTICIPATE WITHOUT WAIVING HIS FIFTH AMENDMENT PRIVILEGE AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that petitioner Woodard lacks a constitutionally protected liberty interest in Ohio’s clemency process. This Court has long recognized that clemency proceedings implicate significant liberty interests. In *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal & Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 18 (1979), the Court held that parole release proceedings create a liberty interest protected by the Due Process Clause because they determine whether a prisoner will remain incarcerated or be released. Similarly, clemency proceedings—particularly in capital cases—determine whether a prisoner will live or die. *See Ohio Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 281 (1998) (plurality opinion) ("The clemency power is the historic remedy for preventing miscarriages of justice where a pardon or commutation is warranted.").

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), is misplaced. *Dumschat* did not address whether clemency proceedings implicate a liberty interest; it merely held that Connecticut had not created a protected liberty interest in the *procedures* used by the parole board. *Id.* at 465. This Court has since clarified that liberty interests may arise from state law or practice, even where no explicit entitlement exists. *See Hewitt v. Helms*, 459 U.S. 460, 471-72 (1983) (finding a liberty interest in prison disciplinary proceedings where state regulations created "substantive predicates" for confinement). Ohio’s clemency process, by providing a structured procedure for review, creates a similar liberty interest.

**II. THE OPTION TO PARTICIPATE IN A CLEMENCY INTERVIEW IMPOSES AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL CONDITION ON THE EXERCISE OF PETITIONER’S FIFTH AMENDMENT RIGHT AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that Ohio’s clemency process does not compel a waiver of Fifth Amendment rights. The Fifth Amendment provides that "[n]o person... shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." U.S. Const. amend. V. This right is not limited to formal criminal proceedings; it applies to any situation where a person’s statements may later be used against him in a criminal case. *See Lefkowitz v. Turley*, 414 U.S. 70, 77 (1973) ("The Fifth Amendment’s privilege against self-incrimination protects witnesses in criminal cases and other proceedings... from being compelled to make statements that might be used against them in a criminal prosecution.").

Ohio’s clemency process conditions the opportunity to present mitigating evidence on a prisoner’s willingness to submit to an interview where his statements may be used against him. The regulation at issue provides that "[a]ny statement given by the prisoner at the interview shall be considered at the clemency hearing." Ohio Admin. Code § 5120:1-1-15(C)(2). This creates a direct incentive to waive Fifth Amendment rights, as failure to participate may result in an incomplete or unfavorable clemency review. *See Lefkowitz*, 414 U.S. at 82 ("A State may not impose substantial penalties because a witness elects to exercise his Fifth Amendment right.").

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), is misplaced. *Baxter* involved a prison disciplinary proceeding where the Court held that a prisoner’s silence could be used as evidence of guilt. *Id.* at 318-19. However, *Baxter* did not address whether a state may *compel* a prisoner to waive his Fifth Amendment rights as a condition of participating in a separate proceeding. The Court has since made clear that the Fifth Amendment prohibits such coercion. *See Garrity v. New Jersey*, 385 U.S. 493, 499 (1967) ("The choice given petitioners was either to forfeit their jobs or to incriminate themselves. The option to lose their means of livelihood or to pay the penalty of self-incrimination... was no choice at all.").

**III. THE SIXTH CIRCUIT’S REMEDY—REMAND FOR FURTHER PROCEEDINGS—IS INADEQUATE TO PROTECT PETITIONER’S CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in remanding the case for further proceedings without addressing the constitutional violation. The Due Process Clause requires that clemency proceedings provide meaningful procedural protections, including the right to participate without waiving Fifth Amendment rights. *See Greenholtz*, 442 U.S. at 18. The Sixth Circuit’s remand fails to ensure that Ohio’s clemency process complies with these requirements.

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that petitioner’s Fifth Amendment claim is premature is incorrect. Petitioner has alleged an actual and ongoing injury: the coercive nature of Ohio’s clemency process forces him to choose between asserting his Fifth Amendment rights and presenting mitigating evidence. *See Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 147 (1908) (holding that a plaintiff need not await an unconstitutional act before seeking injunctive relief). This Court should grant the petition for certiorari and reverse the Sixth Circuit’s judgment.
