The Massachusetts statute creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that does not violate the First Amendment. As the First Circuit correctly held, the law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant government interest, and leaves open adequate alternative channels of communication.

The buffer zone is a reasonable measure to ensure public safety and protect the rights of individuals seeking to enter healthcare facilities. The Supreme Court has previously upheld a similar Colorado statute in Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), which prohibited certain activities within 100 feet of healthcare facility entrances. The Court in Hill recognized that sidewalks and areas outside healthcare clinics are "quintessential public forums" but held that the buffer zone was a valid time, place, and manner regulation.

The petitioners argue that the buffer zone restricts their ability to engage in effective "sidewalk counseling." However, as the First Circuit noted, the record shows that the petitioners have achieved some success in their counseling efforts despite the buffer zone. Moreover, the law does not prohibit all forms of communication; it only restricts the location and manner of speech. The petitioners remain free to engage in oral speech, distribute literature, display signage and symbols, wear evocative garments and costumes, and pray alone and in groups.

The petitioners' reliance on recent Supreme Court decisions, such as Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), and Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc., — U.S. -, 131 S.Ct. 2653 (2011), is misplaced. These cases involved content-based and speaker-based speech restrictions, which are not at issue here. The Massachusetts statute is a content-neutral regulation that applies equally to all individuals, regardless of their viewpoint or message.

The law of the case doctrine also supports the First Circuit's decision. The court had previously upheld the facial constitutionality of the statute in McCullen I, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir.2009), and the petitioners have not shown that controlling legal authority has changed dramatically or that significant new evidence has come to light.

The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the petitioners' motion to amend their complaint to include a direct challenge to the Attorney General's guidance letter. The petitioners had ample time to raise this issue earlier, and their delay was inordinate.

In conclusion, the Massachusetts statute is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that balances the rights of individuals to free speech with the need to ensure public safety and protect the rights of those seeking to enter healthcare facilities. The First Circuit's decision should be affirmed.
