## The Plain-Error Inquiry Requires Appellate Courts to Consider the Whole Record, Not Merely the Evidence Presented to the Jury

The plain-error doctrine has never confined reviewing courts to the four corners of the evidence a jury actually heard. Since this Court first articulated the doctrine, it has directed courts to look at the entirety of the proceedings—and the record surrounding them—to determine whether an unpreserved error requires correction. That approach controls here.

### A. The Plain-Error Standard Was Born From, and Requires, Contextual Judgment

The modern plain-error framework traces directly to *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157 (1936), which held that appellate courts may notice unpreserved errors "if the errors are obvious, or if they otherwise seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Id.* at 160. That standard is inherently evaluative and contextual; it asks not whether a discrete trial exhibit was technically deficient, but whether, taking the proceeding as a whole, the outcome can be trusted. A court cannot answer that question by artificially blinding itself to matters of record that bear directly on whether the defendant was actually prejudiced.

This Court confirmed as much in *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), when translating the "substantial rights" inquiry into a workable test. The Court explained that the harmless-error question turns on whether, "after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole," the reviewing court can say with fair assurance that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error. *Id.* at 764–65. *Kotteakos* thus rejects the notion that a reviewing court must isolate the error from its surroundings; the very instruction is to consider "all that happened"—not merely what the jury was shown, but the totality of the record before the court.

### B. This Court's Harmless-Error Precedents Confirm That Courts Look Beyond the Evidence Submitted to the Jury

The same principle animates this Court's related harmless-error decisions, which are the doctrinal cousins of plain-error review and inform its application. In *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60 (1942), the Court examined the full course of proceedings—not an isolated evidentiary ruling—to determine whether a defendant's substantial rights had actually been compromised. Similarly, in *Bihn v. United States*, 328 U.S. 633 (1946), the Court assessed the probable effect of an erroneously admitted statement by weighing it against the remainder of the record, including evidence and circumstances not directly before the jury on the disputed point. And in *Weiler v. United States*, 323 U.S. 606 (1945), the Court evaluated the strength of the properly admitted evidence in the record as a whole to determine whether an instructional error was prejudicial, rather than confining its review to the specific testimony implicated by the error. Each of these decisions treats the "record" for prejudice purposes as the complete proceeding, including undisputed facts and circumstances that never required jury resolution because they were not contested.

*Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78 (1935), reflects the same methodology outside the instructional context: in assessing whether prosecutorial misconduct was harmless, the Court situated the misconduct within the full context of the trial rather than treating it as an isolated event divorced from everything else the record revealed. This consistent methodology—assessing prejudice by reference to the entire proceeding—forecloses any rule limiting plain-error review to the evidence formally placed before the jury.

### C. Longstanding Appellate Practice Confirms That Reviewing Courts May Look Beyond the Trial Record When Justice Requires

This Court has long recognized that appellate courts retain discretion to consider matters ordinarily outside the strict confines of a party's preserved presentation when necessary to prevent injustice. In *Hormel v. Helvering*, 312 U.S. 552 (1941), the Court explained that "there may always be exceptional cases or particular circumstances which will prompt a reviewing or appellate court, where injustice might otherwise result, to consider questions of law which were not pressed or passed upon" below. *Id.* at 557. That same flexibility undergirds plain-error review itself, which exists precisely to address errors that escaped contemporaneous objection.

So too with waiver and forfeiture determinations more generally. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), this Court examined the totality of the circumstances surrounding a claimed waiver of a fundamental right, rather than restricting itself to a single transcript excerpt. The inquiry into whether an error truly affected a defendant's rights is analogous: it requires the same holistic assessment of the record as a whole, including undisputed matters that never needed submission to a jury because no rational dispute existed as to them.

### D. Application Here

Under this unbroken line of authority, the Eleventh Circuit properly considered the entire record—including Greer's prior felony convictions and the absence of any indication he was unaware of his status—in concluding that the *Rehaif* error did not affect his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his proceedings. That approach is precisely what *Atkinson*, *Kotteakos*, *Hormel*, and their progeny require. Respondent respectfully submits the judgment below should be affirmed.
