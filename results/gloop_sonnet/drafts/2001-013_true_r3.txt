# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 4(b)(1) Preempts OSHA Only Where Another Agency Has Actually Exercised Regulatory Authority Over the Specific Working Conditions at Issue

Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA's jurisdiction only over "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The statute's plain terms condition preemption on actual exercise of authority, not on the theoretical existence of jurisdiction to regulate. This Court's precedents confirm that federal maritime regulatory schemes displace other law only to the extent they actually occupy the field in question.

In *Huron Portland Cement Co. v. City of Detroit*, 362 U.S. 440 (1960), this Court distinguished between local rules that conflict with an actual federal standard and those that operate alongside a federal license without displacing it. The Court explained that where local requirements are "not at war with the federal license, but complementary to it," no preemption occurs. *Id.* at 446. The touchstone is not whether the federal government *could* regulate, but whether it has, in fact, promulgated a standard that the second regulator's rule contradicts. The same logic governs the Coast Guard's relationship to OSHA. Where the Coast Guard has not prescribed working-condition standards for a category of vessel, there is no federal standard for OSHA regulation to conflict with, and hence nothing for Section 4(b)(1) to protect.

This Court reached a parallel conclusion in the commerce-power context in *Sherlock v. Alling*, 93 U.S. 99 (1876), sustaining state wrongful-death remedies for maritime torts because the challenged law did not "impose[] a tax upon some instrument or subject of commerce" or "create[] an impediment to the free navigation of some public waters." *Id.* at 103. The Court there rejected the notion that federal maritime primacy automatically ousts all other regulation touching the same subject matter; exclusivity attaches only where the state or second regulator's rule actually burdens the specific federal interest at stake. So too here: OSHA's citation to Mallard does not conflict with any Coast Guard regulation, because the Coast Guard has issued none addressing the hazard at issue on uninspected vessels.

## II. The Coast Guard Has Not Exercised Comprehensive Authority Over Occupational Safety Aboard Uninspected Vessels

The Coast Guard's authority over vessels, while broad in geographic reach, has always been construed by this Court according to the specific grants Congress has made, not as an undifferentiated general police power over everything that floats. In *Maul v. United States*, 274 U.S. 501 (1927), the Court undertook a careful, multi-part examination of the precise statutory authority conferred on the Coast Guard before recognizing its power to seize a vessel on the high seas, addressing separately whether the seizure was authorized by treaty, by statute, and by the vessel's registry status, rather than treating the agency's authority as boundless. *Id.* at 507–13. Likewise, in *Cook v. United States*, 288 U.S. 102 (1933), the Court held that Coast Guard boarding-and-seizure authority did not extend beyond the specific statutory limits Congress had drawn, even though the general subject—enforcement on the high seas—fell within the Coast Guard's competence. *Id.* The lesson of *Maul* and *Cook* is that the Coast Guard's jurisdiction must be measured by what Congress actually authorized and what the agency actually did, vessel type by vessel type, not by sweeping reference to its general mission of "safety of life and property" on the water.

Applying that method here defeats preemption. The Coast Guard's uninspected-vessel regulations address discrete items of safety equipment—fire extinguishers, life preservers, backfire flame control, ventilation—not the comprehensive occupational safety and health conditions that OSHA's citation addressed. Nothing in the Coast Guard's investigation of the Rig 52 explosion, which simply noted the vessel's uninspected status without lodging any accusation of wrongdoing against Mallard, reflects any exercise of authority over the specific hazard at issue. Where, as in *Roper v. United States*, 368 U.S. 20 (1961), a vessel's actual status and function determine which body of law applies—there, whether the ship was "in navigation" for purposes of the seaworthiness warranty, *id.* at 22—the actual character of Rig 52's operations, not its abstract eligibility for future Coast Guard inspection, must control whether OSHA's standards were displaced.

## III. Congress Intended the Act to Reach Industrial Workplaces Like Rig 52 Operating Within State Territorial Waters

Rig 52 was drilling a well within Louisiana's territorial waters, precisely the kind of state-adjacent industrial worksite Congress meant the Act to cover. This Court has recognized that fixed and semi-fixed drilling structures within a State's waters are treated as distinct from vessels navigating the high seas for regulatory purposes. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969) (addressing artificial drilling islands on the outer Continental Shelf). And this Court's careful delineation of territorial and inland waters in *United States v. Louisiana*, 394 U.S. 11 (1969), confirms that jurisdictional lines in the maritime context are drawn with precision, not by loose analogy to blue-water shipping. See also *Street v. Shipowners' Ass'n of the Pacific Coast*, 263 U.S. 334 (1923) (seamen's working conditions traditionally analyzed by reference to the vessel's actual service and route). Rig 52's function as a stationary drilling platform in state waters, uninspected and unregulated for occupational hazards, places it squarely within OSHA's remedial jurisdiction.
