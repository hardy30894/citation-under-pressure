ARGUMENT

I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR STATE COURT JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE AGE DISCRIMINATION IN EMPLOYMENT ACT (ADEA)

The ADEA prohibits employers from discriminating against employees on the basis of age, but it exempts certain individuals, including "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The question presented here is whether Missouri's appointed state court judges fall within this exemption.

The Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals correctly held that appointed state court judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" and therefore are excluded from the ADEA's protection. As the First Circuit noted in EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), "policymaking" is a part of the function of judging, as judges create law by filling the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents. Missouri's appointed judges exercise significant policymaking responsibility, particularly in their supervisory roles. For example, the Missouri Supreme Court is responsible for adopting rules governing the professional conduct of the Missouri judiciary and the Missouri Bar. Mo. Const. art. V, Sec. 4.

The legislative history of the ADEA exemption supports this interpretation. The Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746 indicates that the exemption is intended to apply to "persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments." 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180. The state judiciary is a powerful "agency" of state government, and appointed judges are policymakers within the meaning of the exemption.

II. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR STATE COURT JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE OF THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT

The Equal Protection Clause requires that state laws be rationally related to a legitimate government interest. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 312-314 (1976) (per curiam). Missouri's mandatory retirement provision for state court judges is rationally related to several legitimate government interests, including ensuring that judges remain mentally and physically capable of performing their duties, reducing the risk of death or disability on the bench, and promoting a more representative judiciary. See Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979); Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988).

The rational basis test is a deferential standard, and this Court has held that a state law will be upheld if it is rationally related to a legitimate government interest, even if the law is not perfectly tailored to achieve that interest. See Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 108-109 (1979). Missouri's mandatory retirement provision is rationally related to legitimate government interests, and therefore does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
