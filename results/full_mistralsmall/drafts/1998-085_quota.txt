**ARGUMENT**

I. The Court of Appeals Erred in Holding That Punitive Damages May Be Awarded Under Title VII Without a Showing of “Egregious” or “Outrageous” Conduct.

The plain text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only where the employer acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” This standard does not require proof of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct independent of the employer’s state of mind. *Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n*, 108 F.3d 1436, 1444 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (en banc), *rev’d*, 527 U.S. 526 (1999). The statute’s text is controlling, and this Court has repeatedly held that statutory text is the “‘first and foremost’” guide to its meaning. *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989) (quoting *United States v. James*, 478 U.S. 597, 606 (1986)).

The legislative history cited by petitioner does not alter this conclusion. While some statements in the Congressional Record suggest punitive damages should be reserved for “extraordinarily egregious cases,” *137 Cong. Rec. S15473* (Oct. 30, 1991) (statement of Sen. Dole), other statements clarify that the standard remains malice or reckless indifference. *Id.* at H9527 (Nov. 7, 1991) (statement of Rep. Edwards). This Court has long held that legislative history is not a “trump card” and cannot override the plain text of a statute. *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 380 n.29 (1968). Moreover, the House Report’s reference to an “even higher standard” was not adopted in the final version of the statute. *Kolstad*, 108 F.3d at 1444.

II. The Court of Appeals’ Interpretation Conflicts with This Court’s Precedent and the Decisions of Other Circuits.

This Court has repeatedly held that punitive damages under Title VII require only a showing of malice or reckless indifference, not egregious conduct. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (holding that punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 require reckless or callous indifference, not egregious conduct). The same standard applies under Title VII. *Kolstad*, 527 U.S. at 536-37. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary interpretation in *Kolstad* was explicitly rejected by this Court. *Id.* at 536.

Other circuits have similarly held that punitive damages under Title VII do not require a showing of egregious conduct. The Seventh Circuit has held that punitive damages are available where the employer’s conduct was “malicious or recklessly indifferent,” without requiring additional proof of egregiousness. *Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co.*, 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996). The Sixth Circuit has likewise held that punitive damages may be awarded where the employer acted with “reckless indifference,” without requiring a showing of egregious conduct. *Bazemore v. Friday*, 478 U.S. 385, 400 (1986) (plurality opinion).

III. The Court of Appeals’ Interpretation Would Undermine the Purpose of the 1991 Amendments.

The 1991 Amendments to Title VII were intended to expand the remedies available to victims of discrimination, not to impose additional burdens on plaintiffs seeking punitive damages. *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994). The Court of Appeals’ interpretation would frustrate this purpose by requiring plaintiffs to prove egregious conduct, a standard not found in the statute’s text. *Kolstad*, 527 U.S. at 536-37.

IV. The Evidence in This Case Does Not Support an Award of Punitive Damages.

Even if the Court were to adopt petitioner’s proposed standard, the evidence in this case does not support an award of punitive damages. The record shows no evidence of egregious or outrageous conduct by the ADA. The decision to promote Spangler over Kolstad was based on legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons, including Kolstad’s lack of recent legislative experience. *Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n*, 912 F. Supp. 13, 14 (D.D.C. 1996). The jury’s finding of intentional discrimination does not, without more, establish egregious conduct. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. at 56.

**CONCLUSION**

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that punitive damages may be awarded under Title VII only where the employer’s conduct is “egregious” or “outrageous.” The plain text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) requires only a showing of malice or reckless indifference. This interpretation is consistent with this Court’s precedent and the decisions of other circuits. The evidence in this case does not support an award of punitive damages under any standard. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
