**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore Act coverage only to injuries occurring *on* the outer Continental Shelf—requires this Court to interpret the precise language of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b), which provides workers’ compensation benefits for “the disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that this provision applies regardless of the *location* of the injury—so long as there is a “substantial nexus” between the injury and offshore operations—distorts the statutory text, ignores binding precedent, and creates a regime of unlimited liability that Congress never intended.

### I. THE STATUTORY TEXT REQUIRES A SITUS-OF-INJURY TEST

The plain language of § 1333(b) makes clear that coverage is triggered only when an injury *occurs* as a result of operations on the outer Continental Shelf. The statute does not say “resulting from operations *conducted in connection with* the outer Continental Shelf,” nor does it say “resulting from operations *related to* the outer Continental Shelf.” It says “occurring as the result of operations conducted *on* the outer Continental Shelf.” The phrase “occurring as the result of” is not a mere formality—it is a *situs* requirement. The injury must occur *in consequence of* an operation that is itself located on the shelf.

This interpretation is consistent with the settled principle that “the meaning of a statute is determined by its text, read in context and in light of its purpose.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 223, 230 (1989). The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test, by contrast, effectively eliminates the geographic limitation entirely. Under that test, an employee injured in a car crash on a highway three miles inland could qualify for OCSLA benefits if the accident occurred while en route to a helicopter that would take him to a platform. *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Serv., Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805, 811 (3d Cir. 1988). But that is not what the statute says. The injury must *occur* as a result of operations *on* the shelf—not merely *related* to them.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the location of the injury is a critical factor in determining whether a claimant is entitled to benefits under the LHWCA and its OCSLA extension.” *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). In *Tallentire*, the Court noted that Congress “determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Id. The Court’s observation was not mere dictum—it was a central holding grounded in the statutory structure. The Court’s footnote in *Tallentire*—which the Ninth Circuit dismisses as “unconsidered”—is not a mere afterthought. It is a *clarification* of the Court’s holding: “Only one provision of OCSLA superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement; § 1333(b) makes compensation . . . payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act.” Id. at 219 n.2. The Court’s use of the phrase “situs requirement” in this context—immediately after affirming that OCSLA coverage is “determined principally by locale”—is not speculative. It is a *judicial construction* of the statute’s geographic limits.

### II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S “SUBSTANTIAL NEXUS” TEST CONTRADICTS BINDING PRECEDENT

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test is not only unsupported by the text but also directly contradicts the Fifth Circuit’s long-standing interpretation of § 1333(b), which has been affirmed by the Supreme Court. In *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), the Court held that a worker injured on a platform located in state waters—three miles from shore—was not entitled to OCSLA benefits because the platform was not on the outer Continental Shelf. The Court emphasized that “the outer Continental Shelf is defined by statute as the submerged lands lying seaward and outside of the area of lands beneath navigable waters.” Id. at 422 (quoting 43 U.S.C. § 1301(a)(2)). The Court did not ask whether the injury was “substantially related” to offshore operations. It asked whether the injury occurred *on* the shelf. The same principle applies here.

The Fifth Circuit has consistently applied this situs-of-injury rule. In *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), the court held that an employee injured during the onshore construction of an offshore platform was not entitled to OCSLA benefits because the injury occurred on land. The court reasoned that “Congress intended to limit OCSLA coverage to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it.” Id. at 362. This interpretation is not arbitrary—it reflects Congress’s intent to confine OCSLA’s reach to the unique legal and regulatory regime of the outer Continental Shelf, not to extend federal workers’ compensation to every worker involved in offshore energy projects, regardless of where the injury occurs.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision creates a circuit split that undermines uniformity in the application of federal law. The Third Circuit’s “but-for” test in *Curtis* is not a valid alternative. That decision was based on a narrow interpretation of the phrase “as the result of,” but it ignored the statutory definition of the outer Continental Shelf and the Supreme Court’s emphasis on *locale*. The Court should not endorse a test that would allow OCSLA benefits to be claimed for injuries occurring in any state, so long as the employee worked on a platform offshore.
