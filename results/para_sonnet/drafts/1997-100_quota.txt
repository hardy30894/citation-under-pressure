# ARGUMENT

## THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE DOES NOT PRECLUDE RETRIAL OF A PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION IN NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDINGS

The Double Jeopardy Clause provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has consistently construed that guarantee to protect against a second prosecution for the same *offense* after acquittal or conviction, and against multiple punishments for the same offense. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). A proceeding to determine whether a defendant's sentence should be enhanced because of a prior conviction is not a prosecution for an "offense" at all—it is a sentencing factor. Because noncapital sentencing enhancement proceedings do not implicate the "twice put in jeopardy" concern that animates the Clause, retrial of a prior conviction allegation following reversal for insufficient evidence does not offend the Constitution.

### A. Sentencing Proceedings Traditionally Have Not Been Treated as Trials for Double Jeopardy Purposes

This Court has long recognized that the procedural formality of a trial on guilt does not extend to sentencing. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court upheld a death sentence imposed after the judge considered information regarding thirty uncharged burglaries never proven at trial, explaining that "[t]he due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure." *Id.* at 251. That principle applies with full force here: because a State is not constitutionally required to provide any adversarial trial-like procedure for proving prior convictions, it follows that retrying such an allegation cannot implicate the trial-based protections of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

This Court reaffirmed *Williams* just last Term in *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), and in *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389 (1995), where the Court explained that due process does not require "courts throughout the Nation [to] abandon their age-old practice of seeking information from out-of-court sources to guide their judgment toward a more enlightened and just sentence." *Witte*, 515 U.S. at 398. If a sentencing court may rely on unadjudicated conduct without any trial protections at all, it follows a fortiori that retrial of a *charged* prior-conviction allegation—where the defendant already receives notice and an opportunity to contest the allegation—raises no double jeopardy concern.

### B. This Court's Capital Sentencing Exception Is Narrow and Should Not Be Extended to Noncapital Cases

Petitioner relies principally on *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), which held that double jeopardy principles bar retrial of a death sentence after a jury's decision not to impose death. But those decisions rested on the unique "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence" that characterized Missouri's bifurcated capital sentencing scheme—including a burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, standards for aggravating and mitigating circumstances resembling elements of a greater offense, and binding sentencing verdicts akin to acquittals. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439.

This Court itself has cautioned against extending that capital-sentencing rationale into the noncapital context. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), the Court observed that "[b]oth *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases, and our reasoning in those cases was based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding." *Id.* at 392. Similarly, in *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28 (1985) (per curiam), the Court permitted resentencing on noncapital counts notwithstanding the vacatur of related convictions, signaling that the *Bullington* trial-like analogy does not transplant easily outside the death-penalty setting, where "the death sentence [is] different in kind from any other sentence." *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 441.

Noncapital sentencing enhancements—such as California's Three Strikes law—lack the defining features that drove *Bullington*. They do not require the jury (or, as here, the court) to weigh statutorily enumerated aggravating and mitigating circumstances against a life-or-death choice; they do not resemble a "trial on the issue of punishment" in the constitutionally significant sense that distinguishes capital sentencing from all other sentencing determinations. *Id.* at 438. Extending *Bullington*'s narrow, capital-specific holding to routine recidivism enhancements would work a sweeping expansion of double jeopardy doctrine unsupported by this Court's precedents.

### C. The State's Interest in Accurate Sentencing Outweighs Any Interest in Repose

The Double Jeopardy Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717. A reversal for evidentiary insufficiency on a sentencing enhancement is not an "acquittal" of an offense; it is a determination that the sentencing record was incomplete. Permitting retrial in that circumstance serves the State's legitimate interest—recognized in *Witte*, 515 U.S. at 400—in ensuring that recidivist offenders receive the enhanced sentences the legislature has mandated, without artificially freezing the sentencing record based on a single evidentiary misstep.

Moreover, this Court's decision in *Monge*'s companion context confirms that where a sentencing factor does not carry the "hallmarks" identified in *Bullington*, ordinary double jeopardy principles yield to the State's interest in accurate and complete sentencing determinations. *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209–10 (asking whether the proceeding possesses characteristics "that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause"). California's prior-conviction proceeding—resolved by the court rather than a jury, governed by state evidentiary and procedural rules of the State's own choosing, and untethered to any binding life-or-death verdict—does not resemble a capital sentencing trial in any constitutionally relevant sense.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not preclude retrial of a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings, and should affirm the judgment of the Supreme Court of California.
