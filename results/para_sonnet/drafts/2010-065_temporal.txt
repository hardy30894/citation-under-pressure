**ARGUMENT**

**Bond Lacks Standing to Challenge Her Conviction on Tenth Amendment Grounds Because the Amendment Protects the Reserved Powers of the States, Not the Personal Rights of Individuals.**

This Court has already answered the question presented. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies argued that a federal instrumentality's competition in the local sale of electricity invaded powers reserved to the States under the Tenth Amendment. This Court rejected the claim not on the merits but for want of standing, holding that the private litigants, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [A]mendment." *Id.* at 144. That holding controls here. Bond, like the utility companies in *Tennessee Electric*, is a private party invoking a constitutional provision whose beneficiary is the State, not the individual defendant.

The logic of *Tennessee Electric* follows directly from the text and function of the Amendment itself. The Tenth Amendment reserves powers "to the States respectively, or to the people," but as this Court explained in *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), the Amendment "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered." *Id.* at 124. It is not an independent source of personal rights that a criminal defendant may assert to escape prosecution; it is a structural principle allocating power between sovereigns. Where the allegedly injured sovereign—the State—has taken no position and asserted no claim, a private citizen cannot manufacture a controversy under the Amendment by asserting an interest that belongs to someone else.

That limitation reflects a broader and long-settled principle of standing: a litigant ordinarily may assert only the violation of his own rights, not those of an absent third party. *Barrows v. Jackson*, 346 U.S. 249, 255 (1953) ("Ordinarily, one may not claim standing … to vindicate the constitutional rights of some third party."). This Court applied the same principle in *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17 (1960), refusing to allow a defendant to challenge a statute's application to persons not before the Court, because "the delicate power of pronouncing an Act of Congress unconstitutional is not to be exercised … with reference to hypothetical cases." *Id.* at 20–21. Bond's Tenth Amendment claim is precisely such a hypothetical assertion: she does not claim that Pennsylvania was denied any power it wished to exercise; she claims only that the Commonwealth theoretically could have prosecuted her instead. That is not her injury to raise.

Nor can Bond satisfy the ordinary requirements of standing independent of the third-party rule. This Court held in *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), that "the party who invokes the power must be able to show not only that the statute is invalid, but that he has sustained or is immediately in danger of sustaining some direct injury as the result of its enforcement, and not merely that he suffers in some indefinite way in common with people generally." *Id.* at 488. Bond's asserted injury—prosecution under a validly enacted federal statute—does not stem from any invasion of a power reserved to the States; it stems from her own conduct in repeatedly attempting to poison Myrlinda Haynes. The constitutional interest she invokes, the allocation of authority between the national government and the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, is not personal to her. As in *Fairchild v. Hughes*, 258 U.S. 126 (1922), where this Court denied standing to a citizen who possessed "only the right, possessed by every citizen, to require that the Government be administered according to law," *id.* at 129–30, Bond's claimed stake in the abstract question of federal-state power allocation is not particularized to her.

This rule is not merely technical; it serves the same interest in judicial restraint that animates the constitutional avoidance doctrine articulated in *Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 297 U.S. 288 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring), under which courts should not reach and decide difficult constitutional questions unnecessarily. Allowing every criminal defendant prosecuted under a federal statute touching on subjects also regulated by the States to litigate the outer boundaries of federal power would convert countless ordinary criminal prosecutions into referenda on federalism, notwithstanding the States' own satisfaction with, or indifference to, federal enforcement. That is precisely the posture in *Tennessee Electric*: Pennsylvania has raised no objection to the exercise of federal jurisdiction over Bond's chemical attacks, and no showing has been made that the Commonwealth objects to the exercise of authority under 18 U.S.C. § 229. Bond's suit to vindicate the State's sovereign interest, over the State's own silence, is exactly what *Tennessee Electric* forecloses.

Finally, this rule leaves no citizen without a remedy, for the interest at stake belongs to the State, whose officials remain free to object, decline to cooperate with federal enforcement, or seek relief in their own right, as parties did successfully in *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920) — a case brought, tellingly, by the State itself, not by a private individual subject to the underlying regulation. Bond's remedy, if any, lies through the ordinary channels available to affect the Commonwealth's own enforcement priorities, not through a private assertion of the State's Tenth Amendment prerogatives in her own criminal defense.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
