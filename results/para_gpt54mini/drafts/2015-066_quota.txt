**Argument**

Section 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment when the Government uses prior tribal-court convictions as predicates. The Constitution guarantees the right to counsel in criminal prosecutions by the United States and the States, but it has never been understood to require tribal courts to mirror federal procedure. See *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 373-74 (1979); *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). Because tribal prosecutions arise from retained tribal sovereignty, not federal or state power, Congress may treat valid tribal convictions as recidivist predicates without converting those convictions into Sixth Amendment violations. See *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978); *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004).

1. **The Sixth Amendment is not violated by reliance on a valid tribal conviction.**  
Bryant’s premise is that, because he had no constitutional right to appointed counsel in tribal court, the later federal use of those convictions is itself unconstitutional. That premise misreads the Sixth Amendment. In *Scott*, the Court held that the right to counsel does not attach when no term of imprisonment is imposed. 440 U.S. at 373-74. In *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 746-47 (1994), the Court held that a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction that was valid under *Scott* could be used to enhance a later sentence. *Nichols* rejected the idea that a constitutionally valid conviction becomes unusable merely because counsel was absent. Id. at 747-48. If that is true for a valid misdemeanor conviction in state court, it is equally true for a valid tribal conviction.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule conflicts with *Nichols*’s core principle that recidivism enhancements punish the new offense, not the prior one. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747; *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008). Section 117(a) likewise “penaliz[es] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747. Congress chose to define the offense to include prior convictions in federal, state, or tribal court. 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). That legislative choice does not transform the predicate convictions into elements subject to a separate Sixth Amendment bar.

2. **Using tribal predicates at the guilt phase does not change the constitutional analysis.**  
Bryant and the Ninth Circuit rely on the distinction between sentencing and guilt. But that distinction cuts against Bryant. In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), the Court held that the Government must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt. That higher burden does not disable Congress from defining recidivism-based felonies. It merely requires proof of the fact of prior convictions. See *United States v. Almendarez-Torres*, 523 U.S. 224, 243-44 (1998). Section 117(a) does exactly that.

Nothing in *Nichols* suggested that a valid prior conviction becomes constitutionally unusable when Congress incorporates it into the definition of a separate offense. To the contrary, *Nichols* approved the use of prior uncounseled convictions in determining punishment. 511 U.S. at 748-49. If a valid prior conviction may support a sentencing enhancement, it may also serve as the fact of recidivism that Congress chose to criminalize.

3. **The tribal-court context makes the Sixth Amendment argument even weaker, not stronger.**  
The “Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.” *Plains Commerce Bank*, 554 U.S. at 337. Tribal courts are governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act, not the Sixth Amendment. See *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 56-57 (1978). Congress has authority to legislate in this area, including by tying federal consequences to tribal convictions. *Lara*, 541 U.S. at 200. It also has long recognized tribal sovereignty and the legitimacy of tribal adjudications. *Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 323-24.

Federal courts have likewise recognized comity toward tribal judgments. See *Iowa Mutual Insurance Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987); *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810-11 (9th Cir. 1997). Bryant’s rule would invert that principle by treating valid tribal convictions as constitutionally suspect solely because tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment. That is not comity; it is constitutional imperialism.

4. **Alabama v. Shelton does not help Bryant.**  
*Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654, 662-68 (2002), held that a suspended sentence of imprisonment may not be imposed based on an uncounseled conviction obtained in a proceeding where counsel was constitutionally required. But Shelton involved a state conviction that itself implicated the Sixth Amendment. Id. at 661-62. Tribal convictions are different because the Sixth Amendment does not apply in tribal court at all. See *Plains Commerce Bank*, 554 U.S. at 337. Shelton therefore does not supply a rule invalidating otherwise lawful tribal convictions as federal predicates.

In short, Section 117(a) uses prior valid tribal convictions as recidivist facts in a federal prosecution. That is consistent with *Scott*, *Nichols*, *Winship*, *Almendarez-Torres*, *Wheeler*, *Lara*, *Santa Clara Pueblo*, *Iowa Mutual*, *Wilson*, and *Plains Commerce Bank*. The judgment should be reversed.
