**ARGUMENT**

I. THE COURT OF APPEALS PROPERLY CONCLUDED THAT BOND LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE THE CONSTITUTIONALITY OF 18 U.S.C. § 229 ON TENTH AMENDMENT GROUNDS.

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge a federal statute on grounds that it exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers under the Tenth Amendment—must be answered in the negative. This Court has long held that private individuals lack standing to assert the Tenth Amendment as a basis for challenging federal legislation, even when such legislation is alleged to encroach upon state sovereignty. The principle is firmly rooted in the constitutional structure of federalism and the limits of judicial jurisdiction.

In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144, 59 S.Ct. 366, 83 L.Ed. 543 (1939), the Supreme Court explicitly rejected a Tenth Amendment challenge brought by private utility companies against the federally chartered Tennessee Valley Authority. The companies argued that the Authority’s sale of electricity in competition with private firms violated the Tenth Amendment by intruding upon state regulatory authority. The Court held that the companies lacked standing to raise the claim, stating: “The sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment. As we have seen, there is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states, and, if this were not so, the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366.

This holding is not dictum. The Court resolved the standing issue as a threshold matter, and did so in the context of a federal court’s jurisdiction. As this Court has recognized, when a constitutional question is raised in a case where the plaintiff lacks standing, the Court may dispose of the matter on that ground without reaching the merits. See *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02, 118 S.Ct. 1003, 140 L.Ed.2d 210 (1998); *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10, 98 S.Ct. 2985, 57 L.Ed.2d 1018 (1978). The *Tennessee Electric Power* decision, therefore, stands as binding precedent that private parties cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal statutes unless they are acting on behalf of a state or its officers.

The rationale behind this rule is clear. The Tenth Amendment does not create rights enforceable by private individuals. It merely reserves to the states or the people those powers not delegated to the federal government. *United States v. Sprague*, 282 U.S. 716, 730, 51 S.Ct. 220, 75 L.Ed. 640 (1931). The amendment is not a source of individual rights, but a structural limitation on federal power. As such, it is the states—not private citizens—who are the proper parties to assert violations of federalism. See *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124, 61 S.Ct. 451, 85 L.Ed. 609 (1941) (“The Tenth Amendment is not a limitation upon the powers of Congress, but a declaration of the principle of federalism.”).

The Third Circuit correctly applied this principle. It recognized that Bond, as a private individual, could not assert a Tenth Amendment claim absent a showing that the state had been injured or that the state’s sovereign interests were directly compromised. See *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (citing *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. at 689 n.10). Bond’s grievance—being prosecuted under a federal statute—is not the kind of injury that confers standing to challenge the constitutional limits of federal power. Her claim is “an undifferentiated, generalized grievance” about the scope of federal authority, which does not satisfy the standing requirements of Article III. See *United States v. Richardson*, 418 U.S. 166, 177, 94 S.Ct. 2940, 41 L.Ed.2d 678 (1974) (“[A] plaintiff must show that he has suffered an injury in fact that is fairly traceable to the challenged action and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision.”).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently held that a criminal defendant may not challenge the constitutionality of a statute on grounds that it exceeds congressional power unless the challenge is based on a provision of the Constitution that protects the individual’s rights. See *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382, 43 S.Ct. 141, 67 L.Ed. 314 (1922) (“The Constitution does not confer upon the courts the power to declare a statute unconstitutional unless it is in conflict with some provision of the Constitution.”). The Tenth Amendment, as a structural limitation on federal power, does not create a personal right that a defendant may invoke in a criminal prosecution. To hold otherwise would allow every defendant to litigate the scope of federal authority, thereby undermining the finality of criminal judgments and the integrity of the federal judicial system.

The Government’s reliance on *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432, 40 S.Ct. 382, 64 L.Ed. 641 (1920), is misplaced. That case upheld the constitutionality of a federal statute implementing a treaty, but it did not address the standing of private parties to challenge such a statute. The Court in *Holland* emphasized that the treaty power is a “plenary” power, but it did not purport to expand the standing of private individuals to challenge federal legislation.
