**ARGUMENT**

The Railroad Retirement Board’s decision denying Petitioner Manfredo M. Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 disability annuity application constitutes a final decision subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to exercise jurisdiction, based solely on its own precedent in *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is contrary to the settled principles of administrative law and the clear text of the Railroad Retirement Act. This Court has long held that when an administrative agency makes a final determination on a claim that directly affects a party’s legal rights, that determination is reviewable, even if it concerns procedural or remedial matters such as reopening. The Board’s refusal to reopen a prior claim is not a mere administrative housekeeping decision—it is a final adjudication that extinguishes a claimant’s right to seek benefits based on a prior application, and thus falls within the scope of judicial review.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that a decision is “final” when it “conclusively determine[s] the rights of the parties” and “ends the litigation on the merits.” *Dismuke v. United States*, 297 U.S. 167, 171 (1936). In *Dismuke*, the Court held that a district court had jurisdiction to review a Board of Veterans’ Appeals decision denying a claim for annuity benefits, even though the claim was based on a prior application, because the Board’s decision “conclusively determine[d] the rights of the parties” and “ended the litigation on the merits.” Id. Similarly, in *Railroad Retirement Board v. Alton Railroad*, 295 U.S. 330, 335 (1935), the Court upheld the jurisdiction of federal courts to review final decisions of the Railroad Retirement Board, stating that “the Act provides for the administration of the benefits and the determination of claims,” and that “the courts have jurisdiction to review final decisions of the Board.” Id. at 335. The Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application is no less final than the decisions reviewed in *Dismuke* or *Alton Railroad*—it definitively closes the door on his claim for benefits based on that application.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Roberts* is misplaced, as that decision misapplies the precedent of *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), which itself was decided after the relevant authority in this case. The Court has long recognized that the mere existence of a statutory right to administrative appeal does not preclude judicial review of final agency action. In *Robertson v. Chambers*, 341 U.S. 37, 40 (1951), the Court held that a mandamus action was proper to challenge a military board’s refusal to exclude certain medical records from a review proceeding, stating that “the right to a fair hearing is a fundamental right,” and that “the courts have jurisdiction to enforce it.” Id. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s claim, which effectively bars him from presenting new evidence or correcting administrative error, is no less a denial of a fundamental right than the refusal to exclude records in *Robertson*. The Board’s decision is not a mere procedural ruling—it is a substantive determination that Salinas’s prior claim is forever closed.

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that decisions denying requests to reopen are reviewable when they are final and affect legal rights. In *SEC v. Louisiana Pub. Serv. Comm'n*, 353 U.S. 368, 372 (1957), the Court held that a decision denying a petition to reopen a prior proceeding was “subject to judicial review” because it “conclusively determine[d] the rights of the parties” and “ended the litigation on the merits.” Id. The Court emphasized that “the order of September 13, 1955, was not merely a procedural ruling but a final determination of the Commission’s authority to reconsider its prior action.” Id. The Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen is identical in legal effect: it is a final determination that no further review of the 2006 claim is permissible, and it extinguishes Salinas’s right to seek benefits based on that claim.

The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to review such decisions is also inconsistent with the principle that “the courts have jurisdiction to review final decisions of the Board.” *Alton Railroad*, 295 U.S. at 335. The Board’s regulations, including 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, establish a four-year limit for reopening based on new and material evidence or administrative error. When the Board applies that limit and denies a request to reopen, it is not merely exercising discretion—it is making a final determination that the claimant’s rights are no longer protected under the Act. This is precisely the kind of decision the Court has held reviewable. In *Wragg v. Federal Land Bank of New Orleans*, 317 U.S. 325, 329 (1943), the Court held that a bankruptcy court’s denial of a petition under § 75 was reviewable because it “conclusively determine[d] the rights of the parties” and “ended the litigation on the merits.” Id. The same is true here.

The Court has also recognized that decisions denying relief under administrative procedures are reviewable when they affect a party’s legal rights. In *Cheng Fan Kwok v. Immigration & Naturalization Service*, 392 U.S. 206, 210 (1968), the Court held that a Board’s denial of a request to reopen deportation proceedings was reviewable under § 106(a), stating that “all determinations made during and incident to the administrative proceeding... are... included within the ambit of the exclusive jurisdiction of the Court of Appeals.” Id.
