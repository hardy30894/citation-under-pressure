**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATES MAY CONSTITUTIONALLY LIMIT THE USE OF EXPERT MENTAL-STATE EVIDENCE TO THE INSANITY DEFENSE WITHOUT VIOLATING DUE PROCESS.**

The Due Process Clause does not require Arizona to permit a defendant to deploy expert evidence of mental disease to negate *mens rea* when the State has chosen to channel such evidence exclusively to the insanity defense. This Court has long recognized that the definition of criminal offenses and defenses belongs to the States. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952). In *Leland*, the Court upheld Oregon’s requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting the argument that due process compelled the State to allow the same evidence to contest specific intent. *Id.* at 795. The Court reasoned that “the defense of insanity is separate from the issue of specific intent,” *id.*, and that Oregon could place the burden of proving insanity on the defendant without shifting the prosecution’s burden on intent. So too here: Arizona has defined first-degree murder’s mental element as a cognitive capacity distinct from the abnormal mental condition that grounds an insanity plea. The State may correspondingly restrict the evidentiary effect of psychiatric testimony to the insanity inquiry.

Arizona’s rule does not deny the defendant a fair opportunity to challenge the prosecution’s case. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), this Court held that “the trial court has a wide discretion in determining the admissibility of evidence,” *id.* at 464, and that discretion includes the power to exclude evidence that is misleading or that would confuse the jury. Expert testimony about paranoid schizophrenia may illuminate whether Clark acted under a delusion that excuses conduct, but it does not logically refute that he intended to kill the officer. Intent and delusion can coexist; a person who believes he is shooting an alien invader nonetheless intends to shoot. Arizona may therefore treat such evidence as relevant only to the excuse of insanity.

**II. THE STATE’S RULE DOES NOT SHIFT THE PROSECUTION’S BURDEN OF PROOF AND COMPORTS WITH DUE PROCESS.**

The cornerstone of criminal due process is that the State must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Miles v. United States*, 103 U.S. 304, 310 (1881). Arizona’s rule does not relieve the State of this burden. The State must still prove that Clark acted intentionally—a mental state that, under Arizona law, is entirely compatible with severe mental illness. The rule merely excludes evidence that is irrelevant to the *mens rea* inquiry, leaving the State to prove intent by other evidence. Nothing in *Miles* or *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), requires the admission of psychiatric testimony on the issue of intent when the State has a rational basis for confining it to the separate defense of insanity.

In *Davis*, the Court held that the presumption of sanity is not a presumption *juris et de jure*, but that “the burden of proof is upon the government to establish every material allegation of the indictment.” *Id.* at 472. Arizona’s rule respects that principle. The government must prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt; the defendant may introduce any non-psychiatric evidence to show he lacked intent. What the defendant may not do is use psychiatric evidence that goes to his general cognitive impairment—evidence that could just as easily support an insanity claim—to create confusion between two distinct legal inquiries. As the Court observed in *Leland*, a State may “require the defense of insanity to be established by the defendant beyond a reasonable doubt” without offending due process. 343 U.S. at 799.

**III. MULTIPLE PRECEDENTS UPHOLD STATE CONTROL OVER THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN INSANITY AND MENS REA.**

Since the earliest days of this Court, the States have been permitted to define the interplay between mental illness and criminal responsibility. In *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574 (1884), the Court recognized that a defendant’s “mental condition” may be relevant to guilt, but that the State may structure its use within the bounds of common law. *Id.* at 583. In *Hotema v. United States*, 186 U.S. 413 (1902), the Court upheld a jury instruction that placed on the defendant the burden of proving “irresistible impulse” as a defense, rejecting the argument that the government should have to disprove it as part of *mens rea*. *Id.* at 418. And in *Salinger v. United States*, 272 U.S. 542 (1926), the Court reaffirmed that “the burden of proving insanity” rests on the defendant. *Id.* at 545.

These cases demonstrate that the Constitution does not mandate the unitary treatment of mental-state evidence. A State may treat insanity as an affirmative defense—as Arizona does—without allowing the same evidence to negate an element of the offense. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 795–96; *Davis*, 160 U.S. at 487–88. Indeed, in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), this Court rejected the argument that chronic alcoholism must be allowed to negate *mens rea*, holding that the criminal law is not “powerless to punish” conduct committed under circumstances that might otherwise excuse. *Id.* at 532. Clark’s claim—that he must be permitted to use his delusional beliefs to negate intent—seeks to constitutionalize a particular evidentiary regime that no prior decision of this Court has required.

**IV. CLARK’S PROPOSED RIGHT WOULD DISRUPT THE STATE’S CAREFULLY CRAFTED INSANITY SCHEME.**

Arizona has chosen to treat insanity as an affirmative defense requiring proof by clear and convincing evidence. That choice reflects a legislative judgment about the allocation of burdens and the definition of responsibility. If Clark were allowed to use the same psychiatric evidence to negate *mens rea*, he could circumvent the higher burden and the specialized adjudication of insanity. This Court has never held that due process forbids such a reasoned distinction. To the contrary, in *Leland*, the Court approved Oregon’s scheme requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt—a standard higher than Arizona’s—without any separate channel for the evidence in the *mens rea* context. 343 U.S. at 798–99.

Arizona’s rule respects the distinct roles of *mens rea* and insanity, does not relieve the State of its burden, and reflects a balanced legislative judgment. It does not violate the Due Process Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
