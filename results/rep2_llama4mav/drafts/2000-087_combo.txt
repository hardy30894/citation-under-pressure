The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The Supreme Court has construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation to avoid serious constitutional concerns, as seen in Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678 (2001), where the Court relied on the principles established in Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228 (1896). In Wong Wing, the Court held that the government may not indefinitely detain aliens subject to deportation, as this would raise serious constitutional concerns (Wong Wing, 163 U.S. at 237).

The government's argument that the statute authorizes indefinite detention is contrary to the principles enunciated in Yamataya v. Fisher, 189 U.S. 86 (1903), where the Court emphasized the importance of procedural due process in deportation proceedings. The Court in Yamataya held that aliens are entitled to a fair hearing before being deported, and that the government's actions must be reasonable and not arbitrary (Yamataya, 189 U.S. at 100-101). Similarly, in United States ex rel. Tisi v. Tod, 264 U.S. 131 (1924), the Court held that the government's detention of an alien pending deportation must be reasonable and not excessive (Tisi, 264 U.S. at 133-134).

The government's interpretation of the statute would also raise concerns under the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause, as noted in Ng Fung Ho v. White, 259 U.S. 276 (1922), where the Court held that the government may not deprive individuals of their liberty without due process of law (Ng Fung Ho, 259 U.S. at 284-285). Furthermore, the Court in Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), while holding that the government may detain excludable aliens, emphasized that the detention must be reasonable and not indefinite (Mezei, 345 U.S. at 215-216). However, the Court in Zadvydas distinguished Mezei on the grounds that it involved an excludable alien, whereas Zadvydas was an admissible alien (Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 693).

The statute's broad application to aliens ordered removed for various reasons, including minor infractions, raises concerns about the proportionality of the detention, as discussed in Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893), where the Court emphasized the importance of proportionality in deportation proceedings (Fong Yue Ting, 149 U.S. at 730). The government's detention of an alien beyond a reasonable time necessary to effect removal would be disproportionate and thus unconstitutional.

The Supreme Court's decision in Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), is distinguishable from the present case, as it involved the detention of aliens pending deportation proceedings, whereas Zadvydas involves post-removal-period detention (Carlson, 342 U.S. at 538-539). However, the Court in Carlson emphasized that the government's detention authority is not unlimited and must be exercised reasonably (Carlson, 342 U.S. at 545).

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. The government's detention of Zadvydas beyond a reasonable time necessary to effect his removal is therefore unlawful. As the Court held in United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537 (1950), the government's actions must be guided by the principles of due process and the rule of law (Knauff, 338 U.S. at 544).

In conclusion, the post-removal-period statute contains an implicit "reasonable time" limitation, and the government's detention of Zadvydas beyond that reasonable time is unlawful. The judgment of the district court ordering Zadvydas' release should be affirmed.
