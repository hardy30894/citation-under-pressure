**ARGUMENT**

The judgment should be affirmed because Rule 32’s advisement requirement is intended to ensure that a defendant who may wish to appeal is informed of that right; it is not a freestanding basis for vacating an otherwise valid sentence where the defendant already knew of the right and chose not to appeal. This Court has repeatedly held that not every violation of a procedural rule warrants collateral relief. See *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (Rule 32 violation not cognizable under § 2255 absent “a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice”); *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 783-85 (1979) (collateral relief unavailable for formal Rule 11 error where no constitutional or jurisdictional defect and no complete miscarriage of justice).

I. **A failure to advise of appellate rights does not itself justify habeas relief when the defendant already knew of the right.**

Rule 32 requires the sentencing court to advise a defendant of any right to appeal. See *Peguero v. United States*, 526 U.S. 23, 25 (1999). But the rule’s object is to protect appellate access, not to create an automatic windfall. Where the defendant was already aware of the right, the omission causes no injury cognizable on collateral review. Id. at 27-28. As this Court explained in *Peguero*, “[w]hen a defendant is aware of his right to appeal and yet chooses not to appeal, he suffers no prejudice from the district court’s failure to advise him of the right.” Id. at 29. That is dispositive here, because the district court found after an evidentiary hearing that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. Under *Peguero*, there is no basis for habeas relief.

This conclusion accords with the Court’s general collateral-review jurisprudence. In *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428, the Court held that a mere failure to follow Rule 32 does not warrant § 2255 relief absent a miscarriage of justice or exceptional circumstances. Likewise, *Timmreck*, 441 U.S. at 784, rejected the proposition that “technical” violations of the criminal rules automatically justify collateral reversal. The same principle governs here: even assuming the court erred by not giving the Rule 32 advisement, the error was harmless because it did not deprive Peguero of any meaningful opportunity to appeal. See *United States v. Lane*, 474 U.S. 438, 449 (1986) (errors that do not affect substantial rights do not require reversal); *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 637-38 (1993) (collateral relief is reserved for errors that had a “substantial and injurious effect or influence” on the proceeding).

II. **Collateral relief is unavailable absent prejudice, and the record establishes none.**

The habeas statutes do not provide a vehicle for correcting every procedural misstep. *Timmreck*, 441 U.S. at 784; *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428. The key question is prejudice. In the context of lost appellate rights, the Court has looked to whether the defendant was deprived of an appeal he otherwise would have taken. See *Rodriquez v. United States*, 395 U.S. 327, 329-30 (1969) (defendant denied appeal because counsel failed to file requested notice entitled to resentencing so appeal could proceed); *Roe v. Flores-Ortega*, 528 U.S. 470, 477 (2000) (ineffective assistance claim requires showing that counsel’s deficient performance caused forfeiture of an appeal the defendant would have pursued). Peguero made no such showing. To the contrary, the lower courts found that he knew of his right but elected not to appeal. That finding defeats any claim of prejudice under *Flores-Ortega*’s logic and forecloses relief under *Rodriquez* as well, because there was no lost appeal caused by governmental omission.

The Court of Appeals properly treated the Rule 32 violation as subject to harmless-error analysis. That approach is consistent with the Court’s treatment of other trial and sentencing errors. See *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967) (constitutional errors may be harmless); *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 8-9 (1999) (instructional error harmless where omitted element uncontested and supported by overwhelming evidence). Even assuming Rule 32 error, the district court’s finding of actual knowledge makes the omission harmless beyond dispute.

III. **Petitioner’s contrary rule would convert Rule 32 into an automatic vacatur provision, contrary to precedent and sound administration.**

Petitioner’s position would require vacatur for any failure to recite appellate rights, regardless of actual knowledge, prejudice, or finality. This Court rejected that kind of per se approach in *Hill*, *Timmreck*, and *Brecht*, each of which emphasizes that collateral relief is extraordinary and reserved for serious, outcome-altering defects. See also *Reed v. Farley*, 512 U.S. 339, 353-54 (1994) (collateral relief unavailable for nonconstitutional error unless it amounts to a complete miscarriage of justice); *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 167-68 (1982) (habeas review requires showing of actual prejudice). A contrary rule would invite needless resentencing even when the defendant suffered no harm, undermining finality without advancing any constitutional value.

Because Peguero knew of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it, the district court’s failure to repeat that right at sentencing caused no prejudice and does not furnish a basis for habeas relief. The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
