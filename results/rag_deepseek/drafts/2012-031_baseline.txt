**ARGUMENT**

The district court certified a class of over one million cable subscribers without requiring adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. That decision cannot stand. This Court has repeatedly emphasized that Rule 23(b)(3) demands a “rigorous analysis” of whether common questions predominate, and that analysis “will entail some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff’s underlying claim.” *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 351 (2011). The district court failed to conduct that rigorous analysis here.

**I. The District Court Erred by Certifying a Class Without Requiring Admissible Evidence That Damages Could Be Measured on a Class-Wide Basis**

The predominance inquiry under Rule 23(b)(3) requires the party seeking certification to demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that damages are capable of proof through common evidence. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 320 (3d Cir. 2008). The district court accepted plaintiffs’ expert’s damages model, but that model was not tied to the sole theory of antitrust impact on which certification was granted—deterrence of overbuilder entry. The expert’s model measured damages from all anticompetitive conduct, not just the overbuilder theory. As this Court later recognized, a damages model that does not isolate the alleged antitrust violation cannot support class certification. *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 35-36 (2013).

The district court’s error was compounded by its failure to require admissible evidence. The court relied on expert reports and testimony that were not subjected to the scrutiny required by *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993). At the certification stage, a district court must “resolve factual disputes” and “evaluate the admissibility of expert evidence” to ensure that the proposed class can be proven through common evidence. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 323. The court here did not do so. It accepted the expert’s model despite its failure to account for individual variations in cable pricing, subscriber behavior, and market conditions across the Philadelphia DMA. That model could not, as a matter of law, provide a common methodology for measuring damages.

**II. The Damages Model Was Not Consistent with the Theory of Liability**

A class cannot be certified unless the plaintiffs’ damages model is “consistent with [their] class-wide theory of antitrust impact.” *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35. Here, the district court limited the class’s theory of impact to deterrence of overbuilder entry. Yet plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. McClave, calculated damages based on a benchmark that compared Comcast’s prices to prices in markets where overbuilders were present. That benchmark did not isolate the effect of the alleged clustering conduct; it captured all price differences, including those attributable to lawful competition or other factors. The model thus failed to “measure only those damages attributable to the theory” of liability. *Id.* at 36.

This Court has long recognized that antitrust damages must be “the result of the unlawful conduct” and not “speculative or remote.” *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946). A model that conflates lawful and unlawful price effects cannot satisfy Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance requirement. The district court’s acceptance of such a model was clear error.

**III. The District Court’s Analysis Was Insufficiently Rigorous**

The district court conducted a four-day evidentiary hearing and issued an 81-page opinion, but that does not immunize its decision from review. The court’s analysis was flawed because it failed to “probe behind the pleadings” and “resolve factual disputes” necessary to determine whether common questions predominated. *Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 350-51. The court did not address the fundamental disconnect between the damages model and the theory of impact. It did not require plaintiffs to show that the model could be applied uniformly to all class members. And it did not consider whether individual issues—such as differences in cable packages, pricing tiers, and subscriber preferences—would overwhelm common questions.

The court’s error is particularly acute given the nature of the cable market. As this Court has observed, cable operators often possess “undue market power” and exercise “control over most (if not all) of the television programming that is channeled into the subscriber’s home.” *Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC*, 512 U.S. 622, 656 (1994). But that market power is not uniform across all subscribers. Pricing and programming vary by franchise area, by subscriber package, and over time. A class-wide damages model that ignores these variations cannot satisfy Rule 23(b)(3).

**Conclusion**

The district court certified a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. The court accepted a damages model that was not tied to the theory of liability and failed to conduct the rigorous analysis required by Rule 23 and this Court’s precedents. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
