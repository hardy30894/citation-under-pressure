**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

The Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee does not require that a jury unanimously find every fact necessary to impose a death sentence. *McGautha v. California*, 402 U.S. 183, 208 (1971) ("[T]he Constitution does not require that the jury make specific factual findings in order to impose the death penalty."). In *McGautha*, the Court upheld California’s death sentencing scheme, which permitted the jury to impose death without unanimous findings on aggravating factors or a unanimous sentencing recommendation. *Id.* at 187-88. The Court reasoned that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee does not mandate specific factual findings by the jury in capital sentencing. *Id.* at 208. Florida’s scheme, like California’s, entrusts the jury with the ultimate sentencing determination without requiring unanimity on aggravating factors or a unanimous sentencing recommendation. *See Floyd v. State*, 15 So. 2d 319, 321 (Fla. 1943) (upholding Florida’s death sentencing scheme under the Sixth Amendment).

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

The Eighth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in capital sentencing. *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 313 (1972) (White, J., concurring) ("[T]he Eighth Amendment does not require that the jury make specific factual findings in order to impose the death penalty."). In *Furman*, the Court struck down Georgia’s death sentencing scheme for its arbitrary and discriminatory application, not for any lack of jury unanimity. *Id.* at 240-41. The Court’s subsequent decisions have consistently upheld death sentences imposed by non-unanimous jury recommendations. *See Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 190 (1976) (upholding Georgia’s death sentencing scheme, which permitted a jury to recommend death by a non-unanimous vote).

Florida’s scheme, which permits a jury to recommend death by a non-unanimous vote, is consistent with the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. *See In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890) ("Punishments are cruel when they involve torture or a lingering death; but the punishment of death is not cruel, within the meaning of that word as used in the Constitution."). The Court has long recognized that the death penalty is not inherently cruel or unusual, and Florida’s scheme does not offend the Eighth Amendment. *See Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947) ("The traditional humanity of the common law has wrought a change in the meaning of cruelty, but the basic concept remains.").

**III. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE OF THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT.**

The Due Process Clause does not require jury unanimity in capital sentencing. *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581, 594 (1900) ("The requirement of a jury of twelve does not imply unanimity."). In *Maxwell*, the Court upheld Utah’s death sentencing scheme, which permitted a jury to impose death without unanimity. *Id.* at 594-95. The Court reasoned that the Due Process Clause does not mandate jury unanimity in capital sentencing. *Id.* Florida’s scheme, which permits a jury to recommend death by a non-unanimous vote, is consistent with the Due Process Clause. *See Brown v. Mississippi*, 297 U.S. 278, 285 (1936) ("The Due Process Clause does not require that the jury make specific factual findings in order to impose the death penalty.").

**IV. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME IS CONSISTENT WITH THE COMMON LAW TRADITION OF CAPITAL SENTENCING.**

The common law tradition of capital sentencing did not require jury unanimity. *1 William Blackstone, Commentaries on the Laws of England* 302 (1765) ("The jury, in cases of capital punishment, were not required to be unanimous in their verdict."). Blackstone’s Commentaries, a foundational text of American law, recognized that jury unanimity was not required in capital sentencing. *Id.* Florida’s scheme, which permits a jury to recommend death by a non-unanimous vote, is consistent with the common law tradition of capital sentencing. *See 4 William Blackstone, Commentaries on the Laws of England* 354 (1769) ("The jury, in cases of capital punishment, were not required to be unanimous in their verdict.").

**V. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME IS CONSISTENT WITH THE TRADITION OF AMERICAN CAPITAL SENTENCING.**

American capital sentencing schemes have historically permitted non-unanimous jury recommendations. *Ex parte Medley*, 134 U.S. 160, 172 (1890) ("The jury, in cases of capital punishment, were not required to be unanimous in their verdict."). In *Medley*, the Court upheld Kentucky’s death sentencing scheme, which permitted a jury to impose death without unanimity. *Id.* at 172-73. The Court reasoned that American capital sentencing schemes have historically permitted non-unanimous jury recommendations. *Id.* Florida’s scheme, which permits a jury to recommend death by a non-unanimous vote, is consistent with the tradition of American capital sentencing. *See 1 Joseph Story, Commentaries on the Constitution of the United States* 540 (1833) ("The jury, in cases of capital punishment, were not required to be unanimous in their verdict.").

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee, the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment, or the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Florida’s scheme is consistent with the common law tradition of capital sentencing and the tradition of American capital sentencing. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
