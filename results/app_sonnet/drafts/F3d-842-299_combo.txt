ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Lacked Subject Matter Jurisdiction Because the Pressls’ Complaint Raises No Federal Question

A. The well-pleaded complaint rule forecloses federal jurisdiction here.

Whether a case “arises under” federal law for purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 1331 is governed by the well-pleaded complaint rule. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149 (1908). Under Mottley, a federal question must appear on the face of the plaintiff’s complaint, unaided by anything alleged in anticipation of a defense. Id. at 152. The Pressls’ complaint seeks a declaration of their rights under a private easement instrument, construed according to Virginia property law. It asks the state court to declare that “APCO has no regulatory authority over the plaintiffs’ property . . . beyond those rights defined by the flowage easement.” Nothing in that pleading invokes federal law; it invokes the language of a 1960 deed and Virginia rules of construction.

That APCO may raise its FERC license, or the Federal Power Act, as a defense to the Pressls’ claim does not convert the action into one arising under federal law. “[A] case is not removable simply because the defendant relies upon a federal defense.” Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 116 (1936) (federal question must be “an essential element of the plaintiff’s cause of action”). Whether the easement’s reservation of rights to the Pressls is “inconsistent with” APCO’s operation of a federally licensed dam is a question of contract and property interpretation, not federal statutory construction. The Federal Power Act may inform the factual backdrop, but a case does not arise under federal law merely because federal regulation forms part of the setting in which a state-law right is asserted. Moore v. Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co., 291 U.S. 205, 216-17 (1934) (right asserted must be “created by” federal law, not merely affected by it). This principle governs squarely here: the fact that APCO operates under a federal license does not transform a landowner’s state-law easement dispute into a federal case. See Pan Am. Petroleum Corp. v. Superior Court, 366 U.S. 656, 663 (1961) (state-law claims against a federally regulated party do not become federal merely because federal regulation touches the relationship between the parties).

B. As master of their complaint, the Pressls were entitled to proceed exclusively under state law.

It is elementary that “the party who brings a suit is master to decide what law he will rely upon.” The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co., 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913). The Pressls could have framed a claim testing APCO’s conduct against the Federal Power Act or FERC’s license conditions; they did not. They chose instead to seek a declaration of rights under the easement as a matter of Virginia law. That choice is dispositive under Mottley and The Fair, and APCO cannot rewrite the complaint on removal to manufacture jurisdiction that the Pressls declined to invoke.

The declaratory posture of the complaint does not change this analysis. A declaratory judgment action does not itself supply an independent basis of federal jurisdiction; a court must look through the declaratory form to the underlying claim that would arise in a coercive suit. Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., 339 U.S. 667, 671-72 (1950) (jurisdiction under the Declaratory Judgment Act depends on whether the coercive action the declaratory defendant could have brought would itself be one “arising under” federal law). Looking through the Pressls’ declaratory complaint, the coercive analogue is a state-law suit to enforce or construe an easement—no different from any quiet-title or property dispute that Virginia courts routinely decide. There is no federal cause of action lurking beneath the declaratory request.

C. Section 825p does not supply jurisdiction because the complaint alleges no violation of the Federal Power Act.

The district court also erred in relying on 16 U.S.C. § 825p, which grants federal jurisdiction over suits to enforce liabilities or duties created by the Federal Power Act or by orders issued under it. The Pressls allege no violation of the Act or of any FERC order; they allege that APCO is demanding more than the easement’s plain terms permit. The Federal Power Act’s licensing scheme, described in First Iowa Hydro-Electric Cooperative v. Federal Power Commission, 328 U.S. 152, 164 (1946), governs the relationship between the licensee and the federal government over the project’s operation; it does not convert every private property dispute touching a licensed project into a federal case. Because the complaint alleges no breach of a duty “created” by the Act, § 825p cannot furnish jurisdiction. Gully, 299 U.S. at 112.

II. The Removal Statute Must Be Strictly Construed Against Federal Jurisdiction

Doubts about jurisdiction are to be resolved in favor of remand. Because federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction, statutes conferring removal jurisdiction are to be strictly construed, and “the policy of the successive acts of Congress” favors restriction, not expansion, of federal jurisdiction on removal. Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets, 313 U.S. 100, 108-09 (1941). The burden of establishing jurisdiction rests on the party seeking removal. Wilson v. Republic Iron & Steel Co., 257 U.S. 92, 97 (1921). APCO cannot meet that burden on this record, where the complaint sounds entirely in state easement law.

CONCLUSION

Because the Pressls’ complaint presents no federal question and § 825p does not apply, the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction. This Court should vacate the judgment of dismissal and remand with instructions to return the case to the Circuit Court for Franklin County, Virginia.
