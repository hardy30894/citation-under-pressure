# ARGUMENT

## I. The Plain Text of the FDCPA Defines a "Debt Collector" by Reference to Whom the Collector Acts For, Not by the Debt's Default Status When Acquired

"[T]he meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain … the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 1692a(6) supplies that plain language: a "debt collector" is a person "who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due *another*." The word "another" is the operative term, and it asks a single question—for whom is the collector acting at the time it collects? It does not ask when, or under what circumstances, the collector came to hold the debt.

Petitioners would rewrite that inquiry by importing the debt's default status—drawn from a *different* provision, the creditor exclusion in § 1692a(4)—into the threshold definition of § 1692a(6). But this Court has long insisted that a qualifying clause attaches to the language it actually modifies, not to language borrowed from elsewhere in the statute for convenience. In *Porto Rico Railway, Light & Power Co. v. Mor*, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920), the Court explained that "[w]hen several words are followed by a clause which is applicable as much to the first and other words as to the last, the natural construction of the language demands that the clause be read as applicable to all." The converse is equally true: a clause that is *not* naturally applicable to a phrase should not be grafted onto it. Nothing in § 1692a(6)'s "owed or due another" language contains any temporal reference to default. That concept appears only in the separate exclusionary provisions of §§ 1692a(4) and 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which serve entirely different structural purposes and should not be read backward into the operative definition.

Once Santander purchased the receivables outright, it was collecting debts owed to *itself*, not to "another." That is dispositive under the statute's own terms. It is "elementary" that this Court gives operative words their ordinary, contemporary meaning, *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485, and the ordinary meaning of collecting a debt "owed … another" does not encompass collecting a debt one now owns outright.

## II. The Statute's Exclusions Do Not Enlarge the Threshold Definition They Qualify

Petitioners' argument depends on treating the § 1692a(4) creditor exclusion and the § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) debt-collector exclusion as though they independently define who is a debt collector. They do not. This Court has repeatedly held that "it is the duty of the court to give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute," *Montclair v. Ramsdell*, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883), but that duty requires reading exceptions and provisos as qualifications of the provisions to which they are attached—not as freestanding definitions that operate independently of, or prior to, the substantive text they modify. As Justice Cardozo explained for the Court, a proviso's "natural" office "is to qualify the sentence next preceding" it, and it should not be extended beyond what its own terms embrace. *American Surety Co. v. Marotta*, 287 U.S. 513, 517 (1933).

Applied here, the fact that § 1692a(4) excludes from "creditor" a person who receives a defaulted debt "solely for the purpose of facilitating collection … for another" says nothing about whether a person who purchases a defaulted debt outright, for its own account, is a "debt collector." That exclusion, by its own terms, addresses only servicing arrangements undertaken *on behalf of* another party. Likewise, the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) for pre-default debt serviced for another presupposes that the person is already collecting "on behalf of another" under the threshold definition; it does not itself supply that predicate. Petitioners' syllogism—that anything excluded from "creditor" must be a "debt collector"—asks this Court to treat isolated provisos as though they were substitutes for the operative text of § 1692a(6), contrary to the settled rule that qualifying language is confined to what it actually modifies. *Porto Rico Ry.*, 253 U.S. at 348; *American Surety Co.*, 287 U.S. at 517.

## III. Congress's Evident Purpose Confirms That a Creditor Collecting Its Own Accounts Is Not Regulated as a Debt Collector

Where statutory language admits of doubt, this Court looks to "the evil which [the legislature] designed to remedy," *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 464 (1892), and to the words by which the legislature "undertook to give expression to its wishes," *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress's target was abusive third-party collection agents pursuing debts on behalf of others—not consumer finance companies collecting accounts they own. A rule turning entirely on default status at acquisition would sweep in every ordinary purchaser of a defaulted receivable, including banks and finance companies collecting their own portfolios, a result nothing in the statute's text or evident purpose supports. The judgment below should be affirmed.
