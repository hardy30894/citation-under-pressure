**Argument**

The Equal Protection Clause does not support a cause of action on the facts alleged here, because Olech does not claim membership in any protected class and does not identify any constitutional basis for treating her different from similarly situated property owners other than the Village’s supposed animus. The court of appeals nonetheless recognized a free-standing “spite” claim. That approach cannot be squared with this Court’s equal protection precedents.

The Equal Protection Clause “is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985). But this Court has repeatedly explained that equal protection challenges ordinarily require the plaintiff to show discriminatory treatment based on membership in a class or some other constitutionally relevant classification. See *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239-42 (1976); *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 274-80 (1979). The Clause is not a general federal tort law for every assertedly unfair act by local government officials. *Baker v. McCollan*, 443 U.S. 137, 146 (1979).

This Court has, in limited circumstances, allowed equal protection claims by plaintiffs who do not allege membership in a suspect class, but those cases still involve a cognizable classification. In *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, the Seventh Circuit below recognized a claim where the plaintiff alleged only that the Village demanded a larger easement from her than from others. But this Court’s decision in *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Commission of Webster County*, 488 U.S. 336, 345-46 (1989), rested on the government’s intentional and systematic unequal assessment of similarly situated property owners, not on subjective hostility alone. Likewise, in *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373-74 (1886), the Court invalidated discriminatory enforcement of an ordinance administered with “an evil eye and an unequal hand.” Those cases involve discriminatory classification or administration, not a generalized constitutional claim for alleged municipal resentment.

The Seventh Circuit’s “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him” formulation also conflicts with this Court’s insistence that equal protection requires more than proof of ill will. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944), the Court held that unequal application of a statute violates equal protection only when there is “an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination.” But purposeful discrimination is a means of proving a constitutionally cognizable classification; it does not itself create a new cause of action untethered to class-based treatment. *Wayte v. United States*, 470 U.S. 598, 608-10 (1985). Otherwise, every discretionary land-use dispute could be converted into a federal constitutional case whenever a plaintiff alleges bad motives.

That is especially true in the municipal permitting context. Local governments necessarily draw lines, make judgments, and apply standards to different properties in different ways. This Court has cautioned against converting ordinary errors or harsh decisions by public officials into constitutional claims. *Daniels v. Williams*, 474 U.S. 327, 332-33 (1986); *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846-47 (1998). If animus alone were enough, federal courts would be forced to supervise routine local land-use disputes, contrary to the limited role of the Equal Protection Clause. See *Collins v. City of Harker Heights*, 503 U.S. 115, 128-29 (1992).

Respondent will likely rely on the premise that she was singled out for a 33-foot easement while others were required to grant only 15 feet. But selective treatment, without more, does not establish a constitutional violation. A plaintiff must still show that the differential treatment lacked a rational basis and was tied to a cognizable equal protection theory. *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313-15 (1993). The complaint here does not identify a suspect class, a fundamental right, or an irrational classification that the Village adopted as a rule applicable to Olech alone. At most, it alleges an individualized dispute over municipal conditions.

This Court should reject the invitation to constitutionalize such claims. The Equal Protection Clause guarantees equal laws, not a federal remedy for every allegation of municipal spite. Because Olech alleged neither membership in a class nor a constitutionally recognized discriminatory classification, her complaint fails to state an equal protection claim. The judgment below should be reversed.
