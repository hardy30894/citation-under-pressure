**Argument**

I. **TPS does not itself supply the “admission” § 1255 demands.**

Section 1255(a) authorizes adjustment only for an alien “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). Congress separately defined “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). That definition controls. See **Loeffler v. Frank**, 486 U.S. 549, 562 (1988) (a statutory term’s meaning is fixed by the text Congress enacted); **United States v. Soto-Olivas**, 44 F.3d 788, 789 (9th Cir. 1995) (text governs where Congress supplied a definition). Because TPS is a status conferred on aliens already present in the United States, not a lawful entry after inspection and authorization, it is not an “admission.” See **Maggio v. Zeitz**, 333 U.S. 56, 61 (1948) (courts must give statutory words their ordinary legal effect); **United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns**, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (statutory terms should be read in light of the words Congress used).

The government’s contrary reading collapses two distinct concepts—entry and status—that Congress kept separate. The Court has long recognized that words must be given distinct meaning when Congress uses them in the same scheme. **Montclair v. Ramsdell**, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883). Here, § 1254a(f)(4) says a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” but it says nothing about being “inspected and admitted.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). Congress knew how to confer admission-like consequences when it wanted to; its omission here is decisive. See **Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Prods. Corp.**, 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (courts may not add words Congress omitted); **Iselin v. United States**, 270 U.S. 245, 250–51 (1926) (judicial construction cannot supply what Congress left out).

II. **The structure of the INA confirms that TPS is a status, not an admission.**

Section 1255(a) requires both a qualifying entry and, separately, continued eligibility for status adjustment. If lawful TPS status were itself an admission, the threshold requirement in § 1255(a) would do no work. Courts avoid interpretations that make statutory language pointless. **Gustavson v. Alloyd Co.**, 513 U.S. 561, 574 (1995); **United States v. Menasche**, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955). Congress also used different language in adjacent provisions, showing it understood the distinction. See **Gozlon-Peretz v. United States**, 498 U.S. 395, 404 (1991) (different terms in a statute are presumed to have different meaning); **Jarecki v. G. D. Searle & Co.**, 367 U.S. 303, 307–08 (1961) (same).

III. **The purpose of TPS is temporary protection from removal, not a backdoor admission.**

TPS was enacted to prevent removal to dangerous countries, not to rewrite the admission requirement for permanent residence. See **Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States**, 143 U.S. 457, 459–60 (1892) (statutes are interpreted in light of their purpose); **United States v. Classic**, 313 U.S. 299, 311 (1941) (purpose informs construction). Reading TPS as admission would convert a temporary humanitarian shield into a permanent immigration reset, contrary to Congress’s design. See **Tennessee Valley Auth. v. Hill**, 437 U.S. 153, 174 (1978) (courts enforce statutory limits even when policy arguments point elsewhere); **Caminetti v. United States**, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (where text is clear, courts apply it as written).

IV. **The contrary rule would improperly expand eligibility by judicial amendment.**

Adjustment of status is a discretionary benefit, and courts may not expand eligibility beyond Congress’s terms. See **Caminetti**, 242 U.S. at 485; **Hepburn v. Griswold**, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 603, 625 (1869) (courts cannot vary the statutory command). Petitioners entered without inspection or admission. TPS later allowed them to remain lawfully, but it did not retroactively create the lawful entry § 1255(a) requires. Because Congress attached adjustment eligibility to admission, and because TPS is only a status, petitioners remain ineligible under the statute as written.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
