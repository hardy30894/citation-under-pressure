**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Admitting Expert Testimony Regarding the Cause of the Line’s Parting, as Such Testimony Was Not Based on Scientific, Technical, or Specialized Knowledge.**

The trial court’s admission of American Steamship’s expert opinion on the cause of the line’s parting constitutes reversible error. Under *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923), expert testimony must be “deduced from a well-recognized scientific principle or discovery.” Here, the expert merely speculated that the line parted due to Cook’s alleged misuse of a propane torch, without any testing, physical evidence, or established methodology. See *Johnston v. United States*, 87 F. Supp. 955 (E.D. Wash. 1949) (rejecting expert opinion lacking factual foundation). The expert’s opinion was no more than conjecture, and its admission prejudiced Cook by confusing the jury on the cause of the accident. *Iacone v. United States*, 197 F.2d 704 (2d Cir. 1952). The judgment must be vacated.

**II. The District Court Erred in Denying Cook’s Motion for a Directed Verdict on Unseaworthiness, Because the Evidence Conclusively Established That the Vessel Was Unseaworthy.**

The Jones Act and general maritime law impose a non-delegable duty to provide a seaworthy vessel. *Seaboard Shipping Corp. v. S/S “Ore Transport”*, 230 F.2d 79 (2d Cir. 1956) (citing *The Steamer Syracuse*, 74 U.S. 167 (1868)). Unseaworthiness exists where a vessel’s appurtenances, including lines and equipment, are not reasonably fit for their intended use. *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96 (1944). Here, the line supporting Cook’s bos’n’s chair parted without warning, rendering it unseaworthy as a matter of law. See *The H.A. Scandrett*, 87 F.2d 708 (3d Cir. 1937) (a parting line establishes unseaworthiness). Cook was not required to prove a specific defect; the mere failure of equipment under normal use suffices. *Cleveland-Cliffs Iron Co. v. Driver*, 107 F.2d 1013 (6th Cir. 1939). The district court erred in submitting this issue to the jury.

**III. The District Court Did Not Err in Denying Cook’s Motion for a Directed Verdict on Comparative Negligence, as the Evidence Raised a Jury Question.**

While Cook was not negligent as a matter of law, the district court properly declined to direct a verdict on this issue. Under the Jones Act, comparative negligence requires a showing that the seaman “knew or should have known of the danger and failed to act with ordinary prudence.” *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424 (1939). Here, the jury could have inferred that Cook’s use of the propane torch near the line was imprudent. However, the erroneous admission of the expert testimony on causation likely tainted the jury’s apportionment of fault. See *Atlantic Coast Line R.R. v. Powe*, 283 U.S. 401 (1931) (error in admitting evidence of damages requires new trial). On remand, the jury must re-determine negligence without the improper opinion.

**IV. The District Court Did Not Err in Admitting Evidence of Cook’s Alcohol-Related Aliments, as Such Evidence Was Relevant to Damages.**

Evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments was properly admitted under *Standard Accident Insurance Co. v. Rossi*, 35 F.2d 667 (6th Cir. 1929), which holds that a plaintiff’s pre-existing condition may be considered by the jury in assessing the extent of injury. The district court did not abuse its discretion, as the evidence was not offered to suggest negligence but rather to mitigate damages. See *Missouri-Kansas-Texas R.R. v. Bounds*, 224 F.2d 774 (10th Cir. 1955). Accordingly, this ruling should be affirmed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below must be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial on all liability and damage issues, with the exclusion of the improper expert testimony and with a directed verdict on unseaworthiness.
