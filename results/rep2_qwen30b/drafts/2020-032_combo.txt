**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The revision of Georgia Gwinnett College’s “Freedom of Expression Policy” and the removal of the challenged provision of its Student Code of Conduct rendered the plaintiffs’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot, and the claim for nominal damages cannot revive a case that has otherwise become moot. This conclusion is compelled by settled principles of standing and mootness, which have long governed the federal judiciary’s power to adjudicate live controversies.

The doctrine of mootness is rooted in the constitutional requirement that federal courts may only decide actual cases or controversies. *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17, 21 (1960). When a party’s claim becomes “academic” or “hypothetical” due to a change in circumstances, the case is no longer justiciable. *See id.* at 20–21. In this case, the challenged policies were entirely revised and replaced with a new regime that permits students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances. The district court correctly concluded that this change “unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies and there is no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them.” *See Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc) (applying *Raines*); *cf. United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950) (holding that “a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer live”).

The Supreme Court has long held that a plaintiff’s standing to pursue injunctive or declaratory relief is extinguished when the challenged conduct ceases and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence. *See *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962) (“[T]he case or controversy requirement is not satisfied when the issue is no longer a live one.”); *cf. *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953) (holding that “a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer live or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome”). Here, the plaintiffs have not alleged any intent to return to the prior policy, nor have they demonstrated any likelihood of future enforcement. Indeed, Uzuegbunam has graduated, and neither he nor Bradford has attempted to engage in expressive activity on campus since the incident in 2016. The absence of any present or imminent threat of enforcement renders the claim for injunctive relief moot.

Nor can the claim for nominal damages save the case. The Supreme Court has consistently held that nominal damages alone do not suffice to maintain standing where the underlying claim has become moot. *See *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 632 (“[W]hen the controversy has ceased, the court has no power to act, even though the plaintiff may have a right to nominal damages.”); *cf. *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. at 39 (“[T]he court may not act when the issues are no longer live.”). In *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, the Court rejected the argument that a claim for nominal damages could preserve jurisdiction over a case that had become moot, emphasizing that “the right to nominal damages is not a substitute for a live controversy.” 345 U.S. at 632. Similarly, in *United States v. Raines*, the Court held that “a case is not justiciable if the issues presented are no longer live,” even if the plaintiff seeks only nominal relief. 362 U.S. at 21.

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied this precedent in *Flanigan’s Enterprises*, where it held that “a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.” 868 F.3d at 1255. The court reasoned that “nominal damages are not a substitute for a live controversy,” and that “the mere possibility of a future violation is insufficient to sustain standing.” *Id.* at 1256. This reasoning is consistent with the long-standing principle that “a plaintiff must show a real and immediate threat of future injury to maintain standing.” *See *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. at 204; *cf. *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 632.

The plaintiffs’ attempt to distinguish their case on the ground that it involves an “as-applied” challenge is unavailing. The Supreme Court has made clear that even as-applied challenges are subject to the mootness doctrine. *See *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. at 39 (“[T]he court may not act when the issues are no longer live.”); *cf. *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. at 21. The fact that the plaintiffs allege they were subjected to enforcement of the prior policy does not revive a claim that has become moot by virtue of the policy’s repeal. *See *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 632.

Finally, the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying leave to amend. *See *Santiago v. Wood*, 904 F.2d 673, 675 (11th Cir. 1990) (reviewing denial of leave to amend for abuse of discretion). The plaintiffs sought to amend their complaint through a response to a motion to dismiss, a procedurally improper method. *See *Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (“[W]here a request for leave to file an amended complaint simply is imbedded within an opposition memorandum, the issue has not been raised properly.”
