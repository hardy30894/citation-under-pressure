## ARGUMENT

The D.C. Circuit erred by perpetuating an artificial distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals that the Supreme Court repudiated in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012). Judicial review of a Merit Systems Protection Board decision dismissing a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds lies in district court—not the Federal Circuit.

### I. The Statutory Text and *Kloeckner* Require District Court Review for All Mixed Cases

The Civil Service Reform Act defines a "mixed case" as one in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board" and "alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination." 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). For such cases, Congress unequivocally directed that judicial review "shall be filed in district court." *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 47 (citing 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2)). The statute makes no distinction based on *why* the Board declined to reach the merits—whether for procedural untimeliness, lack of jurisdiction, or any other ground.

In *Kloeckner*, the Board dismissed the employee's mixed-case appeal as untimely—a procedural ground. *Id.* at 46. This Court held that the proper forum for judicial review was district court. *Id.* at 56. Critically, the Court explained that "the proper forum for judicial review is thus determined by the nature of the employee's claim, not by the nature of the Board's decision." *Id.* at 54. That holding applies with equal force here. Mr. Perry alleged discrimination and brought his case to the Board. The Board dismissed on jurisdictional grounds—a decision about the nature of his claim. Under *Kloeckner*, the forum turns on the employee's initial allegations, not on the Board's later characterization of its reasons for dismissal.

The D.C. Circuit relied on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), which held that jurisdictional dismissals go to the Federal Circuit. But *Kloeckner* effectively overruled *Powell* by rejecting the very premise that the basis for dismissal determines the forum. This Court in *Kloeckner* cited with approval the Second and Tenth Circuit decisions that had distinguished procedural from jurisdictional dismissals—but did so only to frame the circuit split it was resolving. *See Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 50 n.3. The Court did not endorse that distinction. To the contrary, it held that the statutory phrase "action which [the employee] may appeal to the Board" focuses on the *type* of action, not on whether the Board ultimately finds it appealable. *Id.* at 50-51.

### II. The Jurisdictional/Procedural Distinction Is Inconsistent with *Kloeckner*

The D.C. Circuit reasoned that jurisdictional dismissals differ because the action was never "appealable to the MSPB." *Perry v. Merit Sys. Prot. Bd.*, 829 F.3d 760, 768 (D.C. Cir. 2016). That reasoning conflates the employee's claim with the Board's ruling. An employee alleging that his retirement was involuntary due to discrimination has, at the pleading stage, brought an appealable action. The Board's subsequent determination that the action was voluntary and thus outside its jurisdiction does not retroactively erase that initial appealability. *Kloeckner* rejected this backward-looking approach, holding that the employee need only allege "an action appealable to the MSPB." 568 U.S. at 50 (emphasis added). The Board's dismissal does not alter what the employee alleged.

The D.C. Circuit also stressed that the Board can waive procedural defects but cannot waive jurisdictional ones. *Perry*, 829 F.3d at 769. That distinction is irrelevant to the statutory question. Congress created one rule for mixed cases: district court. The Board's ability to waive procedural errors says nothing about where judicial review lies when the Board refuses to hear the case. The Federal Circuit's contrary decision in *Conforto v. Merit Sys. Prot. Bd.*, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013), is inconsistent with *Kloeckner* and should be disapproved.

### III. Uniformity Concerns Cannot Override the Plain Statute

The D.C. Circuit invoked a policy interest in uniform interpretation of MSPB jurisdiction by a single forum—the Federal Circuit. *Perry*, 829 F.3d at 770. But this Court in *Kloeckner* rejected similar policy arguments, holding that "Congress has given the district courts jurisdiction to review mixed cases, and we must respect that choice." 568 U.S. at 55. Any concern about fragmented review is outweighed by the statutory directive.

Mr. Perry brought a mixed case alleging discrimination. The Board dismissed on jurisdictional grounds. Under *Kloeckner*, he is entitled to judicial review in district court. The D.C. Circuit's transfer to the Federal Circuit should be reversed.
