**Argument**

A grant of Temporary Protected Status does not itself satisfy § 1255(a)’s threshold requirement that an applicant have been “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). Congress defined “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). That definition turns on an entry event, not on a later grant of immigration status. The Court has already recognized that “the date of gaining a new status is not the same as the date of the physical event of entering the country.” Hanif v. Attorney General, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012). Likewise, “The words ‘entry’ and ‘into’ plainly indicate that ‘admission’ involves physical entrance into the country.” Taveras v. Attorney General, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013). TPS changes status; it does not retroactively create the lawful entry § 1255(a) demands.

The TPS statute confirms that distinction. Congress provided that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for adjustment purposes. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). But Congress did not say that a TPS recipient shall be “considered admitted,” “inspected and admitted,” or “paroled.” The omission is dispositive. When Congress includes particular language in one part of a statute and omits it in another, courts presume the difference is intentional. I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987). Congress elsewhere in § 1255 expressly created exceptions to the admission requirement for certain categories of aliens, see 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i), but it did not create such an exception for TPS recipients. Under the canon against surplusage, the Court must give effect to all of the statute’s words. Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004).

The structure of § 1255 also shows that admission and lawful status are distinct prerequisites. Section 1255(a) requires that the alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled.” Section 1255(c)(2) separately bars adjustment when the alien has failed to maintain lawful status. If lawful status itself satisfied the admission requirement, § 1255(a)’s threshold language would do no work. Courts should avoid reading statutes in a way that renders part of the text “inoperative or superfluous.” Hibbs, 542 U.S. at 101.

This Court’s precedent on TPS likewise points in the same direction. TPS is not “a program of entry for an alien.” De Leon-Ochoa v. Attorney General, 622 F.3d 341, 353–54 (3d Cir. 2010). Rather, it is a humanitarian protection for those already present in the United States who cannot safely be removed. Id. Congress designed TPS to prevent removal during temporary emergencies abroad, not to convert unlawful entrants into lawful admittees by implication. See id. at 353–54.

The Government’s contrary position would also create serious tension with § 1254a(h), which provides a special supermajority mechanism for Congress to adjust the status of TPS beneficiaries. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(h)(2). If § 1254a(f)(4) itself already made TPS recipients satisfy § 1255(a), then § 1254a(h)(2) would be largely unnecessary. Courts presume Congress does not legislate redundantly. Hibbs, 542 U.S. at 101.

The lower courts that have rejected petitioner’s position have conflated “lawful status” with “admission,” but this Court has already recognized that the concepts are different. Hanif, 694 F.3d at 485; Taveras, 731 F.3d at 290. Because petitioner entered without inspection or admission, and because TPS does not transform that unlawful entry into a lawful one, he remains statutorily ineligible for adjustment under § 1255(a). See Serrano v. Attorney General, 655 F.3d 1260, 1265 (11th Cir. 2011) (per curiam) (“That an alien with Temporary Protected Status has ‘lawful status as a nonimmigrant’ for purposes of adjusting his status does not change § 1255(a)’s threshold requirement that he is eligible for adjustment of status only if he was initially inspected and admitted or paroled.”).

For those reasons, the Court should hold that conferral of TPS under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a is not an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.
