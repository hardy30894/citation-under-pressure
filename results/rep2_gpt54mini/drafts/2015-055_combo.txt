**Argument**

The question is whether *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively on collateral review. It does. Under the controlling retroactivity framework, a new rule applies retroactively when it is substantive, not merely procedural. *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 310–11 (1989). A rule is substantive when it alters “the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). *Johnson* did precisely that: it invalidated the ACCA residual clause and thereby removed from the statute’s reach a category of defendants whose prior convictions were counted only under that clause. 576 U.S. at 597–602. Because *Johnson* narrowed the scope of ACCA punishment, it is substantive and therefore retroactive. *Welch v. United States*, 578 U.S. 120, 129–31 (2016).

That conclusion is also compelled by long-settled retroactivity principles predating *Teague*. The Court has repeatedly held that decisions construing a criminal statute to exclude conduct previously thought punishable apply to cases on collateral review because “a conviction and punishment are invalid under the new law.” *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620–21 (1998). Likewise, where a decision “places conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe,” it applies retroactively. *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667, 693–94 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in judgments). *Johnson* is of that character. Once the residual clause was struck down, the Government lacked authority to impose ACCA’s enhanced punishment based on predicates that qualified only under that clause. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 606.

The Government may argue that *Johnson* announced a rule about procedure because it concerned how courts assess predicate offenses. But the Court has rejected that sort of formalism. In *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616, 625 (1912), the Court explained that a change affecting the legal consequences of prior convictions is applied to later proceedings because the new rule determines the punishment authorized by law. And in *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 376–77 (1880), the Court held that an unconstitutional statute is void and cannot support punishment. *Johnson* follows the same logic: the residual clause could not lawfully support an ACCA sentence after the Court declared it void for vagueness.

Retroactivity is especially appropriate here because *Johnson* did not merely refine the method for classifying offenses; it removed an entire basis for enhanced punishment. The ACCA imposes a mandatory minimum of fifteen years only when the defendant has three qualifying predicates. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1). After *Johnson*, convictions that qualified only under the residual clause no longer count. That is the hallmark of a substantive rule. *Davis v. United States*, 417 U.S. 333, 346–47 (1974), held that a statutory interpretation narrowing criminal liability applies retroactively on collateral review because it means the defendant stands convicted of an act that the law does not make criminal. The same principle applies here: Welch stands subject to a sentence the law no longer authorizes.

Nor does any concern for finality overcome that constitutional defect. This Court has long recognized that “the finality of a judgment” cannot save a conviction or sentence imposed without lawful authority. *United States v. Mayer*, 235 U.S. 55, 67–69 (1914). And where a conviction rests on a legal rule later held invalid, collateral relief is available to prevent the continued operation of an unlawful sentence. *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178–79 (1947). Denying retroactive effect would leave in place sentences imposed under a clause the Constitution does not permit. That result is inconsistent with the basic rule that “[n]o man shall be punished criminally save upon clear and definite laws.” *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926).

Finally, this case fits comfortably within the Court’s broader retroactivity doctrine. In *Griffith v. Kentucky*, 479 U.S. 314, 328 (1987), the Court emphasized that new constitutional rules apply to all cases still pending on direct review. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 307, preserved collateral retroactivity for substantive rules, and *Johnson* falls squarely within that category. The decision invalidated part of the ACCA itself and thereby altered the range of punishment the statute permits. That is retroactive by definition. *Welch*, 578 U.S. at 129–31.

For those reasons, the Court should hold that *Johnson* applies retroactively to cases on collateral review and reverse the judgment below.
