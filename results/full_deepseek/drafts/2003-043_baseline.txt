**ARGUMENT**

The plain text of the Internal Revenue Code requires that the government assess each individual taxpayer before extending the statute of limitations for collection. Because the IRS never assessed the Gallettis or Briguglios individually, the three-year limitations period under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a) bars its untimely claims against them.

**I. The Code Requires Assessment Against Each Separate Taxpayer**

Section 6203 provides that an “assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer.” The term “taxpayer” is defined as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes both “an individual” and a “partnership.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). A partnership and its individual partners are therefore distinct taxpayers. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (recognizing that an assessment must identify the individual taxpayer). The implementing regulation confirms that a valid assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1.

When the IRS timely assessed the partnership here, it identified only the partnership as the taxpayer. That assessment extended the statute of limitations for collection to ten years solely as to the partnership. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a)(1). But the partners are separate taxpayers. The IRS never assessed them, nor did it bring a collection action within the three-year period of § 6501(a). Consequently, the limitations period for the partners expired long before the IRS filed its proofs of claim in bankruptcy.

**II. Ninth Circuit Precedent Confirms That Individual Assessment Is Required**

In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the IRS assessed unpaid taxes against a partnership and later claimed a lien against an alleged partner’s property. The district court held that “a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property … unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). This court affirmed, albeit on other grounds, but did not reject the district court’s reasoning. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458, 462-63.

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. There, a partner voluntarily paid a tax assessed against the partnership and then sought a refund. The court held only that the partner could not recover a refund of taxes he had paid, not that the IRS could collect from an unwilling partner without an individual assessment. *Id.* at 910. As the district court in *Young* explained, individual assessment is required “to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action.” *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). The Ninth Circuit’s affirmance did not contradict that principle.

**III. The Seventh Circuit’s Decision in *Wright* Is Distinguishable**

In *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only issue was whether the limitations period for the partners was tolled during the partnership’s bankruptcy. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562-63. That case does not address the question here: whether an assessment against the partnership alone suffices to extend the limitations period for the partners. It does not.

**IV. State Law Does Not Provide an Alternative Route to Collection**

The IRS contends that California partnership law makes partners jointly and severally liable, thus allowing collection without individual assessment. But California law is to the contrary: “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The IRS never obtained a judgment against the Gallettis or Briguglios. The time for doing so has expired under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). A state-law claim for which the limitations period has run is unenforceable and properly disallowed in bankruptcy. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year statute of limitations bars its untimely claims. The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
