Petitioner is entitled to relief for two independent reasons: the Board’s polling standard is inconsistent with the Act, and the Board’s application of that standard is unsupported by substantial evidence.

First, the Act does not authorize the Board to foreclose a narrowly tailored employee poll unless the employer already has evidence sufficient to withdraw recognition. The National Labor Relations Act protects employees’ freedom to choose whether to be represented, and the Board may not adopt a rule that impairs that choice by making a lawful poll practically unavailable. The Board’s policy choices must remain tied to the statute’s text and purposes. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33-34 (1937); NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp., 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963). Here, the Board’s rule collapses the distinction between a pre-withdrawal inquiry and the ultimate act of withdrawing recognition. That is irrational. A poll is a means of testing union support before an employer takes the far more consequential step of ending recognition. A standard that requires the employer already to possess proof adequate to justify withdrawal deprives polling of independent utility.

The Board’s own stated rationale confirms the error. The Act favors stable collective bargaining relationships, yet it also protects employees’ freedom to decide whether they wish union representation. See NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33-34 (1937). A poll conducted with notice, secrecy, and neutral supervision is a limited inquiry, not an anti-union weapon. Stripping employers of the ability to test objective indications of disaffection leaves them with a Hobson’s choice: bargain with a union they reasonably suspect has lost support or withdraw recognition at their peril. Nothing in the Act compels that choice. In analogous settings, this Court has rejected Board rules that transformed statutory standards into rigid formulas detached from practical realities. NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co., 304 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1938); Southern Steamship Co. v. NLRB, 316 U.S. 31, 47 (1942).

Second, even under the Board’s chosen framework, the finding that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. The substantial-evidence standard requires more than isolated references and conclusory skepticism; it requires record evidence that, on the record as a whole, reasonably supports the conclusion. Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229-30 (1938). And while reviewing courts defer to agency factfinding, they do not abdicate where the Board has ignored or distorted the record. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488-91 (1951).

Here, the record showed substantial employee disaffection. Prior to the poll, employees had made statements suggesting the union had lost support. The company did not rely on rumor alone; it used a secret-ballot poll supervised by a priest, and the result was 19 to 13 against the union. The Board nevertheless discounted this evidence by parsing individual comments in a manner inconsistent with common sense and the record as a whole. That is not substantial-evidence review.

Pre-1970 precedent confirms that the Board must evaluate all objective circumstances bearing on majority support. In NLRB v. R. H. Oswald Co., 217 F.2d 193 (7th Cir. 1954), the court recognized that an employer need not await formal decertification before acting on objective indications of lost support. And in NLRB v. Somerville Packing Co., 211 F.2d 574 (1st Cir. 1954), the court required the Board to assess the probative value of employee statements in context rather than dismiss them mechanically. Those principles were ignored here.

The Board’s treatment of the evidence was especially arbitrary because it discounted statements from employees who were in the unit at the time of the poll, treated equivocal comments as irrelevant despite their obvious probative force, and refused to credit reports from employees with firsthand knowledge of workplace sentiment. That approach conflicts with the settled rule that administrative findings cannot rest on isolated snippets when the record as a whole points the other way. NLRB v. Pittsburgh Steamship Co., 337 U.S. 656, 659-60 (1949). It also conflicts with the principle that an agency must explain why apparently probative evidence is rejected. Columbia Envelope Co. v. NLRB, 151 F.2d 571, 575 (7th Cir. 1945).

Finally, the Board’s order cannot be sustained because the company’s conduct was orderly and lawful under longstanding labor-law principles. The employees voted in secret; the poll was supervised by a neutral priest; and there is no finding of coercion. Under these circumstances, the Board’s insistence on a contrary inference from a thin and selectively viewed record is unreasonable. Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 798-99 (1945); NLRB v. Falk Corp., 308 U.S. 453, 460-61 (1940).

For these reasons, the Court should hold that the Board’s polling standard is not rationally consistent with the Act, and that the finding against Allentown Mack is unsupported by substantial evidence.
