ARGUMENT

**I. The Jury’s Invalidity Verdict Lacks Substantial Evidence Because Leader’s Pre-Critical-Date Activities Did Not Constitute a “Public Use” or “On Sale” Bar Under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b).**

A patent is invalid under § 102(b) only if the claimed invention was in public use or on sale more than one year before the filing date. The party asserting invalidity must prove each element by clear and convincing evidence. *Morgan v. Daniels*, 153 U.S. 120, 124 (1894). The district court erred in denying Leader’s motion for judgment as a matter of law because the evidence fails to show that the Leader2Leader® product embodied every limitation of the asserted claims before December 10, 2002.

**A. The Leader2Leader® Product Was Not a Reduction to Practice of the Claimed Invention.**

To establish a public use bar, the accused product must be a “completed, operative” embodiment of the invention. *Smith v. Goodyear Dental Vulcanite Co.*, 93 U.S. 486, 495 (1876). A prototype that does not incorporate all claim limitations cannot invalidate. *Consolidated Fruit-Jar Co. v. Wright*, 94 U.S. 92, 95 (1876). Here, Leader’s founder testified that the patent covers the “underlying engine,” not the Leader2Leader® user interface. J.A. 25585–86. The white paper touted the product as “fully developed” and “operational now,” but that paper described only high-level functionality—not the “dynamic metadata association” and “tracking movement” required by claim 9. The paper’s reference to a “Browser” and “Big Board” is insufficient to prove that the Digital Leaderboard® system performed the patented method. Without evidence that the pre-2002 product dynamically associated metadata with data, tracked user movement between environments, and updated stored metadata accordingly, the jury had no basis to find that the invention was in public use.

**B. The Evidence Does Not Establish a Completed Sale or Offer for Sale.**

The “on sale” bar requires a definite offer to sell a product that embodies the invention. *Loom Co. v. Higgins*, 105 U.S. 442, 446 (1881) (mere negotiations insufficient). Leader’s January 2002 white paper to Wright Patterson offered “20,000 software licenses” but did not specify price, delivery terms, or binding acceptance. This was a preliminary proposal, not a firm offer. *See American Paper Bottle Co. v. Nixon*, 30 F.2d 430, 432 (2d Cir. 1929) (offer must be “definite and unconditional”). Moreover, the patent’s critical date is December 10, 2002, and the white paper predates that date by nearly a year; even if considered an offer, it does not prove that the invention was *ready for patenting* at that time. The Supreme Court has long held that an inventor may make experimental sales without forfeiting patent rights. *City of Elizabeth v. American Nicholson Pavement Co.*, 97 U.S. 126, 137 (1878) (use for testing is not public use).

**C. Leader’s Activities Were Experimental in Nature.**

Experimental use negates the public use bar. *City of Elizabeth*, 97 U.S. at 137. The record shows that the Leader2Leader® product was still under development through “2002ish,” J.A. 24829, with a team of 15–20 people working to refine the underlying engine. The white paper’s statement that the platform was “operational now with low user volumes” reflects early-stage testing, not commercial exploitation. Leader’s demonstrations to potential customers were aimed at perfecting the invention, not deriving profit. *See Wright v. Coombs*, 55 F. 648, 650 (C.C.D. Mass. 1893) (use for experimentation does not invalidate). The jury was erroneously permitted to disregard this evidence.

**II. The Verdict Must Be Reversed or a New Trial Ordered.**

Because no substantial evidence supports the jury’s finding that the asserted claims were in public use or on sale before the critical date, the district court should have granted judgment as a matter of law. *Metropolitan Engineering Co. v. Coe*, 73 F.2d 947, 948 (D.C. Cir. 1934) (patent invalidity requires clear proof). At minimum, Leader is entitled to a new trial because the jury’s verdict is contrary to the great weight of the evidence. *See Carroll v. Lanza*, 349 U.S. 408, 414 (1955) (new trial appropriate when verdict is against the clear weight of the evidence).

For these reasons, the judgment of invalidity should be reversed.
