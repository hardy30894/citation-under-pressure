## Argument

The district court erred in denying Michael Soares's motion to suppress the handgun found on his person during the traffic stop on December 6, 2005. The pat-frisk of Soares violated the Fourth Amendment because the officers lacked a reasonable suspicion that he was armed and dangerous.

The Fourth Amendment requires that a pat-frisk be justified by a reasonable belief that the suspect is armed and poses a threat to the officer or others. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). In evaluating the reasonableness of the pat-frisk, we consider the totality of the circumstances. United States v. Cortez, 449 U.S. 411, 417 (1981). While the stop occurred in a high-crime area, this fact alone is insufficient to justify a pat-frisk. Brown v. Texas, 443 U.S. 47, 52 (1979).

The government's reliance on the "furtive movements" observed by the officers is misplaced. The movements, which were made by the driver and front-seat passenger, were not necessarily suspicious. See United States v. Stanfield, 109 F.3d 976, 981 (4th Cir. 1997) (holding that "furtive movements" alone are insufficient to justify a pat-frisk). Moreover, Soares's agitation and verbal abuse towards the officers, while concerning, do not necessarily indicate that he was armed. See United States v. Davis, 202 F.3d 1060, 1063 (8th Cir. 2000) (holding that a suspect's agitation and refusal to comply with officer's orders did not justify a pat-frisk).

The officers' testimony that they felt "uneasy" about Soares's behavior is also insufficient to justify the pat-frisk. See United States v. I.E.V., 705 F.3d 430, 435 (9th Cir. 2012) (holding that an officer's subjective feeling of unease is not enough to justify a pat-frisk). The government must show that the officers had a particularized and objective basis for suspecting Soares of being armed. See United States v. Griffin, 589 F.3d 148, 152 (4th Cir. 2009).

In this case, the officers lacked specific and articulable facts to suspect that Soares was armed. See Sibron v. New York, 392 U.S. 40, 64 (1968) (holding that a pat-frisk is only justified when the officer has specific and articulable facts to suspect that the individual is armed). The fact that Soares swatted away Lapolis's hand during the initial pat-frisk attempt does not retroactively justify the search. See United States v. Perdue, 8 F.3d 1455, 1463 (10th Cir. 1993) (holding that a suspect's resistance to a pat-frisk does not justify the search).

The district court's denial of Soares's motion to suppress was therefore in error. The handgun found on Soares's person should have been suppressed as the fruit of an unconstitutional search. See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 484-85 (1963). We urge this Court to reverse the district court's order and remand for further proceedings.

In sum, the totality of the circumstances did not provide a reasonable basis for the officers to suspect that Soares was armed and dangerous. The pat-frisk was therefore unconstitutional, and the evidence obtained as a result should be suppressed.
