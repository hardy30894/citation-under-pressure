**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service (“NPS”) lawfully prohibited John Sturgeon from operating a hovercraft on the Nation River within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. Section 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (“ANILCA”) does not strip NPS of regulatory authority over non-federal lands and waters within conservation system units. The plain text of § 103(c) limits only regulations that “apply solely to public lands”—a phrase that does not encompass the generally applicable hovercraft ban. This Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit’s judgment.

**I. The Hovercraft Ban Is a Regulation of General Applicability, Not One That “Applies Solely to Public Lands.”**

Section 103(c) provides that “no such regulation shall apply to any land or waters … that are not public lands.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). But the hovercraft ban, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), is not a regulation that “applies solely to public lands.” It is a uniform prohibition on hovercraft operation throughout all NPS-administered areas, regardless of land ownership. The statute’s limiting language reaches only those regulations that are by their terms confined to federally owned lands. Because the hovercraft ban applies equally to all lands and waters within the preserve—federal, state, and private—it is not a regulation “applying solely to public lands” and thus falls outside § 103(c)’s restriction.

**II. The Federal Government Possesses Plenary Authority Over Navigable Waters Within National Parks.**

Even if the submerged lands beneath the Nation River are owned by Alaska, the United States retains sovereign authority to regulate navigation and protect park resources on those waters. This Court has long recognized that “the United States has a dominant navigational servitude” over navigable waters. *United States v. Pacific & Arctic Railway & Navigation Co.*, 228 U.S. 87, 106 (1913) (holding that federal laws may control transportation in U.S. territory). The hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of that servitude and of Congress’s power under the Commerce Clause to regulate activities that affect navigable waters. *See Interstate Commerce Commission v. United States ex rel. Humboldt Steamship Co.*, 224 U.S. 474, 484 (1912) (Alaska is a territory subject to federal commerce regulation). The ban protects wildlife, prevents erosion, and ensures public safety—all legitimate federal interests.

**III. Congress Has Delegated Broad Regulatory Authority to NPS Over All Lands and Waters Within Park Units.**

The NPS Organic Act authorizes the Secretary of the Interior to “make and publish such rules and regulations as he may deem necessary or proper for the use and management of the parks.” 54 U.S.C. § 100751(a). This authority extends to all areas within park boundaries, not merely federally owned tracts. *Collins v. Yosemite Park & Curry Co.*, 304 U.S. 518, 530 (1938) (upholding federal jurisdiction over Yosemite National Park after California ceded exclusive jurisdiction). When Congress created the Yukon-Charley Preserve, it intended NPS to manage the entire unit as an integrated whole. The hovercraft ban is a reasonable exercise of that management authority.

**IV. ANILCA Does Not Alter the Traditional Federal Role Over Navigable Waters.**

ANILCA’s savings clauses confirm that the Act does not diminish existing federal regulatory authority. Section 1319 provides that “[n]othing in this Act shall be construed as … superseding, modifying, or repealing … existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to … exercise licensing or regulatory functions.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. The hovercraft ban predates ANILCA and is grounded in NPS’s long-standing authority. *See Curtin v. Benson*, 222 U.S. 78, 86 (1911) (upholding NPS predecessor’s regulations on patented lands within Yosemite). ANILCA’s limited restriction on regulations “applying solely to public lands” cannot be read to impliedly repeal NPS’s general regulatory power over non-federal lands.

**V. Sturgeon’s Interpretation Would Create an Unworkable Patchwork of Jurisdiction.**

If NPS could not regulate non-federal lands within park units, the agency would be powerless to prevent activities that harm park resources—such as poaching, pollution, or unsafe boating—on the vast majority of Alaska park lands that are not federally owned. This Court has rejected similar attempts to fragment federal authority. *Colorado v. Toll*, 268 U.S. 228, 230 (1925) (rejecting state challenge to NPS regulations on private lands within Rocky Mountain National Park). Congress did not intend such a result when it enacted ANILCA.

**VI. The State of Alaska’s Sovereign Interests Do Not Bar Federal Regulation.**

Alaska’s ownership of submerged lands does not confer immunity from otherwise valid federal regulations. *Organized Village of Kake v. Egan*, 369 U.S. 60, 75 (1962) (federal jurisdiction over Indian property does not exclude state conservation laws, but the converse is also true). The federal government’s regulatory authority over navigable waters is paramount. *Metlakatla Indian Community v. Egan*, 369 U.S. 45, 54 (1962) (upholding federal regulations over state trap ban). Alaska’s proprietary interest cannot override Congress’s power to protect national park resources.

**VII. The Hovercraft Ban Is a Reasonable Exercise of NPS’s Discretion.**

NPS determined that hovercrafts cause noise pollution, disturb wildlife, and damage riverbanks. That determination is entitled to deference. *Territory of Alaska v. Troy*, 258 U.S. 101, 109 (1922) (upholding federal merchant marine act’s application to Alaska). Sturgeon’s personal preference for hovercraft does not outweigh the agency’s considered judgment.

**VIII. Sturgeon’s Reliance on § 103(c) Is Misplaced.**

Sturgeon argues that § 103(c) prohibits any NPS regulation on non-federal lands. But the statute’s text and structure refute that reading. Section 103(c) is a narrow exception to NPS’s general authority, not a wholesale bar. *Ward v. Race Horse*, 163 U.S. 504, 510 (1896) (treaty right to hunt on unoccupied federal lands did not survive statehood). Similarly, ANILCA’s limited restriction does not eliminate NPS’s power to regulate activities that threaten park resources.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the hovercraft ban is not a regulation “applying solely to public lands” and therefore falls outside § 103(c)’s limitation. NPS has ample authority to regulate navigable waters within park units, and ANILCA does not strip that authority. The judgment should be affirmed.
