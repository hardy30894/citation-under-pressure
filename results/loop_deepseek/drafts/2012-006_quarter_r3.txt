# ARGUMENT

The government bears the burden of proving every element of a charged conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the statute-of-limitations period. This fundamental principle, rooted in the presumption of innocence and the requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, compels reversal. *See Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 525 (1958) (due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime); *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 795 (1952) (state must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt). A conspiracy is a continuing offense; it must extend into the limitations period for a conviction to stand. *United States v. Rabinowich*, 238 U.S. 78, 86 (1915) (conspiracy continues until the objective is accomplished, and evidence must show continuity into the limitations period). Consequently, the jury must find that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy within the statutory period.

Withdrawal from a conspiracy, when it occurs before the limitations period, does not shift the burden of persuasion to the defendant. Rather, it negates an essential element of the government's case—membership during the limitations period. This Court has long recognized that a defendant's unilateral acts may sever his connection to the conspiracy, but the government must still prove beyond a reasonable doubt that, notwithstanding any evidence of withdrawal, the defendant remained a member during the relevant time. *Holland v. United States*, 348 U.S. 121, 127 (1954) (the government must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt, and no statutory presumption can relieve it of that burden).

The lower court erred by effectively requiring petitioners to disprove their membership during the limitations period. The government's evidence showed that the alleged leaders of the conspiracy split up before the limitations period began. That evidence, at a minimum, created a reasonable doubt as to whether petitioners were still part of any single, ongoing conspiracy. The jury should have been instructed that the government had the burden of proving membership within the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt, and that any evidence of withdrawal—even if insufficient to constitute an affirmative defense—could be considered in determining whether the government had carried its burden. *United States v. Mennuti*, 239 F.2d 747, 750 (2d Cir. 1957) ("The burden is on the Government to prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy within the period of the statute of limitations."); *United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d 834, 845 (2d Cir. 1938) (government must prove "that the conspiracy continued into the period of limitations and that the defendant was then a party to it"). The court's failure to place this burden squarely on the government cannot be excused as harmless error, because the jury could have credited the withdrawal evidence and still convicted on the mistaken belief that the defendants bore the burden of proving they were not members during the limitations period.

Accordingly, the judgment should be reversed, and the cause remanded for a new trial with proper instructions on the burden of persuasion.
