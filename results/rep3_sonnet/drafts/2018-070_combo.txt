# ARGUMENT

## I. OCSLA's Text, Read Against Its Federal-Enclave Model, Adopts State Law Only to Fill Gaps in Federal Law

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act declares that "the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State" are "the law of the United States" for fixed structures on the Shelf only "[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This Court has explained that OCSLA's purpose was "to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures" on the Shelf by treating them "as federal enclaves." *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355, 365 (1969). Congress modeled this provision on the established regime governing federal enclaves within the States, under which state law persists only until Congress displaces it, and only where federal law has not already occupied the field.

That enclave model has deep roots. In *Fort Leavenworth R.R. Co. v. Lowe*, 114 U.S. 525 (1885), this Court held that when the United States acquires exclusive jurisdiction over territory, the laws of the ceding State "would remain in force" only "until abrogated or changed" by federal legislation—not because state law applied of its own force alongside federal law, but because it filled the space federal law had not yet occupied. *Id.* at 533-34. The companion case, *Chicago, Rock Island & Pacific Ry. Co. v. McGlinn*, 114 U.S. 542 (1885), likewise held that state law "not obnoxious" to federal policy continues only as "the law of the territory ceded" until Congress "substitute[s] other regulations." *Id.* at 546. And in *James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula*, 309 U.S. 94 (1940), this Court confirmed that a state safety statute could operate on a federal enclave precisely because there was "no legislation of Congress" occupying that subject. *Id.* at 100. These enclave cases establish the baseline principle OCSLA imported wholesale: state law functions as a gap-filler, not as a freestanding, concurrent source of regulation whenever it happens to touch the same subject matter as federal law.

## II. This Court's Enclave and Preemption Precedents Confirm That State Law Recedes Once Federal Law Comprehensively Occupies the Field

Where Congress or a federal agency has comprehensively regulated a subject within an enclave, this Court has held that state law affecting the same subject is displaced altogether, without regard to whether particular provisions are technically "inconsistent." In *Pacific Coast Dairy, Inc. v. Department of Agriculture*, 318 U.S. 285 (1943), the Court held that California's milk-price regulations could not apply on a federal enclave because the federal government had already established its own control over the subject, reasoning that "the Federal Government has exclusive jurisdiction" and that permitting state regulation to persist "would be inconsistent with the scheme of federal control." *Id.* at 294-95. Similarly, in *Howard v. Commissioners of Sinking Fund of Louisville*, 344 U.S. 624 (1953), the Court examined whether state taxation could reach federal enclave employees, asking not merely whether the state law was "inconsistent" with any specific federal statute, but whether the enclave's federal status left "room" for the state law to operate. *Id.* at 627-28.

This inquiry into whether federal law leaves "room" for state law—rather than whether state law is technically inconsistent—is precisely the gap analysis the Fifth Circuit later crystallized for the OCS itself. In *Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners' Mutual Insurance Association*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law under § 1333(a)(2)(A) only where there exists "a significant void or gap" that requires filling. *Id.* at 1036. That standard follows ineluctably from the enclave precedents above: state law is borrowed out of necessity, not extended as an independent regulatory layer whenever a plaintiff can characterize it as merely "not inconsistent" with a federal statute that already speaks to the subject.

## III. The FLSA Comprehensively Regulates Wage-and-Hour Standards, Leaving No Gap for California Law on the Shelf

Congress enacted the Fair Labor Standards Act as a nationally uniform "comprehensive legislative scheme" addressing minimum wages, overtime, and related standards. *See Powell v. United States Cartridge Co.*, 339 U.S. 497, 515-16 (1950) (describing the FLSA's broad remedial design and national scope). Where Congress legislates comprehensively over a subject touching a federal enclave, this Court has consistently held that the enclave's federal law—not the surrounding State's law—governs, and *Sharpnack*'s validation of Congress's assimilation technique, *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 293-94 (1958), confirms that state law is borrowed *as* federal law, subject to displacement whenever federal law itself supplies the governing rule. The FLSA supplies that rule for wage-and-hour claims. Because the FLSA already comprehensively addresses the minimum-wage and overtime subject matter Newton invokes, there is no "significant void" for California law to fill, and the Ninth Circuit's "not inconsistent" test cannot be reconciled with *Rodrigue*, *Fort Leavenworth*, *McGlinn*, *Pacific Coast Dairy*, *Howard*, or *Continental Oil*. The judgment below should be reversed.
