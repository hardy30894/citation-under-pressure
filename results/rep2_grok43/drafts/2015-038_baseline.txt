**ARGUMENT**

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that non-federal lands “shall be deemed to be excluded from the [conservation system unit]” and that “no lands” so excluded “shall be subject to the regulations applicable solely to public lands within such units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The hovercraft prohibition, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), is not a regulation “applicable solely to public lands within” Alaska units. It applies uniformly to all lands and waters administered by the National Park Service throughout the United States. Because the regulation is not limited to public lands inside Alaska conservation system units, § 103(c) leaves it undisturbed.

This reading follows from the statute’s plain text. “The preeminent canon of statutory interpretation requires us to presume that the legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there.” *BedRoc Ltd. v. United States*, 541 U.S. 176, 183 (2004) (internal quotation marks omitted). Section 103(c) addresses only those rules that “apply solely to public lands within” the units; it does not purport to exempt non-federal inholdings from generally applicable Park Service regulations.

The same conclusion is compelled by the structure of ANILCA. Congress simultaneously preserved the Secretary’s preexisting authority to promulgate rules “necessary and proper for the use and management of the National Park System.” 54 U.S.C. § 100751(a). ANILCA itself confirms that nothing in the Act “supersede[s], modif[ies], or repeal[s], except as specifically set forth,” existing regulatory authority. 16 U.S.C. § 3207. Reading § 103(c) to withdraw that authority would render these provisions superfluous, contrary to the rule that “a statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.” *Corley v. United States*, 556 U.S. 303, 314 (2009) (internal quotation marks omitted).

Petitioner’s contrary construction would also produce results Congress could not have intended. It would exempt state, Native, and private lands from every Park Service rule of general application—fire prevention, wildlife protection, and visitor safety alike—while leaving identical rules in force on federal lands a few feet away. Such a checkerboard regime finds no support in the statutory language and would undermine the uniform administration the Park Service has long exercised over the System. *See Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 539 (1976) (recognizing Congress’s “complete power” over federal property).

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the hovercraft ban survives § 103(c) because it is not a regulation applicable solely to public lands within Alaska units. That holding should be affirmed.
