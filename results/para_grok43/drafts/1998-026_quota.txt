ARGUMENT

A district court's technical violation of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) supplies no ground for collateral relief when the defendant knew of his appellate rights and elected not to pursue them. Collateral attack is "extraordinary" and limited to errors that render the proceeding "fundamentally unfair." United States v. Addonizio, 442 U.S. 178, 184-85 (1979); Hill v. United States, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). A Rule 32 omission that causes no prejudice falls outside this narrow category. United States v. Timmreck, 441 U.S. 780, 783-84 (1979).

Harmless-error analysis governs Rule 32 violations on both direct review and collateral attack. The Court has applied the same standard to sentencing defects, requiring the defendant to show that the error "had substantial and injurious effect or influence" on the outcome. Brecht v. Abrahamson, 507 U.S. 619, 637 (1993) (quoting Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946)). Where the record establishes that the defendant was already aware of his right to appeal, the omission produces no such effect. See Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 468 (1997); United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 734-35 (1993).

This Court has repeatedly declined to vacate sentences for Rule 32 violations absent prejudice. In Timmreck, a guilty-plea defendant who received an incorrect maximum-sentence warning was denied § 2255 relief because he showed no actual harm. 441 U.S. at 784. The same principle controls here: Peguero's conceded knowledge of his appellate rights eliminated any possibility of prejudice. See also Machibroda v. United States, 368 U.S. 487, 493 (1962) (requiring factual showing of prejudice before collateral relief may be granted).

The Third Circuit correctly treated the Rule 32 error as subject to harmless-error review rather than automatic reversal. Structural errors are confined to a "very limited class" that affect the framework of the trial itself. Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279, 309-10 (1991); Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 23-24 (1967). A post-sentencing notice omission does not belong in that class. Neder v. United States, 527 U.S. 1, 8 (1999). Because the purpose of the rule—to ensure defendants know of their appellate rights—was fulfilled by Peguero's independent knowledge, the judgment should be affirmed.
