**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Smoot’s Railway Labor Act Claim**

The district court concluded that Smoot’s claim under the Railway Labor Act (“RLA”), 45 U.S.C. § 153, failed as a matter of law. That conclusion was erroneous because the record demonstrates genuine issues of material fact regarding whether the UTU and CSX conspired to deprive Smoot of a fair arbitration proceeding.

Under the RLA, a union owes its members a duty of fair representation. *Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 177 (1967). That duty requires the union to act “without hostility or discrimination toward any of its members.” *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co.*, 323 U.S. 192, 204 (1944). Smoot presented evidence that UTU Vice-Chairman Robert Earley, during the executive session of the Public Law Board hearing, argued against Smoot’s claim—directly contrary to the UTU’s official position. This conduct, captured on Smoot’s tape recording, creates a triable issue as to whether Earley breached the duty of fair representation by actively undermining Smoot’s grievance.

Moreover, Smoot alleged that CSX participated in this breach by colluding with Earley and other UTU representatives to ensure an adverse outcome. The district court improperly resolved this factual dispute on summary judgment. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). A reasonable jury could infer from Earley’s statements in the executive session—and from the PLB’s ultimate decision against Smoot—that CSX and the UTU acted in concert. Summary judgment was therefore inappropriate.

**II. The District Court Erred in Awarding Damages Under the Federal Wiretap Act Because Smoot Did Not “Intercept” an Oral Communication Within the Meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 2510**

The district court held Smoot liable under the Federal Wiretap Act for recording the executive session. That holding contravenes the plain text of the statute. The Act defines “intercept” as the “aural or other acquisition of the contents of any wire, electronic, or oral communication through the use of any electronic, mechanical, or other device.” 18 U.S.C. § 2510(4). An “oral communication” is defined as “any oral communication uttered by a person exhibiting an expectation that such communication is not subject to interception under circumstances justifying such expectation.” 18 U.S.C. § 2510(2).

The executive session took place in a conference room at CSX’s offices, not in a private, secured location. Critically, Smoot did not surreptitiously place a listening device; he left his own tape recorders in the room before the session began. The PLB members knew Smoot had brought recorders and did not remove them or request that they be turned off. Under these circumstances, no reasonable expectation of privacy existed. *United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745, 751 (1971) (plurality opinion) (no reasonable expectation of privacy for statements made to a known informant). Because the PLB members failed to take minimal steps to exclude Smoot’s devices, their expectation of privacy was not objectively reasonable. *See Hoffa v. United States*, 385 U.S. 293, 302 (1966). The district court therefore erred as a matter of law.

**III. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Awarding Punitive Damages and Attorneys’ Fees**

Even assuming a violation of the Wiretap Act occurred, the district court abused its discretion in awarding $100,000 in punitive damages and $86,308.74 in attorneys’ fees. Punitive damages under 18 U.S.C. § 2520(b)(2) require a showing that the defendant acted “willfully or intentionally.” Smoot recorded the session not for any malicious purpose, but to preserve evidence of potential union misconduct. This good-faith effort to document his own arbitration proceeding cannot support a finding of willfulness. *See Reynolds v. Spears*, 93 F.3d 428, 435 (8th Cir. 1996) (good-faith belief that recording was lawful precludes willfulness finding). The award of $250,000 in statutory damages was also excessive, as Smoot’s conduct caused no actual damages to either defendant.

Similarly, the attorneys’ fee award was unreasonable. The district court failed to assess the reasonableness of the hours billed or the hourly rates, as required by *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433 (1983). Defendants’ counsel incurred fees litigating against Smoot’s RLA claim, which was not frivolous, and the fee award should have been apportioned accordingly.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment on Smoot’s RLA claim, vacate the damages award, and remand for further proceedings.
