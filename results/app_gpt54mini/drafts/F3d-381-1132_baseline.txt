**Argument**

The Court of Federal Claims erred in entering summary judgment for the United States because the government’s conduct went beyond the easement it acquired and effected a physical occupation of Tuthill’s property. The Fifth Amendment “protects private property” from uncompensated appropriation by the government, and a permanent physical occupation is a taking per se. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 426, 435-36 (1982). When the government authorizes a third party to occupy private property, the taking is not avoided merely because the physical invasion is accomplished through an easement holder’s instrumentality. *Nollan v. California Coastal Comm’n*, 483 U.S. 825, 831-32 (1987); *Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164, 179-80 (1979).

Here, BPA’s original easement allowed it to erect, operate, maintain, repair, rebuild, and patrol “electrical power transmission lines and appurtenant signal lines” necessary in connection with the transmission system. But undisputed facts show that BPA intentionally built a fiber-optic system far exceeding its present operational needs, then leased unused capacity to a private telecommunications company for profit. That use is not “necessary in connection therewith” within the ordinary meaning of the easement. Under Washington law, the scope of an easement is defined by its terms, and use beyond those terms is a trespass or overburdening, not an authorized use. *Brown v. Voss*, 105 Wash. 2d 366, 371-72, 715 P.2d 514, 517-18 (1986); *Crown Point Land Co. v. City of Kent*, 87 Wash. App. 556, 561-62, 943 P.2d 1076, 1079-80 (1997). A governmental occupier that exceeds the scope of its property right necessarily occupies a property interest it does not own. That is the essence of a physical taking. *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 435-36.

The government’s position, accepted below, mistakenly treats the present burden alone as dispositive and ignores the separate property interest invaded by commercial leasing to strangers. A taking occurs when the government takes “something more than a mere physical invasion” only in the sense that the invasion must be of a cognizable property interest; once that invasion is shown, compensation is required. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 261-62 (1946). This Court has recognized that the government may not “appropriate” private property rights and then avoid compensation by characterizing the use as incidental to another public purpose. *Ruckelshaus v. Monsanto Co.*, 467 U.S. 986, 1003-04 (1984). Here, BPA appropriated a private commercial communications use that Tuthill never conveyed.

The Court of Federal Claims also erred in concluding that because BPA may someday need the extra fibers, their present lease to third parties creates no taking. A taking is assessed based on the government’s actual, current occupation and use, not speculative future necessity. *Horne v. Dep’t of Agric.*, 576 U.S. 350, 357-58 (2015). The government cannot justify a private commercial use by invoking hypothetical future demand. *Loretto* makes clear that a permanent physical occupation is per se compensable even where the invasion is modest and even where the owner retains other uses of the property. 458 U.S. at 435-38. The relevant question is not whether the entire parcel remains usable, but whether the government has permanently installed and dedicated private parties to occupy a portion of the property interest without compensation. *Kaiser Aetna*, 444 U.S. at 179-80.

Nor does the fact that the fiber optic cable lies within a corridor originally encumbered by an easement defeat Tuthill’s claim. A limited servitude does not give the government carte blanche to transform an easement for transmission lines into a revenue-generating telecommunications facility. When the government exceeds the defined scope of a property interest, it has crossed from authorized use into unauthorized occupation. *Nollan*, 483 U.S. at 831-32. The Court of Federal Claims’ statement that “misuse of an easement may be a trespass to real property” concedes the central point: unauthorized occupation is a cognizable invasion of private property. Tuthill’s claim sounds in takings because the government has permanently installed and leased a commercial communications system on Tuthill’s land, thereby appropriating a property use that belonged to Tuthill alone. *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 426, 435-36.

Finally, summary judgment for the government was improper because, at minimum, there is a genuine dispute whether BPA’s present lease of excess fiber to a private carrier exceeds the easement and constitutes a permanent physical occupation. Under Rule 56, the moving party must show entitlement to judgment as a matter of law, and all reasonable inferences must favor the nonmovant. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248-50 (1986); *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986). The undisputed facts establish a commercial occupation beyond the easement’s necessary transmission function. That is enough to require reversal.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a determination of just compensation.
