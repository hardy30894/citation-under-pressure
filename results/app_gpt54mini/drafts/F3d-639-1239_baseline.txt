**Argument**

The district court erred in dismissing this putative class action as moot after BCR unilaterally offered Lucero complete relief before the court ruled on class certification. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a live controversy in a class action is not necessarily extinguished when the named plaintiff’s individual claim becomes moot before certification, and this Court has recognized that the “relation back” doctrine may preserve jurisdiction where the challenged practice is capable of evading review. *Sosna v. Iowa*, 419 U.S. 393, 399–402 (1975); *U.S. Parole Comm’n v. Geraghty*, 445 U.S. 388, 404–07 (1980). The judgment below conflicts with those authorities and with the policies underlying Rule 23.

First, a defendant may not defeat a class action by “picking off” the named plaintiff with a Rule 68 offer of complete relief before the court has an opportunity to decide certification. In *Sosna*, the Court held that once a class is certified, the case does not become moot when the named plaintiff’s claim expires because the class acquires a legal status separate from the named plaintiff’s individual interest. 419 U.S. at 399. In *Geraghty*, the Court extended that principle, holding that the named plaintiff could appeal the denial of class certification even after his personal claim had expired, because the controversy over certification remained live. 445 U.S. at 404–07. The Court explained that a proposed class representative “retains a personal stake” in obtaining certification. *Id.* at 404. That reasoning applies with even greater force here, where Lucero moved for certification within the scheduling deadlines set by the district court and BCR’s tender came before any certification ruling.

Second, the district court’s categorical rule—that “a suit brought as a class action must be dismissed for mootness when the personal claims of the named plaintiffs are satisfied and no class has been properly certified”—is too broad. *Clark v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 590 F.3d 1134, 1138 (10th Cir. 2009). *Clark* itself did not require dismissal in every case where the named plaintiff’s claim is satisfied before certification; it reiterated only the general rule, subject to the recognized exceptions rooted in *Sosna* and *Geraghty*. See *id.* at 1138–39. Those exceptions exist because otherwise defendants could strategically moot class actions before certification and thereby insulate unlawful conduct from judicial review. See *Geraghty*, 445 U.S. at 401–02, 406. The district court’s ruling invites exactly that manipulation.

Third, this case is especially appropriate for relation back because the timing of certification was controlled by the court’s own scheduling order, not by any dilatory conduct by Lucero. The parties agreed to phased discovery focused first on certification, and the district court set the certification motion deadline for February 23, 2010. Lucero filed his motion on February 22, 2010, one day before the deadline. When a named plaintiff timely seeks certification but the defendant attempts to moot the claim before a ruling can issue, relation back prevents the case from becoming an empty procedural game. See *Sosna*, 419 U.S. at 402 n.11 (recognizing that the relation-back doctrine may apply where the claim is “inherently transitory”); *Gerstein v. Pugh*, 420 U.S. 103, 110 n.11 (1975). While Lucero’s FDCPA claim is not inherently transitory in the same sense as pretrial detention, the same practical concern exists: the defendant’s tender came before the court could resolve certification, and the claim could repeatedly evade review if defendants were allowed to “buy off” named plaintiffs at the outset. See *County of Riverside v. McLaughlin*, 500 U.S. 44, 52 (1991).

Fourth, BCR’s reliance on Rule 68 does not change the jurisdictional analysis. A Rule 68 offer is only an offer; it does not itself extinguish the plaintiff’s claim absent acceptance or entry of judgment. See *Campbell-Ewald Co. v. Gomez*, 577 U.S. 153, 162–66 (2016). Although *Campbell-Ewald* postdates this case, its reasoning confirms the basic point that an unaccepted offer cannot unilaterally strip federal courts of jurisdiction. The district court thus erred in treating BCR’s offer as if it had actually satisfied Lucero’s claim and deprived the court of power to act on the pending certification motion.

Finally, the policies of Rule 23 counsel strongly against dismissal. Class actions exist to aggregate low-value claims that might otherwise escape review. See *Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 617 (1997). Allowing a defendant to moot a class action before certification would undermine that function and reward strategic behavior. The proper course is to recognize that Lucero’s timely filed class allegations and pending certification motion preserved a live controversy notwithstanding BCR’s offer.

Because the district court prematurely dismissed this action, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for consideration of class certification on the merits.
