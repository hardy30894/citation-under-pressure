The Center Moriches Union Free School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series on family values and child rearing constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech.

The School District's facilities are a limited public forum because they have been opened for use by the general public for certain purposes. As such, the District's regulation of expression must be viewpoint-neutral and reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum. See Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983); Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series, which is a form of expression, is not viewpoint-neutral because it is based on the religious content of the speech. See Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940).

The Supreme Court has held that a state may not exclude speech from a public forum based on its content unless the exclusion is necessary to serve a compelling state interest and is narrowly drawn to achieve that end. See Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 161 (1939); Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941). In this case, the School District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series is not necessary to serve a compelling state interest, as the District has not demonstrated that allowing the film series would interfere with the school's educational mission or create a disruption. See Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949).

Furthermore, the District's policy of excluding religious speech from its facilities is not reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum. The facilities are used for a variety of community purposes, and there is no evidence that allowing Lamb's Chapel to show its film series would be inconsistent with the District's educational mission. See Saia v. New York, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948).

The Court of Appeals' reliance on the fact that the School District has not previously allowed religious groups to use its facilities is misplaced. The Supreme Court has held that a public forum may be created by a policy or practice of allowing access to a particular group or type of speech, even if that access has not been universal. See Kunz v. New York, 340 U.S. 290, 293 (1951). In this case, the District's policy of excluding religious speech is not justified by a compelling state interest, and it is not reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum.

The exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series also raises concerns under the Establishment Clause, as it may be seen as hostile to religion rather than neutral. See Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952); McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 211 (1948). The District's policy of excluding religious speech from its facilities may be seen as discriminatory against religious groups, which is not permissible under the First Amendment. See Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951).

In conclusion, the School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use its facilities for a religious-oriented film series constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The District's policy of excluding religious speech is not justified by a compelling state interest, and it is not reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum.
