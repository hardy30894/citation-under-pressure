**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. Petitioner Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on the ground that the statute exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers and violates the Tenth Amendment. The Tenth Amendment is a structural protection of state sovereignty, not a personal right enforceable by a private criminal defendant.

**I. The Tenth Amendment Does Not Confer Individual Rights Enforceable by Private Parties**

The Tenth Amendment provides that “the powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.” U.S. Const. amend. X. This Amendment is a “truism” that “states but a necessary consequence of our federal system.” *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). It does not create individual rights. *See id.* (the Tenth Amendment “states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered”). As this Court has long recognized, the Tenth Amendment “is not a source of individual rights.” *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. TVA*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939). Rather, it “is a restraint upon the exercise of power by the United States, and not a grant of power to the States.” *United States v. Sprague*, 282 U.S. 716, 733 (1931).

Because the Tenth Amendment protects the sovereignty of the States, not the rights of individuals, a private party lacks standing to invoke it. *Tennessee Electric*, 306 U.S. at 144 (holding that private utility companies “have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment” because the Amendment “is not a grant of power to the States, but a limitation upon the power of the Federal Government”). This Court has consistently applied that principle. *See, e.g., Steward Machine Co. v. Davis*, 301 U.S. 548, 585 (1937) (rejecting a Tenth Amendment challenge by a private employer because the Amendment “is not a limitation upon the power of the United States to tax”); *Ashwander v. TVA*, 297 U.S. 288, 330 (1936) (holding that private parties cannot “set up the rights of the States” under the Tenth Amendment). The Tenth Amendment “is not a source of private rights.” *Darby*, 312 U.S. at 124.

**II. Petitioner’s Claim Is a Structural Challenge That Only a State May Assert**

The Tenth Amendment “reserves to the States” powers not delegated to the United States. This reservation is a structural feature of the Constitution, designed to protect the “balance of power between the States and the Federal Government.” *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer*, 343 U.S. 579, 635 (1952) (Jackson, J., concurring). As such, it is “a restraint upon the exercise of power by the United States, and not a grant of power to the States.” *Sprague*, 282 U.S. at 733. Only a State—the entity whose sovereignty is allegedly infringed—may assert a Tenth Amendment claim. *See Tennessee Electric*, 306 U.S. at 144 (denying standing to private parties because “the States or their officers” are the proper parties to raise such claims); *see also Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 485-86 (1923) (holding that a private citizen cannot “set up the rights of the States” under the Tenth Amendment).

This Court has never permitted a private party to challenge a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds. *See, e.g., Steward Machine*, 301 U.S. at 585 (rejecting a private employer’s Tenth Amendment challenge); *Ashwander*, 297 U.S. at 330 (same). The reason is clear: the Tenth Amendment “is not a source of individual rights.” *Darby*, 312 U.S. at 124. A private party “cannot, by merely asserting that the statute violates the Tenth Amendment, obtain a judicial determination of that question.” *Mellon*, 262 U.S. at 485.

**III. Petitioner’s Conviction Does Not Violate the Tenth Amendment in Any Event**

Even if petitioner had standing, her Tenth Amendment challenge would fail. The statute under which she was convicted, 18 U.S.C. § 229, was enacted pursuant to the Treaty Power and the Necessary and Proper Clause. *See Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920) (holding that a statute implementing a valid treaty is “necessary and proper” under Article I, Section 8). The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and Congress had authority to enact implementing legislation. *Id.* at 433 (“If the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, Section 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.”). The Tenth Amendment does not bar such legislation because “the treaty power is not limited by the Tenth Amendment.” *Id.* at 434.

Moreover, the statute does not invade a core area of state sovereignty. The States retain “primary authority” over local criminal matters, *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 635 (1993), but the federal government has “the power to punish offenses against the United States,” *United States v. Fox*, 95 U.S. 670, 672 (1877). Section 229 prohibits the use of chemical weapons—a matter of national and international concern. *See Holland*, 252 U.S. at 435 (upholding a treaty regulating migratory birds because the subject “is a national interest of very nearly the first magnitude”). The statute does not “displace the States’ police powers” over purely local conduct. *Darby*, 312 U.S. at 124.

**IV. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
