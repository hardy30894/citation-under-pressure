ARGUMENT

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on "cruel and unusual punishments" does not erect a categorical bar to the execution of persons who suffer from mental retardation. This Court has long recognized that the Amendment "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). That standard, however, is not a license for this Court to substitute its own judgment for that of the People acting through their legislatures. See Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 373 (1910) (the Amendment's meaning must be measured against actual and demonstrated societal judgment, not judicial preference). Virginia respectfully submits that no such settled national consensus condemns the practice at issue here, and that the Constitution instead commits the assessment of an offender's mental capacity to the individualized judgment of the sentencing jury.

I. The Eighth Amendment Has Never Been Understood to Impose a Categorical Bar Based on an Offender's Mental Capacity.

This Court's cruel-and-unusual-punishments jurisprudence has traditionally focused on the nature of the punishment itself, not on characteristics of the individual offender. In In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890), the Court explained that punishments are "cruel" within the meaning of the Amendment only when they involve "torture or a lingering death," and that the Amendment was never understood to forbid the death penalty as such. Death by lawful process for the crime of murder is not, and never has been, considered a "cruel and unusual punishment" merely because of the characteristics of the person condemned. See Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947) (recognizing broad state authority over the administration of capital punishment, subject only to prohibitions on "inhuman" methods).

Nor does Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), support a categorical exemption here. Robinson held that the state may not punish a person for the mere "status" of being an addict, id. at 666, but respondent does not seek to punish Atkins for a status; he is punished for the calculated crime of abduction, armed robbery, and capital murder—crimes he was found competent to commit and for which a properly instructed jury found him criminally responsible.

II. Diminished Mental Capacity Has Traditionally Been Treated as a Question of Individualized Mitigation, Not a Categorical Constitutional Exemption.

American criminal law has long distinguished between insanity, which negates criminal responsibility altogether, and lesser degrees of intellectual impairment, which bear on the weight a sentencer should give to culpability but do not extinguish it. See Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 800–01 (1952) (upholding a State's authority to define and allocate the burden of proving legal insanity, and declining to constitutionalize any particular standard of mental capacity as a matter of due process). This Court has likewise resisted efforts to constitutionalize gradations of mental capacity short of insanity. In Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 535–36 (1968), a plurality of this Court declined to adopt a constitutional doctrine of diminished responsibility, explaining that such determinations "resist resolution by [the] elaboration of a general constitutional rule" and are properly left "to the States for correction." The same restraint counsels against transforming Atkins' claim of mental retardation into an absolute Eighth Amendment bar.

Instead, this Court's capital sentencing jurisprudence has consistently entrusted the individualized assessment of an offender's characteristics—including intellectual capacity—to the jury. In Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 247 (1949), the Court emphasized that a sentencer must be permitted the "fullest information possible concerning the defendant's life and characteristics" in order to render a just and individualized sentence. And in Witherspoon v. Illinois, 391 U.S. 510, 519 (1968), this Court reaffirmed the central role of the capital jury in rendering the "highly subjective" judgment whether a particular defendant should live or die, a judgment that necessarily accounts for evidence of intellectual limitation offered in mitigation. That is precisely the process Virginia afforded Atkins: his forensic psychologist's testimony regarding his mild mental retardation was placed squarely before the jury as a mitigating circumstance, and the jury—having heard both his evidence and the Commonwealth's rebuttal—rendered its individualized judgment that death was the appropriate sentence.

III. Established Procedures Already Protect Against the Execution of Persons Who Lack Capacity, Without Resort to a New Categorical Rule.

Where mental incapacity is severe enough to implicate the offender's very competency, this Court's precedents have addressed that concern through procedural safeguards administered by the States rather than through a substantive categorical exemption. In Solesbee v. Balkcom, 339 U.S. 9, 12 (1950), the Court held that the manner of determining whether a condemned prisoner's sanity precludes execution may be left "to the discretion of the state," subject to due process, rather than fixed by a rigid constitutional rule. That approach—procedural safeguard plus individualized mitigation—adequately protects offenders like Atkins without requiring this Court to declare, as a categorical matter, that no mildly retarded offender may ever be sentenced to death.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
