ARGUMENT

The Texas courts possessed subject-matter jurisdiction over this state-law malpractice action. A claim arises under federal patent law only when federal law creates the cause of action or the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law that forms an essential element of one of the well-pleaded claims. Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897); Wilson v. Sandford, 51 U.S. (10 How.) 99, 101-02 (1850). The well-pleaded complaint rule confines the inquiry to the face of the plaintiff's pleading, unaided by anticipated defenses. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908).

Minton's malpractice petition alleges only that Gunn negligently failed to assert the experimental-use exception in the underlying infringement suit, thereby causing the loss of that action and any attendant settlement value. The petition does not seek to enforce any right created by the patent statutes, nor does it require, as an essential element, a determination of patent validity or infringement. The experimental-use question appears only as a hypothetical defense that might have altered the outcome of the federal litigation; it is not an ingredient of the state cause of action itself. Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912) ("A suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws.").

Where a state claim merely requires reference to federal law as one of several possible theories of causation or damages, federal jurisdiction does not attach. Gully v. First Nat'l Bank, 299 U.S. 109, 116 (1936) (federal question must be "an element, and an essential one, of the plaintiff's cause of action"). The mere possibility that a federal patent issue might arise in proving the "case within a case" does not convert the malpractice claim into one arising under federal law. See American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co., 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916) (a suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action).

Congress has not displaced the historic presumption that state courts may adjudicate common-law malpractice claims even when those claims touch upon federal patent questions incidentally. See The Moses Taylor, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 411, 430 (1866). Because Minton's well-pleaded complaint presents no substantial, disputed question of patent law as an essential element of the state claim, exclusive federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) is absent. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
