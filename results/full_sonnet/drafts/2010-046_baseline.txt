# ARGUMENT

## THE THREE-JUDGE COURT'S ORDER SATISFIES THE REQUIREMENTS OF THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT

### A. The PLRA Permits Population Reduction Where the Court Makes the Required Findings

The Prison Litigation Reform Act does not categorically forbid population caps; it conditions them on particularized findings. Under 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E), a court may enter an order that has "the purpose or effect of reducing or limiting the prison population" only if it first finds that "crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right" and that "no other relief will remedy the violation." The three-judge court made both findings after eight days of trial testimony from experts on medical and mental-health care, corrections administration, and epidemiology. That is precisely the process Congress contemplated. The PLRA's structure presupposes that population limits remain an available remedy when the statutory prerequisites are met; it does not, as petitioners suggest, eliminate the remedy altogether. See *Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail*, 502 U.S. 367, 383 (1992) (institutional reform decrees must be modified, not dissolved, when "a significant change in facts" bears on compliance, reflecting the settled principle that equitable remedies for ongoing constitutional violations must be responsive to conditions on the ground).

### B. The Underlying Constitutional Violation Is Undisputed and Severe

This Court has long recognized that conditions of confinement violate the Eighth Amendment when they involve "the wanton and unnecessary infliction of pain." *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981). Overcrowding that produces the denial of "the minimal civilized measure of life's necessities," *id.* at 347, or that exposes prisoners to a substantial risk of serious harm from disease or violence, *Farmer v. Brennan*, 511 U.S. 825, 834 (1994), is not a matter of prison administration convenience but a matter of constitutional command. Deliberate indifference to a prisoner's serious medical needs "violates the Eighth Amendment because society does not expect that prisoners will be denied basic elements of hygiene ... or that they will be left without the basic elements of health care." *Helling v. McKinney*, 509 U.S. 25, 32 (1993). The three-judge court found, based on extensive record evidence, that California's severe overcrowding was the "primary cause" of systemic failures resulting in needless suffering and preventable deaths—one inmate dying, on average, every six to seven days from constitutionally deficient care. Petitioners do not seriously contest these findings; they instead attack the remedy.

### C. The Order Is Narrowly Drawn, Extends No Further Than Necessary, and Is the Least Intrusive Means of Correcting the Violation

Section 3626(a)(1)(A) requires that prospective relief be "narrowly drawn," "extend no further than necessary to correct the violation," and represent "the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation." The three-judge court's order satisfies each requirement.

First, the order does not mandate release of any specific prisoner or dictate the means by which the State achieves compliance. It leaves California free to choose among a range of options—expanding good-time credits, transferring inmates to county facilities, diverting low-level offenders, or constructing additional capacity—so long as the population is reduced to 137.5% of design capacity. This flexibility is the hallmark of a properly tailored remedy. Cf. *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 n.9 (1978) (recognizing that federal courts have "broad" but not unlimited "equitable powers to remedy past wrongs," while affording the State discretion in the mode of compliance).

Second, the court did not select the population cap arbitrarily. It heard testimony from corrections experts establishing that 137.5% was the threshold at which the State's own consultants and other courts overseeing California's prisons had determined constitutionally adequate care become administratively possible. The finding that "no other relief" would suffice was not conclusory: the court catalogued years of failed remedial efforts, including consent decrees, the appointment of special masters, and ultimately a receivership—each of which proved unable to cure the violation because overcrowding itself, not simply mismanagement, was the root cause. As this Court has recognized in the related receivership proceedings arising from this very litigation, California's own conduct—including years of non-opposition to remedial plans and consent to the underlying orders—undercuts any claim that less intrusive measures were foreclosed to it. *Cf.* *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, No. 08-17412 (9th Cir. Mar. 25, 2009) (noting that the State's "original consent... to the proceedings may affect or even negate its claim" that the remedial structure exceeded what was necessary).

Third, the three-judge court did not order immediate mass release. It gave the State up to two years and required a phased implementation plan, subject to further court supervision and modification if population reduction proved achievable through less drastic means than release. This built-in flexibility mirrors the "flexible remedy" contemplated by institutional-reform jurisprudence, under which decrees must be capable of modification as facts and options change. *Rufo*, 502 U.S. at 383.

### D. Deference to the District Court's Findings Is Warranted

Because the PLRA requires factual findings on primary cause and the absence of alternative relief, and because the three-judge court made those findings only after extensive evidentiary proceedings, its determinations are entitled to the deference ordinarily accorded factfinders in institutional-reform litigation. See *Lewis v. Casey*, 518 U.S. 343, 362 (1996) (courts must remain sensitive to the practical difficulties of prison administration, but that sensitivity does not permit disregard of documented constitutional violations). Petitioners' quarrel is not that the court applied the wrong legal standard, but that they disagree with its factual conclusions—a disagreement that does not establish a violation of the PLRA.

The judgment of the three-judge district court should be affirmed.
