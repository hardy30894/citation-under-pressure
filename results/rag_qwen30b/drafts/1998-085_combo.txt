**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous” independent of its state of mind in order to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—must be answered in the negative. Congress, in enacting the Civil Rights Act of 1991, did not impose a heightened standard of conduct beyond intentional discrimination; rather, it made punitive damages available when an employer acts with *malice* or *reckless indifference* to federally protected rights. The lower court’s refusal to allow the jury to consider punitive damages was error, and this Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals to the extent it remanded for trial on punitive damages.

The statutory language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) is clear: punitive damages may be awarded “if the [plaintiff] demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” This standard does not require proof of conduct that is “egregious” or “outrageous” in the absence of a culpable mental state. Indeed, the very purpose of punitive damages—“to punish the wrongdoer and to deter similar conduct”—is fulfilled precisely when the defendant’s state of mind reflects a disregard for the law. As this Court held in *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 112 (1893), punitive damages are “awarded ... as a punishment to the guilty, to deter from any such proceeding for the future, and as a proof of the detestation of the jury to the action itself.” The focus is not on the objective severity of the act, but on the defendant’s culpable intent.

The requirement of *malice* or *reckless indifference* is not a threshold of “egregiousness” in the absence of fault. Rather, it is a standard of culpability. In *St. Amant v. Thompson*, 390 U.S. 727, 731 (1968), this Court held that “actual malice” under the First Amendment requires “reckless disregard of whether the statements were true or false.” The Court emphasized that “reckless disregard” is not mere negligence, but “a conscious disregard of the truth or falsity of the statements.” This standard is identical to the one Congress adopted in § 1981a(b)(1). Thus, the same mental state that supports a finding of liability under *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964), and its progeny, is sufficient to support punitive damages under the Civil Rights Act of 1991.

The dissent’s reliance on legislative history to impose a higher standard is misplaced. The House Report’s reference to “an even higher standard” is not binding, and the Court has long held that legislative history cannot override clear statutory language. See *Inglehart v. Stansbury*, 151 U.S. 68, 74 (1894) (“The construction of a statute by the court must be governed by the language used, and not by the supposed intention of the legislature, unless that intention is clearly expressed”). Moreover, the dissent’s citation to Senator Dole’s statement that punitive damages should be reserved for “extraordinarily egregious cases” is not dispositive. As this Court has repeatedly held, “the views of individual legislators are not conclusive.” *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130, 150 (1967). The statute itself defines the standard, and Congress did not require conduct beyond intentional discrimination.

The lower court’s error was compounded by its failure to recognize that the jury had already found intentional discrimination. In *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 557 (1886), this Court held that a court’s discretion to dismiss a case “must be exerted in view of the facts sufficiently proven, and controlled by fixed rules of law.” Here, the jury had found that ADA intentionally discriminated against Kolstad on the basis of sex. That finding, once made, created a basis for punitive damages under § 1981a(b)(1), so long as the plaintiff also proved malice or reckless indifference. The district court erred in refusing to instruct the jury on punitive damages, thereby depriving Kolstad of her statutory right to a jury determination on that issue.

The dissent’s argument that punitive damages should be limited to “extraordinarily egregious” conduct is inconsistent with the common law. In *Denver & Rio Grande Railway v. Harris*, 122 U.S. 597, 605 (1887), this Court affirmed the jury’s power to award punitive damages when the defendant acted “with bad intent, and in pursuance of an unlawful purpose.” The Court did not require that the conduct be “egregious” in the absence of intent. Similarly, in *Bell v. Preferred Life Assurance Society*, 320 U.S. 238, 244 (1943), the Court upheld a punitive damages award where the insurer’s conduct was “wantonly” or “oppressively” done, even though the amount was substantial. The Court emphasized that punitive damages are not limited by a “definite mathematical relationship” to compensatory damages, but are “within the discretion of the jury” so long as they are not “so excessive as to demand the interposition of this court.” Id. at 245.

Finally, the dissent’s reliance on *Henry v. Collins*, 380 U.S. 356 (1965), is misplaced. That case involved a jury instruction on malice in a libel action, not a statutory standard for punitive damages. The instruction in *Henry* defined malice as “culpable recklessness or a wilful and wanton disregard,” which is consistent with the standard in § 1981a(b)(1). The Court in *Henry* did not require “egregious” conduct, but rather focused on the defendant’s state of mind. The same principle applies here.
