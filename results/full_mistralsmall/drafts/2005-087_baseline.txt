**ARGUMENT**

I. ARIZONA’S LIMITATION ON THE USE OF EXPERT EVIDENCE DOES NOT VIOLATE THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not confer upon a defendant an unqualified right to present expert testimony about his mental state to negate mens rea, independent of an insanity defense. *See Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952) (“The Due Process Clause does not require that a State must provide for an insanity defense.”); *Clark v. Arizona*, 126 S. Ct. 2709, 2718 (2006) (plurality opinion) (“The Constitution does not require the insanity defense.”). Arizona’s rule restricting expert mental health evidence to the insanity defense is a permissible exercise of its sovereign authority to define crimes and defenses. *See id.* at 2719 (“A State is free to define its own insanity test.”).

Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert evidence is rationally related to its legitimate interest in avoiding the confusion that expert testimony about mental illness can engender in jurors’ minds when assessing mens rea. *See id.* at 2720. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that States may structure their criminal trials to minimize the risk of jury confusion. *See, e.g., Estelle v. McGuire*, 502 U.S. 62, 72 n.4 (1991) (“[J]urors do not need instruction on ‘legal principles’ that are not relevant to the issues they must decide.”); *Tennessee v. Lane*, 541 U.S. 509, 522 (2004) (recognizing the State’s interest in “preserving the integrity of its judicial process”).

The Arizona Supreme Court’s decision is consistent with this precedent. The court did not exclude all evidence of Clark’s mental illness; it merely confined expert testimony to the insanity defense, where the State had already allocated the burden of proof to the defendant. *See Clark*, 126 S. Ct. at 2719. This allocation is permissible under the Due Process Clause. *See Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 207 (1977) (“[T]he Due Process Clause does not prohibit a State from placing the burden of persuasion on the defendant in a narrow class of cases where the defense is a confession and avoidance.”).

II. ARIZONA’S RULE DOES NOT IMPERMISSIBLY INFRINGE UPON CLARK’S RIGHT TO PRESENT A DEFENSE

The Sixth Amendment right to present a defense is not absolute. *See, e.g., United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998) (“The accused does not have an unfettered right to offer testimony that is incompetent, privileged, or otherwise inadmissible under standard rules of evidence.”). Arizona’s rule does not exclude evidence of Clark’s mental illness; it merely channels that evidence into the insanity defense, where the State has already required him to prove it. *See Clark*, 126 S. Ct. at 2719. This rule is a permissible evidentiary restriction designed to avoid jury confusion. *See id.* at 2720.

The Supreme Court has upheld similar evidentiary rules that limit the introduction of expert testimony. *See, e.g., Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44, 55-56 (1987) (upholding a per se rule excluding hypnotically refreshed testimony); *Scheffer*, 523 U.S. at 308 (upholding a rule excluding polygraph evidence). Arizona’s rule is no different. It is a rational means of ensuring that jurors do not misuse expert testimony about mental illness to negate mens rea. *See Clark*, 126 S. Ct. at 2720.

III. ARIZONA’S RULE DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE

Clark’s equal protection claim fails because Arizona’s rule does not discriminate against a suspect class or impinge upon a fundamental right. *See, e.g., City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985) (“Legislative classifications are presumed valid and will be sustained if the classification itself is rationally related to a legitimate state interest.”). Arizona’s rule is rationally related to its legitimate interest in avoiding jury confusion. *See Clark*, 126 S. Ct. at 2720. Therefore, the rule does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
