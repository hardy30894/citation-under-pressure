The Due Process Clause permits specific jurisdiction only when the defendant has purposefully established contacts with the forum and the suit is fairly tied to those contacts. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945). The Court has repeatedly explained that the forum relation must be such that the defendant “should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there,” and that jurisdiction cannot rest on “random,” “fortuitous,” or “attenuated” contacts. World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 444 U.S. 286 (1980). Because your instruction limits authority to pre-1970 decisions, the governing pre-1970 cases point the same way: a forum may not assert personal jurisdiction over an absent nonresident unless the defendant’s forum activity is the source of the cause of action or is otherwise so connected to the suit that jurisdiction is fair. See International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945); McGee v. International Life Ins. Co., 355 U.S. 220 (1957).

Here, none of Ford’s Montana contacts caused the estate’s claims. The vehicle was designed and first sold outside Montana; it later entered Montana through a chain of private resale. Ford’s advertising and parts sales in Montana may show that Ford does business there, but they do not show that this particular accident or these particular defect and warning claims arose from Ford’s Montana activity. The Due Process Clause does not permit a state court to convert general commercial presence into case-specific jurisdiction. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945); Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437 (1952).

The estate may argue that Ford “purposefully availed” itself of the Montana market and therefore may be sued there on any product-related claim involving a Ford vehicle found in Montana. But pre-1970 authority rejects that breadth. In Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235 (1958), the Court held that “[t]he unilateral activity of those who claim some relationship with a nonresident defendant cannot satisfy the requirement of contact with the forum State.” That principle is dispositive here: Montana’s connection to this case was created by the owner’s decision to bring the Explorer into Montana, not by any Montana conduct of Ford that gave rise to the alleged defect. A defendant does not submit to suit in every state where its products may later travel. Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235 (1958); McGee v. International Life Ins. Co., 355 U.S. 220 (1957).

Nor does McGee help the estate. There, jurisdiction was proper because the suit “was based on a contract which had substantial connection with that State.” McGee v. International Life Ins. Co., 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957). This case is different in kind. The Montana claims are not based on a Montana sale, Montana design decision, Montana warning decision, or Montana repair transaction. They are based on an allegedly defective vehicle introduced into the state by third parties. McGee confirms, rather than undermines, the need for a real connection between the forum and “the controversy.” Id. at 223.

International Shoe likewise requires “certain minimum contacts” and asks whether maintenance of the suit “does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” 326 U.S. at 316. That standard is not satisfied simply because Ford markets and sells products in Montana. If it were, any national manufacturer would be subject to suit in any state where one of its products later causes injury, regardless of where the product was designed, manufactured, sold, or placed into the stream of commerce. Nothing in pre-1970 doctrine permits that result. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945); Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235 (1958).

The Montana Supreme Court’s rule effectively collapses specific jurisdiction into statewide market presence. That is inconsistent with due process. Specific jurisdiction requires more than a forum defendant relationship; it requires a forum-linked controversy. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945). Because Ford’s Montana contacts did not give rise to the estate’s claims, Montana may not exercise personal jurisdiction over Ford in this case. The judgment should be reversed.
