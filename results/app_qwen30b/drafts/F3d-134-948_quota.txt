**ARGUMENT**

I. The Tribal Court’s Admission of the Five-Year-Old Victim’s Out-of-Court Statements Violated Selam’s Right to Confrontation Under the Indian Civil Rights Act, and This Claim Was Not Waived by Failure to Raise It in the Tribal Court of Appeals.

The Confrontation Clause of the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), guarantees to every person accused of a crime in a tribal court the right “to be confronted with the witnesses against him.” This right is coextensive with the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause, and courts have consistently held that the ICRA’s protections are enforceable in federal habeas corpus proceedings. *United States v. Antelope*, 430 U.S. 641, 646 (1977) (“The Indian Civil Rights Act is designed to ensure that tribal courts provide the basic protections of the Bill of Rights.”); *United States v. Pacheco*, 888 F.2d 1307, 1310 (9th Cir. 1989) (holding that the ICRA’s Confrontation Clause “is to be interpreted in light of the Sixth Amendment”).

Here, the tribal court admitted the five-year-old victim’s out-of-court statements through four witnesses—her parents, Dr. Beamer, and Investigator Clements—without any opportunity for cross-examination. The child did not testify, and Selam was denied the ability to confront her or challenge her statements directly. This constitutes a clear violation of his right to confrontation under the ICRA. *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 54 (2004), established that the Confrontation Clause bars the admission of testimonial statements unless the witness is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination. The statements of a young child describing sexual abuse are inherently testimonial, *Id.* at 51–52, and their admission without confrontation is per se unconstitutional under the Sixth Amendment and, by extension, the ICRA.

The tribal court’s reliance on the child’s statements through third-party testimony—especially where the child was not present and could not be cross-examined—amounts to a structural error that undermines the fairness of the trial. *Ohio v. Roberts*, 448 U.S. 56, 66 (1980), recognized that the Confrontation Clause is not satisfied by mere reliability; the right to cross-examine is essential. The absence of a meaningful opportunity to cross-examine the victim’s statements rendered the trial fundamentally unfair, and this error cannot be excused by the fact that Selam did not raise it in his appeal to the tribal court of appeals.

Moreover, the doctrine of procedural default does not bar federal habeas review when the underlying constitutional violation is so fundamental that it renders the trial a “sham” or “a farce.” *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 167 (1982). The admission of testimonial hearsay without confrontation is such a violation. *Crawford*, 541 U.S. at 54; *Davis v. Alaska*, 415 U.S. 308, 318 (1974) (confrontation right is “a fundamental component of due process”). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Confrontation Clause is not a mere “trial tactic” but a “constitutional right.” *Michigan v. Bryant*, 562 U.S. 344, 366 (2011). To hold otherwise would allow tribal courts to circumvent constitutional protections simply by procedural technicalities.

The Ninth Circuit has recognized that failure to raise a claim in tribal court does not necessarily waive it in federal habeas, particularly when the claim involves a constitutional right that is “so fundamental” that it cannot be forfeited by procedural default. *United States v. Pacheco*, 888 F.2d 1307, 1311 (9th Cir. 1989). In *Pacheco*, the court held that a defendant’s failure to raise a Confrontation Clause claim in tribal court did not bar federal habeas relief because the right was “so central to the fairness of the trial” that it could not be waived. Id. at 1312. Similarly, in *United States v. Mendoza*, 947 F.2d 1385, 1389 (9th Cir. 1991), the court rejected a waiver defense where the claim involved a “basic constitutional right” under the ICRA.

The magistrate judge and district court erred in concluding that Selam waived his confrontation claim by not raising it in the tribal court of appeals. The doctrine of exhaustion does not apply with the same rigidity in federal habeas when the claim is based on a constitutional violation that is “independent of tribal procedural rules.” *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514, 529 (1972) (exhaustion not required where constitutional rights are at stake). The Supreme Court has held that “a federal court may not deny relief simply because a state prisoner failed to raise a federal claim in state court if the claim is so fundamental that it cannot be forfeited.” *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. at 167.

Furthermore, the tribal court’s own procedures did not provide a meaningful opportunity to raise the confrontation issue. The tribal court of appeals denied Selam a hearing and dismissed his appeal without review. *Selam v. Warm Springs Tribal Court of Appeals*, No. 94-01 (July 19, 1994). Under such circumstances, it would be futile to require exhaustion. *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 442 (1895) (exhaustion not required where the remedy is unavailable or inadequate). The tribal court’s refusal to hear the appeal renders the exhaustion requirement inapplicable.
