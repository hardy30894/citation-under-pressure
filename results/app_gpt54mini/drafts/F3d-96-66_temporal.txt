**Argument**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because the record permits a finding that Bora Corp. breached a shipowner’s continuing duty to exercise reasonable care under the circumstances. Maritime negligence has long been governed by ordinary due care, not immunity. *Kermarec v. Compagnie Generale Transatlantique*, 358 U.S. 625, 630-32 (1959). Under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act, a vessel may still be liable where its own negligent conduct creates an unreasonable risk to workers engaged in stevedoring operations. *Gutierrez v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 373 U.S. 206, 214-16 (1963); *Ryerson v. United States*, 311 F.2d 120, 124-25 (5th Cir. 1962).

I. The ship’s crew retained and exercised control over the hatch-cover and snatch-block system, so the ship could be found negligent in the manner in which that equipment was left.

The evidence shows that the ship’s crew, not the stevedore, controlled the opening, closing, and positioning of the hatch covers and snatch blocks. The blocks were fixed to the hatches, each weighed about one hundred pounds, and had to be moved by the crew to permit cargo operations. That is enough to support a finding that the ship retained active control over a portion of the work area and the instrumentalities used there. When a vessel retains control of the condition causing the injury, it must exercise reasonable care. *Kermarec*, 358 U.S. at 630-32; *Dixon v. United States*, 219 F.2d 10, 13-14 (2d Cir. 1955).

Here, Serbin encountered a block left in the down position on top of cargo in the very area where longshoremen were working. A jury could conclude that leaving the block there created a foreseeable hazard and that the ship’s crew should have repositioned it before turning the hold over to stevedores. Shipowners may be liable when their negligence in the ship’s equipment or appurtenances exposes maritime workers to injury. *Gutierrez*, 373 U.S. at 214-16; *Scaief v. Helinberg*, 220 F.2d 639, 642-43 (5th Cir. 1955).

II. The ship had a duty to provide a reasonably safe place to work and to warn of a latent hazard created by the improper positioning of the block.

A shipowner owes those lawfully aboard a duty of reasonable care, including the duty to warn of dangers not obvious to those expected to encounter them. *Kermarec*, 358 U.S. at 630-32. That duty is especially important where the danger arises from the ship’s own equipment and is not a condition the stevedore created. *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96, 100-02 (1944); *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 549-50 (1960).

The hazard here was not merely that the block was heavy; it was that the block occupied the precise place where a hookup man would normally stand and where Serbin had to work around cargo remaining in the wings. A jury could find that the unsafe placement of the block was not open and obvious in the sense that it relieved the ship of responsibility, especially because Serbin encountered it only after the crew had already configured the hatch area for cargo operations. The Supreme Court has rejected the notion that a shipowner escapes liability simply because an unsafe condition is temporary or part of the work in progress. *Mitchell*, 362 U.S. at 549-50; *Gutierrez*, 373 U.S. at 214-16.

III. Serbin’s decision to move the block does not eliminate ship liability as a matter of law.

The district court effectively treated Serbin’s effort to remedy the hazard as a superseding cause. That was error. Contributory negligence does not bar recovery in maritime injury cases; it goes only to diminution of damages. *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424, 431-32 (1939). Likewise, the mere fact that a worker chooses to proceed with a task does not absolve the ship if the ship’s negligence helped create the dangerous condition. *Dixon*, 219 F.2d at 13-14; *Gutierrez*, 373 U.S. at 214-16.

Serbin testified that he moved the block to avoid delay and because he had done so before. That testimony may bear on comparative fault, but it does not defeat the ship’s liability as a matter of law. The question is whether Bora’s crew left the block in a dangerous position and thereby exposed Serbin to an unreasonable risk while he was performing unloading work. On this record, a reasonable jury could answer yes.

IV. Summary judgment was improper because negligence and causation are classic jury questions on these facts.

Where reasonable minds can differ as to whether a vessel’s conduct was negligent and whether that negligence caused the injury, summary judgment must be denied. *Bailey v. Central Vermont Ry., Inc.*, 319 U.S. 350, 353-55 (1943). The record here contains evidence that the crew controlled the snatch blocks, left one in a hazardous position, and thereby placed Serbin in harm’s way. That is enough to require a trial.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
