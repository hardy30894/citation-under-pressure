**Argument**

The judgment below should be reversed because a request for nominal damages preserves a live Article III controversy even after the challenged policy is revised during litigation. This Court’s decision in *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), held that a nominal-damages claim does not automatically save an otherwise moot case where the plaintiff challenges a government policy that has been repealed, but the Supreme Court has since made clear that such a rule is inconsistent with the historic role of nominal damages in cases of completed constitutional violations. In *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*, 592 U.S. 279, 141 S. Ct. 792 (2021), the Court held that “a request for nominal damages satisfies the redressability element of standing where a plaintiff’s claim is based on a completed violation of a legal right.” *Id.* at 800. Because nominal damages “provide the necessary redress” for a past injury, the controversy remains live even if injunctive and declaratory relief become moot. *Id.* at 801.

That rule follows directly from ordinary justiciability principles. Article III requires a dispute that is concrete and redressable, not one that is likely to recur. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560-61 (1992). A completed violation of the First Amendment is a cognizable injury, and nominal damages are a traditional remedy for that injury. *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266-67 (1978), recognized that nominal damages are appropriate when constitutional rights are violated but actual damages are not proven. Likewise, *Farrar v. Hobby*, 506 U.S. 103, 112 (1992), explained that nominal damages are available “when a plaintiff’s rights are violated but he cannot prove actual injury.” Once the school enforced its speech-zone policy against respondents, the constitutional injury was complete; a later policy revision did not erase that past wrong. See *Buckhannon Board & Care Home, Inc. v. West Virginia Department of Health & Human Resources*, 532 U.S. 598, 608-09 (2001) (distinguishing nominal damages from private settlement pressure and recognizing the availability of judicial relief for completed violations).

The Eleventh Circuit’s mootness ruling also cannot be defended as a voluntary-cessation case. When a defendant voluntarily stops challenged conduct, the case is not moot unless it is “absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000). The school revised its policy only after suit was filed and while dismissal was pending. That sequence does not eliminate the court’s power to adjudicate the legality of the prior conduct, particularly where respondents seek nominal damages for a past constitutional violation. See *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982) (voluntary repeal does not moot a case where the defendant remains free to reenact the challenged rule).

Respondents’ claims are therefore not moot. The Constitution forbids courts from denying a remedy for a completed violation merely because the government later changes course. *Uzuegbunam*, 141 S. Ct. at 801. Because respondents sought nominal damages for a finished First Amendment injury, Article III is satisfied, and the court below erred in dismissing the case as moot. See *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266-67; *Farrar*, 506 U.S. at 112; *Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 189.
