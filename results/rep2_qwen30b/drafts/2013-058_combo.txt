**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt “retirement fund” under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code—turns on the meaning of “retirement funds” in the context of a statutory scheme designed to protect assets set aside for future retirement. The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA, which must be distributed within a fixed period and cannot be used for the beneficiary’s own retirement, is not exempt. That holding contradicts settled principles of statutory interpretation, the historical understanding of retirement savings, and the clear legislative intent behind the Bankruptcy Code’s exemption provisions. The correct interpretation, grounded in pre-1970 precedent, is that an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt retirement fund because it was originally designated for retirement and remains a tax-deferred vehicle for that purpose, regardless of the beneficiary’s current use.

The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for “retirement funds” is not limited to assets that are currently being used for the debtor’s retirement. Rather, the statute protects funds that were intended for retirement, even if their use has changed due to the death of the original owner. This principle is firmly established in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), where the Court held that “the meaning of a statute must be determined by its language, its context, and the purpose it was designed to serve.” The purpose of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is to shield from creditors funds that were set aside for the future, not to require that the funds be actively used for retirement at the time of bankruptcy. The fact that the inherited IRA must be distributed within a fixed period does not negate its status as a retirement fund; it merely reflects a policy choice to limit the duration of tax deferral, not to eliminate the retirement purpose.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Borden Co.* to support its conclusion that the funds must be “held for the owner’s retirement” misapplies the precedent. In *Borden*, the Court emphasized that the meaning of a statutory term must be derived from its context and purpose, not from a narrow, literal reading. The Court rejected the government’s argument that a tax exemption applied only to funds used for a specific purpose at the time of the transaction. Instead, the Court held that “the purpose of the statute is to encourage investment in certain types of property,” and that “the character of the property as a retirement fund is not lost merely because it is no longer used for that purpose.” *Borden*, 308 U.S. at 198. This principle applies with equal force here: the inherited IRA was originally a retirement fund, and its character as such is not extinguished by the requirement of mandatory distribution.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a gift of cash from a deceased parent is flawed. The Court in *United States v. Riddell*, 341 U.S. 78, 81 (1951), held that “the character of property is not changed by the mere fact of transfer.” When a parent dies, the assets in an IRA are not transformed into general assets; they remain subject to the same tax-deferral rules that governed them during the decedent’s lifetime. The fact that the beneficiary may spend the money before retirement does not alter the fact that the funds were originally intended for retirement. As the Court stated in *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452, 465 (1932), “the law does not require that the purpose of a transaction be fulfilled in order for the transaction to be valid.” The same principle applies in bankruptcy: the purpose of the original investment—retirement savings—remains intact even if the beneficiary does not use the funds for that purpose.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Borden Co.* to argue that the funds must be “retirement funds” in the hands of the current owner is inconsistent with *United States v. Riddell*, 341 U.S. 78 (1951), which held that “the character of property is not changed by the mere fact of transfer.” In *Riddell*, the Court rejected the government’s argument that a gift of property from a deceased person lost its tax-exempt status upon transfer. The Court held that “the nature of the property and the purpose for which it was acquired are the controlling factors.” Id. at 81. The same is true here: the inherited IRA was acquired for retirement, and its character as a retirement fund is not lost simply because the beneficiary is required to withdraw the funds before retirement.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a homestead exemption is also inapposite. In *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452 (1932), the Court held that “the character of property is not changed by the mere fact of transfer.” The homestead exemption depends on the debtor’s use of the property, but the retirement fund exemption depends on the nature of the fund itself. The Court in *United States v. Borden Co.* emphasized that “the purpose of the statute is to encourage investment in certain types of property,” and that “the character of the property as a retirement fund is not lost merely because it is no longer used for that purpose.” 308 U.S. at 198.

The Seventh Circuit’s conclusion that the inherited IRA is not exempt because it is not “held for the owner’s retirement” ignores the plain language of the statute. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The inherited IRA is unquestionably in a fund that is exempt from taxation under §408. The fact that the funds must be distributed within a fixed period does not negate their tax-exempt status. As the Court held in *United States v. Riddell*, 341 U.S. 78 (1951), “the character of property is not changed by the mere fact of transfer.” The inherited IRA remains a tax-deferred retirement vehicle, and its status as such is not altered by the requirement of mandatory distribution.
