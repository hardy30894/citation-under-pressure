**Argument**

The court of appeals erred in requiring the Government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that petitioner remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period. In a conspiracy prosecution, the Government must prove the conspiracy’s existence during the relevant time and the defendant’s membership in it, but a defendant who claims he withdrew bears the burden of establishing that affirmative defense. Smith v. United States, 568 U.S. 106, 110-11 (2013); United States v. Jimenez Recio, 537 U.S. 270, 274 (2003); Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 367-68 (1912). Withdrawal does not negate an element of the offense; it cuts off further liability for later acts and, if timely, can make the statute of limitations a bar. Smith, 568 U.S. at 110-11, 118-19. Because withdrawal is an affirmative defense, the burden of persuasion rests on the defendant, not the Government. Id. at 110-11, 118.

That allocation follows the longstanding rule that “the burden of proving withdrawal rests on the defendant.” Hyde, 225 U.S. at 368. The Court reaffirmed that principle in Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396-97 (1957), explaining that a conspirator remains liable unless he affirmatively withdraws by taking “affirmative action” inconsistent with the object of the conspiracy and communicating that withdrawal in a manner reasonably calculated to reach his confederates. See also United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 438 U.S. 422, 464-65 (1978); Krulewitch v. United States, 336 U.S. 440, 442-43 (1949). A defendant seeking the benefit of the limitations period therefore must prove that withdrawal occurred outside the limitations window. Smith, 568 U.S. at 110-11, 118-19.

The court of appeals’ contrary approach effectively converted withdrawal into an element the Government must disprove beyond a reasonable doubt. That is inconsistent with the Court’s decisions distinguishing elements of the offense from affirmative defenses. In Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 210-11 (1977), the Court held that due process is not offended when a State places on the defendant the burden of proving an affirmative defense that does not negate any element the prosecution must prove. The same principle governs here. The Government must prove the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation within the charging period; if the defendant contends he withdrew earlier, that is a separate issue on which he bears the burden. Smith, 568 U.S. at 110-11; Patterson, 432 U.S. at 210-11.

Nor does the statute of limitations change the burden. A limitations defense ordinarily must be raised and supported by the defendant when the claim depends on facts peculiarly within his knowledge. See United States v. Cook, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 168, 179-80 (1872). That rule applies with special force to withdrawal, which turns on the defendant’s own conduct and communications. Smith, 568 U.S. at 110-11; Hyde, 225 U.S. at 368. Requiring the Government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that no withdrawal occurred would be especially illogical because conspiracies are secretive by nature and withdrawal is “a matter peculiarly within the knowledge of the defendant.” Smith, 568 U.S. at 111.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision also conflicts with the settled rule that a conspiracy continues until its objectives are achieved or abandoned, and that each member is presumed to continue unless he proves withdrawal. United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607-08 (1910); United States v. Jimenez Recio, 537 U.S. at 274-75. Petitioner’s claim that the Government had to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he remained in the conspiracy during the limitations period reverses that presumption and imposes a burden this Court has never recognized. Smith, 568 U.S. at 110-11.

This Court should therefore hold that when a defendant asserts withdrawal before the limitations period, the burden of persuasion remains on the defendant to prove withdrawal. The Government need not disprove withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt; it need only prove the conspiracy and the defendant’s membership in it. Smith, 568 U.S. at 110-11, 118-19; Hyde, 225 U.S. at 367-68; Patterson, 432 U.S. at 210-11. Because the court of appeals required more, its judgment should be reversed.
