**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation in the instant case. The record reveals substantial evidence of Richard Allan Moran’s mental incapacity at the time he discharged his counsel and pleaded guilty—evidence that should have triggered a good faith doubt about his competency and compelled a hearing under due process. The Supreme Court of Nevada’s post-conviction determination, resting on an erroneous legal standard and inadequate factual findings, cannot shield the state from constitutional error. This Court must affirm the judgment below and hold that the failure to apply the “reasoned choice” standard violated Moran’s fundamental right to due process.

The constitutional right to counsel and the right to plead guilty are not mere procedural conveniences; they are essential components of a fair trial. *McNabb v. United States*, 318 U.S. 332, 342 (1943), recognized that the right to counsel is “a fundamental right essential to a fair trial.” When a defendant seeks to waive that right, the court must ensure the waiver is “knowing, intelligent, and voluntary.” *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). This standard, however, is not satisfied by a mere showing of rational understanding of the proceedings. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the capacity to waive constitutional rights demands a higher level of mental functioning than mere competency to stand trial.

In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), the Court held that a defendant is competent to stand trial if he “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” But the Court made clear that this standard is not the same as the standard for waiving counsel. *Drope* itself distinguished between competency to stand trial and the capacity to make a reasoned choice in waiving counsel. The Court emphasized that “the right to counsel is not a mere formality,” and that “a defendant must be able to make a reasoned choice among alternatives.” *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172. This “reasoned choice” standard is the constitutional benchmark for evaluating the validity of a waiver of counsel or a guilty plea.

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this principle. In *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973), this Court held that “a defendant must have the capacity to make a reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him” before a waiver of counsel can be valid. *Sieling* was not an isolated holding. In *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1973), we reiterated that “the standard for waiver of counsel is higher than that for competency to stand trial,” and that “a defendant must be able to understand the consequences of his actions and to make a rational decision.” The same principle applies to guilty pleas. In *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969), the Court held that a guilty plea must be “entered into voluntarily, knowingly, and intelligently,” and that “the record must show that the defendant understood the nature of the charge and the consequences of the plea.” The Court emphasized that “the defendant must be able to understand the nature of the proceedings and the consequences of his actions.” *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 243.

The record in this case demonstrates that Moran lacked the capacity for such a reasoned choice. He had recently attempted suicide, was hospitalized, and was taking four different medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—all of which affect cognitive function, mood, and judgment. The record shows that Moran responded to the court’s questions with monosyllabic answers, indicating a profound lack of engagement. He stated he wanted to fire his attorney to prevent any mitigating evidence from being presented, a decision that defied rational self-interest and suggested a disconnection from reality. This behavior is inconsistent with the ability to make a reasoned choice.

The state court’s post-conviction finding that Moran was competent rests on psychiatric evaluations conducted months earlier, which were designed solely to assess competency to stand trial. *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172, makes clear that the standard for standing trial is lower than that for waiving counsel. The reports of Dr. O’Gorman and Dr. Jurasky, which focused on Moran’s ability to assist counsel and understand the charges, are irrelevant to the higher standard of “reasoned choice.” *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214. The state court’s failure to apply the correct standard renders its finding constitutionally inadequate.

Moreover, the Due Process Clause requires courts to initiate a competency hearing whenever there is a good faith doubt about a defendant’s capacity to waive rights. *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214; *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 885 (9th Cir. 1986). The record here presents such a doubt: a suicide attempt, severe depression, medication affecting cognition, and a plea that was irrational and self-destructive. The court’s failure to inquire further violated due process.

Finally, the Supreme Court has long held that “the right to a fair trial is a fundamental right protected by the Due Process Clause.” *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). When a defendant’s mental state undermines the fairness of the proceedings, due process demands more than a perfunctory inquiry. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the Constitution requires that a defendant be able to make a reasoned choice among alternatives.” *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172. The state court’s failure to apply this standard in Moran’s case deprived him of that fundamental right.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit
