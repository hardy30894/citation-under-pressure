**ARGUMENT**

**I. The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Smoot’s RLA conspiracy claim because the record supports a triable inference that UTU and CSX acted in concert to deprive him of the contractual stock benefit and of fair representation.**

Summary judgment is improper where the evidence would permit a reasonable factfinder to infer concerted action and unlawful purpose. See *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). Under the Railway Labor Act, a union’s duty of fair representation is breached when its conduct toward a member is arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith. *Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 190 (1967); *Czosek v. O’Mara*, 397 U.S. 25, 27 (1970). A carrier may be liable where it knowingly participates in a union’s breach or joins a scheme to deprive the employee of contractual rights. *Bhd. of R.R. Trainmen v. Howard*, 343 U.S. 768, 774-75 (1952); *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co.*, 323 U.S. 192, 204 (1944).

The record, viewed favorably to Smoot, shows more than a mere disagreement over contract interpretation. Smoot sought representation for stock owed under a labor agreement; when UTU General Chairman Elswick refused, the internal UTU appeals board ruled that Smoot was entitled to the shares and directed Elswick to proceed. Earley then represented Smoot before PLB No. 3882, where CSX’s senior director, the union representative, and the neutral arbitrator met in executive session. Smoot’s proof that the executive session was recorded and later revealed internal union opposition to Smoot’s claim supports an inference that the union’s representation was compromised and that CSX benefitted from, or at least exploited, that compromise. A factfinder could conclude that UTU’s conduct was not merely negligent but undertaken in bad faith or in concert with CSX. See *Vaca*, 386 U.S. at 190; *Howard*, 343 U.S. at 774-75.

The district court’s summary judgment ruling also cannot stand if the evidence would permit a finding that the grievance machinery was tainted by divided loyalties or collusion. The Supreme Court has recognized that the duty of fair representation is violated when union conduct is infected by hostility, discrimination, or perfunctory handling. *Air Line Pilots Ass’n, Int’l v. O’Neill*, 499 U.S. 65, 67, 75-76 (1991); *Hines v. Anchor Motor Freight, Inc.*, 424 U.S. 554, 563-64 (1976). Smoot’s proof that the union’s own appeals body rejected Elswick’s initial refusal, yet Earley and CSX proceeded in a manner that denied the benefit, is sufficient to require a trial.

**II. The damages award on defendants’ wiretap counterclaims should be reversed or, at minimum, vacated and remanded because the district court’s award is legally and constitutionally excessive on this record.**

The Federal Wiretap Act authorizes statutory and punitive damages, but the district court must still apply the statute in a manner consistent with the evidence and with due process. See 18 U.S.C. § 2520(c)(2), (c)(2)(B); *Stolz v. United Brotherhood of Carpenters & Joiners of Am., Local Union No. 971*, 315 F.3d 80, 99-100 (2d Cir. 2002) (recognizing discretionary statutory damages under the Act); *State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408, 416-18 (2003) (punitive damages may not be grossly excessive). Even where punitive damages are available, they must bear a reasonable relationship to the harm and the defendant’s conduct. *BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 574-85 (1996).

Here, the district court entered $250,000 in statutory damages, $100,000 in punitive damages, and $86,308.74 in fees and costs against an individual litigant based on the recording of an executive session that was directly tied to an ongoing labor dispute. The record reflects no showing of commercial gain, repeated exploitation, or substantial pecuniary injury to defendants. That makes the combined award punitive in effect and vulnerable under *Gore* and *State Farm*. Because statutory damages under the Act are discretionary, the court was required to tailor the award to the circumstances; a mechanical or maximal award is reversible error. See *Stolz*, 315 F.3d at 99-100.

Moreover, fees and costs may be awarded only to the extent authorized by statute and supported by reasonableness. See *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433-37 (1983). The district court’s award should be vacated if it rested on an unduly broad view of recoverable damages or on an improper punitive multiplier unsupported by the record.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, Smoot respectfully requests that the Court reverse the summary judgment on his RLA claim and vacate the damages award on defendants’ wiretap counterclaims, or at minimum remand for trial and recalculation of any recoverable damages.
