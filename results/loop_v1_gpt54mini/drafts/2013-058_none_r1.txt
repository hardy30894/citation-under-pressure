**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt an inherited IRA from the debtor’s estate. Section 522 exempts only “retirement funds,” and an inherited IRA is not retirement funds in the hands of the debtor. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). Statutory words must be read in their ordinary context and effect. *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Here, Congress chose a term tied to retirement purpose, not merely to tax status. An inherited IRA is the opposite of a fund reserved for the owner’s retirement: the beneficiary cannot add contributions, cannot roll over the account, and must begin taking distributions immediately. That account is therefore a vehicle for current consumption, not retirement savings. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559 (7th Cir. 2013), reversed, 573 U.S. 122 (2014).

The better reading of “retirement funds” is confirmed by the structure of the Bankruptcy Code. Exemptions are creatures of statute and are confined to the scope Congress plainly provided. *Sutherland v. United States*, 264 U.S. 87, 88–89 (1924). When Congress intended to exempt property based on the debtor’s present use or status, it said so. *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543. By contrast, an inherited IRA is not held for the debtor’s retirement; it is inherited wealth subject to mandatory depletion. The Seventh Circuit correctly observed that when the original owner dies, the funds “became no one’s retirement funds.” *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 562.

This Court’s precedents support that reading. In *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917), the Court held that where statutory language is plain, courts must apply it as written and may not enlarge it by policy preference. Likewise, *Crooks v. Harrelson*, 282 U.S. 55, 61 (1930), rejected a construction that would add words not enacted by Congress. If Congress had wanted to exempt inherited IRAs, it could have said so; it did not. The exemption reaches retirement funds, not all tax-favored accounts that once held such funds.

The tax-law treatment of inherited IRAs confirms that they are not ordinary retirement accounts. Congress created special, restrictive rules for inherited accounts: no additional contributions, no rollover, and accelerated distributions. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 560–61. Courts do not treat a label as controlling when the substance differs. *Old Colony R. Co. v. Commissioner*, 284 U.S. 552, 560 (1932). A fund that must be distributed before the beneficiary’s retirement cannot sensibly be treated as retirement savings. *United States v. Pelzer*, 312 U.S. 399, 402–03 (1941). An inherited IRA may preserve tax deferral, but tax deferral is not the same thing as retirement funds.

The respondent’s reading also fits the Court’s repeated insistence that statutory text governs over judicial notions of desirable policy. *Helvering v. Stockholms Enskilda Bank*, 293 U.S. 84, 93 (1934). The debtor’s position would expand the exemption beyond the text, on the theory that because the funds once served a retirement purpose, they should remain exempt forever. But courts may not rewrite a statute to achieve a broader policy aim. *Badaracco v. Commissioner*, 464 U.S. 386, 398 (1984). Bankruptcy exemptions must be applied according to the language Congress enacted, not according to a court’s view of what would be economically preferable.

The account here is also unlike a spouse’s inherited IRA, where the surviving spouse may roll over the funds and continue treating them as the spouse’s own retirement account. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 560. Heidi Clark, as a nonspouse beneficiary, could not do that. Her account functioned as an accelerating withdrawal vehicle, not as a retirement reserve. Under the Bankruptcy Code’s text and structure, the inherited IRA was properly included in the bankruptcy estate.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be affirmed.
