**ARGUMENT**

I. **Equitable Tolling Based on Actual Innocence Does Not Require Proof of Reasonable Diligence**

Perkins respectfully submits that the district court erred in requiring him to demonstrate reasonable diligence as a precondition to equitable tolling based on a credible claim of actual innocence. This Court has long recognized that actual innocence alone may warrant equitable tolling of AEDPA’s statute of limitations. See, e.g., *Schlup v. Delo*, 513 U.S. 298, 324 (1995) (holding that a credible showing of actual innocence can overcome procedural bars to federal habeas review); *House v. Bell*, 547 U.S. 518, 537 (2006) (explaining that a petitioner’s “miscarriage of justice” claim, grounded in actual innocence, may excuse a procedural default).

The Warden’s reliance on *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), is misplaced. In *Pace*, the Supreme Court addressed whether a petitioner had procedurally defaulted a claim by failing to raise it in state court, not whether equitable tolling based on actual innocence requires diligence. *Id.* at 414-15. The Court did not impose a diligence requirement for actual innocence claims; rather, it reiterated that actual innocence can overcome procedural bars. *Id.* at 419. Thus, *Pace* does not alter the well-established rule that a credible claim of actual innocence alone may justify equitable tolling.

This Court has explicitly held that “actual innocence alone is sufficient to warrant equitable tolling of AEDPA’s statute of limitations.” *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010) (emphasis added). The Supreme Court in *Holland* rejected the argument that diligence was a prerequisite to equitable tolling, explaining that the focus must remain on the petitioner’s innocence, not his procedural conduct. *Id.* at 649-50. The Court further cautioned that imposing a diligence requirement would “risk circumvention of the limitations period in every case where a petitioner claims innocence.” *Id.* at 650.

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit has consistently applied this principle. In *Searcy v. Carter*, 246 F.3d 515 (6th Cir. 2001), this Court held that a petitioner’s “credible claim of actual innocence” could toll AEDPA’s limitations period, without requiring diligence. *Id.* at 518. Similarly, in *Bryant v. United States*, 499 F.3d 500 (6th Cir. 2007), the Court reiterated that actual innocence alone may justify equitable tolling, citing *Schlup* and *House*. *Id.* at 504.

The district court’s reliance on *Pace* to impose a diligence requirement conflates two distinct inquiries. The first inquiry—whether a petitioner has a credible claim of actual innocence—is governed by *Schlup* and *House*. The second inquiry—whether the petitioner has been diligent—is irrelevant to the actual innocence analysis. *Holland*, 560 U.S. at 649. The Supreme Court has made clear that the “focus” of the actual innocence inquiry is on the petitioner’s innocence, not his procedural conduct. *Id.*

II. **Perkins Has Made a Credible Claim of Actual Innocence**

Perkins has presented compelling new evidence that directly undermines the prosecution’s case and points to Jones as the actual perpetrator. The affidavits from Hudson, Louis, and Fleming establish that Jones (1) bragged about killing Henderson, (2) was seen with bloodstained clothing on the night of the murder, and (3) attempted to clean bloodstained clothes at a dry cleaner. This evidence was unavailable at trial and directly contradicts the prosecution’s theory that Perkins was the killer.

This Court has held that such newly discovered evidence can support an actual innocence claim. See *Searcy*, 246 F.3d at 518 (finding actual innocence where new evidence implicated another suspect). The Supreme Court has likewise recognized that newly discovered exculpatory evidence can demonstrate a petitioner’s innocence. *Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 324-25. The evidence Perkins presents is precisely the type of evidence that can support an actual innocence claim.

The district court erred in concluding that this evidence was “substantially available” at trial. The affidavits were signed years after Perkins’s conviction, and the record does not reflect that Perkins could have discovered them through due diligence. The Supreme Court has cautioned against “second-guessing” a petitioner’s inability to uncover such evidence earlier. *House*, 547 U.S. at 537. Thus, Perkins has made a credible claim of actual innocence that warrants equitable tolling.

III. **The Warden’s Interpretation Would Undermine AEDPA’s Purpose**

The Warden’s interpretation—that diligence is a prerequisite to equitable tolling based on actual innocence—would frustrate AEDPA’s goal of finality. *See Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001) (explaining that AEDPA seeks to “further the principles of comity, finality, and federalism”). If petitioners must demonstrate diligence to toll the statute of limitations, even when they have credible claims of innocence, AEDPA’s limitations period would become an insurmountable bar in many cases. *Holland*, 560 U.S. at 650. This would risk “circumvent[ing] the limitations period in every case where a petitioner claims innocence.” *Id.*

The Supreme Court has rejected such rigid interpretations of AEDPA’s limitations period. In *Holland*, the Court refused to impose a “blameworthiness” requirement on petitioners seeking equitable tolling, emphasizing that the focus must remain on innocence. *Id.* at 649. The Sixth Circuit has likewise rejected arguments that would impose undue burdens on petitioners with credible innocence claims. *See Searcy*, 246 F.3d at 518.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Perkins respectfully submits that the district court erred in requiring him to demonstrate reasonable diligence as a precondition to equitable tolling based on a credible claim of actual innocence. This Court should reverse the judgment below and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
