**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of the text, structure, and purpose of the multidistrict litigation statute. The answer is no. Congress has drawn a clear line: the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) may transfer cases for *pretrial proceedings*, but the statute mandates that each such action be *remanded* to the original district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The Ninth Circuit’s decision to uphold the District of Arizona’s self-transfer under § 1404(a) not only contravenes this express statutory command but also undermines the foundational policy of § 1407: to ensure that plaintiffs retain their constitutional right to trial in their chosen forum.

The plain language of § 1407(a) is unambiguous. After authorizing the JPML to transfer cases “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” the statute explicitly provides that “each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” at or before the conclusion of those proceedings. This is not a mere suggestion; it is a mandatory directive. The word “shall” imposes a legal obligation, not a discretionary option. As this Court has repeatedly held, “Congress’ use of the word ‘shall’ in a statute is generally regarded as mandatory.” *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative*, 532 U.S. 483, 492 (2001). The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that the transferee court may “transfer the action to itself for trial” under § 1404(a) effectively nullifies this mandatory remand provision. It transforms a statutory command into a mere suggestion, allowing district courts to circumvent Congress’s intent through judicial fiat.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), is misplaced. Those decisions, and the JPML’s own Rule 14(b), which permits a transferee court to transfer a case to itself for trial, are not binding authority. They represent a long-standing judicial practice that has ignored the clear text of § 1407(a). This Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that judicial convenience or administrative efficiency can override a statutory mandate. In *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 634 (1964), this Court emphasized that § 1404(a) was enacted “because broad venue provisions in federal Acts often resulted in inconvenient forums,” and Congress responded by permitting transfer “in the interest of justice.” But that power is not unlimited. It applies only to cases *already brought* in a federal court, not to cases transferred under § 1407(a) for a limited, pretrial purpose.

The distinction is critical. Section 1407(a) does not confer on the transferee court the power to decide the *ultimate venue* for trial. It grants authority only over *pretrial proceedings*—discovery, motions, and other matters common to multiple actions. As this Court held in *Ferens v. John Deere Co.*, 494 U.S. 516, 527 (1990), “the transfer of a case under § 1404(a) is not a transfer of jurisdiction, but a transfer of venue.” The transferee court’s jurisdiction is derivative and limited. It does not gain the power to resolve dispositive issues or conduct trial, especially when Congress has explicitly reserved that power to the original district.

The Ninth Circuit’s dissenting opinion correctly identifies the flaw: “Section 1407(a) authorizes the Multidistrict Panel to transfer cases for pretrial proceedings; after those proceedings are completed, and before trial, ‘each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel.’” *Lexecon, Inc. v. U.S. Dist. Ct.*, 61 F.3d 911, 914 (9th Cir. 1995) (Kozinski, J., dissenting). The dissent further notes that “the Multidistrict Panel assigns cases only for pretrial proceedings; it doesn’t grant proprietary rights in them.” This is the essence of the matter. The transferee court is a temporary custodian, not a permanent forum. To allow it to retain the case for trial is to transform a temporary assignment into a permanent transfer, contrary to the statute’s design.

Moreover, the practice of self-transfer under § 1404(a) undermines the very purpose of § 1407: to prevent forum shopping and ensure fairness in complex litigation. Plaintiffs are entitled to trial in the district where they originally filed their suit. As this Court held in *Stewart Organization, Inc. v. Ricoh Corp.*, 487 U.S. 22, 30 (1988), “the choice of forum is a fundamental right of the plaintiff.” Allowing a transferee court to retain jurisdiction for trial effectively strips that right from the plaintiff, especially when the transfer was not of the plaintiff’s choosing. The inconvenience and expense of litigating in a distant district—where the plaintiff has no local counsel, no familiarity with the court, and no connection to the community—are not merely “prejudice” correctable on appeal. They are the very harm § 1407(a) was designed to prevent.

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that “the district court’s order transferring this case to itself for trial simply leaves the parties where they are” (61 F.3d at 913) is a legal fiction. The parties are not “where they are” in the sense of their original forum. They are in a district that was never their chosen venue, and the transfer was not for trial but for pretrial coordination. To allow the court to retain the case for trial is to convert a temporary assignment into a permanent one, in direct violation of § 1407(a).
