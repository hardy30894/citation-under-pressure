ARGUMENT

Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act provides that nothing in the Act “shall apply to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies * * * exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The court of appeals held that the Coast Guard’s general statutory authority over vessels, 14 U.S.C. § 2; 46 U.S.C. § 6301, automatically displaces OSHA jurisdiction on every vessel, inspected or uninspected. That conclusion misreads the statute and conflicts with this Court’s precedents.

Preemption under § 4(b)(1) requires that another agency have actually “exercise[d]” its authority by promulgating “standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Fifth Circuit itself recognized this limitation in Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), stating that the Coast Guard’s “comprehensive regulation and supervision of seamen’s working conditions” creates the exemption. Id. at 826. Where, as here, the Coast Guard has issued only minimal equipment rules for uninspected vessels, 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq., and has promulgated no standards governing the well-control and blowout-prevention procedures at issue, the statutory predicate for preemption is absent.

Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), is not to the contrary. There the court excluded OSHA evidence because the Coast Guard had already addressed the precise hazard (deck markings) through its own regulations. Id. at 1122. The same is true of Donovan, which involved inspected vessels subject to detailed Coast Guard manning and safety rules. 720 F.2d at 827. Neither decision addressed an uninspected vessel on which the Coast Guard had declined to exercise its authority.

The Act’s text and structure confirm that mere possession of unused power does not trigger preemption. Congress used the verb “exercise,” not “possess,” and the legislative history reflects an intent to avoid duplicative regulation only where another agency has acted. Because the Coast Guard has not acted here, OSHA retains jurisdiction to enforce the cited standards on Rig 52.
