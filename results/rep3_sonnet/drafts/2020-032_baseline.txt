# ARGUMENT

## I. A CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES ALONE CANNOT SUSTAIN FEDERAL JURISDICTION AFTER A CHALLENGED POLICY IS RESCINDED

Article III limits federal courts to deciding "Cases" and "Controversies," and this requirement "subsists through all stages of federal judicial proceedings." *Spencer v. Kemna*, 523 U.S. 1, 7 (1998). A case becomes moot when "the issues presented are no longer 'live.'" *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). Once Georgia Gwinnett College rescinded the Freedom of Expression Policy and Student Code of Conduct provision at issue, there was no live controversy left for Petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief to redress—a conclusion Petitioners do not contest. The question is whether a bare request for nominal damages, untethered to any actual injury, can revive jurisdiction that would otherwise be extinguished.

It cannot. This Court has repeatedly held that mootness turns on whether a decision would have any "effect in the real world." *Chafin v. Chafin*, 568 U.S. 165, 172 (2013). Nominal damages, by definition, have no such effect—they are "not compensation for the injury caused by the defendant's actions" but rather "the law's recognition" of a technical violation. *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978). This Court in *Carey* explained that nominal damages exist precisely because "the value of [certain rights] is not readily translated into monetary terms," and awarding a token sum vindicates the abstract right without redressing any concrete harm. *Id.* at 266–67. A remedy designed to acknowledge a violation without compensating for injury cannot supply the redressability that Article III demands. *See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 561 (1992) (requiring that a favorable decision "likely" would "redress the injury").

The Eleventh Circuit's decision below, following its en banc holding in *Flanigan's Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017), correctly recognized that "a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law." That rule is compelled by this Court's repeated admonition that federal courts do not sit to resolve abstract disagreements about the constitutionality of policies that no longer operate against anyone. *See Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013) (a case is moot when "it is impossible for a court to grant any effectual relief whatever to the prevailing party").

## II. PETITIONERS' OWN PLEADING CONFIRMS THAT ONLY NOMINAL, NOT COMPENSATORY, DAMAGES REMAINED AT ISSUE

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the First Amended Complaint sought only nominal damages, not compensatory damages. As this Court has held, compensatory damages in a § 1983 action must rest on "actual injury caused by the defendant's actions," not on "the abstract 'value' or 'importance' of constitutional rights." *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 308 (1986) (quoting *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 254). Petitioners' complaint alleged no such actual injury—no lost wages, no medical expenses, no specified emotional harm—and their prayer for relief requested only "nominal damages" alongside injunctive and declaratory relief. The Eleventh Circuit properly declined to construe vague boilerplate phrases like "monetary damages" as a hidden claim for compensatory relief, particularly where Petitioners' own litigation conduct—seeking leave to "clarify" their complaint only after mootness became apparent—confirmed that no such claim had been pled. This Court has cautioned against permitting plaintiffs to manufacture continuing jurisdiction through after-the-fact characterizations of their pleadings. *Cf. Lujan*, 504 U.S. at 561 (the party invoking jurisdiction bears the burden of establishing its elements "with the manner and degree of evidence required at the successive stages of the litigation").

## III. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT DID NOT ABUSE ITS DISCRETION IN DECLINING TO PERMIT AN IMPROPERLY RAISED AMENDMENT

Petitioners' attempt to inject a compensatory damages claim through a response brief, rather than a proper motion, was correctly rejected. This Court has long required that requests for leave to amend be presented in a manner that allows the district court to evaluate the proposed amendment's substance. *See Foman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962) (leave to amend should be freely given "in the absence of any apparent or declared reason," such as futility or undue delay, but the decision remains committed to the "discretion" of the trial court). Here, Petitioners never specified what additional factual allegations would support a compensatory damages claim; they proposed only to add the words "compensatory and" to their prayer for relief. A court cannot assess futility, prejudice, or undue delay under *Foman* without knowing the substance of the proposed pleading. The Eleventh Circuit's affirmance of the denial of leave to amend under these circumstances was consistent with this Court's guidance that discretion over amendment remains with the trial court and depends on the amendment being properly and substantively presented.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
