The Occupational Safety and Health Act reaches employment performed in a workplace in a State, including vessels operating in territorial waters. Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 39 (1932). Section 4(b)(1) withdraws coverage only when another federal agency has exercised statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health. Preemption therefore turns on actual exercise of that authority. Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (“the scheme of federal regulation [must be] so pervasive as to make reasonable the inference that Congress left no room for the States to supplement it”).

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited to discrete equipment requirements. Kelly v. Washington ex rel. Foss Co., 302 U.S. 1, 8 (1937) (federal inspection statutes “do not cover the whole field of possible safety regulation”). Where federal regulation is not comprehensive, concurrent authority remains. Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941); Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co., 272 U.S. 605, 611 (1926).

Vessels in territorial waters are subject to the same general principles of concurrent federal regulation that govern other workplaces. Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205, 216 (1917); Panama Railroad Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375, 388 (1924). The Coast Guard has not issued standards addressing the working conditions at issue here. Absent such exercise, section 4(b)(1) does not displace the Act. The Osceola, 189 U.S. 158, 175 (1903); Knickerbocker Ice Co. v. Stewart, 253 U.S. 149, 160 (1920).

Congressional purpose confirms this result. The Act was designed to fill regulatory gaps, not to create an industry-wide exemption for any class of vessels. Crowell, 285 U.S. at 39; Rice, 331 U.S. at 230. Because the Coast Guard has not occupied the field with respect to uninspected vessels, OSHA retains jurisdiction to issue the citations.
