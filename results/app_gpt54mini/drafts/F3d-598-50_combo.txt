**ARGUMENT**

A new trial is required because the jury heard repeated opinion testimony from government witnesses on the very issues the jury alone was sworn to decide: credibility, probable cause, and the meaning of the evidence. Long before the Federal Rules of Evidence, courts held that a witness may not usurp the jury’s function by stating an ultimate legal conclusion or by offering another witness’s credibility as his own. See *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 503, 519 (1943); *Berry v. City of Chicago*, 105 U.S. 41, 49-50 (1881); *Gunning v. Cooley*, 281 U.S. 90, 94-95 (1930). The same principle controls here. The district court permitted two Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant to tell the jury, in substance, that Ramos and Rivera were truthful, that probable cause existed, and that the officers’ version of events was corroborated by the evidence. That was reversible error.

First, the challenged testimony was classic vouching. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the credibility of witnesses is for the jury, not for another witness to certify. *Quercia v. United States*, 289 U.S. 466, 470-72 (1933); *Alford v. United States*, 282 U.S. 687, 694-95 (1931). Opinion testimony from neutral-seeming government actors is especially prejudicial because jurors may credit it as official validation rather than as evidence. See *Vicksburg & Meridian R.R. Co. v. Putnam*, 118 U.S. 545, 553-54 (1886); *United States v. Hall*, 44 F.2d 918, 919-20 (2d Cir. 1930). Here, the ADAs and lieutenant did not merely recount facts; they supplied the jury with their own assessments of whether the officers were telling the truth. That invaded the jury’s role and improperly bolstered the defense.

Second, the witnesses were allowed to state legal conclusions that the law reserves to the court and jury. A witness may describe facts, but may not tell the jury what result those facts require. *United States v. Spaulding*, 293 U.S. 498, 506 (1935); *Myers v. United States*, 17 F.2d 230, 234 (8th Cir. 1927). Whether the officers had probable cause, and whether the arresting officers were acting lawfully for purposes of the obstruction charge, were precisely the disputed issues. Yet the government witnesses were permitted to answer those questions in effect for the jury. That was beyond the permissible scope of testimony. See *Throckmorton v. Holt*, 180 U.S. 552, 570-71 (1901); *Mutual Life Ins. Co. v. Hillmon*, 145 U.S. 285, 295-96 (1892).

Third, the witnesses were allowed to interpret evidence that the jurors could interpret equally well on their own. Courts have long rejected testimony that merely tells the jury what to infer from ordinary evidence. *Pennsylvania Co. v. Roy*, 102 U.S. 451, 456-57 (1880); *The Hattie Palmer*, 58 F. 921, 922-23 (2d Cir. 1893). The photographs, the police account, and the sequence of events were all matters the jurors could analyze independently. But the government’s witnesses were permitted to tell the jury what those items “meant,” thereby displacing the jurors’ own judgment. That is not proper testimony; it is argument disguised as evidence.

These errors were not harmless. An error requires reversal where it may have influenced the verdict. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946); *Fillippon v. Albion Vein Slate Co.*, 250 U.S. 76, 82-83 (1919). The improper opinions here went to the central disputed questions in the case: whether the officers fabricated their account, whether probable cause existed, and whether the prosecution of Cameron was justified. Because the jury’s verdict likely turned on which side it believed, the admission of official opinion testimony cannot be dismissed as harmless.

Appellants are also entitled to a new trial because the court should have instructed the jury that the offense of obstructing governmental administration requires lawful police conduct. New York law has long required that an obstruction charge be tied to a lawful official action. See *People ex rel. Varriale v. Gaggino*, 236 N.Y. 268, 270-71 (1923); *People v. Case*, 42 N.Y. 1, 7-8 (1870). An instruction omitting that limitation permitted the jury to convict the officers’ conduct as “obstruction” even if the arrest itself was unlawful, which in turn undermined the false-arrest claims. The requested charge should have been given.

Finally, the court should have instructed on punitive damages. Punitive damages are available where the evidence permits an inference of malice or wanton wrongdoing. See *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851); *Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107-08 (1893); *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 565-66 (1886). Appellants’ testimony, if believed, showed knowing fabrication by police officers to justify an arrest and prosecution. That was more than enough to warrant a punitive-damages instruction.

For these reasons, the judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
