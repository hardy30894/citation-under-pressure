**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Plaintiff’s class-action complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on the ground of mootness, despite the fact that the named plaintiff’s individual claim was satisfied by a Rule 68 offer of judgment. The court’s conclusion—that a class action must be dismissed when the named plaintiff’s personal claim is extinguished, regardless of the pending class certification motion—contravenes well-established precedent recognizing that a class action is a distinct legal proceeding with a public interest in its resolution, even when the named plaintiff’s individual stake is satisfied. This case presents a clear instance where the public interest in determining the validity of a class-wide claim, and the need to preserve the integrity of the class action mechanism, outweighs the defendant’s unilateral attempt to terminate the litigation through a pre-certification offer of judgment.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a class action is not merely a vehicle for resolving individual claims, but a procedural device designed to address widespread harm and promote judicial efficiency. In *Sosna v. Iowa*, 419 U.S. 393, 399 (1975), the Court emphasized that “a class action is not simply a collection of individual claims aggregated for convenience,” but rather “a distinct form of litigation” with “a public dimension” that justifies jurisdiction even when individual claims are resolved. The Court further held that “the mere fact that a named plaintiff’s individual claim has been satisfied does not necessarily render the entire class action moot,” particularly where the class certification issue remains unresolved and the public interest in the outcome persists. Id. at 402.

This principle was reaffirmed in *U.S. Parole Commission v. Geraghty*, 445 U.S. 388, 401 (1980), where the Court stated that “a case may be moot as to the named plaintiff, but not as to the class,” and that “the class action device is not rendered ineffective by the satisfaction of the individual claim of the named plaintiff.” The Court explained that “the class action is a procedural mechanism designed to resolve issues of general importance,” and that “the resolution of those issues may be essential to the protection of the rights of absent class members.” Id. at 402. Thus, the existence of a pending class certification motion—especially one filed before the offer of judgment was made—creates a live controversy that satisfies the constitutional requirement of a “case or controversy” under Article III.

The Tenth Circuit has previously acknowledged this principle. In *Clark v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins.*, 590 F.3d 1134, 1138 (10th Cir. 2009), the court held that “a suit brought as a class action must be dismissed for mootness when the personal claims of the named plaintiffs are satisfied and no class has been properly certified.” However, *Clark* did not address the situation where a motion for class certification has been timely filed and is pending at the time of the offer of judgment. The court’s statement in *Clark* must be read in context: it applies only where no certification motion has been filed or where the motion is not pending. Here, Lucero filed his motion for class certification on February 22, 2010—well before the district court dismissed the case—and the court had already set a hearing date for March 31, 2010. The motion was not filed after the offer of judgment; it was filed in accordance with the court’s own discovery schedule and was pending at the time of dismissal. Under these circumstances, *Clark* does not control.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that a defendant may unilaterally end a class action by offering to satisfy the named plaintiff’s individual claim. In *Sosna*, the Court rejected the idea that a plaintiff’s withdrawal of a claim automatically extinguishes the class action, stating that “the class action is not a mere aggregation of individual claims, but a distinct form of litigation.” 419 U.S. at 399. The Court emphasized that “the class action device is not rendered ineffective by the satisfaction of the individual claim of the named plaintiff.” Id. at 402. This principle is particularly salient in the context of the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, where the enforcement of statutory rights is not only personal but also serves a broader public interest in deterring abusive collection practices.

The defendant’s reliance on *Reed v. Heckler*, 774 F.2d 1005 (10th Cir. 1985), and *Weiss v. Regal Collections*, 385 F.3d 337 (3d Cir. 2004), is misplaced. *Reed* involved a claim that had been voluntarily dismissed by the plaintiff before any class certification motion was filed, and the court held that the case was moot. 774 F.2d at 1008. *Weiss* similarly involved a situation where the named plaintiff had not filed a class certification motion and the offer of judgment was made before any such motion was pending. 385 F.3d at 342. Neither case supports the proposition that a pending class certification motion is irrelevant when an offer of judgment is made. Indeed, the Third Circuit in *Weiss* acknowledged that “a class action may survive the satisfaction of the named plaintiff’s individual claim if the class certification motion is pending.” Id. at 343.

The district court’s failure to recognize the distinction between a pending class certification motion and a motion filed after the offer of judgment renders its decision inconsistent with both Supreme Court precedent and the Tenth Circuit’s own jurisprudence. The court’s conclusion that jurisdiction is lacking simply because the offer of judgment was not accepted—without requiring Lucero to accept it or entering judgment—undermines the purpose of Rule 68 and the class action mechanism. Rule 68 is designed to encourage settlement, not to allow defendants to circumvent the class action process by offering full relief to the named plaintiff before certification.

For these reasons, the district court erred in dismissing the complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The pending motion for class certification, filed in compliance with the court’s discovery schedule and supported by a detailed memorandum, creates a live controversy that satisfies Article III. The public interest in resolving the legality of BCR’s collection practices on a class-wide basis remains intact. The judgment of the district court must be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings on the class certification motion.
