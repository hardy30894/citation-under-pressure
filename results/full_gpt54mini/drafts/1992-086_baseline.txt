**ARGUMENT**

The School District violated the First Amendment when it denied Lamb’s Chapel access to school premises because its films were “church related.” The District had opened its facilities for after-hours community use, and once it did so, it could not exclude Lamb’s Chapel’s presentation on the basis of the religious viewpoint it expressed. That is settled First Amendment law. *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 113 S. Ct. 2141 (1993); *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45-46 (1983); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802-03 (1985).

The governing principle is that when government creates a forum for expressive activity, it may not discriminate against speech because of its viewpoint. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46; *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806. Even in a limited or designated public forum, the State must respect First Amendment limits as to the class of speakers and subjects it has chosen to admit. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46; *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 802. The School District here had already permitted community access to its facilities during non-school hours for a range of uses. Having opened the premises for such expressive activity, it could not single out religious speech for exclusion merely because it was religious. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 113 S. Ct. 2141; *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267-70 (1981).

This Court’s precedents are directly in Lamb’s Chapel’s favor. In *Widmar*, a public university that had made its facilities generally available to student groups could not deny access to a religious group because of the religious content of its meetings. 454 U.S. at 267-70. The Court held that once a forum is opened for expressive activity, exclusion of speech based on its religious character is content-based discrimination requiring a compelling justification. Id. at 269-70. The same logic controls here. The District allowed after-hours use by community groups but excluded Lamb’s Chapel because its films addressed family life “from a Christian perspective.” That is content discrimination at minimum, and viewpoint discrimination in substance. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 113 S. Ct. 2141; *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829-30 (1995).

The State cannot defend the exclusion by recasting it as a neutral application of a no-religion policy. A rule that permits discussion of family values, child rearing, or morality but bars the same discussion when presented from a religious perspective is not viewpoint neutral. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831; *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 533 U.S. 98, 107-12 (2001). In *Lamb’s Chapel*, the Court held that permitting the group to show films on child-rearing and family values would not have amounted to endorsement of religion, because the school would simply have allowed religious speech on an otherwise available subject. 113 S. Ct. 2141. The same result follows here: the District could not suppress Lamb’s Chapel’s speech because it spoke to a permitted topic—family values—from a religious viewpoint. *Id.*; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831-32.

Nor does the Establishment Clause justify the exclusion. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the proposition that the Establishment Clause requires government to shut religious speakers out of an otherwise open forum. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 274-75; *Lamb’s Chapel*, 113 S. Ct. 2141; *Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 248-53 (1990). In *Widmar*, the Court explained that equal access to a forum does not imply endorsement of religion; rather, the relevant inquiry is whether a reasonable observer would understand the government to be facilitating private speech in a neutral forum. 454 U.S. at 274-75. Likewise, in *Mergens*, the Court held that allowing a religious student club equal access to school facilities did not violate the Establishment Clause. 496 U.S. at 250-53. The District therefore had no constitutional warrant to exclude Lamb’s Chapel out of an unfounded fear of establishment concerns.

The Second Circuit’s contrary conclusion rested on the mistaken premise that the District had not opened its facilities to religious use at all. But the First Amendment inquiry does not permit the government to define the forum so narrowly as to exclude religious viewpoints from otherwise admitted subjects. *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 802-06; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829-31. Once the District created a forum for after-hours community presentations, it could not reserve the subject of family values for secular speakers only. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 113 S. Ct. 2141; *Good News Club*, 533 U.S. at 107-12.

The judgment should be reversed.
