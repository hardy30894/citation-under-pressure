**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth Amendment as interpreted in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). In *Ring*, the Supreme Court held that Arizona’s sentencing scheme violated the Sixth Amendment because it permitted a judge, rather than a jury, to find the aggravating factors necessary to impose the death penalty. *Id.* at 609. The Court reasoned that those factors “operate as the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense,” and thus must be found by a jury. *Id.* at 609.

Florida’s scheme is fundamentally different. Unlike Arizona, Florida does not permit a judge to find aggravating factors. *See* § 921.141(3), Fla. Stat. (2012); *Hurst v. State*, 202 So. 3d 42, 60 (Fla. 2016). Instead, the jury is tasked with finding the existence of aggravating factors beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* The trial court then considers those findings—along with any mitigating evidence—in determining whether to impose the death penalty. *Id.* This process ensures that the jury, not the judge, makes the critical factual determinations required by *Ring*. *See Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 647-48 (1990) (upholding Arizona’s scheme because the jury found the aggravating factors, even though the judge made the ultimate sentencing decision).

Hurst’s reliance on *Ring* is misplaced. The Court in *Ring* expressly distinguished Florida’s scheme, noting that it “requires the jury to make the critical findings” of aggravating factors. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609 n.6. Florida’s scheme thus satisfies the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee.

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

Hurst’s Eighth Amendment claim fails for two independent reasons. First, Florida’s scheme does not impose the death penalty without sufficient safeguards to ensure reliability. Second, Florida’s scheme does not depart from the “evolving standards of decency” that govern the Eighth Amendment.

**A. Florida’s Scheme Ensures Reliability in Capital Sentencing.**

The Eighth Amendment requires that the death penalty be imposed only after “heightened reliability.” *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). Florida’s scheme satisfies this requirement. The jury must unanimously find at least one aggravating factor beyond a reasonable doubt, and the trial court must weigh that finding against mitigating evidence before imposing the death penalty. *See* § 921.141(3), Fla. Stat. (2012); *Hurst*, 202 So. 3d at 60. This process ensures that the death penalty is imposed only in cases where the jury has found the presence of statutory aggravators and the trial court has considered all relevant mitigating evidence.

Hurst’s reliance on *Ring* is again misplaced. The Court in *Ring* did not hold that the Sixth Amendment requires jury unanimity in capital sentencing. Instead, it held that the Sixth Amendment requires that the jury, not the judge, find the aggravating factors. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609. Florida’s scheme satisfies this requirement, as the jury makes the critical factual findings. *See Walton*, 497 U.S. at 647-48.

**B. Florida’s Scheme Does Not Depart from Evolving Standards of Decency.**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are “cruel and unusual” in light of “evolving standards of decency.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). Hurst’s claim that Florida’s scheme violates this principle is foreclosed by this Court’s precedents. In *Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242 (1976), the Court upheld Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, noting that it provides “sufficient indicia of reliability” and “narrow[ed] the class of death-eligible defendants.” *Id.* at 258. The Court reaffirmed this holding in *Walton*, explaining that Florida’s scheme ensures that the death penalty is imposed only after careful consideration of aggravating and mitigating evidence. *Walton*, 497 U.S. at 647-48.

Hurst’s reliance on *Ring* is unavailing. The Court in *Ring* did not address the Eighth Amendment, and its holding was limited to the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609. Florida’s scheme thus does not violate the Eighth Amendment.

**III. FLORIDA’S SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT WITH RESPECT TO MENTAL RETARDATION.**

Hurst’s claim that Florida’s scheme violates the Eighth Amendment with respect to mental retardation is foreclosed by *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002). In *Atkins*, the Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of mentally retarded defendants. *Id.* at 317. The Court, however, left it to the states to determine the manner in which this constitutional restriction is enforced. *Id.*

Florida has complied with *Atkins* by enacting a statutory scheme that sets forth a three-pronged test for mental retardation. *See* § 921.137(1), Fla. Stat. (2012). Under this scheme, a defendant must prove by clear and convincing evidence that he has significantly subaverage intellectual functioning, concurrent deficits in adaptive behavior, and that the condition manifested before age eighteen. *Id.* The trial court then determines whether the defendant has met this burden. *See Nixon v. State*, 2 So. 3d 137, 142 (Fla. 2009).

Hurst’s claim that Florida’s scheme violates the Eighth Amendment is without merit. The scheme ensures that mentally retarded defendants are not executed, and it provides a clear and workable standard for determining mental retardation. *See Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317. The trial court’s finding that Hurst is not mentally retarded is supported by substantial evidence and does not violate the Eighth Amendment.
