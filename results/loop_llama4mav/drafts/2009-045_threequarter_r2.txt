The question presented is whether a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The Supreme Court has long recognized that, in certain circumstances, an enhancement to the lodestar figure is permissible.

In Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1882), the Court established that a court may award a reasonable attorneys' fee to a prevailing party. The Court noted that the allowance is made with reference to the labor and expense involved. Id. at 532-33. This principle has been reaffirmed in subsequent cases, including Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), where the Court held that a court may consider the skill and standing of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 124-25.

In Hartford Fire Insurance Co. v. Bradley, 142 U.S. 651 (1892), the Court further emphasized the importance of considering various factors when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee. The Court stated that the ascertainment of the reasonable amount of the fee is a matter for the exercise of the sound discretion of the court. Id. at 655.

The principle that a court may enhance a fee award based on exceptional performance and results is also supported by Anglo-American law predating the federal fee-shifting statutes. In Copp v. Cheshire, 1 Vern. 259 (1683), the English Court of Chancery recognized that a court may award a liberal allowance to a solicitor who has rendered exceptional service.

Furthermore, in Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court held that a court has the power to award attorneys' fees in a case where a party has created, preserved, or protected a fund or property for the benefit of others. The Court noted that the allowance is part of the historic equity jurisdiction of the federal courts. Id. at 164.

Additionally, the Court in Ex parte Peterson, 253 U.S. 300 (1920), recognized that a court has the authority to allow a reasonable fee to a party who has incurred expenses in litigating a matter that benefits others. The Court stated that the court has the power to "fix the fee of the auditor." Id. at 316.

These authorities demonstrate that, under the principles established by the Supreme Court and other courts, a district court has the discretion to enhance a fee award based on exceptional performance and results. The lodestar figure is not always a sufficient measure of the value of the services rendered, and a court may consider other factors when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee, as seen in cases like Internal Improvement Fund v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1882), and  Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Railroad Commission, 184 U.S. 503 (1902), where the Court considered the Court's consideration of various factors when determining a reasonable fee.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the district court's decision to enhance the lodestar figure in this case was not an abuse of discretion. The court's detailed findings and specific evidence on the record supported the enhancement, and the Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court's decision. The longstanding principles established by the Supreme Court and other courts support the conclusion that a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based on the quality of performance and results obtained.
