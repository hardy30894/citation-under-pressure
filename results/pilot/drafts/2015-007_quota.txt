**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue here—under which a non-unanimous jury may recommend death and a trial court may impose the death penalty based on findings of aggravating circumstances not required to be unanimously determined—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. This Court has repeatedly upheld Florida’s capital sentencing scheme as consistent with both constitutional protections, and the decision in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 122 S.Ct. 2428, 153 L.Ed.2d 556 (2002), does not compel a different result.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require a Jury to Unanimously Find Aggravating Circumstances or Determine Mental Retardation as a Bar to Death**

*Ring v. Arizona* held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find the existence of aggravating factors that increase the maximum penalty for a crime. 536 U.S. at 584, 122 S.Ct. 2428. However, *Ring* did not hold that every factual determination necessary to impose the death penalty must be made unanimously by the jury. The Court emphasized that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee is satisfied when the jury determines the facts that “the law makes essential to the punishment.” Id. at 609, 122 S.Ct. 2428. The Court did not require unanimity in *Ring*, nor has it since required unanimity in any capital sentencing context.

This Court has consistently upheld Florida’s non-unanimous jury recommendation scheme. In *Hurst v. State*, 18 So.3d 975, 985 (Fla.2009), the Florida Supreme Court affirmed that “the Sixth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury verdict in the penalty phase of a capital case.” The Court reasoned that the jury’s role is advisory, and the trial court retains the ultimate authority to impose the death penalty. Id. at 986. This interpretation is consistent with *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490, 120 S.Ct. 2348, 147 L.Ed.2d 435 (2000), which held that any fact increasing the penalty beyond the statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury. But *Apprendi* did not require unanimity, nor did *Blakely v. Washington*, 542 U.S. 296, 301, 124 S.Ct. 2531, 159 L.Ed.2d 403 (2004), which applied *Apprendi* to sentencing enhancements. In *Hurst*, the Florida Supreme Court correctly concluded that the jury’s recommendation of death is not a “finding” that increases the penalty beyond the statutory maximum, but rather an advisory opinion on a discretionary sentencing decision. See *Hurst*, 18 So.3d at 986.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has never held that a jury must unanimously determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 122 S.Ct. 2242, 153 L.Ed.2d 335 (2002), held that executing mentally retarded defendants violates the Eighth Amendment. But *Atkins* did not require that a jury determine mental retardation as a bar to execution. The Court left the procedural mechanism for determining mental retardation to the states. Id. at 317, 122 S.Ct. 2242. Florida’s three-pronged test—requiring a full-scale IQ of 70 or below, adaptive deficits, and onset before age 18—is a constitutionally permissible method of determining mental retardation. See *Nixon v. State*, 2 So.3d 137, 142 (Fla.2009). The burden of proving mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence rests on the defendant, and the trial court, not the jury, makes the final determination. This procedure is consistent with *Hurst*, where the trial court found Hurst’s mental impairments significant but concluded he did not meet the statutory definition of mental retardation. See *Hurst*, 18 So.3d at 987.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that the jury’s role in capital sentencing is advisory, not determinative. In *Spencer v. State*, 476 U.S. 363, 372, 106 S.Ct. 1878, 90 L.Ed.2d 375 (1986), the Court upheld Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, stating that “the jury’s recommendation is not binding on the trial judge.” Similarly, in *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994, 111 S.Ct. 2680, 115 L.Ed.2d 836 (1991), the Court emphasized that “the Eighth Amendment does not require that a jury unanimously find aggravating circumstances.” The Court has never held that a non-unanimous jury recommendation violates the Sixth Amendment, and this Court should not now do so.
