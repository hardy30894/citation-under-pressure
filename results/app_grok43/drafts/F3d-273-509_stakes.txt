ARGUMENT

I. The Release Does Not Bar Appellant's Claims.

The June 6, 1988 release discharges the FDIC only from claims "arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985." Appellant's claims concern the FDIC's post-closure conduct as receiver, including its March 1991 payment of post-insolvency interest and its charging of legal and office expenses to the receivership estate. These matters lie outside the release's plain terms.

New York law requires that a release "be read as a whole" and "construed in accordance with the intent of the parties." Bank of Am. Nat'l Trust & Sav. Ass'n v. Gillaizeau, 766 F.2d 709, 713 (2d Cir. 1985). The release was executed to settle the September 1985 and May 1986 actions, which addressed the OCC's closure decision and related pre-receivership events. It does not mention, and therefore does not reach, the FDIC's subsequent administration of the receivership. See Mangini v. McClurg, 24 N.Y.2d 556, 562 (1969) (release covers only matters the parties intended to dispose of).

II. The Statute of Limitations Does Not Bar Appellant's Claims.

Appellant's claims for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste are subject to the six-year period of N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7). Because the FDIC acts as receiver until the receivership terminates, the claims do not accrue until that termination on November 1, 1995. Appellant's complaint, filed October 31, 1995, is therefore timely.

Under New York law, a cause of action against a fiduciary for self-dealing or waste "accrues when the fiduciary's misconduct is discovered or should have been discovered." See Armstrong v. McAlpin, 699 F.2d 79, 88-89 (2d Cir. 1983). The FDIC's final accounting and distribution of any surplus could occur only upon termination of the receivership. Until that date, appellant could not ascertain the full extent of the alleged overcharges and improper interest payments. The district court's contrary ruling therefore misapplied the accrual rule.
