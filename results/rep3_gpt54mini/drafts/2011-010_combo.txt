**Argument**

The question presented should be answered no. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore Act coverage only where the injury itself occurs on the outer Continental Shelf. The statutory text, the Act’s structure, and the Supreme Court’s guidance all point to a geographic limitation tied to the place of injury, not merely to some antecedent offshore connection.

Section 1333(b) covers “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Congress used a territorial term—“on the outer Continental Shelf”—to define the operations that trigger coverage, and then linked compensation to an “injury occurring as the result of” those operations. That language is naturally read to require a shelf-based injury. Statutes are ordinarily construed according to their text and place-based terms are given effect. See *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); *United States v. Goldenberg*, 168 U.S. 95, 102–03 (1897).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that OCSLA is a locale-centered statute. In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Court explained that “the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage” is determined “principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Id.* at 219. Although *Tallentire* involved § 1333(a)(2)(A), its reasoning is directly relevant: OCSLA coverage turns on the situs Congress chose. The Court’s separate observation that § 1333(b) “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement” confirms that situs remains central. *Id.* at 219 n.2. This Court should not divorce § 1333(b) from that locale-based framework.

That understanding is reinforced by *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985). There, the Court stressed that OCSLA incorporates the Longshore Act only within a statute drawing “a clear boundary,” and that coverage depends on the “explicit geographic limitation” Congress imposed. *Id.* at 423–24. The Court refused to extend Longshore Act coverage to a worker whose offshore-related employment lacked the necessary statutory nexus. *Id.* at 424–25. Likewise here, a claim based on a land-based fatal accident falls outside the geographic reach Congress created.

Older decisions construing similarly territorial statutory schemes point the same way. In *Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc.*, 314 U.S. 244 (1941), the Court looked to the place of injury in applying the Longshore Act’s coverage boundaries. *Id.* at 249–50. In *O’Loughlin v. Parker*, 163 F.2d 1012 (4th Cir. 1947), the court likewise treated situs as the decisive feature in determining Longshore coverage. *Id.* at 1014–15. And in *Davis v. Department of Labor and Industries*, 317 U.S. 249 (1942), the Court held that coverage under a territorial workers’ compensation regime depended on the location of the injury and the statute’s jurisdictional reach. *Id.* at 252–53. Those cases reflect the settled rule that when Congress ties compensation to a defined area, courts should not enlarge the statute beyond that area.

Petitioner’s theory would also upset the balance Congress struck between federal and state compensation systems. The Supreme Court has long cautioned against reading compensation statutes to sweep broadly where Congress drew a narrower line. See *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 41–42 (1932); *Cudahy Packing Co. of Nebraska v. Bazanos*, 245 U.S. 524, 528 (1918). The OCSLA was enacted to fill a gap for offshore injuries where state law does not reach, not to federalize all land accidents with some offshore antecedent. See *Parker*, 314 U.S. at 249–50; *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219.

Nor does petitioner’s reliance on operational causation solve the problem. “Resulting from” is not a license to ignore the statute’s geographic text. The causal words in § 1333(b) identify the type of offshore operations covered; they do not eliminate the requirement that the injury occur within the statute’s offshore situs. See *Gulf Refining Co. v. Leahy*, 93 F.2d 332, 334–35 (8th Cir. 1937) (rejecting an interpretation that would “extend the statute beyond its plain territorial scope”). To read the statute otherwise would turn every land-based accident involving offshore employees or equipment into an OCSLA claim, a result inconsistent with the statute’s text and with the Court’s longstanding insistence on respecting congressionally fixed boundaries. See *United States v. Wong Kim Ark*, 169 U.S. 649, 702–03 (1898); *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95–96 (1820).

Finally, the broader compensation statutes confirm that Congress knew how to write status-and-causation coverage when it wanted to. The Longshore Act itself requires both a maritime status and a maritime situs. *Parker*, 314 U.S. at 249–50. OCSLA’s incorporation of that Act should not be read to jettison situs entirely and replace it with an amorphous “substantial nexus” test untethered to the statutory text. See *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219 n.2; *Herb’s Welding*, 470 U.S. at 423–25.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed. OCSLA does not extend Longshore Act coverage to a worker injured on land merely because the worker’s employment was connected to offshore operations. The injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf itself.
