**Argument**

I. The term "debt" under § 523(a)(2)(A) encompasses statutory damages resulting from fraud.

The Third Circuit erred in holding that statutory treble damages are nondischargeable. The Bankruptcy Code defines a nondischargeable debt as one "for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by... actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The term "debt" is not defined in the Bankruptcy Act, but it is clear that it includes all liabilities arising out of a transaction, contract, or other legal relationship. *United States v. O'Brien*, 386 U.S. 784, 787 (1967). This definition is broad enough to encompass statutory damages awarded under state law. *United States v. Kassab*, 321 F.2d 511, 513 (2d Cir. 1963). Here, the treble damages are a liability arising directly from the transaction of renting the apartments and the resulting fraud. Therefore, they constitute a "debt" under the Code.

II. The "fresh start" policy does not preclude the discharge of debts obtained by fraud.

The Third Circuit relied on the "fresh start" policy to deny discharge. *United States v. Whiting Pools, Inc.*, 364 U.S. 641, 649 (1960). However, this policy applies only to the "honest but unfortunate debtor." *Id.* at 649. Petitioner is not an "honest" debtor; he committed actual fraud to obtain money. Consequently, the policy does not protect him from the consequences of his actions. The debt, including statutory damages, is a direct result of his misconduct and must be treated as a debt obtained by fraud.
