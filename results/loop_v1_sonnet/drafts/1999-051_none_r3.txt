**ARGUMENT**

A defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may still serve as "cause" to excuse the default of the claim counsel failed to raise. That conclusion follows from four settled principles of this Court's habeas corpus jurisprudence: default requires the petitioner's own deliberate choice, not counsel's neglect; the right to counsel is fundamental and cannot be forfeited by counsel's own default; exhaustion is satisfied once the State has had a fair opportunity to correct the error, without regard to the outcome of that opportunity; and rigid, mechanical forfeiture rules have no place in a proceeding where liberty is at stake.

**I. Procedural Default Requires the Petitioner's Own Deliberate Choice, Not Counsel's Neglect**

This Court has long insisted that default in habeas corpus is measured by the petitioner's own conduct, not his lawyer's. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court held that a federal court may withhold relief on default grounds only where the petitioner himself has "deliberately bypassed the orderly procedure of the state courts." *Id.* at 438. Carpenter made no such choice. His appellate counsel—not Carpenter—failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence on direct appeal, and later filed an untimely application to reopen. Under *Fay*, counsel's failure cannot be attributed to Carpenter as though it were his own considered abandonment of the claim.

This principle traces to *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), which defined waiver as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Id.* at 464. Nothing is relinquished, in the sense *Zerbst* requires, when counsel rather than the client fails to preserve a claim. To treat counsel's oversight as the client's own waiver would invert *Zerbst*'s central premise—that constitutional rights are lost only through the client's own knowing and voluntary act.

**II. The Ineffective-Assistance Claim Retains Independent Constitutional Force Because the Right to Counsel Is Fundamental**

The right to effective appellate representation is not a procedural nicety but an incident of the fundamental right to counsel this Court has repeatedly protected. In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), the Court declared that "the right of one charged with crime to counsel may not be deemed fundamental and essential to fair trials in some countries, but it is in ours." *Id.* at 344. That same Term, in *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), the Court extended that guarantee to the first appeal as of right, holding that a State cannot condition vindication of federal rights on the fortuity of retained counsel while indigent appellants proceed unrepresented. *Id.* at 357–58.

Carpenter's claim—that his appellate lawyer failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence—asserts precisely the deprivation *Gideon* and *Douglas* were designed to remedy. A later default of that claim, itself the product of the very disabling failure it describes, should not be permitted to strip it of its capacity to excuse the underlying default. Were it otherwise, a single failure of counsel could shield itself from review merely by going uncorrected long enough to compound into a second procedural bar—precisely the kind of self-perpetuating deprivation *Gideon* and *Douglas* sought to prevent.

**III. Comity Does Not Require Independent Exhaustion of the Ineffective-Assistance Claim Before It May Serve as Cause**

Exhaustion exists to give state courts, as this Court explained in *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), "the first opportunity to pass upon and correct alleged violations of . . . constitutional rights." *Id.* at 118. It is a doctrine of comity, not a mechanism for extinguishing meritorious federal claims. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), likewise grounded exhaustion in "comity between the state and federal sovereignties." *Id.* at 204.

Ohio had exactly the opportunity *Hawk* and *Darr* require: Carpenter presented his ineffective-assistance claim to the Ohio Court of Appeals in an application to reopen his direct appeal. That the state court declined to reach the merits on timeliness grounds does not diminish the fact that Ohio's courts were given the first chance to correct the error. Nothing in the comity rationale underlying *Hawk* and *Darr* requires that the claim survive that state-court gauntlet successfully before it may perform its traditional function of explaining a related, intertwined default in federal habeas.

**IV. This Court's Habeas Jurisprudence Counsels Against Rigid, Mechanical Forfeiture Where Liberty Is at Stake**

The writ has always been administered with the flexibility necessary to prevent the loss of liberty through default rather than through guilt. In *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), Justice Holmes explained that where constitutional guarantees are fundamentally undermined, "it is a duty of the United States . . . to secure" that those guarantees are not defeated by "the machinery of the state." *Id.* at 91.

*Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), cautioned that "conventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake," *id.* at 8, and required equitable, individualized judgment rather than rigid, rule-bound dismissal. *Id.* at 17–18. And *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), confirmed the federal judiciary's independent responsibility to examine the substance of constitutional claims in habeas rather than treating state procedural rulings as automatically dispositive. *Id.* at 458.

**Conclusion**

*Fay*, *Zerbst*, *Gideon*, *Douglas*, *Hawk*, *Darr*, *Moore*, *Sanders*, and *Brown* together establish that a default attributable to counsel's own failure—rather than to the petitioner's deliberate choice—should not compound itself into an absolute bar to relief. The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim, though itself defaulted through no fault of his own, retained sufficient constitutional force to excuse the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The judgment below should be affirmed.
