The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. Moran v. Godinez, 972 F.2d 263 (9th Cir. 1992). The circuit court properly distinguished between the standard for competency to stand trial and the standard for competency to waive constitutional rights, holding that the latter requires a "reasoned choice" among available alternatives. Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); see also Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211, 214-15 (9th Cir. 1973).

The trial court's failure to apply this heightened standard when accepting Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty pleas violated his due process rights. The state court's post-conviction hearing, held more than three years after Moran's pleas, did not cure this error because it applied the wrong legal standard. The state court's reliance on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran's competency to stand trial, rather than his competency to waive constitutional rights, was misplaced. See Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171-72 (1975) (defining competency to stand trial as the capacity to understand the proceedings and assist counsel).

The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the record did not support a finding that Moran was competent to make a reasoned choice among available alternatives. Moran's recent suicide attempt, his ingestion of multiple medications, and his monosyllabic responses to the court's questions raised significant doubts about his competency. See Chavez v. United States, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981) (reversing conviction where defendant's waiver of rights was not knowing and intelligent due to mental condition).

The Due Process Clause requires a trial court to ensure that a defendant's waiver of constitutional rights is knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. See Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969). To satisfy this requirement, the court must apply a heightened standard of competency when a defendant seeks to waive counsel or plead guilty. The Ninth Circuit's decision should be affirmed, as it correctly applied this principle to Moran's case.

In sum, the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard when determining a defendant's competency to waive constitutional rights. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court's failure to do so in Moran's case violated his due process rights, and its decision should be affirmed.
