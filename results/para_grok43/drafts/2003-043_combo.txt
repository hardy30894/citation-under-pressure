The IRS may collect a tax only from the taxpayer against whom it has made a timely assessment. Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247, 260 (1935) (“The assessment is given the force of a judgment.”). Each partner is a distinct taxpayer. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14) (1954); United States v. Rindskopf, 105 U.S. 418, 422 (1882). An assessment against a partnership therefore does not extend the limitations period for collection from the partners. Lewis v. Reynolds, 284 U.S. 281, 283 (1932).

The statute requires that an assessment “record the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. Because the regulation demands “identification of the taxpayer,” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1, an assessment naming only the partnership leaves the partners’ separate liabilities unassessed. Burnet v. Sanford & Brooks Co., 282 U.S. 359, 365 (1931). The three-year period of § 6501(a) therefore expired as to each partner, and the ten-year collection period of § 6502(a) never arose.

State-law joint-and-several liability does not dispense with this requirement. Phillips v. Commissioner, 283 U.S. 589, 602 (1931). A creditor must first reduce the partner’s liability to judgment before reaching personal assets. Helvering v. Mitchell, 303 U.S. 391, 396 (1938). The IRS obtained no such judgment within the three-year period.

Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is not to the contrary. There the partner had already paid; the court merely refused a refund. Id. at 910. Coson v. United States, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961), confirms that an assessment against the partnership alone creates no lien against a partner’s property. Commissioner v. Sunnen, 333 U.S. 591, 598 (1948), likewise treats each taxpayer’s assessment as an independent event.

Because the IRS assessed only the partnership, the statute of limitations bars collection from the individual partners. The bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the claims.
