### ARGUMENT

The judgment should be reversed because the record does not permit a finding that Sieben discharged or disciplined Feltmann because of sex or in retaliation for protected activity. Title VII forbids discrimination “because of” sex, and the plaintiff bears the burden of proving that challenged employment actions were motivated by unlawful intent. See *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802–04 (1973). Even under the flexible proof methods recognized in discrimination cases, the plaintiff must still establish facts from which unlawful discrimination may reasonably be inferred. See *Bailey v. Southeastern Belt Tel. Co.*, 196 F.2d 300, 304 (5th Cir. 1952); *Bing v. Roadway Express, Inc.*, 485 F.2d 441, 445 (5th Cir. 1968).

#### I. The evidence of sales criticism and probation shows legitimate business judgment, not sex discrimination.

Sieben’s evidence showed a straightforward business reason for Rich’s actions: after the incentive program ended, sales declined, profits fell, and Rich evaluated consultants based on performance. Feltmann’s own record confirms that her average gross profit from December 1992 through April 1993 was the lowest of all consultants. An employer may make personnel decisions based on efficiency, performance, and profitability, even if the employee disagrees with the criticism. Courts have long recognized that discrimination statutes do not convert ordinary management judgments into legal wrongs. See *Norton v. Sam’s Club, Inc.*, 154 F. Supp. 785, 787 (D. Md. 1957); *Bailey*, 196 F.2d at 304.

The fact that Feltmann had a strong year overall, or was once tied for monthly sales, does not establish unlawful sex bias. Employers may lawfully evaluate employees by the specific metric they deem important, here gross profit and sales technique. See *Murray v. D.R. Horton, Inc.*, 153 F. Supp. 280, 283 (S.D. Cal. 1957); *Bing*, 485 F.2d at 445. Rich testified that Feltmann’s sales method produced low profit per vehicle because she did not fully explain the cars’ features. That is a classic nondiscriminatory rationale, and nothing in the record shows that similarly situated male consultants with comparable profit performance were treated more favorably. See *Payne v. Western & Atl. R.R. Co.*, 81 F. Supp. 226, 229 (N.D. Ga. 1948).

The same is true of the January memo and March probation. A supervisory memorandum criticizing work habits and a probation decision tied to low sales performance are ordinary disciplinary measures. Without proof that those actions were a pretext for sex discrimination, they cannot support liability. See *St. Louis & S.F. Ry. Co. v. Terry*, 69 F. 539, 542 (8th Cir. 1895); *Bailey*, 196 F.2d at 304.

#### II. The Chicago trip and demo-car restriction were facially neutral and not shown to be sex-based.

The Chicago sales trip was offered to the two top consultants; when they declined, Rich did not extend it to several male consultants either. That fact defeats any inference that Feltmann was singled out because she was female. Title VII does not require an employer to create parity where the decision at issue was applied neutrally. See *Furnco Constr. Corp. v. Waters*, 438 U.S. 567, 577 (1978). But even without resort to later authority, earlier decisions require proof of unequal treatment, not merely disappointment with a business decision. See *Bing*, 485 F.2d at 445.

The demo-car limitation was likewise a business rule adopted after concern over dealership costs. Rich restricted all consultants’ out-of-area use, and Feltmann alone continued to violate the rule by driving weekly to Marion, Illinois, after receiving a memo reiterating the restriction. Enforcement of a neutral rule cannot be converted into sex discrimination absent proof that men were similarly situated and exempted. See *Norton*, 154 F. Supp. at 787; *Payne*, 81 F. Supp. at 229. Feltmann’s assertion that some men “occasionally” took cars out of the area does not establish comparable, repeated defiance after a direct written warning. The law does not require an employer to ignore an employee’s repeated noncompliance. See *St. Louis & S.F. Ry. Co.*, 69 F. at 542.

#### III. The retaliation claim fails because the record does not show a causal connection between the complaint and the challenged actions.

Feltmann complained about Anzalone’s vulgar remark in November 1992, and Sieben promptly investigated, reprimanded Anzalone, and warned that future misconduct would result in termination. That prompt response undermines any claim that Sieben retaliated for the complaint. Retaliation requires proof that protected conduct caused adverse action. See *Sullivan v. Little Hunting Park, Inc.*, 396 U.S. 229, 237 (1969); *Bing*, 485 F.2d at 445.

Here, the challenged employment actions were driven by later business concerns: declining sales, low gross profit, work habits, and violation of the demo-car restriction. Temporal sequence alone is not enough, particularly where the employer has an independent, documented reason for acting. See *Bailey*, 196 F.2d at 304; *St. Louis & S.F. Ry. Co.*, 69 F. at 542. Moreover, the only evidence of hostility after the complaint was Barnstead’s comment that Feltmann had “polarized” herself and would have a thick personnel file; that remark was not made by the decisionmaker and is too attenuated to prove retaliatory motive. See *Bing*, 485 F.2d at 445.

Because the record shows legitimate, performance-based reasons for Sieben’s actions and no substantial evidence of sex-based or retaliatory intent, the judgment cannot stand. The Court should reverse.
