# ARGUMENT

## THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE TO ANY INJURY CAUSED BY OPERATIONS ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF, REGARDLESS OF WHERE THE INJURY OCCURS

### A. The Statutory Text Contains No Situs-of-Injury Requirement

Section 1333(b) of OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage for "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This text imposes a causation requirement—the injury must result from OCS operations—not a location requirement for the injury itself. The statute's sole geographic limitation attaches to "operations," not to "injury." Had Congress wished to limit coverage to injuries occurring on the OCS itself, it knew how to do so, as it did elsewhere in OCSLA. See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (extending state law "to the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon").

This Court has recognized that OCSLA's provisions are not uniform in their geographic scope. In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Court expressly distinguished § 1333(b) from the choice-of-law provision at issue there, noting that § 1333(b) "superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement" found in other sections. *Id.* at 219 n.2. This observation confirms that § 1333(b) operates differently from provisions like § 1333(a)(2)(A), which the Court held was "determined principally by locale." *Id.* at 219. The *Tallentire* footnote itself acknowledged that § 1333(b) "has no bearing" on cases not involving that specific provision, *id.*, precisely because its causation-based structure differs from the pure situs test governing choice of law.

### B. A "But-For" or "Substantial Nexus" Test Faithfully Implements Congress's Chosen Language

The court below correctly held that an injury is "the result of" OCS operations when there exists "a substantial nexus between the injury and extractive operations on the shelf." This standard gives effect to the statutory phrase "resulting from" without importing an extratextual situs limitation.

The Third Circuit reached the same conclusion in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), holding that a claimant satisfies § 1333(b) by showing that the injury would not have occurred "but for" operations on the OCS. *Id.* at 810-11. There, an employee injured in a car accident en route to a helicopter that would carry him to an offshore platform was covered under OCSLA, even though the accident occurred entirely onshore. *Id.* at 806, 811. The Third Circuit rejected the argument that *Tallentire*'s dictum controlled, recognizing that *Tallentire* "simply not on point" because it addressed a different provision with different statutory text. *Id.* at 810.

By contrast, the Fifth Circuit's situs-of-injury test, adopted in *Mills v. Director, OWCP*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), rewrites the statute to require that the injury occur "on a[n OCS] platform or on the waters above the [OCS]," *id.* at 362, a requirement nowhere found in the statutory text. That approach conflates the location of "operations" with the location of "injury," when § 1333(b) plainly distinguishes the two.

### C. The Tallentire Footnote Is Unconsidered Dictum Entitled to No Deference

Petitioner's reliance on the *Tallentire* footnote is misplaced. As the court below correctly recognized, that footnote is "textbook dictum"—the § 1333(b) issue "was not before the Court, was not briefed by the parties, and had no relevance to the case before it." The Court itself disclaimed any holding on the point, stating that § 1333(b) "has no bearing on this case." *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219 n.2.

This Court has long cautioned against extending "general expressions" beyond the context in which they were "used," because "their possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." *Humphrey's Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935) (quoting *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399-400 (1821)). An unreasoned aside on an issue neither presented nor briefed cannot bear the weight Petitioner assigns it.

### D. The Purpose of OCSLA Supports Coverage Based on Causation, Not Geography

OCSLA's remedial purpose—ensuring adequate compensation for workers engaged in the extraction of resources from the OCS—is served by a causation test and disserved by an arbitrary situs rule. A worker injured while performing duties essential to OCS operations should not lose coverage merely because the fortuity of his employer's operational structure placed him onshore at the moment of injury. The substantial-nexus test ensures that coverage tracks the statute's functional concern: whether the injury truly "result[ed] from" operations extracting resources from the Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b).

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit.
