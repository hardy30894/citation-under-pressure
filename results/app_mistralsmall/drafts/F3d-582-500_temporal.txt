**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Erdman Failed to Accumulate Sufficient Hours to Qualify for FMLA Leave.**

A. The FMLA’s statutory eligibility requirement of 1,250 hours in the preceding 12 months is satisfied where an employee’s extra hours were “suffered or permitted” by the employer, even if unapproved. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A) (1994); *Mitchell v. Director, OWCP*, 400 F.2d 599, 604 (4th Cir. 1968) (construing analogous “suffered or permitted” language in the Fair Labor Standards Act to include hours worked with employer’s knowledge and acquiescence).

B. Here, the record shows that Nationwide permitted Erdman to work and be compensated for extra hours for years, including under Sarno’s tenure and initially under Getgen’s. *See* 29 C.F.R. § 785.11 (1999) (explaining that an employer’s actual or constructive knowledge of extra work, coupled with payment or acceptance of benefits, establishes compensable time). Getgen’s belated objection in February 2003 came only after she had already acknowledged Erdman’s use of “comp” time as valid in January 2003. This is sufficient to raise a genuine dispute of material fact as to whether Nationwide “suffered or permitted” those hours. *See Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co.*, 328 U.S. 680, 687 (1946) (holding that time worked with employer’s knowledge is compensable under the FLSA).

C. The District Court’s contrary conclusion rested on an unduly narrow reading of the statute. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A). As this Court has recognized in analogous contexts, the FMLA’s eligibility provision must be construed in light of its remedial purpose: to balance the demands of the workplace with the needs of families. *See* *McTernan*, 564 F.3d at 646 (citing *Bowers v. Federal Express Corp.*, 548 F.3d 346, 352 (3d Cir. 2008)). Construing “suffered or permitted” to exclude hours that an employer tacitly approved would frustrate that purpose. *See* *Phillips v. Smith*, 363 F.2d 418, 422 (5th Cir. 1966) (construing “suffered or permitted” under the FLSA to include hours worked with employer’s tacit approval).

D. Because a reasonable jury could find that Nationwide suffered or permitted Erdman’s extra hours, summary judgment was inappropriate. *See* *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986) (summary judgment is inappropriate where the record contains evidence from which a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party).

**II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Erdman’s FMLA Interference Claim.**

A. The FMLA prohibits employers from “interfer[ing] with, restrain[ing], or deny[ing] the exercise of or the attempt to exercise” FMLA rights. 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(1) (1994). This includes discouraging an employee from requesting leave or manipulating hours to avoid eligibility. *See* *Wascura v. Carver*, 169 F.3d 104, 108 (2d Cir. 1999) (citing *Hodgens v. General Dynamics Corp.*, 144 F.3d 151, 160 (1st Cir. 1998)).

B. Here, Nationwide’s sudden and unexplained change in policy—barring “comp” time just weeks after Erdman requested FMLA leave—raises a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Nationwide interfered with her FMLA rights. *See* *Nevada v. Burdick*, 369 F.2d 712, 715 (9th Cir. 1966) (holding that unexplained adverse actions taken shortly after protected activity may give rise to an inference of discrimination).

C. The temporal proximity between Erdman’s request for FMLA leave and Nationwide’s adverse actions, coupled with the lack of any legitimate non-discriminatory explanation for the policy change, creates a jury question as to pretext. *See* *Phillips v. Martin Marietta Corp.*, 363 F.2d 124, 127 (5th Cir. 1966) (holding that proximity in time between protected activity and adverse action, without more, may suffice to raise an inference of discrimination).

**III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Erdman’s FMLA Retaliation Claim.**

A. To prevail on a retaliation claim under the FMLA, Erdman must show that Nationwide’s proffered reason for her termination was pretextual. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(2) (1994); *Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins*, 490 U.S. 228, 244 (1989) (plurality opinion) (holding that pretext may be shown by evidence that the employer’s stated reason was false or discriminatory).

B. Here, Nationwide’s claim that Erdman was terminated for using profanity during a monitored call is undermined by the fact that the call was personal and the company’s policy explicitly prohibited monitoring personal calls. *See* *Mitchell v. Automobile Owners Ins. Co.*, 330 F.2d 63, 66 (6th Cir. 1964) (holding that an employer’s violation of its own policy may give rise to an inference of pretext).

C. Moreover, the timing of Erdman’s termination—just weeks after she requested FMLA leave—raises a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Nationwide’s stated reason was pretextual. *See* *Phillips v. Martin Marietta Corp.*, 363 F.2d at 127 (holding that proximity in time between protected activity and adverse action may suffice to raise an inference of retaliation).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the District Court’s grant of summary judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s opinion.
