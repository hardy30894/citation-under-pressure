**ARGUMENT**

I. **Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code Excepts from Discharge Only Debts "Obtained by" Fraud, Not All Debts Incurred by a Fraudulent Debtor.**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that *any* debt owed by a debtor who committed fraud—including statutorily imposed treble damages—is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). The plain text of the statute limits nondischargeability to debts "to the extent obtained by" fraud. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A) (emphasis added). This Court has long construed similar statutory language narrowly to effectuate the Bankruptcy Code’s "fresh start" policy. *See Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 708–09 (1878) (interpreting predecessor statute to exclude only debts "created by the fraud" of the bankrupt, not all debts owed by a fraudulent debtor). The same principle applies here: a debt is nondischargeable only if the fraud *procured* the debt itself, not if the debtor’s general misconduct tainted unrelated obligations. *See id.*; *Chapman v. Forsyth & Limerick*, 43 U.S. 202, 206 (1844) (discharge voids only provable debts, and fraud must relate to the specific debt in question).

II. **Treble Damages Under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act Are Not "Obtained by" Fraud.**

The Third Circuit’s holding that statutorily mandated treble damages are nondischargeable because they "flowed from" fraud ignores the statute’s text. Treble damages are not "obtained by" fraud; they are imposed *as punishment* for fraud already committed. *See Missouri State Life Ins. v. Jones*, 290 U.S. 199, 202 (1933) (distinguishing between compensatory damages, which compensate a loss, and penalties, which punish wrongdoing). This Court has repeatedly refused to expand discharge exceptions beyond their text. *See Neal*, 95 U.S. at 709 (narrow construction of fraud exception); *Scott v. Ellery*, 142 U.S. 381, 385 (1892) (discharge extinguishes only provable debts, and penalties are not provable). Treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are punitive in nature and thus fall outside § 523(a)(2)(A)’s scope.

III. **The Bankruptcy Code’s "Fresh Start" Policy Requires a Narrow Construction of § 523(a)(2)(A).**

This Court has emphasized that the Bankruptcy Code’s discharge provisions should be construed liberally to give debtors a "fresh start." *See Bruning v. United States*, 376 U.S. 358, 361 (1964) (noting the "humane purpose" of bankruptcy law). Expanding § 523(a)(2)(A) to encompass punitive damages would undermine this policy by allowing creditors to transform ordinary debts into nondischargeable obligations through state-law penalties. *See Neal*, 95 U.S. at 709 (warning against constructions that "would be inconsistent with the liberal spirit which pervades the entire bankrupt system"). The Third Circuit’s approach does precisely that, converting a state-created penalty into a nondischargeable debt.

IV. **Pre-1970 Precedent Supports Petitioner’s Construction of § 523(a)(2)(A).**

This Court’s pre-1970 decisions confirm that discharge exceptions must be strictly limited to debts *procured* by fraud, not all debts owed by a fraudulent debtor. In *Neal*, the Court rejected a broad construction of the fraud exception, holding that only debts "created by the fraud" of the bankrupt were excepted. 95 U.S. at 708–09. Similarly, in *Chapman*, the Court refused to extend discharge exceptions to debts not directly tied to the bankrupt’s fraudulent conduct. 43 U.S. at 206. These cases foreclose the Third Circuit’s expansive reading of § 523(a)(2)(A).

V. **The Third Circuit’s Holding Conflicts with This Court’s Precedent on Punitive Damages.**

This Court has repeatedly distinguished between compensatory and punitive damages in bankruptcy contexts. *See Missouri State Life Ins. v. Jones*, 290 U.S. at 202 (holding that penalties are not dischargeable, but only because they are not provable debts, not because they are "obtained by" fraud). The Third Circuit’s conflation of punitive damages with debts "obtained by" fraud is unsupported by this Court’s precedent. *See id.*; *Scott*, 142 U.S. at 385.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed. The debts at issue—including the statutorily imposed treble damages—are dischargeable because they were not "obtained by" fraud within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A).
