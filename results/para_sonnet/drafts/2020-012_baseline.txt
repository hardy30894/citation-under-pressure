# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN LANGUAGE OF SECTION 355(f) LIMITS JUDICIAL REVIEW TO FINAL DECISIONS ON THE MERITS OF BENEFITS CLAIMS

Section 355(f) of the Railroad Unemployment Insurance Act, incorporated into the Railroad Retirement Act, grants circuit courts jurisdiction to review "any final decision of the Board." 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). This Court has long recognized that judicial review provisions of this type must be construed against the backdrop of traditional principles governing agency reopening decisions. In *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), this Court held that a decision by the Social Security Administration denying a petition to reopen a prior benefits determination is not a "final decision" subject to judicial review under the analogous provision of the Social Security Act. The Court explained that permitting review of refusals to reopen "would frustrate the congressional purpose, plainly evidenced in [the Act], to impose a 61-day limitation upon judicial review of the Secretary's final decision on the initial claim for benefits." *Id.* at 108.

*Sanders* controls here. The reasoning that animated this Court's decision—that reopening determinations are discretionary, agency-created mechanisms not woven into the statutory scheme creating a right to a hearing on the merits—applies with equal force to Board decisions under the Railroad Retirement Act. As the Fifth Circuit correctly held in *Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), "the jurisdiction of the federal courts of appeals is limited to the review of Board decisions on the merits of a claim for benefits after administrative appeals have been exhausted." *Id.* at 140. A decision declining to reopen is not such a decision; it is instead an exercise of administrative grace addressed to the Board's own regulations governing when a closed claim may be revisited. *See* 20 C.F.R. § 261.2.

## II. THE WEIGHT OF CIRCUIT AUTHORITY CONFIRMS THAT REOPENING DECISIONS ARE UNREVIEWABLE

The Fifth Circuit's decision in *Roberts* did not stand alone. It "joined several of our sister circuits in determining that we have no jurisdiction [under section 355(f)] to review the Board's decision not to reopen a prior claim for benefits." *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 140. Specifically, the Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, and Tenth Circuits had each reached the same conclusion applying *Sanders* to the Railroad Retirement Act's judicial-review provision. *See Harris v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 198 F.3d 139, 142 (4th Cir. 1999); *Abbruzzese v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 63 F.3d 972, 974 (10th Cir. 1995); *Gutierrez v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 918 F.2d 567, 570 (6th Cir. 1990); *Steebe v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 708 F.2d 250, 254–55 (7th Cir. 1983).

This consensus reflects sound statutory reasoning, not mere deference. The Fifth Circuit expressly "f[ound] the reasoning of the Fourth, Sixth, Seventh and Tenth circuits persuasive" precisely because that reasoning tracked this Court's analysis "in light of *Califano v. Sanders*." *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 141. Each of these courts recognized that Congress structured the statutory review scheme around timely challenges to the merits of benefits determinations, not around open-ended requests to revisit stale claims years after the administrative process has concluded.

## III. THE MINORITY VIEW ADOPTED BY THE D.C. CIRCUIT CANNOT BE RECONCILED WITH SANDERS

Petitioner urges this Court to adopt the reasoning of *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), which held that "the Railroad Retirement Act grants the [circuit] Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations." *Id.* at 502. That decision, however, departs from the majority rule "disagreeing with majority of circuits, including *Roberts*." *Id.* at 504. The D.C. Circuit's approach cannot be squared with *Sanders*'s core holding that reopening decisions—precisely because they are discretionary and regulation-based rather than statutorily mandated merits determinations—fall outside the ordinary understanding of a "final decision" subject to judicial review.

Nothing in the Railroad Retirement Act's text or structure distinguishes it from the Social Security Act in a manner that would justify departing from *Sanders*'s framework. Both statutes create comprehensive administrative schemes with defined limitations periods for seeking review of benefits denials. Both agencies have promulgated regulations, not statutory mandates, establishing narrow windows for reopening final decisions based on new evidence or administrative error. *See* 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. Treating reopening denials as reviewable "final decisions" would eviscerate the limitations periods Congress established, allowing claimants to circumvent statutory deadlines simply by recasting an untimely renewed claim as a request to reopen.

## IV. THE FIFTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY DISMISSED THE PETITION FOR LACK OF JURISDICTION

Applying *Roberts*, the Fifth Circuit correctly held that it lacked jurisdiction to review the Board's refusal to reopen Petitioner's 2006 application. The panel acknowledged the existence of a circuit split, noting that the Second and Eighth Circuits had reached a contrary conclusion, *see Sones v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991); *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253, 254–55 (2d Cir. 1966), but properly adhered to binding circuit precedent under the rule of orderliness. This Court should resolve that split by affirming the majority position, which alone remains faithful to *Sanders* and to the statutory text limiting judicial review to final decisions "on the merits of a claim for benefits." *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 140.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be affirmed.
