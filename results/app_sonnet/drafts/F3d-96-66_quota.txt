ARGUMENT

I. STANDARD OF REVIEW

Summary judgment is appropriate only where "there is no genuine issue as to any material fact." Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). In deciding whether such a dispute exists, the district court—and this Court on de novo review—must view the evidence in the light most favorable to Serbin and draw all justifiable inferences in his favor. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). Summary judgment is particularly disfavored where, as here, the dispute turns on the reasonableness of a party's conduct, a question that "requires the drawing of inferences which a jury might reasonably draw." Anderson, 477 U.S. at 255. The district court instead resolved contested inferences against Serbin, disregarding the teaching of Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986), that the nonmoving party is entitled to every favorable inference the record will support.

II. THE VESSEL RETAINED ACTIVE CONTROL OVER THE SNATCH BLOCK AND THEREFORE OWED SERBIN A DUTY OF REASONABLE CARE

Section 5(b) of the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C. § 905(b), permits a longshoreman to sue the vessel for its own negligence. The Supreme Court in Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos, 451 U.S. 156 (1981), identified three distinct duties owed by a shipowner to longshoremen working aboard its vessel. The second of these—the duty at issue here—requires the vessel to "exercis[e] reasonable care to prevent injuries to longshoremen in areas that remain under the active control of the vessel." Scindia, 451 U.S. at 167. This Circuit has recognized that where the vessel's own crew retains operational responsibility for a piece of gear, that gear remains under the vessel's "active control" even while stevedores are working nearby. Kirsch v. Plovidba, 971 F.2d 1026, 1029-30 (3d Cir. 1992).

The undisputed facts establish that the snatch blocks were exactly such gear. The blocks were fixed to the hatch covers, were moved exclusively by the ship's crew using the vessel's own cable and crane system, and were never intended to be handled by longshoremen at all. As in Martinez v. Korea Shipping Corp., 903 F.2d 606, 611 (9th Cir. 1990), where the court held that equipment operated by the crew as part of the vessel's own cargo-handling apparatus remained subject to the vessel's active-control duty even as stevedores worked in adjoining areas, the snatch block here was never turned over to the stevedore's control. Nor did the fact that the crew had, on this occasion, negligently left the block in the down position and departed the area extinguish the vessel's active-control duty; to the contrary, it is precisely this kind of transient, operational hazard—created by the crew's own conduct during ongoing cargo operations—that the active-control duty is designed to reach. See Bjaranson v. Botelho Shipping Corp., 873 F.2d 1204, 1208 (9th Cir. 1989) (vessel's active-control duty extends to hazards created by the negligent operation of ship's gear during cargo operations). A jury could reasonably find that the crew's failure to return the block to its proper position, combined with its failure to warn the incoming gang, breached the vessel's duty of reasonable care.

III. THE VESSEL ALSO BREACHED ITS DUTY TO INTERVENE

Even if this Court concludes that the area had passed from the vessel's active control, summary judgment remains improper because the record raises a genuine factual dispute as to the vessel's duty to intervene. Under Scindia, a shipowner must intervene when it has "actual knowledge of the danger" and "actual knowledge that the stevedore, in the face of that knowledge, is acting unreasonably in continuing to work." Scindia, 451 U.S. at 175-76. The Supreme Court reaffirmed and clarified this standard in Howlett v. Birkdale Shipping Co., 512 U.S. 92 (1994), explaining that the duty to intervene arises when the shipowner has actual knowledge both of the hazard and of the fact that the stevedore, exercising "obviously improvident" judgment, means to continue working despite the danger. Howlett, 512 U.S. at 105 (quoting Scindia, 451 U.S. at 175-76).

Here, the crew—an extension of the vessel itself—created the very hazard that injured Serbin by leaving a one-hundred-pound block improperly positioned atop a cargo unit in an area where, as Serbin testified, "the hookup man would normally stand." A reasonable jury could find that the crew's own negligent placement of the block, combined with its knowledge that unloading would proceed through that hatch, gave the vessel actual or constructive knowledge that a longshoreman would encounter the hazard and attempt to correct it without assistance, particularly given the competitive time pressures of the fruit trade that Serbin described. See Davis v. Portline Transportes Maritime Internacional, 16 F.3d 532, 539-40 (3d Cir. 1994) (summary judgment inappropriate where record permits inference that vessel knew or should have known stevedore employees would confront a hazard created by the crew's own operations). Whether the vessel possessed such knowledge, and whether it was unreasonable not to act on it, are quintessentially questions for the jury, not for resolution on summary judgment. Anderson, 477 U.S. at 255.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's grant of summary judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
