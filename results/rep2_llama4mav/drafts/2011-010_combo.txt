 ARGUMENT

I. The OCSLA Workers’ Compensation Provision Requires a Situs-of-Injury Test

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends workers’ compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) to injuries occurring “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain meaning of this provision requires that the injury occur as a result of operations on the outer Continental Shelf. The Fifth Circuit correctly held in Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, that an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above the outer continental shelf. 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1949) (citing Herb’s Welding v. Gray (Herb’s Welding II), 766 F.2d 898, 900 (5th Cir. 1985)).

The Supreme Court’s dictum in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire supports this interpretation, stating that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). This language suggests that the location of the injury is a critical factor in determining OCSLA coverage. See also London Guarantee & Accident Co. v. Industrial Accident Commission, 279 U.S. 109, 123-24 (1929) (holding that a state workers’ compensation statute could not be applied to an injury occurring on navigable waters).

The Third Circuit’s decision in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., which rejected the situs-of-injury test, is unpersuasive. 849 F.2d 805, 809-11 (3d Cir. 1988). The Curtis court relied on a “but for” test, which is overly broad and ignores the statutory language requiring a connection between the injury and operations on the outer Continental Shelf. See id. at 811. In contrast, the Fifth Circuit’s approach in Mills provides a more nuanced understanding of the statutory requirements. 877 F.2d at 362.

Other cases have also recognized the importance of the location of the injury in determining workers’ compensation coverage. See, e.g., Davis v. Department of Labor and Industries, 317 U.S. 249, 256 (1942) (holding that a state workers’ compensation statute could be applied to an injury occurring on a maritime situs if the employment was “local in character”); Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, 314 U.S. 244, 249-50 (1941) (holding that a state workers’ compensation statute could not be applied to an injury occurring on navigable waters); Northern Coal & Dock Co. v. Strand, 278 U.S. 142, 144 (1928) (holding that a state workers’ compensation statute could be applied to an injury occurring on a dock).

In this case, Juan Valladolid’s injury occurred onshore, at Pacific Operations’s oil-processing facility, La Conchita. The injury did not occur as a result of operations on the outer Continental Shelf, and therefore, the OCSLA workers’ compensation provision does not apply.

II. La Conchita is Not a Maritime Situs

The LHWCA requires that the injury occur on a maritime situs, defined as “any adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Ninth Circuit has adopted a “functional relationship” test to determine whether a particular facility is a maritime situs. Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978).

La Conchita does not meet this test. Although it is located near the ocean, it is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks and has no direct access to any pier, dock, or other loading facility. See Motoviloff v. Dir., Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs, 692 F.2d 87, 89 (9th Cir. 1982). The facility’s primary purpose is to process crude oil slurry, a non-maritime activity. Herb’s Welding I, 470 U.S. 414, 422-24 (1985).

The Ninth Circuit has previously held that a facility must be “used as an integral part of longshoring operations” to be considered a maritime situs. Herron, 568 F.2d at 141. La Conchita does not meet this requirement, as it is not directly involved in the loading or unloading of vessels. See id. The handling of scrap metal at La Conchita is not sufficient to establish a maritime situs, as it is not an integral part of longshoring operations. See P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford, 444 U.S. 69, 82-83 (1979).

The cases cited by Petitioner are distinguishable. In Pfeiffer, the Supreme Court held that two warehousemen were engaged in maritime employment because they were handling cargo within a port. 444 U.S. at 83. In contrast, La Conchita is not a maritime facility, and the handling of scrap metal there is not an integral part of longshoring operations.

The BRB correctly denied benefits under the LHWCA, as La Conchita is not a maritime situs. See Williams v. Dir., Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs, 825 F.2d 246, 247 (9th Cir. 1987); Hurston v. Dir., Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs, 989 F.2d 1547, 1548 n.3 (9th Cir. 1993).

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should affirm the BRB’s denial of benefits under the OCSLA and LHWCA.
