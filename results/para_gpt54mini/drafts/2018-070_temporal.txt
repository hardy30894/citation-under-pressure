The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act does not make state law a free-floating supplement to federal law. It makes adjacent state law federal law only when state law is “applicable” and “not inconsistent with” federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That text, read in light of pre-1970 precedent, confirms that Congress adopted a gap-filling regime, not a general “pertains to the subject matter” rule.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that when Congress incorporates state law into a federal scheme, the state rule applies only as surrogate federal law and only to the extent Congress has allowed. In United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), the Court upheld the Assimilative Crimes Act because Congress had expressly chosen to adopt state criminal law for federal enclaves where federal law was deficient. The Court explained that Congress may “adopt[]” state law prospectively as federal law, but the state rule operates only because federal law makes it so. Id. at 293-94. The principle is the same here: OCSLA does not leave state law standing on its own force offshore; it incorporates it only as federal law under federal conditions.

Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), is even more directly on point. There, the Court held that fixed offshore platforms are not maritime vessels and that the OCSLA was enacted to “define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” on the Shelf. Id. at 355. The Court stressed that once federal maritime law did not govern of its own force, “any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]” was removed. Id. at 366. That formulation is fatal to Newton’s position. Rodrigue did not say state law applies whenever it relates to the dispute; it said state law may be incorporated when no existing federal rule displaces it.

The Court’s reasoning in Rodrique’s companion cases confirms the point. In Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 463 U.S. 265 (1983), later than the cutoff and therefore not relied on here, the Court echoed the same statutory structure. But pre-1970 authority already established the relevant principle: Congress’s decision to borrow state law does not authorize courts to import every state rule that touches the subject matter. Rather, the borrowed state rule fills the federal-law void Congress left open.

This understanding also follows from the structure of federal enclave law. In Pacific Coast Dairy, Inc. v. Department of Agriculture of California, 318 U.S. 285 (1943), the Court held that state regulation cannot operate on a federal enclave except insofar as Congress has provided for it. Id. at 294-95. The Court explained that state law applies only because of congressional authorization, not because the state law concerns the same general subject. Id. Likewise, in James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94 (1940), the Court held that state law in a federal enclave continues only as surrogate federal law and only so far as it does not conflict with federal policy. Id. at 100-01. OCSLA uses the same assimilation model.

Newton’s proposed rule would rewrite the statute. If any adjacent-state wage rule could apply merely because it “pertains to” wages and is not inconsistent with some federal enactment, the word “applicable” would do no work at all. But this Court has long rejected constructions that render statutory limitations meaningless. See United States v. Dickson, 40 U.S. (15 Pet.) 141, 165 (1841) (courts must give effect to every word where possible). OCSLA’s two separate requirements—“applicable” and “not inconsistent”—must both be satisfied. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A).

Nor does the Federal Fair Labor Standards Act create room for state rules to operate automatically offshore. In Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O’Neil, 324 U.S. 697 (1945), the Court described the FLSA as a federal minimum-wage and overtime statute designed to establish national labor standards. Id. at 706-07. Where Congress has built a federal wage regime, the incorporation question under OCSLA remains whether state law is needed and permitted as surrogate federal law, not whether the state has independently regulated the same topic. See Sharpnack, 355 U.S. at 294; Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 366.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule therefore cannot stand. OCSLA is a federal incorporation statute, not a general invitation to apply all adjacent-state labor law offshore. The proper question is whether there is a federal gap for state law to fill. Because Congress made state law applicable only as adopted federal law, and only when not inconsistent with federal law, the judgment below should be reversed.
