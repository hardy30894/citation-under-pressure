Argument

The district court erred in denying the Pressls' motion to remand this case to state court because it lacked subject matter jurisdiction. The court's jurisdiction was premised on 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and 16 U.S.C. § 825p, but neither provision confers jurisdiction over this dispute.

First, the district court's reliance on 28 U.S.C. § 1331 was misplaced. Federal question jurisdiction under § 1331 requires a claim that arises under federal law. Louisville & Nashville R. Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). The Pressls' complaint, which seeks a declaration of their rights under a flowage easement, is a state-law claim. See Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938) (holding that state law governs property rights). The fact that APCO operates the Smith Mountain hydroelectric project under a FERC license does not transform the Pressls' state-law claim into a federal question. See Gully v. First Nat'l Bank, 299 U.S. 109, 115-16 (1936) (holding that a federal issue must be an essential element of the plaintiff's claim to support federal jurisdiction).

Second, 16 U.S.C. § 825p does not provide an independent basis for jurisdiction. Section 825p grants federal courts jurisdiction over suits arising under the Federal Power Act. However, the Pressls' complaint does not allege a violation of the Federal Power Act or any other federal statute. See Montana-Dakota Utilities Co. v. Northwestern Public Service Co., 341 U.S. 246, 249 (1951) (holding that § 825p does not create a new cause of action, but rather provides jurisdiction over existing federal claims). The district court's conclusion that the Pressls needed to raise their complaints with FERC prior to filing suit is also erroneous. See City of Tacoma v. Taxpayers of Tacoma, 357 U.S. 320, 335-36 (1958) (holding that the Federal Power Act does not preempt state-law claims that do not conflict with federal law).

Furthermore, the district court's analysis of the flowage easement under Virginia law was improper because it lacked jurisdiction to decide the merits of the case. See Ex parte McCardle, 74 U.S. 506, 514 (1868) (holding that a court's jurisdiction is a threshold issue that must be resolved before it can decide the merits of a case). The court's holding that APCO has the right to remove any dock built below the 800-foot elevation contour is a matter of state law that should be decided by the state court. See Penn General Casualty Co. v. Commonwealth of Pennsylvania ex rel. Schnader, 294 U.S. 189, 197 (1935) (holding that state courts have jurisdiction over state-law claims unless Congress has explicitly provided otherwise).

In the absence of subject matter jurisdiction, the district court should have remanded the case to state court. See Robertson v. Cease, 97 U.S. 646, 649 (1878) (holding that a federal court must remand a case if it determines that it lacks jurisdiction). The court's failure to do so was error.

The judgment of the district court should be vacated, and the case should be remanded to the district court with instructions to remand it to the state court. See Mansfield, C. & L. M. Ry. Co. v. Swan, 111 U.S. 379, 382 (1884) (holding that a court of appeals may direct a district court to remand a case to state court if it lacks jurisdiction).
