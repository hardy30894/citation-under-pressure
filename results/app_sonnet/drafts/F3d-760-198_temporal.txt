ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISREGARDING FACIALLY SEX-NEUTRAL CONDUCT AND EVALUATING MOLL'S HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT ALLEGATIONS IN ISOLATION RATHER THAN IN THEIR TOTALITY

A. Summary judgment is disfavored where a claim turns on an employer's motive and intent, and doubts must be resolved in favor of trial.

This Court's predecessors have long cautioned that summary judgment "should be used sparingly" in cases where motive and intent play a central role and where much of the relevant proof rests in the hands of the party opposing disclosure. Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). That caution applies with full force here, where Moll's hostile work environment claim depends on the cumulative significance of a course of workplace conduct—some overtly sexual, some not—whose discriminatory character can be assessed only by viewing the incidents together.

On summary judgment, "the inferences to be drawn from the underlying facts contained in [the] materials must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion." United States v. Diebold, Inc., 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962) (per curiam). The district court did not apply that standard. Rather than drawing reasonable inferences from Moll's evidence as a whole, the court parsed her Complaint incident by incident, discarding every allegation that was not itself sexually offensive and considering only what remained. That approach inverts the settled rule that the party opposing summary judgment is entitled to have the entire record, and every reasonable inference from it, weighed in her favor. See Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944) (summary judgment improper where the force of the evidence depends on credibility and weight properly left to the trier of fact).

B. Discriminatory intent is properly proven by the cumulative effect of a course of conduct, not by evaluating each incident in a vacuum.

Long before Title VII, Pub. L. No. 88-352, 78 Stat. 241 (1964), courts recognized that a pattern of discriminatory treatment must be assessed by its aggregate effect, because discrimination rarely announces itself in a single act. In Norris v. Alabama, 294 U.S. 587 (1935), the Supreme Court examined years of jury-selection practices in the aggregate—rather than testing each exclusion in isolation—to determine whether the process as a whole reflected a purpose to discriminate. Id. at 591–99. The same principle controls here: whether Moll's workplace was permeated with discriminatory hostility must be answered by considering the entire course of conduct directed at her—the notes, the restrictions on communication, the denial of promotions and vacation, and the exclusion from office social events—not by isolating only the facially sexual incidents and disregarding the rest.

The district court's error was compounding: it treated Moll's claim as resting solely on "sexually offensive acts," then dismissed it because none of those particular acts fell within the limitations period. But facially sex-neutral conduct—unequal work-at-home privileges, exclusion from outings, disparate promotion and vacation treatment—can supply evidence of a hostile environment when considered alongside overtly sexual conduct. A rule requiring courts to blind themselves to sex-neutral evidence contradicts the teaching of Norris that discriminatory purpose is proven by the whole record, not by disaggregating it into pieces no reasonable factfinder would examine separately. This Court should vacate the grant of summary judgment and remand for evaluation of Moll's hostile work environment claim under the correct, totality-based standard.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN APPLYING THE RULE AGAINST SELF-SERVING CONTRADICTORY AFFIDAVITS TO DISREGARD THE SUBSEQUENT TESTIMONY OF A NON-PARTY WITNESS

The rule barring a litigant from manufacturing a triable issue through an affidavit that contradicts his own prior deposition testimony rests on a narrow rationale: a party who has been examined under oath has an obvious incentive to shade later testimony to avoid the consequences of his own admissions. Perma Research & Dev. Co. v. Singer Co., 410 F.2d 572 (2d Cir. 1969). That rationale has no purchase over a non-party witness who has no comparable stake in the litigation's outcome and no similar incentive to tailor later testimony to defeat summary judgment.

Where the credibility of a witness—including one who appears disinterested—bears on the weight of the evidence, that question belongs to the trier of fact, not to the court on a motion for summary judgment. Sartor, 321 U.S. at 628 (cross-examination of even an apparently disinterested witness may disclose facts affecting the weight of his testimony, a matter for the jury, not the court, to resolve). The district court's mechanical application of the sham-issue doctrine to a non-party witness, without regard to whether that witness had any incentive to fabricate, deprived Moll of the benefit of the settled principle that credibility disputes and evidentiary conflicts must be resolved in her favor at this stage. Diebold, 369 U.S. at 655.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should vacate the judgment of the district court and remand for further proceedings.
