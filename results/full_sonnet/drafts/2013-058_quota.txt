**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code exempts from a debtor's estate "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation" under specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. 11 U.S.C. §§ 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The question is whether an inherited IRA—which cannot receive new contributions, cannot be rolled over, and must be drawn down on an accelerated schedule unrelated to the beneficiary's own retirement—qualifies as "retirement funds" in the beneficiary's hands. It does not. The statute asks what the funds *are*, not what they once were, and an inherited IRA's defining legal characteristics show that it functions as a source of current wealth, not a retirement reserve.

**I. The Statutory Test Turns on the Objective Character of the Funds, Not Their Origin.**

This Court has construed the Code's exemption provisions functionally, asking what an asset actually does for the debtor rather than what label it once carried. In *Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320 (2005), the Court exempted traditional IRAs because the "right to receive" payments from them is "on account of" age—precisely the wage-replacement function Congress sought to protect for a debtor's own retirement years. *Id.* at 325, 331. That functional inquiry forecloses petitioners' claim that any asset qualifies as a "retirement fund" merely because a decedent's contributions once enjoyed tax-favored status. An inherited IRA cannot perform the function identified in *Rousey*, because the beneficiary must withdraw the entire balance on a schedule disconnected from her own retirement, sometimes in as little as five years, regardless of her age.

*Patterson v. Shumate*, 504 U.S. 753 (1992), is equally instructive. There the Court protected ERISA pension interests because their anti-alienation provisions guaranteed that "the employee's accrued benefits" would remain available for retirement. *Id.* at 760. *Patterson* protected assets precisely because their governing terms ensured continued dedication to retirement. Inherited IRAs offer no such guarantee; once inherited, the funds must begin distributing immediately and cannot be preserved for the beneficiary's later years. That absence of any retirement-preserving mechanism distinguishes this case from *Patterson* and confirms that the exemption does not reach inherited IRAs.

**II. The Objective Features of Inherited IRAs Show They Are Not "Retirement Funds."**

Statutory terms must be given their ordinary meaning in light of surrounding text. *Hamilton v. Lanning*, 560 U.S. 505, 513 (2010); *Ransom v. FIA Card Services, N.A.*, 562 U.S. 61, 69 (2011) (Code terms construed according to "ordinary meaning" and statutory purpose). "Retirement funds" ordinarily means money set aside to support a person after she stops working. An inherited IRA cannot meet that description: the beneficiary may withdraw the entire balance immediately, spend it on present consumption, and pay no early-withdrawal penalty—privileges and burdens wholly unlike those attached to an owner's own account. These are not incidental differences; they are defining characteristics showing that the funds are no longer held for retirement.

Petitioners, following *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (B.A.P. 8th Cir. 2010), argue that because the funds were "retirement funds" in the decedent's hands, they retain that character forever. But nothing in the text treats "retirement funds" as a permanent status immune to later use. This Court has cautioned against reading statutes to serve their general purpose beyond their textual limits. As the Seventh Circuit recognized below, quoting *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522 (1987), "it frustrates rather than effectuates legislative intent simplistically to assume that whatever furthers the statute's primary objective must be the law." *Id.* at 526. Just as this Court declined a limitless reading of statutory purpose in *Rodriguez*, it should reject petitioners' rule that any asset once labeled an "IRA" remains exempt no matter how it is later used.

**III. Expanding the Exemption to Inherited IRAs Would Exceed the Statute's Textual Bounds.**

This Court has repeatedly declined to stretch bankruptcy exemptions beyond what the text supports. In *Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz*, 503 U.S. 638 (1992), the Court enforced the Code's procedural limits on exemption claims even where the result seemed harsh, explaining that "the statute is clear." *Id.* at 644. In *Schwab v. Reilly*, 560 U.S. 770 (2010), the Court held that an exemption's scope is measured by objective statutory criteria, not by a debtor's subjective intent or valuation. *Id.* at 791–92. These cases confirm that exemptions rise or fall on objective statutory characteristics, not on speculative bequest-planning incentives of the kind petitioners invoke. Reading §§ 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) to cover inherited IRAs would transform a shield for retirement savings into a vehicle for sheltering inherited wealth from creditors—a result the statute's text does not authorize and this Court's precedents counsel against.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
