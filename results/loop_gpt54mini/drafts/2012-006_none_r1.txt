The judgment below should be reversed. When the government prosecutes a conspiracy within a limitations period, it must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused was a member of the conspiracy during that period. If the defendant produces evidence of withdrawal before the limitations window, the burden does not shift to the defendant to disprove timeliness; rather, the government remains obligated to prove continued membership as part of its case. That allocation follows from the constitutional requirement that guilt be proved beyond a reasonable doubt and from this Court’s conspiracy cases recognizing withdrawal as the event that ends a conspirator’s liability. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970); Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912); Kissel v. United States, 218 U.S. 601, 607-08 (1910).

Conspiracy is a continuing offense. Hyde held that a conspirator remains liable while he continues to be part of the unlawful combination, and that “mere cessation of activity” is not enough; there must be proof of withdrawal or disassociation sufficient to end membership. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70. Kissel likewise explained that a conspiracy “lives” so long as its unlawful agreement continues in effect, and that membership does not end without some affirmative termination. Kissel, 218 U.S. at 607-08. Brown v. Elliott, 225 U.S. 392, 401-02 (1912), confirms that conspiracy liability turns on the defendant’s continued participation in the agreement, not simply on the fact that the conspiracy once existed.

That framework matters because limitations is not a free-floating defense to be disproved by the accused. In Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 216-17 (1946), the Court held that conspiracy continues until it is accomplished, abandoned, or withdrawn from, and that the limitations period runs from the last overt act only so long as the defendant remains a participant. Fiswick thus treats continued membership during the operative period as a fact that keeps the prosecution alive. Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396-97 (1957), likewise stressed that the statute of limitations serves to prevent prosecution after the legally defined period has expired, and that the government cannot enlarge that period by mere inference. See also Pendergast v. United States, 317 U.S. 412, 418-19 (1943) (limitations governs the time within which prosecution may be brought for the offense charged).

The government therefore bears the burden of proving timeliness just as it bears the burden of proving the elements of the offense. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 274-75 (1952), reaffirmed that criminal liability cannot rest on judicially created shortcuts that relieve the prosecution of proving the facts that establish guilt. Tot v. United States, 319 U.S. 463, 469-70 (1943), rejected presumptions that shift the burden of proof on an essential fact. And in United States v. Cook, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 168, 179-80 (1872), the Court explained that when timeliness is a material fact brought into issue, the prosecution must meet it with proof rather than demand that the accused disprove it. In criminal cases, the Constitution requires more than a plausible inference; it requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt. In re Winship, 397 U.S. at 364.

The rule urged by the government would invert those principles. If withdrawal before the limitations period is shown, the government cannot rely on the mere historical existence of the conspiracy to secure conviction. It must prove that the defendant still belonged to the conspiracy during the relevant period. Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 334-35 (1957), recognized that a conspirator who withdraws ceases to be part of the agreement, and thus ceases to incur continuing liability. United States v. Bayer, 331 U.S. 532, 542-43 (1947), similarly reflects the basic point that conspiracy liability does not continue after participation ends. Once the defendant has adduced evidence of withdrawal, the government must prove the contrary beyond a reasonable doubt if it wishes to avoid the bar of limitations.

That is especially true here, where the prosecution itself depends on continuing membership in a conspiracy that allegedly persisted into the limitations period. The government cannot both invoke conspiracy’s continuing nature and deny the logical consequence of that doctrine: each defendant’s liability continues only so long as the government can prove continued participation. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70; Fiswick, 329 U.S. at 216-17. A rule placing the burden of persuasion on the defendant would allow conviction despite reasonable doubt whether he remained a conspirator when the statute ran. That is incompatible with Winship and with the settled understanding that criminal statutes of limitation are a substantive protection, not an evidentiary luxury. See Cook, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) at 179-80; Grunewald, 353 U.S. at 396-97.

The Court should therefore reverse and hold that, once a defendant presents evidence of withdrawal before the limitations period, the government bears the burden of persuasion to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period.
