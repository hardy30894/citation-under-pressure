**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption Favoring Scienter Requires This Court to Construe §875(c) to Demand Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Communication as a Threat.**

Section 875(c) is silent as to the mental state required for conviction, but that silence does not authorize a strict-liability or purely objective reading. This Court has long held that "the contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion. It is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will and a consequent ability and duty of the normal individual to choose between good and evil." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Congressional silence as to intent is ordinarily read as an invitation to import the traditional mens rea requirement, not to dispense with it. *Id.* at 263.

That presumption applies with full force here, because §875(c) does not resemble the narrow category of regulatory offenses in which this Court has permitted liability without proof of intent. In *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250 (1922), this Court allowed such liability only where the statute at issue was regulatory in nature and where requiring proof of scienter would have obstructed the statute's protective purpose. *Id.* at 251–252. A prosecution for threatening speech transmitted over the internet is far removed from that regulatory context; it is a true crime carrying substantial felony penalties and stigma, the paradigm case for reading in a subjective intent element. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260.

This Court has likewise construed ambiguous criminal statutes to require a heightened intent standard where a purely objective reading would sweep in defendants who did not appreciate the wrongful character of their conduct. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the plurality construed the word "willfully" in a federal civil-rights statute to require proof of a specific intent to deprive a person of rights made definite by law, precisely so that defendants would not be punished for conduct they could not have known was unlawful. *Id.* at 101–105. And in *United States v. Cardiff*, 344 U.S. 174 (1952), this Court refused to read a criminal statute to impose liability beyond what its text plainly supported, declining to extend criminal exposure by inference where Congress had not clearly done so. *Id.* at 176–177. The same interpretive caution compels reading §875(c) to require that the defendant subjectively intend to communicate a threat.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Turns on the Speaker's Intent, Not Merely a Listener's Reaction.**

Even apart from statutory construction, the Constitution independently requires proof of subjective intent before speech may be punished as a "true threat." This Court has explained that the First Amendment protects all expression save for a few "well-defined and narrowly limited classes of speech, the prevention and punishment of which have never been thought to raise any Constitutional problem." *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–572 (1942). Such categories must be defined with precision, not left to the unpredictable reactions of listeners.

This Court's own application of the true-threat concept confirms that the inquiry cannot rest on how a reasonable listener might construe ambiguous or hyperbolic language. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), the Court held that "we do not believe that the kind of political hyperbole indulged in by petitioner fits within that statutory term," *id.* at 708, even though the words at issue—that if the speaker were made to carry a rifle, the first man he wanted "in [his] sights" was the President—could have been understood by some listeners as menacing if read literally and objectively. The Court's focus was on the character of the speech and the speaker's evident meaning, not merely on how an audience might receive it.

An objective standard threatens to chill vast amounts of protected expression—dark humor, hyperbole, venting, and other forms of speech that speakers cannot know in advance will be understood as threatening by an unknown audience. This Court has recognized that "[b]ecause First Amendment freedoms need breathing space to survive, government may regulate in this area only with narrow specificity." *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963). Separately, this Court has explained that where a statute dispenses with any requirement of knowledge on the part of the speaker, "the ordinance tends to impose a severe limitation on the public's access to constitutionally protected matter," because speakers of ordinary prudence will restrict their speech far more than necessary to remain safely outside a lawful boundary they cannot clearly perceive. *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 153–154 (1959). This Court has similarly cautioned that the separation of legitimate from illegitimate speech calls for more sensitive tools than an objective standard untethered from the speaker's actual intent, given the risk that citizens will forgo protected expression rather than risk prosecution for words they never meant as threats. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 525–526 (1958). Where advocacy or expression is at issue, this Court has likewise insisted on proof of intent before liability may be imposed. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951).

**III. An Objective, Negligence-Based Standard Punishes the Merely Careless and Cannot Be Reconciled With Due Process.**

Finally, dispensing with subjective intent effectively converts §875(c) into a negligence offense—punishing defendants for failing to anticipate how their words might be received, rather than for what they meant to convey. This Court has cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where fair notice is at a premium. *Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337, 340 (1952); *Screws*, 325 U.S. at 101–102. The judgment below should be reversed, and this Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statement to be understood as a threat.
