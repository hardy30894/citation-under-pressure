The Controlled Substances Act's delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General is constitutional. Congress may obtain assistance from coordinate branches when it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to exercise the delegated authority is directed to conform. J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Act supplies such a principle by enumerating eight specific factors the Attorney General must consider, requiring a scientific and medical evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services, and limiting temporary scheduling to substances posing an imminent hazard to public safety. Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892); Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904).

This framework is no broader than delegations sustained when Congress confronted complex or rapidly changing conditions. Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 424-425 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). The two 1935 decisions invalidating statutes involved delegations lacking any policy declaration or criterion to guide the executive. Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935); A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 521-522 (1935). Those infirmities are absent here.

The Attorney General's subdelegation to the DEA Administrator is likewise authorized. When a statute grants an officer authority to administer a regulatory scheme, that officer may assign performance of ministerial functions to subordinates. United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516-517 (1911); Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland, 315 U.S. 357, 366 (1942). The regulation effectuating the assignment, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), merely transmits functions already vested by statute and does not enlarge the scope of the original delegation. See also District of Columbia v. John R. Thompson Co., 346 U.S. 100, 109-110 (1953).

Petitioners' reliance on the absence of pre-scheduling judicial review is misplaced. The limitation on review does not extend to constitutional challenges to the delegation itself. Wichita Railroad & Light Co. v. Public Utilities Commission, 260 U.S. 48, 59 (1922). Adequate safeguards remain through the statutory criteria, the Secretary's binding recommendation, and eventual permanent scheduling proceedings. Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542-544 (1952).

The convictions should be affirmed.
