# ARGUMENT

## I. The Conferral of Temporary Protected Status Constitutes an "Admission" Under 8 U.S.C. § 1255

### A. Ambiguities in the immigration statutes must be resolved in favor of the alien.

This Court has long instructed that provisions of the immigration laws bearing on an alien's ability to remain lawfully in this country must be construed with sensitivity to the harsh consequences of a contrary reading. In Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan, 333 U.S. 6 (1948), the Court explained that "we resolve the doubts in favor of that construction because deportation is a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile." Id. at 10. The same principle governed Barber v. Gonzales, 347 U.S. 637 (1954), where the Court again construed a disputed provision of the deportation laws against the harsher result urged by the Government. And in Costello v. INS, 376 U.S. 120 (1964), the Court reaffirmed that where the statutory text permits more than one reasonable reading, the reading that spares the alien the loss of status should prevail.

Section 1255(a)'s "admission" requirement is precisely the kind of provision to which this canon applies. Petitioners' interpretation—that a grant of TPS, which requires the Government to inspect, screen, and formally approve an alien's presence, satisfies § 1255(a)—is at least as reasonable as the Government's cramped reading. Under Fong Haw Tan, Barber, and Costello, that reasonable reading must control.

### B. This Court has repeatedly employed legal fictions to treat statutorily authorized presence as equivalent to admission.

The concept of "admission" in the immigration laws has never been a rigid, purely physical event; this Court has consistently treated it as a legal construct that Congress and the courts may shape to serve statutory purposes. In Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding, 344 U.S. 590 (1953), the Court held that a lawful permanent resident returning from a brief voyage abroad retained the rights of one who had never left the country, refusing to treat the physical fact of departure and return as controlling over the alien's continuing lawful status. The Court extended this reasoning in Rosenberg v. Fleuti, 374 U.S. 449 (1963), holding that an "innocent, casual, and brief excursion" outside the country did not constitute the kind of "departure" that would require a new "entry" analysis, id. at 462, because Congress could not have intended so technical a reading to defeat the alien's continuing status.

Delgadillo v. Carmichael, 332 U.S. 388 (1947), is to the same effect. There, a seaman whose ship was torpedoed during wartime and who was involuntarily brought ashore in the United States was held not to have made an "entry" for purposes of the deportation laws, notwithstanding his physical presence, because a contrary rule would produce a result Congress could not have intended. These cases confirm that "admission," like "entry," is not a term this Court reads mechanically; it is construed in light of the statutory scheme's purposes. Section 1254a(f)(4)'s command that a TPS recipient "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" is exactly the sort of statutory fiction Kwong Hai Chew, Fleuti, and Delgadillo instruct courts to credit as satisfying admission-related requirements elsewhere in the Act.

### C. Congress's use of exclusionary language elsewhere in the Act, and its absence here, confirms that TPS satisfies § 1255(a).

In Leng May Ma v. Barber, 357 U.S. 185 (1958), the Court held that an alien paroled into the United States had not been "admitted," relying on the statute's express command that parole "shall not be regarded as an admission." Id. at 188. That express exclusion was decisive. Section 1254a contains no comparable exclusion. Congress knew how to withhold admission status when it wished to do so, as Leng May Ma demonstrates; its silence in § 1254a(f)(4) should not be read to impose the same limitation by implication.

### D. Reading § 1254a(f)(4) to satisfy § 1255(a) gives effect to every clause of the statute, as this Court's canons require.

This Court has long held that statutes must be read so that no clause is rendered idle. In Market Co. v. Hoffman, 101 U.S. 112 (1879), the Court explained that a statute "ought, upon the whole, to be so construed that... no clause, sentence, or word shall be superfluous, void, or insignificant." Id. at 115–16. If § 1254a(f)(4)'s grant of "lawful status as a nonimmigrant" does not also satisfy § 1255(a)'s threshold inspection-and-admission requirement, the provision accomplishes little for aliens who, like Petitioners, entered without inspection—precisely the class most in need of the relief Congress designed TPS to provide.

That reading also honors the purpose-driven method of construction this Court applied in United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), where the Court observed that "[t]here is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes," but cautioned that where a literal reading "led to absurd or futile results," courts must look to "the purpose of the act." Id. at 543. Congress created TPS to protect vulnerable aliens already present in this country from the ravages of war, disaster, and upheaval abroad. Reading § 1255(a) to strand such aliens permanently in temporary status—unable ever to adjust—defeats that remedial purpose.

### Conclusion

Under Fong Haw Tan, Barber, Costello, Kwong Hai Chew, Fleuti, Delgadillo, Leng May Ma, Market Co. v. Hoffman, and American Trucking, the conferral of TPS should be held to constitute an "admission" satisfying 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). The judgment below should be reversed.
