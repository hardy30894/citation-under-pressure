**Argument**

ERISA’s three-year limitation period runs only “from the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). That text requires awareness of the breach itself, not merely that disclosures containing the relevant facts were available somewhere in the record. This Court has repeatedly distinguished actual knowledge from constructive or imputed knowledge, and it has refused to collapse the statutory requirement into a presumption that participants know what plan documents say. *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985); *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988); *Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.*, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990); *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Rest. Emps. Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509, 520-21 (9th Cir. 1991).

Intel’s contrary rule would effectively revive the constructive-knowledge regime Congress removed in 1987. Congress originally paired “actual knowledge” with a constructive-knowledge provision keyed to reports filed with the Secretary, but it later repealed that second prong and retained only “actual knowledge.” Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330; see *Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma*, 909 F.3d 1069, 1076 (9th Cir. 2018). As this Court explained, “knowledge of the law” is not enough, *Blanton*, 760 F.2d at 991-92, and the statute is not triggered by mere access to information unless the plaintiff was actually aware of the underlying transaction or breach. *Ziegler*, 916 F.2d at 552; *Phillips*, 944 F.2d at 520-21. The statute’s focus on “the earliest date” of actual knowledge confirms that Congress chose a subjective trigger, not a notice-based one. 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2).

This Court’s cases also show that actual knowledge turns on what the plaintiff actually knew, not what was hypothetically knowable from disclosures. In *Blanton*, the trustees had actual knowledge because they were “parties to the transaction” and “actually made the decision” to do it. 760 F.2d at 991. In *Meagher*, the plaintiff knew of the reduced checks he received and thus knew of the transaction, even if he did not know it was unlawful. 856 F.2d at 1423. In *Ziegler*, the administrators were “informed” that their election would retain a substantial portion of plan assets. 916 F.2d at 552. And in *Phillips*, the Court held that once a participant had actual knowledge of one continuing breach, later repetitions did not restart the limitations clock. 944 F.2d at 520-21. None of those decisions held that unread disclosures alone establish actual knowledge.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach in this case conflicts with that settled understanding. The court acknowledged that Intel’s materials “demonstrate[d] that Sulyma had sufficient information available to him,” but said that was “insufficient.” *Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1078. That conclusion was correct. A participant cannot “have actual knowledge” of a breach he did not know occurred, and a court cannot infer actual awareness merely because a fiduciary disclosed facts in documents the participant did not read or remember. See *Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1078-79.

This Court’s later decision in *Sulyma v. Intel Corp. Investment Policy Committee*, 140 S. Ct. 768 (2020), confirms the point: “actual knowledge” means “what it says—actual knowledge.” Id. at 774. The Court held that “a plaintiff must in fact have become aware of” the information constituting the breach; merely furnishing the information is not enough. Id. at 774-75. The Court further explained that “common sense” and ordinary usage distinguish between being “exposed to” information and actually knowing it. Id. at 776. That reasoning accords with ERISA’s text and this Court’s precedents.

Intel’s position also clashes with general limitations doctrine. Where Congress uses an actual-knowledge standard, courts do not impute knowledge from constructive notice absent statutory text to that effect. See *TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 27-28 (2001) (rejecting implied discovery rule where Congress specified actual knowledge); *Rotella v. Wood*, 528 U.S. 549, 555 (2000) (limitations rules depend on the chosen statutory trigger). ERISA’s text is more demanding than mere availability of information, and courts must respect that choice. *Gabelli v. SEC*, 568 U.S. 442, 448-49 (2013).

Nor does *Tibble v. Edison International*, 575 U.S. 523 (2015), help Intel. *Tibble* addressed the prudent-investor duty as a continuing duty and did not dilute ERISA’s separate actual-knowledge requirement under § 1113(2). Id. at 530-31. If anything, *Tibble* reinforces that fiduciary-breach claims turn on concrete fiduciary conduct, not on whether a participant could have discovered the breach by reviewing disclosures. Id. at 529-31.

Because ERISA requires actual awareness of the breach, a defendant cannot obtain summary judgment by showing only that documents were posted or mailed. The question is whether the plaintiff actually knew of the breach more than three years before suit. *Sulyma*, 140 S. Ct. at 775-76; *Ziegler*, 916 F.2d at 552. On petitioner’s reading of the statute, the three-year period begins only when actual knowledge exists, and Intel’s evidence of disclosures alone cannot satisfy that burden. That is the rule Congress enacted, and this Court’s precedents require it.
