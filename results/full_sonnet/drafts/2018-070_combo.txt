ARGUMENT

I. OCSLA borrows the federal-enclave model of assimilation, under which state law fills only the gaps federal law leaves open.

Congress modeled OCSLA's choice-of-law provision on the framework long applied to federal enclaves, under which state law is not a freestanding source of regulation but is "declared to be the law of the United States" only to supply what federal law omits. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This Court recognized in Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), that "[t]he purpose of the [OCSLA] was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf," id. at 355, accomplished by treating such structures as federal enclaves. That characterization matters, because this Court has long held that within a federal enclave, state law does not operate of its own force; it applies only through congressional adoption, and only where federal law has left a subject unaddressed. In James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94 (1940), the Court sustained a state safety statute's application to construction on a federal enclave precisely because no federal law occupied that subject, treating the state rule as a stopgap. Conversely, in Pacific Coast Dairy, Inc. v. Department of Agriculture, 318 U.S. 285 (1943), the Court held that a state milk-control order could not reach transactions on a federal enclave, because the State had ceded exclusive jurisdiction and no federal enactment had reincorporated state law on that subject. In Howard v. Commissioners of Sinking Fund of Louisville, 344 U.S. 624 (1953), the Court reaffirmed that a State's authority over conduct on a federal enclave survives only where Congress has reserved or restored it. See also United States v. Unzeuta, 281 U.S. 138 (1930) (a State retains no authority over land ceded to exclusive federal jurisdiction absent reservation). Congress's decision to borrow this enclave framework for the outer Continental Shelf, rather than ordinary principles of concurrent state jurisdiction, confirms that state law under § 1333(a)(2)(A) operates the same way: as a gap-filler adopted as surrogate federal law only where federal law is otherwise silent.

This Court's decision in United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), sustaining the Assimilative Crimes Act's prospective incorporation of state law into federal enclaves, rested on the same premise—that Congress may borrow state law to supply content federal law does not itself provide, not to let state law operate alongside a complete federal scheme. OCSLA's parallel structure, borrowing state law "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), reflects the identical legislative technique and should serve the identical function.

II. The "significant void or gap" standard correctly implements this structure, and the FLSA leaves no gap for state wage-and-hour law to fill.

The Fifth Circuit—the court with the longest experience administering OCSLA—has required "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" in federal law before state law may be assimilated as surrogate federal law on the Shelf. Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). That standard tracks the enclave precedents above and honors the judgment, reflected in the FLSA itself, that federal law should govern wages and hours comprehensively. Where Congress has enacted "a complete scheme of regulation," state law may not "complement" it merely because the two are not technically inconsistent. Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (States may not, "inconsistently with the purpose of Congress," "complement[] the federal law, or enforce additional or auxiliary regulations"). The FLSA comprehensively addresses minimum wages, overtime, and recordkeeping, including through its own savings clause specifying when more generous state rules may operate. That comprehensive design leaves no "void or gap" of the kind OCSLA's assimilation clause was written to fill.

III. The Ninth Circuit's "not inconsistent" standard cannot be squared with OCSLA's text or purpose.

The court of appeals held that state law applies whenever it is not affirmatively inconsistent with federal law, without asking whether federal law otherwise occupies the field. That approach treats "not inconsistent" as a substitute for, rather than a limitation on, the threshold requirement that state law be "applicable"—a term this Court has construed, consistent with the enclave cases, to require that federal law leave room for state law to operate in the first instance. See Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (courts must ask whether "the historic police powers of the States were . . . superseded" only where doing so reflects the "clear and manifest purpose of Congress"). Congress's manifest purpose in OCSLA was to create a single, stable federal body of law for the Shelf's fixed structures, Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355—an aim the Ninth Circuit's rule defeats by inviting each adjacent State's law to supplement federal wage-and-hour regulation platform by platform. This Court should restore the gap-filling standard Congress intended and the Fifth Circuit has long applied.
