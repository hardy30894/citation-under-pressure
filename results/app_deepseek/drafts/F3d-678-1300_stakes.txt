ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING JMOL BECAUSE THE JURY’S INVALIDITY VERDICT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

This Court reviews the denial of a motion for judgment as a matter of law de novo, applying the same standard as the district court. *In re Google Inc.*, 56 F.4th 1375, 1384 (Fed. Cir. 2023). A jury’s finding of invalidity under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) must be supported by substantial evidence, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to Facebook. *Id.* Facebook, as the party asserting invalidity, bore the burden of proving by clear and convincing evidence that the asserted claims were either in public use or on sale before December 10, 2002. *Pfaff v. Wells Electronics, Inc.*, 525 U.S. 55, 60 (1998). This burden requires evidence that “can be the basis for a confident decision.” *SRI International, Inc. v. Internet Security Systems, Inc.*, 511 F.3d 1186, 1192 (Fed. Cir. 2008). The record here falls far short.

A. The Evidence Failed to Show that the Leader2Leader® Product Embodied the Asserted Claims

To establish a prior public use or on-sale bar, the accused prior art must “disclose[] all of the limitations of the claimed invention.” *Scaltech, Inc. v. Retec/Tetra, L.L.C.*, 178 F.3d 1378, 1383 (Fed. Cir. 1999). Facebook’s case rested almost entirely on a white paper given to Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in January 2002. That white paper described a product that was “already commercializing” and “operational now with low user volumes.” J.A. 27203, 27207. But the paper did not—and could not—disclose the specific claim elements that define the patented invention. Crucially, the asserted claims require “dynamically associating metadata with the data” and “dynamically updating the stored metadata with an association of the data, the application, and the second user environment.” ’761 patent, col.21 ll.43-44, 49-50, 55-57. The white paper contains no reference to metadata, dynamic association, or dynamic updating. It only describes a general “Big Board” collaboration tool. J.A. 27210. Mere ambition to commercialize a product does not prove that the product actually practiced the claimed invention. See *In re Nuijten*, 515 F.3d 1361, 1365 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (rejecting a prior art reference that “does not disclose the claimed invention”).

Moreover, Leader’s founder, Michael McKibben, testified that the patented invention covered the “underlying engine” (Digital Leaderboard®), not the Leader2Leader® product itself. J.A. 25585-86. The district court acknowledged this distinction. *Leader Techs., Inc. v. Facebook, Inc.*, 770 F. Supp. 2d 686, 717 (D. Del. 2011). No witness testified that the Digital Leaderboard® engine was ever used, sold, or publicly demonstrated before the critical date. The jury therefore had no basis to conclude that the Leader2Leader® product—which was merely a branded application—embodied every limitation of the asserted claims. *In re Cyclobenzaprine Hydrochloride Extended-Release Capsule Patent Litig.*, 676 F.3d 1063, 1081 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (holding that a prior art product must be “the same as the claimed invention” to invalidate). Because the evidence was insufficient on this elemental point, the verdict cannot stand.

B. The Alleged “On Sale” Bar Was Not Established

Even if the product had been shown to be within the claims, the on-sale bar requires a “definite offer for sale” of the patented invention. *Pfaff*, 525 U.S. at 66. The white paper offered 20,000 software licenses, but that offer was prospective: it described a product that was “already commercializing” and “operational now,” J.A. 27203, 27207, but it did not specify a price, delivery date, or other terms that would constitute a commercial offer. The Federal Circuit has held that a “presentation of a product to a potential customer, without more, does not constitute a commercial offer for sale.” *Linear Tech. Corp. v. Micrel, Inc.*, 275 F.3d 1040, 1052 (Fed. Cir. 2001). Here, the white paper was a solicitation of interest, not a definite offer. The jury’s contrary conclusion was unreasonable.

C. The Alleged Public Use Was Not “Public” and Did Not Enable the Invention

A public use occurs when the invention is “used in a public setting” without any confidentiality obligation. *In re Epstein*, 32 F.3d 1559, 1563 (Fed. Cir. 1994). But the evidence here showed only that Leader presented a white paper to a single government agency—a setting that is often confidential by nature. More importantly, the use must be of the *claimed invention*, not just a product that shares a general concept. *Id.* at 1564. The white paper’s description of a “Browser” for “Input & Display Collaboration Devices” (J.A. 27210) is far too generic to prove that the patented metadata management system was publicly used. *See Eolas Techs., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.*, 399 F.3d 1325, 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (requiring that the prior use “enable a person of ordinary skill to make and use the invention”).

Because the evidence was legally insufficient to prove invalidity by clear and convincing evidence, the district court should have granted Leader’s motion for judgment as a matter of law. Alternatively, at a minimum, the jury’s verdict was against the great weight of the evidence, warranting a new trial. *See, e.g., *Unisplay, S.A. v. Am. Elec. Sign Co.*, 69 F.3d 512, 517 (Fed. Cir. 1995).

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and enter judgment for Leader, or, in the alternative, vacate and remand for a new trial.
