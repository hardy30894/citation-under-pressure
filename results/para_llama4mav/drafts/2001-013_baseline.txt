ARGUMENT

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels, which are subject to minimal Coast Guard regulation. The Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precludes OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act, and that this pre-emption encompasses both inspected and uninspected vessels.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). However, the Coast Guard's authority to regulate working conditions on vessels is not as comprehensive as the Fifth Circuit suggested. While the Coast Guard has authority to issue safety regulations for uninspected vessels, its regulations are limited to specific areas such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation of engine and fuel tank compartments. 46 U.S.C. § 4102.

The Fifth Circuit's reliance on Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. In Clary, the court held that OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation, but the vessel in Clary was not explicitly identified as inspected or uninspected. Moreover, the court's broad language was not necessary to its holding, and the case did not involve a detailed examination of the Coast Guard's regulatory authority over uninspected vessels.

Furthermore, the Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels are not as comprehensive as those for inspected vessels. The Coast Guard has not exercised its authority to regulate many aspects of working conditions on uninspected vessels, such as those related to the specific hazards faced by workers on drilling barges like the MR. BELDON. In contrast, OSHA has promulgated regulations that address these specific hazards.

The Fifth Circuit's holding would create an anomaly where workers on uninspected vessels are left without the protections afforded by OSHA regulations. This is contrary to the purpose of the Act, which is to ensure safe and healthy working conditions for all employees. See Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Ass'n, 505 U.S. 88, 96 (1992) (noting that the Act was enacted to "assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions").

In Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. Usery, 539 F.2d 386 (5th Cir. 1976), this Court held that the Federal Railroad Administration's (FRA) exercise of authority to regulate certain aspects of railroad safety did not pre-empt OSHA's jurisdiction over other aspects of railroad safety that were not regulated by the FRA. Similarly, in this case, the Coast Guard's limited regulation of uninspected vessels does not pre-empt OSHA's jurisdiction over working conditions on these vessels.

Therefore, OSHA has jurisdiction to issue citations to uninspected vessels like the MR. BELDON, and the Fifth Circuit's holding to the contrary should be reversed.
