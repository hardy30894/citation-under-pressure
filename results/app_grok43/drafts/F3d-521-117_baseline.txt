I. The Pat-Frisk Violated the Fourth Amendment.

A traffic stop, though lawful at inception, permits a pat-frisk of a passenger only upon individualized reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968); Arizona v. Johnson, 555 U.S. 323, 327 (2009). Mere presence in a stopped vehicle supplies no such suspicion. Ybarra v. Illinois, 444 U.S. 85, 91 (1979). The government must point to “specific and articulable facts” showing the suspect posed an immediate threat. Terry, 392 U.S. at 21.

The officers observed only that Soares, a front-seat passenger, moved his hands downward during the stop and later became verbally agitated while rocking in his seat. These facts fall short of the threshold. See United States v. McKoy, 428 F.3d 38, 41 (1st Cir. 2005) (nervous behavior and furtive hand movements insufficient without more); United States v. Brown, 510 F.3d 57, 66 (1st Cir. 2007) (same). Soares complied with initial commands to keep his hands visible; his subsequent profanity and rocking occurred only after officers approached with drawn weapons and repeatedly ordered stillness. No bulge, no admission, and no report of a weapon preceded the frisk. Compare United States v. Romain, 393 F.3d 63, 71 (1st Cir. 2004) (frisk upheld where defendant reached toward waistband after officer saw bulge).

Maryland v. Wilson, 519 U.S. 408, 414-15 (1997), authorizes ordering passengers from the vehicle but does not relax the separate requirement of reasonable suspicion for a frisk. See also Pennsylvania v. Mimms, 434 U.S. 106, 111-12 (1977) (per curiam). The arrival of backup officers and the driver’s consent to a frisk likewise created no suspicion transferable to Soares. See United States v. Henderson, 463 F.3d 27, 46 (1st Cir. 2006).

Because the pat-frisk rested on nothing more than Soares’s presence and agitation in a high-crime area at night, it exceeded constitutional bounds. The district court therefore erred in denying suppression.
