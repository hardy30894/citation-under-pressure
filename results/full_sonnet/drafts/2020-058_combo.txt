**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Statutory Fiction Created by § 1254a(f)(4) Satisfies § 1255(a)'s Admission Requirement**

Congress provided that an alien granted Temporary Protected Status "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant." 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This Court has long recognized that Congress may create legal fictions regarding an alien's presence and status that operate independently of the alien's actual manner of entry. In *Kaplan v. Tod*, 267 U.S. 228 (1925), the Court held that an alien detained at Ellis Island, though physically within the United States, was treated by law as never having entered the country at all—a fiction Congress and the courts imposed to serve the purposes of the immigration statutes then in force. If Congress may deem a physically present alien not to have entered, it may likewise deem an alien granted TPS to have been admitted, notwithstanding the circumstances of his original entry. Nothing in the structure of Title 8 forecloses Congress from using status-conferring fictions to satisfy adjacent statutory requirements, and § 1254a(f)(4)'s command that the alien be "considered" to hold lawful nonimmigrant status is such a fiction.

This reading also coheres with this Court's teaching that nonimmigrant classifications generally presuppose an admission. In *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590 (1953), the Court treated a lawfully admitted resident alien's brief absence as legally insignificant, preserving his status as one who had been admitted, because the practical realities of his situation—not the bare mechanics of physical entry—controlled his legal treatment under the immigration laws. The same logic applies here: Congress's designation of TPS recipients as being in lawful nonimmigrant status necessarily incorporates the admission that ordinarily accompanies such status, just as it incorporated an admission for the resident alien in *Kwong Hai Chew*.

**II. Established Canons of Statutory Construction Require Harmonizing §§ 1254a and 1255 to Effectuate Congress's Remedial Purpose**

This Court has consistently instructed that "a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers." *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892). TPS is a humanitarian, remedial program designed to protect aliens from removal to countries suffering armed conflict or natural disaster. A cramped construction of § 1255(a) that forecloses adjustment for every TPS recipient who did not enter through a port of inspection—even after Congress has deemed him to be in lawful nonimmigrant status for years or decades—cannot be reconciled with the evident congressional design of integrating TPS recipients into the ordinary channels of lawful immigration status. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (courts must not adopt "the literal words" of a statute where doing so "would thwart the obvious purpose" of the legislation).

Sections 1254a and 1255 must be read together as parts of a single coherent statutory scheme, not as isolated provisions. *Helvering v. Hallock*, 309 U.S. 106, 119 (1940) (the meaning of a statute must be sought "in its object and policy," not in isolated clauses). Likewise, this Court has directed that related statutory provisions be harmonized rather than construed to work at cross purposes. *FTC v. Mandel Brothers, Inc.*, 359 U.S. 385, 389 (1959). Reading § 1254a(f)(4) to satisfy § 1255(a)'s admission clause harmonizes the two provisions: it allows the "lawful status" that Congress conferred to carry the practical and legal consequences Congress intended, rather than treating that grant as a legal nullity for adjustment purposes.

**III. Any Doubt Concerning the Scope of § 1255(a) Must Be Resolved in Favor of the Alien**

Even if the statutory text left room for the government's narrower reading, settled principles of construction in the immigration context require this Court to adopt the interpretation more favorable to the alien. This Court has repeatedly emphasized that ambiguity in the immigration laws should not be resolved to the alien's detriment. In *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948), the Court explained that doubts concerning the scope of statutes bearing on an alien's right to remain in this country should be "resolve[d]... in favor of" the alien, given the severity of the consequences of an adverse construction. This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Barber v. Gonzales*, 347 U.S. 637, 642–43 (1954), and again in *Costello v. INS*, 376 U.S. 120, 128 (1964), and applied it to require generous construction of remedial provisions of the INA in *INS v. Errico*, 385 U.S. 214, 225 (1966) (construing statute to "avoid harsh results" and effectuate remedial purpose).

That principle applies with full force here. Petitioners have lived under a congressionally sanctioned grant of lawful nonimmigrant status for nearly two decades. To construe § 1255(a) to exclude them from adjustment eligibility, despite that status, would visit exactly the kind of harsh and anomalous result this Court has cautioned against. Cf. *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698, 730 (1893) (recognizing that the power to admit and exclude aliens carries weighty consequences warranting careful statutory construction). Petitioners' interpretation—that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission satisfying § 1255(a)—both accords with the text and structure of the INA and avoids the harsh, purpose-defeating consequences that would follow from the Third Circuit's contrary rule. The judgment below should be reversed.
