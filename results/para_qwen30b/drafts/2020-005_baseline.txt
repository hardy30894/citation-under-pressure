**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a state court from exercising personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant unless the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the forum state’s laws and the litigation arises out of or relates to those contacts. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). The Montana Supreme Court’s decision in this case—holding that Ford Motor Company is subject to specific personal jurisdiction in Montana based on its nationwide advertising and parts sales—violates this foundational principle by permitting jurisdiction where no contact with Montana caused the plaintiff’s injury. This Court should reverse.

### I. THE MONTANA SUPREME COURT’S DECISION VIOLATES THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE BY EXERCISING PERSONAL JURISDICTION WHERE NO CONTACT WITH MONTANA CAUSED THE PLAINTIFF’S CLAIM

Personal jurisdiction under the Due Process Clause requires a “direct relationship” between the defendant’s forum-related conduct and the plaintiff’s claim. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985). Specific jurisdiction exists only when the claim “arises out of or relates to” the defendant’s forum activities. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 294 (1980). Here, Ford did not manufacture, sell, or distribute the vehicle in Montana. The Explorer was assembled in Kentucky, first sold in Washington, then transferred through Oregon before being brought into Montana by a private purchaser. No Ford dealership ever sold the vehicle in Montana. Ford’s only connection to Montana was the sale of replacement parts—parts unrelated to the tire defect at issue—and general advertising directed at consumers nationwide.

This Court has repeatedly held that such passive, incidental contacts do not satisfy due process. In *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 112 (1987), the Court rejected jurisdiction over a Japanese manufacturer where the product was sold abroad and the injuries occurred outside the forum. Similarly, in *J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 919 (2011), the Court emphasized that “mere foreseeability of harm” is insufficient; there must be “purposeful availment” of the forum’s market. Here, Ford did not target Montana consumers. It did not advertise directly to Montana residents. It did not sell vehicles in Montana. Its parts sales were not tied to the defective tire or the accident. The causal chain linking Ford’s Montana activities to Gullett’s death is severed by multiple intervening transactions and geographic distances.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on “advertising and selling parts” as sufficient for jurisdiction ignores the core requirement: the claim must arise from the defendant’s forum contacts. *Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 789 (1984) (“[T]he defendant must have done something purposeful, something definite, something that can be said to invoke the benefits and protections of the forum’s laws.”). Selling replacement parts in Montana does not constitute purposeful availment of Montana’s legal system for the purpose of manufacturing or distributing vehicles. The parts sold were not used in the vehicle involved in the accident. They are irrelevant to the design-defect claim. Thus, the claim does not “arise out of or relate to” Ford’s Montana activities.

### II. THE MONTANA SUPREME COURT’S REASONING UNDERMINES DUE PROCESS AND INVITES ABUSE OF JURISDICTION

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion—that Ford “availed itself of the privilege of doing business” in Montana—rests on an impermissible expansion of the concept of “purposeful availment.” *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 775 (1984). A corporation may not be deemed to have “availed” itself of a state’s laws simply because it sells products in that state through third parties. *See id.* at 776 (“[A]n individual who merely sends his goods into a State through independent distributors cannot be said to have ‘submitted’ himself to the jurisdiction of that State.”).

Montana’s approach allows plaintiffs to transform any national distributorship into a jurisdictional hook. If a car manufacturer sells parts in every state, even incidentally, then every state could assert jurisdiction over any product liability claim—even if the product was never sold, driven, or even seen in that state. This would render the Due Process Clause meaningless. As this Court warned in *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 923 (2011), “a defendant’s mere foreseeability that he might be haled into court in a particular State is not enough.” The Montana Supreme Court’s decision permits exactly that kind of jurisdictional reach.

Moreover, the Court’s reasoning contradicts precedent. In *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. ___ (2017), the Court held that California could not exercise jurisdiction over a drug manufacturer in a case involving injuries suffered by non-California residents. The Court stressed that “the connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue” must be meaningful. Here, the connection is nonexistent. The tire failure occurred on a Montana highway, but Ford had no role in the vehicle’s presence in Montana. The claim does not arise from any activity in Montana.

### III. THE MONTANA SUPREME COURT’S DECISION CONFLICTS WITH THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT ON PERSONAL JURISDICTION

This Court has consistently required a nexus between the defendant’s conduct and the plaintiff’s injury. *See, e.g.,* *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958) (“The very idea of justice demands that a person should not be subjected to the burdens of litigation in a foreign land without some substantial connection with that land.”). The Montana Supreme Court’s decision fails this test. There is no substantial connection between Ford’s Montana activities and Gullett’s death. The injury occurred after the vehicle left the control of any Ford entity in Montana. The defect was not discovered or reported in Montana. No Ford representative ever interacted with Gullett or her estate in Montana.
