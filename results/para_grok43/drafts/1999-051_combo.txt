ARGUMENT

The court of appeals erred in holding that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may supply cause to excuse the default of another federal claim. Principles of comity and federalism require that a prisoner first present all claims to the state courts in accordance with their procedural rules.

This Court has long recognized that federal habeas corpus is not available where the prisoner has failed to exhaust state remedies. Ex parte Royall, 117 U.S. 241, 251 (1886). The exhaustion requirement rests on “the respect due to the state courts” and the need to avoid “unnecessary conflict between courts equally bound to guard and protect rights secured by the Constitution.” Urquhart v. Brown, 205 U.S. 179, 182 (1907). A claim omitted from the state proceeding cannot later be revived in federal court. Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309, 329 (1915).

Where state procedural rules bar further consideration, the federal courts have consistently declined to intervene. Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86, 91 (1923). Only when the state process itself denies due process may a federal court look behind the default. Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 115 (1935). No such denial appears here; the Ohio courts simply enforced their timeliness rule.

A prisoner cannot circumvent these limits by asserting that counsel’s omission supplies the necessary excuse. The claim of ineffective assistance is itself a distinct constitutional contention that must be presented to the state courts. Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944). Allowing a defaulted ineffectiveness claim to excuse another default would nullify the exhaustion doctrine. Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950).

The requirement that every ground for relief be first submitted to the state judiciary applies with equal force to claims of counsel’s inadequacy. Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 486 (1953). Because Carpenter never timely presented his ineffectiveness claim to the Ohio courts, that claim cannot serve as cause. The district court therefore lacked authority to reach the merits of the sufficiency challenge.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
