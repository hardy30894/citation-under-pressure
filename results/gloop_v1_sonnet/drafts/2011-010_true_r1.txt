# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEXT OF SECTION 1333(b) CONTAINS NO SITUS-OF-INJURY REQUIREMENT

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage to "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). By its plain terms, the statute imposes a causation requirement—the injury must occur "as the result of" shelf operations—not a situs requirement demanding that the injury itself occur on the shelf. Congress located the geographic limitation precisely where it intended: on the "operations," not on the "injury."

This textual choice stands in sharp contrast to the Longshore Act's own coverage provision, which Congress drafted using explicit situs language. Section 903(a) provides that compensation is payable "only if the disability or death results from an injury occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States." 33 U.S.C. § 903(a); see *Nogueira v. New York, New Haven & Hartford Railroad Co.*, 281 U.S. 128, 129 (1930) (quoting this coverage provision). Congress thus knew how to draft a situs-of-injury requirement when it wanted one. It did not use that language in Section 1333(b). Where Congress uses different language in related statutory provisions, courts should not read a limitation into one provision that Congress placed only in another. Petitioners' proposed situs test would require this Court to import language into Section 1333(b) that Congress conspicuously omitted.

## II. THIS COURT'S DECISIONS CONSTRUING THE LONGSHORE ACT CONFIRM THAT CAUSATION, NOT SITUS, GOVERNS COVERAGE ANALYSIS UNDER RELATED PROVISIONS

This Court's longstanding jurisprudence interpreting the Longshore Act's coverage requirements demonstrates that when Congress intends a strict geographic limitation, it says so explicitly, and courts must not manufacture additional situs barriers beyond what the statutory text commands. In *Pennsylvania Railroad Co. v. O'Rourke*, 344 U.S. 334 (1953), this Court, reaffirming its earlier decision in *Nogueira*, quoted that decision's observation that the Act's coverage provision "is manifestly broad enough to embrace a railroad company, provided it has employees who 'are employed in maritime employment, in whole or in part, upon the navigable waters of the United States.'" *O'Rourke*, 344 U.S. at 336 (quoting *Nogueira*, 281 U.S. at 129). The Court's willingness to construe the Act's coverage provisions broadly, consistent with their remedial purpose, counsels against grafting additional restrictive requirements onto Section 1333(b) that its text does not contain.

Similarly, in *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251 (1940), this Court construed the Longshore Act's coverage provision to extend to workers whose circumstances placed them "in the position of longshoremen or other casual workers on the water," *id.* at 260, reflecting this Court's practice of construing the Act functionally rather than through rigid geographic formalism untethered from the statute's remedial purposes. And in *Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc.*, 314 U.S. 244 (1942), the Court examined whether an employee's death "arose out of and in the course of his employment" and occurred "upon navigable waters," *id.* at 246, treating the statutory situs requirement as a specific textual command to be applied according to its terms—not as license for courts to invent parallel situs requirements in statutes lacking such language.

## III. THE THIRD CIRCUIT'S "BUT FOR" CAUSATION TEST IN *CURTIS* CORRECTLY IMPLEMENTS CONGRESS'S CHOSEN STATUTORY LANGUAGE

The court below correctly followed the Third Circuit's decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), which rejected a situs-of-injury test in favor of a causation-based inquiry asking whether the injury occurred "as the result of" operations on the shelf. *Id.* at 809–11. This approach honors the statutory text while avoiding the anomalous results that flow from the Fifth Circuit's contrary approach in *Mills v. Director, OWCP*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc).

Petitioners' reliance on *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), is misplaced. That decision addressed an entirely different statutory provision—the choice-of-law provision in Section 1333(a)(2)(A)—not the benefits provision at issue here. This Court expressly stated that Section 1333(b) "has no bearing on this case." *Id.* at 219 n.2. The Court's passing footnote observation was unaccompanied by briefing, argument, or analysis directed at Section 1333(b)'s causation language. As this Court has instructed, "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used," and their "possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." *Humphrey's Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935) (quoting *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399–400 (1821)). This unconsidered dictum cannot bear the weight petitioners place upon it.

## IV. THE HISTORY OF FEDERAL-STATE JURISDICTIONAL ALLOCATION OVER THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF SUPPORTS A CAUSATION-BASED TEST

This Court's decisions in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), and *United States v. Louisiana*, 363 U.S. 1 (1960), confirm that OCSLA's overarching purpose was to establish federal jurisdiction over shelf resources and operations—not to create a hypertechnical situs trap defeating recovery for workers whose injuries are causally connected to those operations. *Rodrigue* quoted the statute's extension of federal authority "to the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and to all artificial islands and fixed structures which may be erected thereon for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, and transporting resources therefrom, to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355 (quoting 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1)). A causation test, focused on whether the injury resulted from shelf-related operations, faithfully implements this remedial jurisdictional scheme.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
