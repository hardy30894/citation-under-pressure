**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction in any cases involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue—requires a careful application of settled principles of federal jurisdiction, grounded in the constitutional and statutory framework governing the division of judicial power between state and federal courts. The Supreme Court of Texas erred in concluding that federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over this state-based legal malpractice claim merely because a patent issue is “necessary, disputed, and substantial.” That conclusion disregards the foundational jurisprudence of the early 20th century, which firmly establishes that federal jurisdiction does not automatically arise when a state-law claim *implicates* federal law, especially where the federal issue is not central to the cause of action and where federal adjudication would disrupt the balance of state and federal judicial responsibilities.

The starting point is *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), which remains the seminal authority on the limits of federal question jurisdiction. There, the Court held that a suit does not “arise under” federal law merely because it may involve a federal question in the course of its resolution. The Court emphasized that “a suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws.” Id. at 117. Instead, the Court adopted a “selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside.” Id. This principle is dispositive here: Minton’s malpractice claim arises under Texas tort law, not federal patent law. The fact that the claim *depends* on a patent law issue does not transform it into a federal cause of action.

This limitation was reaffirmed in *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), where the Court held that a suit does not arise under federal law unless it “really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends.” Id. at 569. The Court further clarified that “the mere fact that a federal question may be involved in the case is not sufficient to give the federal court jurisdiction.” Id. In this case, the patent issue—whether the experimental use exception applies—is not the *basis* of Minton’s claim, but rather a *defense* to his own prior litigation. The malpractice claim turns on whether Gunn’s conduct fell below the standard of care under Texas law, not on the validity of the patent itself. Thus, the patent issue is not “substantial” in the constitutional sense required by *Shulthis*.

The Court in *Henderson v. United States*, 268 U.S. 326 (1925), reinforced this principle by holding that federal jurisdiction does not extend to cases where the federal issue is “merely incidental” to the main controversy. The Court stated: “The mere fact that a federal question is involved in the case does not make it a suit arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States.” Id. at 337. Here, the patent issue is not the subject of the malpractice suit; it is a *background fact* that may affect causation. The claim is not about patent law, but about legal representation. As such, the federal issue is incidental, not substantial.

The Court in *United States v. Kimbrough*, 264 U.S. 144 (1924), further emphasized that federal jurisdiction must be narrowly construed to preserve the integrity of state courts. The Court held that “the federal courts are not to be made the general forum for the adjudication of all controversies which may have some connection with federal law.” Id. at 150. This principle is particularly relevant where, as here, the federal issue is one of *interpretation* rather than *creation* of rights. The patent law issue in this case does not create the malpractice claim; it only informs whether Minton suffered damages. That is not enough to trigger federal jurisdiction.

The Court in *Henderson v. United States*, 268 U.S. 326 (1925), also rejected the notion that federal jurisdiction arises simply because a federal law is “necessary” to the resolution of a claim. The Court stated: “The necessity of deciding a federal question does not, in itself, confer jurisdiction.” Id. at 337. In this case, the patent issue is necessary only to the extent that it bears on causation—a matter of fact, not law. The resolution of that issue does not require the application of federal patent law in a way that would threaten uniformity or federal interests.

The Court in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), reaffirmed that federal jurisdiction is not triggered by “the mere possibility” of a federal question. The Court held: “The presence of a federal question in a case does not necessarily make it a suit arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States.” Id. at 195. Here, the federal issue is not “actually disputed” in the sense required by *Grable*—it is a hypothetical, raised only in defense. The claim does not seek to enforce a patent right, nor does it challenge the validity of a patent. It is a state tort claim, and the federal issue is not “actually disputed” in the sense of being a live controversy between the parties.

Finally, the Court in *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33 (1952), held that federal jurisdiction must be “certain and clear” and not based on speculative or remote federal implications. The Court stated: “The federal question must be substantial and not merely colorable.” Id. at 37. The patent issue here is not substantial in the constitutional sense; it is a narrow, factual question about the purpose of a lease. It does not involve the construction of patent law, nor does it threaten the uniformity of federal patent jurisprudence.

In sum, the Supreme Court of Texas misapplied the doctrine of federal question jurisdiction by treating a state tort claim as if it were a federal cause of action simply because it involves a patent issue.
