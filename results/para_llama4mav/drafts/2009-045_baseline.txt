The Supreme Court has consistently held that a district court has the discretion to enhance a lodestar attorney's fee award in "rare" and "exceptional" cases, supported by specific evidence on the record and detailed findings by the lower court. Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984); Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air ("Delaware Valley I"), 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986). The district court's decision to enhance the lodestar fee in this case was not an abuse of discretion, as it was based on the exceptional quality of representation and results obtained by the plaintiffs' counsel.

In Blum, the Supreme Court indicated that an upward adjustment of the lodestar figure may be justified in the "rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was 'exceptional.'" 465 U.S. at 899. The Court in Delaware Valley I reaffirmed this principle, stating that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." 478 U.S. at 565.

The district court in this case made detailed findings regarding the exceptional quality of representation and results obtained by the plaintiffs' counsel, citing its own "substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court's observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs' counsel throughout this long and difficult case." Kenny A. v. Perdue, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286 (N.D.Ga. 2006). The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the district court did not abuse its discretion in enhancing the lodestar fee, as the court's findings were based on specific evidence and reflected the exceptional nature of the case. Kenny A. v. Perdue, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008).

The petitioners argue that the district court's enhancement was improper because it was based on the court's own "testimony" rather than evidence in the record. However, the district court's observations and findings were not "testimony" in the classical sense, but rather an explanation for the enhancement based on the court's own experience and familiarity with the case. As the concurring opinion in the Eleventh Circuit noted, the district judge is in the best position to evaluate trial-level advocacy and identify when that advocacy is exceptional.

Furthermore, there is no circuit split on this issue. The majority of circuits have held that a district court may enhance a lodestar fee award based on exceptional performance and results obtained. See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co., 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co., 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999).

In conclusion, the district court's decision to enhance the lodestar fee award was not an abuse of discretion, as it was based on specific evidence and detailed findings regarding the exceptional quality of representation and results obtained by the plaintiffs' counsel. The Supreme Court's precedents in Blum and Delaware Valley I support the district court's decision, and there is no circuit split on this issue. Therefore, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
