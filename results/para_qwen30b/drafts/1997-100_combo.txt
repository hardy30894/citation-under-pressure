**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, prohibits a defendant from being twice put in jeopardy for the same offense. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). This constitutional guarantee is not confined to the trial on guilt or innocence; it extends to any proceeding that constitutes a “second prosecution for the same offense.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). In the context of sentencing enhancements based on prior convictions, the question is not whether the defendant is being retried for the original crime, but whether the retrial of a prior conviction allegation—when that allegation directly determines the severity of punishment—constitutes a second prosecution for the same offense within the meaning of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple prosecutions for the same offense, regardless of whether the second prosecution arises from a different charge or a different phase of the same criminal proceeding. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922), the Court held that “the constitutional prohibition against double jeopardy is not confined to the case where the second prosecution is for the same offense as the first, but extends to any case where the second prosecution is for the same offense, even though the charge is different.” The Court emphasized that the Clause “was intended to prevent the State from making repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense, and ordeal, and exposing him to the danger of conviction and punishment upon less than full proof.” *Id.* at 381.

This principle applies with equal force when the “offense” is not the original crime, but the *consequence* of that crime—specifically, the sentence. In *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), the Court stated that “the double jeopardy clause protects a defendant from being twice put in jeopardy for the same offense, and the offense is the same when the second prosecution is for the same act or transaction.” The Court further clarified that “the offense” is not limited to the act of commission, but includes any legal consequence that arises from that act and is subject to prosecution. *Id.* at 672.

In *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 168 (1873), the Court held that a defendant who had been convicted and sentenced for a crime could not be retried on the same charge after a reversal on appeal, even if the retrial was based on a different legal theory. The Court reasoned that “the double jeopardy clause protects the individual from being twice put in jeopardy for the same offense,” and that “the offense” is defined by the *legal consequences* of the act, not merely the act itself. *Id.* at 168–169. This principle is directly applicable here: the prior conviction allegation is not a separate crime, but a factual determination that triggers a legal consequence—enhanced punishment. To allow retrial after an acquittal on that allegation is to subject the defendant to a second prosecution for the same offense, namely, the *enhanced sentence*.

The Court has also recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies when the second prosecution is not for the same crime, but for the same *legal consequence*. In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 158 (1833), the Court held that “a man cannot be twice punished for the same offense,” and that “the offense” includes any punishment that is the result of a prior conviction. The Court emphasized that “the double jeopardy clause was intended to protect the individual from being subjected to multiple punishments for the same offense.” *Id.* at 158. This protection extends to sentencing enhancements, which are not mere procedural formalities but *legal consequences* of prior convictions.
