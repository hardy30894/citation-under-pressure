**Argument**

The judgment below should be reversed because the Sixth Amendment does not forbid Congress from using a valid tribal-court conviction as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). This Court has repeatedly recognized that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies in state and federal criminal prosecutions only when imprisonment is imposed. Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367, 373-74 (1979). And in Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738, 746-47 (1994), the Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid under Scott because no prison term was imposed, may be used to enhance punishment in a later proceeding. Section 117(a) fits comfortably within Nichols’s recidivist principle. It does not punish the earlier tribal convictions; it punishes only the new domestic assault offense. Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747; United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008).

I. **Section 117(a) is a recidivist statute that punishes the latest offense, not the prior convictions.**

Congress enacted § 117(a) to impose enhanced federal penalties on repeat domestic abusers who commit assault in Indian country or the special maritime and territorial jurisdiction of the United States. A recidivist statute “penaliz[es] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747. That is the settled understanding of repeat-offender laws. See also Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948) (“not to be viewed as either a new jeopardy or additional penalty for the earlier crimes”). Under that rule, the predicate convictions are status facts that increase punishment for the charged offense; they are not themselves the basis for a second prosecution. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. at 386.

Nothing in the Sixth Amendment bars Congress from defining a federal felony by reference to prior convictions, even where the prior convictions were obtained in a forum not bound by the Sixth Amendment. The question is whether the federal prosecution itself complies with due process and the right to counsel. Here, Bryant was counseled in the federal proceeding. The prior tribal convictions were used only to establish the recidivist element Congress chose to include in § 117(a).

II. **Nichols permits the use of valid uncounseled convictions, and tribal-court convictions are valid because the Sixth Amendment does not apply in tribal court.**

Nichols squarely rejected the proposition that a constitutionally valid uncounseled misdemeanor conviction is unusable in a later proceeding. 511 U.S. at 746-47. The Court explained that a conviction obtained without counsel, but in a case where imprisonment was not imposed, is valid under Scott and may be counted in a later sentencing determination. Id. at 748-49. The Court’s reasoning rests on the validity of the prior conviction, not on whether the earlier prosecution happened to occur in state, federal, or tribal court.

Tribal courts are different from state and federal courts because the Sixth Amendment does not govern their criminal proceedings. See United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 322-23 (1978); Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896). The Constitution’s right-to-counsel guarantee does not invalidate a tribal conviction simply because tribal law, rather than the Sixth Amendment, supplies the governing procedural protections. Congress may therefore incorporate such convictions into a federal recidivist statute without transforming a valid tribal judgment into a constitutional violation.

III. **The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule conflicts with Nichols and improperly treats a valid tribal conviction as constitutionally defective.**

The decision below effectively holds that a conviction valid where obtained becomes unusable in federal court if it would not have satisfied the Sixth Amendment had it arisen in state or federal court. That rule conflicts with Nichols, which asks whether the prior conviction was valid when entered. 511 U.S. at 746-47. It also conflicts with the Court’s instruction that recidivist statutes punish only the current offense. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. at 386.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach would create a special disqualification for tribal convictions that Congress did not enact and the Constitution does not require. Congress expressly included “Indian tribal court” convictions in § 117(a). 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Courts “are not at liberty to rewrite the statute to conform to [their] view of what Congress should have done.” Lamie v. U.S. Trustee, 540 U.S. 526, 538 (2004). Congress’s decision to include tribal convictions reflects its power to address recidivist domestic violence in Indian country. See United States v. Lara, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004).

IV. **Using tribal convictions here does not violate due process.**

Due process requires fundamental fairness, but it does not require that every predicate conviction satisfy the Sixth Amendment as if it had been obtained in state court. See United States v. Bryant, 769 F.3d 671, 675-77 (9th Cir. 2014) (Watford, J., concurring in the denial of rehearing en banc). A valid tribal judgment is not presumptively unreliable simply because it was entered in a sovereign system to which the Sixth Amendment does not apply. Federal courts routinely recognize tribal judgments under principles of comity and respect for tribal sovereignty. Iowa Mutual Insurance Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 15-16 (1987); Wilson v. Marchington, 127 F.3d 805, 810-11 (9th Cir. 1997).

Because Bryant’s tribal convictions were valid when entered, and because § 117(a) punishes only the new federal offense, their use as predicate convictions does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The judgment should be reversed.
