**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School District’s Facilities Constitute a Public Forum Open to All Speech, Including Religious Speech.**

The First Amendment forbids government from “abridging the freedom of speech.” U.S. Const. amend. I. When a state opens its property for public use, it creates a public forum in which the government may not discriminate against speech based on its viewpoint. *Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 15–16 (1947) (recognizing that the Establishment Clause does not permit states to exclude religious speech from public forums). The School District’s facilities—including its auditoriums and gymnasiums—are public forums because the District has long permitted community groups, including nonreligious organizations, to use them for expressive activities. *See Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (holding that public streets and parks are traditional public forums where speech cannot be restricted based on content).

The District’s policy of allowing secular groups to use its facilities while excluding religious groups violates the First Amendment. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), this Court struck down a city’s refusal to allow a religious group to use a public park for a meeting while permitting secular groups to do so. The Court held that such selective exclusion based on religious content was an unconstitutional prior restraint on speech. *Id.* at 272–73. Similarly, the School District’s policy here is a content-based restriction that cannot stand.

**II. The District’s Policy Is Not Neutral and Violates the Free Exercise Clause.**

The District’s policy is not neutral toward religion. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), the Court held that a state may not enforce a law that, while facially neutral, is applied in a manner that discriminates against religious speech. The Court explained that such selective enforcement violates the Free Exercise Clause because it targets religious belief itself. *Id.* at 307–08. Here, the District’s policy explicitly excludes religious speech while permitting secular speech, demonstrating hostility toward religion. This is precisely the kind of discrimination the Free Exercise Clause forbids.

**III. The District’s Policy Does Not Violate the Establishment Clause.**

The District argues that its policy is justified by the Establishment Clause, but this contention is meritless. In *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), the Court upheld a program allowing public school students to leave school for religious instruction, rejecting the claim that such accommodation violated the Establishment Clause. The Court reasoned that the state was not endorsing religion but merely accommodating religious exercise. *Id.* at 314–15. Similarly, the School District’s policy here does not violate the Establishment Clause because it does not coerce support for religion or penalize religious speech. *See also McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 210–11 (1948) (holding that the state may not use its facilities to promote religious instruction).

**IV. The District’s Policy Is an Unconstitutional Prior Restraint on Speech.**

The District’s policy functions as a prior restraint on speech, which is presumptively unconstitutional. In *Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson*, 283 U.S. 697 (1931), the Court held that prior restraints on speech are invalid unless they are narrowly tailored to serve a compelling state interest. The School District’s policy is not narrowly tailored because it excludes religious speech entirely, without any justification. *See also Lovell v. Griffin*, 303 U.S. 444, 451 (1938) (holding that a city may not impose a prior restraint on speech by requiring a permit for distribution of religious literature).

**CONCLUSION**

The School District’s policy violates the First Amendment by discriminating against religious speech in a public forum. The judgment below should be reversed.
