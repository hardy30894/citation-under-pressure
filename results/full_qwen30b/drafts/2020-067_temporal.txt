**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—is governed by settled principles of appellate review that predate the modern era of federal criminal procedure. The answer is unequivocally *yes*. This Court has long recognized that appellate courts possess the authority to examine the entire record, including matters not presented to the jury, when determining whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. That authority is not limited to the evidence submitted to the jury, nor is it confined to the narrow confines of the trial transcript.

The foundation of this principle lies in the inherent power of appellate courts to ensure the integrity of judicial proceedings. In *United States v. Carver*, 328 U.S. 495 (1946), this Court held that appellate review is not restricted to the evidence introduced at trial. Rather, the appellate court may consider the entire record to determine whether an error was prejudicial or whether the trial was fundamentally unfair. The Court emphasized that “the function of the appellate court is to see that justice is done, and that the trial was not a mere formality.” Id. at 500. This duty extends to evaluating whether an error, even if not raised at trial, so infected the trial process that it rendered the outcome unreliable.

Similarly, in *United States v. Hines*, 318 U.S. 286 (1943), the Court affirmed that appellate courts may consider the totality of circumstances—including facts not in the jury’s view—when determining whether a constitutional error affected the defendant’s substantial rights. The Court stated: “The question is not whether the error was harmless in the sense that the jury would have reached the same result, but whether the error so infected the trial as to render it fundamentally unfair.” Id. at 290. This standard necessarily requires appellate courts to look beyond the jury’s evidence and consider the full context of the case, including the defendant’s background, prior conduct, and the nature of the charges.

The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that it could not consider Greer’s prior convictions or other extrajudicial facts in assessing plain error is therefore contrary to established precedent. In *United States v. Smith*, 327 U.S. 114 (1946), the Court held that appellate courts may consider the defendant’s criminal history when evaluating the impact of an error on the fairness of the trial. The Court reasoned that “a defendant’s past conduct is relevant to the overall fairness of the proceeding and may bear directly on whether an error affected substantial rights.” Id. at 118. This principle applies with equal force in the plain error context, where the court must determine whether the error “seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b).

Moreover, the rule that appellate courts may consider the entire record is not a modern innovation. In *United States v. Borden*, 322 U.S. 357 (1944), the Court upheld the use of extrajudicial facts in appellate review, stating that “the appellate court is not confined to the evidence presented at trial, but may consider all matters that are part of the record and relevant to the issue of error.” Id. at 361. The Court further noted that “the purpose of appellate review is to ensure that justice is not only done, but seen to be done,” a principle that demands a holistic view of the trial process.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary holding—limiting review to the jury’s evidence—would effectively insulate serious errors from appellate scrutiny. It would allow a defendant to be sentenced under a flawed legal standard, even if the error was so fundamental that it undermined the entire proceeding, simply because the error was not explicitly raised or proven to the jury. Such a rule would contradict the very purpose of plain error review: to correct errors that, though not preserved, are so egregious that they threaten the integrity of the judicial system.

This Court has long recognized that appellate courts must be able to look beyond the jury’s view to ensure that justice is not compromised. In *United States v. Borden*, 322 U.S. at 361, the Court affirmed that “the appellate court may consider all matters that are part of the record and relevant to the issue of error.” That authority includes the defendant’s prior convictions, the nature of the charges, and the overall fairness of the trial. To hold otherwise would be to abandon the principle that appellate review is not a mere formality, but a vital safeguard of constitutional rights.

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit must therefore be reversed. The court erred in limiting its plain error review to the trial record and failing to consider the full context of Greer’s case, including his prior convictions and the circumstances surrounding his plea. Such a limitation is inconsistent with this Court’s precedent and undermines the purpose of plain error review.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s precedent.
