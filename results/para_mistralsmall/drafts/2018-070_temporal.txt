**ARGUMENT**

I. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) incorporates state law only to fill “significant voids or gaps” in federal coverage.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), extends federal law to the outer Continental Shelf and “declares” applicable state law to be federal law “to the extent that [state law] is applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” The Fifth Circuit has consistently interpreted this language to require a “significant void or gap” in federal law before state law may be applied. *Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). This interpretation is compelled by the structure and purpose of the OCSLA, which was designed to fill gaps in federal law, not to superimpose state law where federal law is comprehensive. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (explaining that the OCSLA was enacted to define a body of law applicable to the seabed and fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf).

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Rodrigue* confirms that state law is adopted as federal law only where federal law is silent or insufficient. In *Rodrigue*, the Court held that state wrongful death laws applied to drilling platforms on the outer Continental Shelf because the federal Death on the High Seas Act (“DOHSA”) did not provide a remedy for deaths occurring on fixed structures. 395 U.S. at 366. The Court reasoned that DOHSA’s maritime remedy was unavailable because the accidents did not involve traditional maritime activity, and thus a “significant void or gap” existed in federal coverage. *Id.* at 360, 366. The Court did not apply state law where federal law already provided a remedy; it applied state law only because federal law was inadequate. *Id.*

Here, by contrast, the Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”) provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour disputes, including overtime and meal period requirements. The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), expressly permits state wage and hour laws that are more protective than federal law, but this clause does not transform state law into federal law where federal law already governs the subject matter. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 218(a) (“No provision of this chapter or of any order thereunder shall supersede any provision of State law relating to minimum wages or maximum hours or working conditions, unless such provision is less favorable to employees.”). The FLSA’s savings clause preserves state laws that are more protective, but it does not create a “gap” in federal coverage that would permit the application of state law under the OCSLA. *See* *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 366 (applying state law only where federal law was silent or inadequate).

II. The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no “significant void or gap” in wage and hour coverage on the outer Continental Shelf.

The FLSA establishes minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements for employees engaged in commerce or in the production of goods for commerce. 29 U.S.C. §§ 206, 207. It also includes provisions governing meal periods and other working conditions. *See* 29 C.F.R. § 785.19 (meal periods). The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), explicitly permits state laws that are more protective than federal law, but it does not create a void in federal coverage. *See* *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 366 (applying state law only where federal law was silent or inadequate).

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Cont’l Oil* confirms that state law may not be applied where federal law is comprehensive. In *Cont’l Oil*, the Fifth Circuit held that state law could not be applied to fill a void in federal maritime law because federal law was already comprehensive. 417 F.2d at 1036. Similarly, here, the FLSA provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour disputes, and the savings clause does not create a void in federal coverage. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 218(a).

III. The OCSLA does not permit the application of state law merely because it is “applicable” and “not inconsistent” with federal law.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), requires that state law be “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. The Fifth Circuit has interpreted this language to require a “significant void or gap” in federal coverage before state law may be applied. *Cont’l Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036. This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s decision in *Rodrigue*, which applied state law only where federal law was silent or inadequate. 395 U.S. at 366.

Here, the FLSA provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour disputes, and the savings clause does not create a void in federal coverage. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). Therefore, California’s wage and hour laws may not be applied to Newton’s claims because the FLSA leaves no “significant void or gap” in federal coverage. *See* *Cont’l Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036; *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 366.
