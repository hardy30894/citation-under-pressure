ARGUMENT

A private criminal defendant lacks standing to challenge a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds. The Tenth Amendment “states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered.” United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). It “is not a source of private rights,” and confers no enforceable interest on individuals. Tennessee Elec. Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939). Where, as here, the States have not objected, a defendant “absent the states or their officers, ha[s] no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id.

This Court has long required that a litigant assert an injury particular to herself rather than a generalized grievance shared with the public. Frothingham v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447, 488 (1923); Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447, 485 (1923). A claim that Congress exceeded its enumerated powers “is essentially a matter of public and not of individual concern.” Id. at 487. Such an objection “is not a judicial controversy” that an individual may litigate. Id. at 488.

Standing doctrine therefore bars a criminal defendant from invoking the Tenth Amendment to defeat prosecution. See Alabama Power Co. v. Ickes, 302 U.S. 464, 479 (1938); Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 297 U.S. 288, 347-348 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring). The defendant must show that the statute invades a right personal to her; she cannot rest on the reserved powers of the States. Tileston v. Ullman, 318 U.S. 44, 46 (1943); Ex parte Levitt, 302 U.S. 633, 634 (1937) (per curiam).

The same principle governs constitutional challenges to federal criminal statutes. A defendant may contest only those provisions that affect her own conduct or rights. United States v. Raines, 362 U.S. 17, 21 (1960); Barrows v. Jackson, 346 U.S. 249, 255 (1953). She may not litigate “the rights of others.” Id. Because the Tenth Amendment protects state sovereignty, not individual liberty, a private defendant has no standing to assert it. Coleman v. Miller, 307 U.S. 433, 441-442 (1939); Tennessee Elec. Power Co., 306 U.S. at 144.

Petitioner’s conviction therefore rests on a statute whose validity she may not question. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
