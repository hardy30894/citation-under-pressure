ARGUMENT

I. The Amendment Mandates Payment of All Net Income to Mrs. Davis; the Trustee's "Reasonable Discretion" Governs Only the Manner of Application, Not the Amount.

Section 2056(b)(7) requires that the surviving spouse be "entitled to all the income from the property." The Amendment satisfies this requirement because it commands the trustee to "pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse ... all of the net income from the trust estate." The word "shall" is mandatory, and the trust nowhere permits the trustee to accumulate income for the remaindermen or to divert any portion of income away from Mrs. Davis. This structure is fundamentally different from the language condemned in Northeastern Pennsylvania National Bank & Trust Co. v. United States, 387 U.S. 213 (1967), where the trustee was authorized to pay "so much of the net income ... as [he] shall deem advisable" and any income not distributed was added to principal for the remaindermen. Id. at 215. The Supreme Court held that arrangement disqualifying because the widow was not "entitled for life to all the income," since the trustee could withhold income from her entirely and redirect it to corpus. Id. at 220-21. Here, by contrast, the trustee has no power to withhold income from Mrs. Davis for the benefit of the remaindermen; the only discretion conferred is as to the "convenient installments" and method of application for her comfort, precisely the sort of administrative discretion that Northeastern Pennsylvania distinguished from a true power to divert income away from the spouse. See id.

II. A Trustee's Discretion Bounded by an Ascertainable Standard for Health, Support, Maintenance, and Comfort Does Not Defeat the Spouse's Enforceable Right to Income.

Even if the phrase "in the trustee's reasonable discretion" were read to qualify the amount payable, that discretion is confined by an ascertainable standard—"health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare ... in accordance with the surviving spouse's accustomed manner of living"—which this Court's predecessors have long recognized as judicially enforceable rather than a matter of unfettered trustee whim. In Merchants National Bank v. Commissioner, 320 U.S. 256 (1943), the Supreme Court held that a power limited by a standard such as "comfort, support, maintenance, and education" is not the kind of discretion that leaves the beneficiary without a fixed and ascertainable interest; such a standard is "fixed in fact and capable of being stated in definite terms of money," and a court may compel its performance. Id. at 261. The same principle governed valuation of a widow's support interest in Ithaca Trust Co. v. United States, 279 U.S. 151 (1929), where Justice Holmes explained that a bequest for support "according to her accustomed manner of living" could be reduced to a value fixed "as of the time of the testator's death" because the standard was objectively ascertainable, not left to caprice. Id. at 155. And in Old Colony Trust Co. v. Commissioner, 301 U.S. 379 (1937), the Court likewise treated support-and-maintenance language as creating a definite, enforceable entitlement rather than illusory discretion. Id. at 383-84. The Amendment's discretionary language tracks these very "support, maintenance, comfort" standards; it does not create the kind of open-ended, unreviewable power that would leave Mrs. Davis without an entitlement to all trust income. Under California law, which governs construction of the trust, Mrs. Davis may compel the trustee to pay the income the ascertainable standard requires—precisely the enforceable right the statute demands. This Court must give "proper regard" to how the state's own law would construe such trust language, Commissioner v. Estate of Bosch, 387 U.S. 456, 465 (1967), and California courts have long construed support standards as creating a fixed, judicially enforceable right rather than a matter of unreviewable trustee grace.

III. Reading the Amendment to Grant Mrs. Davis a Qualifying Income Interest Advances the Terminable Interest Rule's Purpose of Ensuring Eventual Taxation of the Property.

The terminable interest rule exists to prevent property from escaping estate taxation altogether by passing through a decedent's estate tax-free and then again through the surviving spouse's estate tax-free. As the Supreme Court explained in United States v. Stapf, 375 U.S. 118 (1963), Congress designed the marital deduction provisions "to defer, and not to avoid, the taxation of the marital property," permitting a deduction only where the property, or its economic equivalent, will ultimately be subject to tax in the survivor's estate. Id. at 128. Construing the Amendment to give Mrs. Davis a qualifying income interest for life serves that statutory design precisely: upon a valid QTIP election, the trust corpus will be included in Mrs. Davis's own gross estate, ensuring the single taxation Congress intended. Denying the deduction here—merely because the trustee's ministerial discretion over installments is bounded by an ascertainable standard identical to those long recognized in Merchants National Bank, Ithaca Trust, and Old Colony Trust—would frustrate rather than serve the statutory purpose approved in Stapf.

For these reasons, the Tax Court's decision should be reversed, and the marital deduction under section 2056(b)(7) should be restored to the Davis estate.
