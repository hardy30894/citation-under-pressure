**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s failure to advise petitioner of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not warrant habeas relief because petitioner undisputedly knew of that right and voluntarily chose not to appeal. The error was harmless, and the court of appeals correctly applied harmless-error review.

**I. A Violation of Rule 32(a)(2) Is Subject to Harmless-Error Review**

Not every procedural irregularity entitles a defendant to vacatur of his sentence. For non-constitutional errors, the harmless-error standard of *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946), governs: relief is warranted only if the error “had substantial influence” on the outcome. The same principle applies to violations of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), this Court held that a sentencing court’s failure to ask the defendant whether he wished to speak—a violation of Rule 32(a)—did not require resentencing because the error was not “jurisdictional” and did not affect “substantial rights.” The rule at issue here is analogous. Rule 32(a)(2) is a procedural safeguard, not a constitutional command. Its purpose is to ensure that a defendant knows of his right to appeal. When that purpose is satisfied—as it was here—the error is harmless.

**II. Petitioner’s Actual Knowledge of His Right to Appeal Renders Any Error Harmless**

The district court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that petitioner knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That finding is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963) (habeas court must defer to state court factual findings unless “not fairly supported by the record”). Because petitioner already possessed the very information that Rule 32(a)(2) was designed to convey, the omission could not have prejudiced him. *Cf. United States v. Hark*, 320 U.S. 531, 535 (1944) (defendant’s actual knowledge of a right negates any claim of prejudice from failure to advise). The rule’s purpose was fully served.

**III. Petitioner’s Knowing Failure to Appeal Constitutes a Waiver or Forfeiture**

A defendant who knows of his right to appeal and deliberately declines to exercise it cannot later complain that he was not informed. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver is “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right”). Here, petitioner’s decision not to appeal—made with full knowledge—operated as a waiver. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 439 (1963) (a defendant may waive the right to appeal by “deliberately by-passing” state procedures). Even if the failure to advise is viewed as a procedural defect, petitioner’s subsequent inaction, coupled with his knowledge, forfeited any claim of error. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 444 (1944) (failure to raise a known objection constitutes waiver).

**IV. The Error Did Not Affect Petitioner’s Substantial Rights**

Under *Kotteakos*, an error is harmless unless it “substantially influenced” the outcome. 328 U.S. at 765. Petitioner does not argue that he would have appealed had he been advised; the district court found he knew of the right and chose not to appeal. Thus, the omission had no effect on his decision. *Cf. Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967) (even constitutional errors may be harmless if “harmless beyond a reasonable doubt”). A fortiori, a non-constitutional error that does not alter the defendant’s conduct is harmless.

**V. The Court of Appeals Properly Applied Harmless-Error Review**

The Third Circuit held that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmless-error analysis and that the error was harmless because petitioner knew of his right. That approach is consistent with *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428, and with the principle that procedural rules should not be applied in a “hypertechnical” manner. *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (appellate courts may notice plain error but should not reverse for “mere technicalities”). The court correctly focused on substance over form.

**Conclusion**

Because petitioner knew of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it, the district court’s failure to advise him under Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
