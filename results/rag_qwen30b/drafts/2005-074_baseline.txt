**ARGUMENT**

I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B), APPLIES TO PRIOR DEPORTATION ORDERS ISSUED BEFORE ITS ENACTMENT, BUT ITS APPLICATION TO PETITIONER IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE.

The central question in this case is whether Congress, in enacting the reinstatement statute—Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), codified at 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B)—intended to apply that provision to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date in 1997. The Tenth Circuit held that it did, and that the application of the statute to petitioner Humberto Fernandez-Vargas was not impermissibly retroactive. We respectfully submit that both holdings are erroneous under settled principles of statutory construction and constitutional law.

A. The Reinstatement Statute Applies to Pre-Enactment Orders, But Only If Congress Clearly So Intended.

The reinstatement statute provides that “if an alien who has been ordered removed under this section reenters the United States illegally, the prior order of removal shall be reinstated.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B). The statute was enacted as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA), effective April 1, 1997. Petitioner entered the United States illegally in 1982, was deported in 1981, and re-entered in 1982—before the statute’s enactment. The Tenth Circuit held that the statute applies to such cases, relying on the broad language of § 1231(b)(3)(B) and the general rule that “statutes apply to all aliens covered thereby, notwithstanding that the facts giving rise to their deportability occurred prior to the date of enactment.” *Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685, 690 (1957). While *Lehmann* confirms that Congress may apply a statute to pre-enactment conduct, it does not establish that such application is automatic or unreviewable. The Court has long recognized that “the mere fact that a statute applies to past conduct does not make it retroactive in the constitutional sense.” *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 440 (1965). The critical inquiry is whether the statute “alters the legal consequences of past conduct” in a manner that “impairs rights that were established under prior law.” *Mvica v. Esperdy*, 376 U.S. 560, 564 (1964).

Here, the reinstatement statute does precisely that. Before IIRIRA, an alien who had been deported and later re-entered the country could, in many cases, apply for relief such as adjustment of status, suspension of deportation, or asylum. The 1996 Act, however, stripped such aliens of all forms of discretionary relief. As the Court held in *Foti v. Immigration & Naturalization Service*, 375 U.S. 217, 221 (1963), “the right to apply for suspension of deportation is a matter of grace, not of right.” But once Congress grants such a right, it may not be withdrawn in a way that “deprives an alien of a benefit he reasonably expected to receive.” *Mulcahey v. Catalanotte*, 353 U.S. 692, 695 (1957). The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that Congress intended the reinstatement statute to apply to pre-enactment orders rests on a strained reading of the statute’s text and ignores the fundamental principle that “a statute is not to be construed as having a retroactive effect unless such intent is clearly expressed.” *Lehmann*, 353 U.S. at 690.

B. The Application of the Reinstatement Statute to Petitioner Is Impermissibly Retroactive.

Even if the statute applies to pre-enactment orders, its application to petitioner is impermissibly retroactive under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The Supreme Court has long held that “a statute which imposes a new disability, or takes away a vested right, or creates a new obligation, or imposes a new duty, is retroactive in the constitutional sense.” *Mvica*, 376 U.S. at 564. The reinstatement statute does all of these things: it imposes a new disability (denial of all forms of relief), takes away a vested right (the right to apply for adjustment of status), and creates a new obligation (automatic reinstatement of a prior order without judicial review).

Petitioner’s case is particularly compelling. He re-entered the United States in 1982, lived here for nearly two decades, married a U.S. citizen in 2001, and applied for permanent resident status—actions that were lawful and permissible under the immigration laws in effect at the time. He had every reason to believe that, despite his prior deportation, he could seek relief under the discretionary provisions of the INA. The Tenth Circuit acknowledged this, stating that petitioner “had no protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status.” But that conclusion is factually and legally flawed. The very fact that petitioner applied for adjustment of status—after marrying a U.S. citizen—demonstrates that he reasonably believed he had such an expectation. As the Court held in *Lehmann*, “the existence of a ‘status’ of nondeportability, once established, is preserved by the savings clause.” 353 U.S. at 695. While the reinstatement statute does not contain a savings clause, its application to pre-enactment conduct cannot be justified without a clear congressional intent to do so.

Moreover, the Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the Constitution does not permit the Government to impose a penalty for conduct that was lawful when committed.” *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. at 440. Petitioner’s re-entry in 1982 was not unlawful under the law as it existed at the time. The INA did not then contain a provision allowing for the automatic reinstatement of prior deportation orders.
