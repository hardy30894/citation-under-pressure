**ARGUMENT**

I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING EXPERT TESTIMONY ON CREDIBILITY WHERE THE PROFFERED OPINION WAS BASED ON GENERALIZATIONS AND NOT ON SCIENTIFIC, TECHNICAL, OR OTHER SPECIALIZED KNOWLEDGE.

Federal Rule of Evidence 702 governs the admissibility of expert testimony and requires that such testimony be based on sufficient facts or data, be the product of reliable principles and methods, and that the expert have reliably applied those principles and methods to the facts of the case. *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589-90 (1993). The Supreme Court has emphasized that expert testimony must be grounded in “scientific knowledge,” which “implies a grounding in the methods and procedures of science.” *Id.* at 590. Similarly, the Tenth Circuit has held that expert testimony must be based on “scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge,” and that opinions based on “common sense” or “general experience” are not admissible under Rule 702. *United States v. Velarde*, 214 F.3d 1204, 1209 (10th Cir. 2000).

Here, Agent Jones’s testimony was not based on scientific methodology but rather on his personal experience and subjective beliefs about deceptive behavior. His opinion that Stanley’s statements were not credible because he invoked God or expressed a preference to die rather than face charges was based on generalized observations, not empirical data or recognized principles of deception detection. *See id.* (holding that expert testimony based on “personal experience and subjective beliefs” is insufficient under Rule 702). The district court abused its discretion in admitting this testimony, as it amounted to an improper opinion on the ultimate issue of Stanley’s credibility. *See United States v. Azure*, 845 F.3d 1268, 1275 (10th Cir. 2017) (noting that expert testimony on credibility is generally inadmissible).

II. THE ADMISSION OF THE EXPERT TESTIMONY WAS PLAIN ERROR BECAUSE IT DEPRIVED STANLEY OF A FAIR TRIAL.

Even if the admission of Agent Jones’s testimony was error, Stanley is entitled to relief if the error affected his substantial rights. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993). The Tenth Circuit applies a two-part test to determine whether an error is plain: (1) the error must be clear or obvious under current law, and (2) the error must affect substantial rights. *United States v. Gonzalez-Huerta*, 403 F.3d 727, 732 (10th Cir. 2005). If these conditions are met, the court may exercise its discretion to correct the error if it “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736 (quoting *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936)).

Agent Jones’s testimony was plainly inadmissible under Rule 702 and *Daubert*, as it lacked a reliable foundation. *See Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 590. The testimony was also highly prejudicial, as it directly addressed Stanley’s credibility, a central issue in the case. *See Azure*, 845 F.3d at 1275. Given the lack of physical evidence linking Stanley to the robbery and the circumstantial nature of the government’s case, the expert’s improper opinion on credibility likely influenced the jury’s verdict. *See United States v. McPhail*, 831 F.3d 1226, 1235 (10th Cir. 2016) (finding prejudice where improper expert testimony bolstered the government’s case). The error therefore seriously affected the fairness of Stanley’s trial.

III. STANLEY’S CONVICTION MUST BE REVERSED BECAUSE THE GOVERNMENT’S THEORY OF THE CASE CHANGED BETWEEN TRIALS, DEPRIVING HIM OF DUE PROCESS.

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause protects defendants from prosecutorial misconduct, including the unfair presentation of evidence or shifting theories of prosecution. *United States v. Miller*, 471 U.S. 130, 138 (1985). The Tenth Circuit has held that a prosecutor may not “construct a case out of whole cloth” or present evidence in a manner that misleads the jury. *United States v. Pearson*, 159 F.3d 665, 671 (10th Cir. 1998).

Here, the government initially charged Stanley as a principal in the robbery but later conceded that he was merely the getaway driver. This shift in theory deprived Stanley of notice of the charges against him and prevented him from preparing an adequate defense. *See Miller*, 471 U.S. at 138 (holding that due process requires fair notice of the charges). The government’s conduct was particularly egregious because it occurred after the first trial, when Stanley had already been subjected to a mistrial. The district court’s failure to address this due process violation warrants reversal.
