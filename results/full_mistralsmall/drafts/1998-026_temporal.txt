**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Rule 32(a)(2) violation is subject to harmless-error review.**

The Supreme Court has long held that procedural errors at sentencing do not warrant collateral relief unless they affect substantial rights. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), the Court explained that a defendant must demonstrate "prejudice" to obtain relief for a sentencing error. The Court reasoned that "[t]he standard for determining whether a sentence is illegal is whether the trial judge was correctly advised as to the applicable law in sentencing." *Id.* at 427. Because the sentencing court’s failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal did not alter the lawfulness of his sentence, it caused no prejudice.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), where the Court held that procedural errors are harmless if they do not affect the outcome of the proceeding. The Court stated that "[t]he inquiry is not whether, in a proper case, the defendant would have been entitled to the benefit he claims, but whether he was denied a right which would have been available to him in the prosecution of his case." *Id.* at 234. Here, Peguero was aware of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it. Thus, the error did not deprive him of any meaningful right.

**II. The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) was satisfied because Peguero knew of his right to appeal.**

The Advisory Committee Notes to Rule 32(a)(2) (1944) emphasize that the rule’s purpose is to ensure defendants are informed of their appellate rights. However, as this Court recognized in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), harmless-error review applies when the defendant’s knowledge renders the error inconsequential. The Court explained that "[b]efore a federal constitutional error can be held harmless, the court must be able to declare a belief that it was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt." *Id.* at 24. Here, the District Court found that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal. Since he was not deprived of any meaningful opportunity to appeal, the error was harmless.

This principle was applied in *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), where the Court held that errors that do not affect the fairness of a proceeding are not grounds for reversal. The Court stated that "[h]armless error is an error which is trivial, or formal, or merely academic, and was not prejudicial to the substantial rights of the accused." *Id.* at 764. The District Court’s failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal was a formal error that did not prejudice him, as he was already aware of that right.

**III. Collateral relief is unwarranted where the defendant suffered no prejudice.**

In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court held that habeas relief is available only where a constitutional error "has resulted in the conviction of one who is actually innocent." *Id.* at 411. Since Peguero was not deprived of any meaningful right—he knew of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it—he suffered no prejudice. Thus, habeas relief is inappropriate.

This principle was reiterated in *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174 (1947), where the Court held that "[a]n error which does not affect substantial rights is not a ground for reversal." *Id.* at 178. The Court explained that "[t]he purpose of the judicial process is to protect those rights of liberty and property which are recognized by society as basic and fundamental." *Id.* at 179. Here, Peguero’s liberty interest was not affected by the sentencing court’s failure to advise him of his right to appeal, as he was already aware of that right.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The District Court’s failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal was harmless because he was aware of that right and elected not to exercise it. Thus, he suffered no prejudice and is not entitled to habeas relief.
