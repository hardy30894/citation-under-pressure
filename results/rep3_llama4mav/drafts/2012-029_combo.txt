The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases that involve patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case. The Supreme Court has established that federal jurisdiction extends to cases "in which a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, in that patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims." Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 809 (1988) (citing Franchise Tax Bd. of Cal. v. Constr. Laborers Vacation Trust for S. Cal., 463 U.S. 1, 27-28 (1983)); see also Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897) ("if it appears that some substantial and relevant question as to the construction or effect of the patent laws is involved in the controversy, the case is one arising under those laws").

In this case, Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, specifically the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar. The experimental use exception is a critical aspect of patent law, and its application is necessary to determine whether Minton's patent infringement claim would have succeeded. See Smith v. Nichols, 88 U.S. 112, 118 (1874) (noting the importance of patent law in determining the validity of a patent).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving patent law. See Pratt, 168 U.S. at 259; see also Dalehite v. United States, 346 U.S. 15, 27-28 (1953) (noting that federal jurisdiction is exclusive when a case involves a substantial question of federal law). In Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 114 (1936), the Court held that a case arises under federal law when it "really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction or effect of [federal law]." The Court has also emphasized the importance of maintaining the balance between state and federal judicial responsibilities. See Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912) ("A suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws...").

Here, the patent issue is substantial and necessary to the resolution of Minton's malpractice claim. The experimental use exception is a complex and nuanced aspect of patent law, and its application requires a deep understanding of federal patent law. See Rubber Co. v. Goodyear, 76 U.S. 788, 794 (1869) (noting the complexity of patent law and the need for expertise in its application). The federal courts are better equipped to handle this issue, and exercising jurisdiction over this case would not disturb the balance between state and federal judicial responsibilities. See Osborn v. Bank of United States, 22 U.S. 738, 822-23 (1824) (noting the importance of federal courts in adjudicating cases involving federal law).

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's case. The court of appeals erred in denying Minton's motion to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, and this Court should reverse that decision and dismiss the case for lack of jurisdiction.
