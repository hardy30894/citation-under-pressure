ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause does not require a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining a defendant’s competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. The constitutional test remains whether the defendant has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam).

This Court has long held that the same standard governs competency to stand trial and competency to waive fundamental rights. Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966). When a defendant seeks to forgo counsel, the inquiry is whether the waiver is made “with eyes open.” Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 279 (1942). The record must show that the accused was offered counsel but “intelligently and understandingly rejected the offer.” Carnley v. Cochran, 369 U.S. 506, 516 (1962). No separate, elevated mental-function test has ever been imposed.

The same principle applies to guilty pleas. A plea constitutes a waiver of the privilege against self-incrimination, the right to trial by jury, and the right to confront witnesses. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969). Yet the validity of that waiver turns on whether it was voluntary and intelligent under the Dusky formulation, not on any heightened capacity for “reasoned choice.” Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), requires only that the relinquishment be made “with sufficient awareness of the relevant circumstances and likely consequences.”

This Court has repeatedly declined to fragment the competency inquiry by stage of proceeding. Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam); Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708, 723-24 (1948). The presumption against waiver remains constant, Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 340 (1963), but the underlying mental-capacity standard does not change. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932), recognized that due process demands adequate assistance, yet it never suggested that the threshold for waiving that assistance differs from the threshold for standing trial.

Because the Ninth Circuit imposed a novel and unsupported “reasoned choice” requirement, its judgment should be reversed.
