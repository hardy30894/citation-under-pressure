**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because Burnett presented sufficient evidence to create genuine disputes of material fact regarding whether he provided adequate notice of his serious health condition under the FMLA and his disability under the ADA.

**I. Burnett Provided Sufficient Notice Under the FMLA**

The FMLA entitles an employee to leave for a “serious health condition.” 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1)(D). An employee need not expressly invoke the FMLA; rather, he must provide “enough information” to put the employer on notice that the leave may be FMLA-qualifying. *Rice v. Sunrise Express, Inc.*, 209 F.3d 1008, 1018 (7th Cir. 2000). The employer’s duty to inquire is triggered when the employee communicates “a condition that a reasonable person would understand to be a serious health condition.” *Byrne v. Avon Prods., Inc.*, 328 F.3d 379, 382 (7th Cir. 2003).

Burnett did precisely that. In October 2003, he told his supervisor Polo that he had a “weak bladder” and was seeing a doctor. In December, he presented a doctor’s order for blood testing, explained that he had high PSA and cholesterol levels, and stated he needed to see a urologist. He then explicitly compared his condition to his brother-in-law’s prostate cancer. A reasonable jury could find that these disclosures—particularly the mention of elevated PSA, a known marker for prostate cancer—conveyed a serious health condition. *See Collins v. NTN-Bower Corp.*, 272 F.3d 1006, 1008 (7th Cir. 2001) (employee’s statement that he had “a serious medical problem” and needed surgery sufficed). Moreover, Burnett gave his supervisor a document describing a prostate biopsy, which stated that the procedure is indicated by “an elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA).” This document, even if not received by Polo, was given to another supervisor under standard policy, creating a factual dispute about notice. The district court erred by concluding that Burnett failed to provide enough information to alert Habitat that his leave might be FMLA-qualifying.

**II. Burnett Provided Sufficient Notice Under the ADA**

The ADA requires an employer to provide reasonable accommodations to a qualified individual with a disability. 42 U.S.C. § 12112(b)(5)(A). The employee must “make a sufficient request” for accommodation, but the request need not be formal or use magic words. *Bultemeyer v. Fort Wayne Cmty. Sch.*, 100 F.3d 1281, 1285 (7th Cir. 1996). The employer must engage in an interactive process once it knows of the disability. *Hendricks-Robinson v. Excel Corp.*, 154 F.3d 685, 693 (7th Cir. 1998).

Burnett repeatedly informed Habitat of his medical condition and its impact on his work. He told Polo that his “weak bladder” made it difficult to work in a location with limited restroom access, and he requested a transfer to a midnight shift to accommodate his medical needs. He also provided medical documentation and explained his need for time off for appointments and a biopsy. These communications were sufficient to put Habitat on notice that Burnett had a disability—prostate cancer or its precursors—and that he needed an accommodation. *See Jovanovic v. In-Sink-Erator*, 201 F.3d 894, 899 (7th Cir. 2000) (employee’s statement that he could not perform certain tasks due to a medical condition sufficed). Instead of engaging in the interactive process, Habitat issued reprimands and ultimately suspended Burnett. A reasonable jury could find that Habitat failed to accommodate Burnett’s disability.

**III. Genuine Disputes of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment**

The district court improperly resolved factual disputes in Habitat’s favor. Burnett’s evidence—his repeated disclosures, the medical document, and his requests for transfer and time off—creates triable issues on whether Habitat had sufficient notice under both statutes. Summary judgment was therefore inappropriate.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
