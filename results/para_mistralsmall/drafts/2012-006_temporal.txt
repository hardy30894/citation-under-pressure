**ARGUMENT**

I. The Government Bears the Burden of Persuasion to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

The government’s burden of persuasion at trial is not diminished by a defendant’s withdrawal from a conspiracy before the statute of limitations has run. In *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601 (1910), this Court held that the government must prove every essential element of the offense, including the existence of a conspiracy, beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 608. The Court explained that the government’s burden does not shift merely because a defendant asserts that he withdrew from the conspiracy. *Id.* Rather, the government must demonstrate that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and that the defendant was a member during that time. *Id.*

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d 834 (2d Cir. 1939), where Judge Learned Hand wrote for the court:

> “The burden is on the prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the conspiracy continued to exist during the period alleged in the indictment and that the defendant was a participant in it during that period. Withdrawal is a defense which the defendant must establish by a preponderance of the evidence, but the government must still prove that the conspiracy persisted and that the defendant was a member at the time the conspiracy was ongoing.” *Id.* at 839 (citations omitted).

Thus, the government’s burden of persuasion remains undiminished even where a defendant claims to have withdrawn from the conspiracy. *Kissel*, 218 U.S. at 608; *Manton*, 107 F.2d at 839.

II. The Jury Must Be Instructed on the Government’s Burden of Proof Regarding Continuing Conspiracy

The trial court’s failure to instruct the jury on the government’s burden of proof regarding the conspiracy’s continuation into the limitations period was reversible error. In *Stromberg v. California*, 283 U.S. 359 (1931), this Court held that where a statute criminalizes multiple, distinct types of conduct, a jury must be instructed on the specific offense for which the defendant is charged. *Id.* at 367-68. The Court reasoned that without such an instruction, the jury might convict based on an uncharged theory of liability. *Id.*

Similarly, in *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607 (1946), this Court held that where the government’s case rests on multiple, alternative theories of liability, the jury must be instructed on the government’s burden of proof for each theory. *Id.* at 614-15. The Court explained:

> “Where the case against a defendant is based on alternative theories, the jury must be instructed that it may not convict unless it unanimously agrees that the government has proven one of the theories beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Id.* at 615.

Here, the government’s case rested on the theory that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. Because the jury was not instructed on the government’s burden of proof regarding this theory, the conviction must be reversed. *Stromberg*, 283 U.S. at 367-68; *Bollenbach*, 326 U.S. at 614-15.

III. The Government’s Failure to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Beyond a Reasonable Doubt Requires Reversal

The record contains no evidence that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. In *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), this Court held that a conspiracy conviction cannot stand where the government fails to prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. *Id.* at 254-55. The Court explained:

> “A conspiracy is a continuing offense, but it cannot be presumed to continue indefinitely. The government must prove that the conspiracy persisted into the limitations period and that the defendant was a member during that time.” *Id.*

Here, the government presented no evidence that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. Thus, the conviction cannot stand. *Socony-Vacuum*, 310 U.S. at 254-55.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
