**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF THE OCSLA LIMITS COVERAGE TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF.**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") coverage only to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The statute’s plain text requires that both the operations *and* the resulting injury occur on the shelf. *See* *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) ("[W]ords in a statute should be given their ordinary meaning unless a contrary intent is clearly manifested."). This interpretation is consistent with the OCSLA’s purpose of extending federal jurisdiction over submerged lands beyond state boundaries. *See* *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 50 (1894) (noting that federal jurisdiction over submerged lands is limited to the area beyond state waters).

The Ninth Circuit’s "substantial nexus" test—under which an injury occurring onshore may be covered if it is sufficiently connected to shelf operations—finds no support in the statutory text. *See* *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) ("Where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning the duty of interpretation does not arise."). The statute does not merely require that an injury *result from* shelf operations; it requires that the injury occur *as the result of operations conducted on the shelf*. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This distinction is critical. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutory coverage provisions must be strictly construed in favor of the employer. *See* *Sun Ship, Inc. v. Pennsylvania*, 447 U.S. 715, 719 (1980) (citing *Voris v. Eikel*, 346 U.S. 328, 333 (1953)).

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), correctly applies this principle. The court held that an injury must occur on the shelf itself to trigger OCSLA coverage, reasoning that the statute’s language "clearly limits coverage to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf." *Id.* at 362. This interpretation aligns with the OCSLA’s structure, which extends federal jurisdiction only to the shelf and adjacent waters. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a) (defining the outer Continental Shelf as "submerged lands lying seaward and outside the area of lands beneath navigable waters").

The Third Circuit’s contrary decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is unpersuasive. The court relied on a "but for" test, under which an injury is covered if it would not have occurred but for shelf operations. *Id.* at 811.
