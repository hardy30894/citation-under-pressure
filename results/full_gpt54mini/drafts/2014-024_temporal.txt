**ARGUMENT**

Section 875(c) does not punish mere angry words or venting. It criminalizes only a “threat” to injure another person, and this Court has long required mens rea before imposing criminal liability for speech-based offenses. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250–63 (1952); *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605–06 (1994) (confirming the “presumption” that Congress intends a scienter requirement, though decided later). That background rule is especially strong where a statute reaches expressive conduct at the core of First Amendment concern. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707–08 (1969) (per curiam). Because the government obtained Anthony Elonis’s conviction without proving that he subjectively intended his Facebook posts as threats, the judgment cannot stand.

1. **Congress did not dispense with mens rea in § 875(c).**

This Court has repeatedly refused to read federal criminal statutes to eliminate intent absent a clear statement. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 263; *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–53 (1922) (noting that omission of intent must be clear before criminal liability is imposed without it). Section 875(c) contains no express language imposing strict liability, nor any textual indication that Congress meant to punish statements the speaker did not mean as threats. Where Congress is silent, the settled presumption is that criminal liability requires awareness of the facts that make the conduct unlawful. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 263.

That presumption matters here because a “threat” is not a purely objective category. The word describes a communication that the speaker means as a threat, not one that a hypersensitive listener might misunderstand. To convict without proof of subjective intent is to criminalize negligence in speech. This Court has never treated negligence as enough to support ordinary criminal liability absent an explicit statutory choice. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250–51.

2. **The First Amendment requires a subjective-intent limitation for true threats.**

In *Watts*, this Court unanimously held that a political statement that was “political hyperbole” was not a true threat. 394 U.S. at 707–08. The Court emphasized the context, the conditional nature of the remark, and the reaction of listeners. Id. at 708. The decision reflects a constitutional principle: when speech is prosecuted as a threat, courts must separate genuine threats from exaggeration, satire, and anger. See *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–72 (1942) (describing narrowly limited categories of unprotected speech).

That constitutional line cannot be drawn reliably by asking only whether a reasonable person might perceive the words as threatening. Objective fear alone sweeps too broadly and invites punishment of speech that the speaker did not mean as a threat. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 707–08. The First Amendment does not permit conviction based on ambiguous expression divorced from the speaker’s own intent. *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447–49 (1969) (speech may be punished only when directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and likely to do so). The same principle applies here: when criminal punishment turns on expressive content, the Constitution requires proof of the defendant’s own culpable purpose.

3. **The government’s objective standard is incompatible with this Court’s threat cases.**

The lower courts relied on an objective “reasonable person” approach, but that standard is foreign to this Court’s treatment of speech offenses. In *Watts*, the Court did not ask whether a reasonable listener could have felt fear in the abstract; it asked whether the statement, in context, was a true threat or protected political speech. 394 U.S. at 707–08. Likewise, *Brandenburg* rejected broad liability for expression based on the effect it might have on hearers absent the speaker’s specific unlawful aim. 395 U.S. at 447–49.

The government’s rule would punish speakers for failing to anticipate how a jury might later interpret a post, lyric, joke, or darkly phrased criticism. That is especially dangerous in modern public discourse, where speakers routinely use hyperbole and persona. The Constitution does not allow a criminal conviction to rest on the happenstance that a listener misunderstood speech the speaker did not mean as a threat.

4. **At minimum, due process requires clearer proof than the government offered here.**

A criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct it forbids. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453–58 (1939). Reading § 875(c) to reach statements lacking subjective intent would blur the line between criminal threats and protected speech, leaving ordinary speakers uncertain about what the law forbids. That lack of clarity is intolerable where the penalty is imprisonment and the regulated medium is speech. *Lanzetta*, 306 U.S. at 453–58.

Because the jury was not required to find that Elonis subjectively intended his posts as threats, his conviction rests on an impermissibly broad theory of liability. The judgment should be reversed.
