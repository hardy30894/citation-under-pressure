**ARGUMENT**

**THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN EXPANDING EXEMPTION 2 BEYOND ITS STATUTORY TEXT AND SUPREME COURT PRECEDENT.**

Congress enacted FOIA to require "full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." *Dep't of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). FOIA's exemptions are "explicitly exclusive" and "must be narrowly construed." *U.S. Dep't of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989); *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361.

Exemption 2 exempts matters that are "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has held that this exemption covers only two categories: (1) "trivial" or "minor employment matters" of no public interest, and (2) materials "the disclosure of which would risk circumvention of agency regulation." *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 363, 369. Critically, *Rose* limited the second category to materials involving "rules and practices *governing agency personnel*." *Id.* at 369 (emphasis added).

The Ninth Circuit's adoption of a "predominantly internal" standard impermissibly expands Exemption 2 beyond its statutory language and this Court's holding in *Rose*.

**A. The "Predominantly Internal" Standard Lacks Any Statutory Basis.**

The plain text of Exemption 2 requires that withheld matters relate "solely" to "internal personnel rules and practices." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The word "solely" is restrictive, not expansive. The Ninth Circuit's "predominantly internal" test rewrites the statute to require merely that materials be *mostly* internal. See *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051, 1056 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc). This dilution of the statutory standard finds no support in *Rose* or any other decision of this Court.

Moreover, the phrase "personnel rules and practices" refers to rules governing *people*—an agency's employees—not rules governing physical objects or geographic areas. The Navy's ESQD arc maps do not tell any employee how to behave; they measure explosive blast radii and inform facility design. These maps are no more "personnel rules" than building blueprints or topographical surveys. See *Maricopa Audubon Soc'y v. U.S. Forest Serv.*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085 (9th Cir. 1997) (rejecting Exemption 2 claim for ecological data because it "does not relate 'solely,' or even predominantly, 'to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency'").

**B. The Ninth Circuit's Prior Precedent Did Not Authorize This Expansion.**

The Ninth Circuit claimed its prior decision in *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), was consistent with the "predominantly internal" standard. But *Hardy*—which involved a law enforcement raid manual—addressed only "law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation." *Id.* at 656. The *Hardy* court never purported to hold that *non-law-enforcement* materials could qualify for Exemption 2. The Ninth Circuit's retroactive reinterpretation of *Hardy* as a "sufficient but not necessary" case contravenes the principle that FOIA exemptions are "narrowly construed." *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361.

**C. The Navy's ESQD Arc Maps Do Not Satisfy Even the Ninth Circuit's Own Test.**

The Navy conceded that the ESQD arc maps "are designed to be a long term planning tool" for facility design and construction. Joint Appendix at 45. These maps do not instruct Navy personnel on how to perform their duties. They are static technical specifications describing blast zones. Such documents are not "predominantly internal" in any meaningful sense; they describe the physical effects of explosions on the surrounding community, including the towns of Port Townsend and Port Hadlock. Cf. *Maricopa Audubon Soc'y*, 108 F.3d at 1086 (rejecting Exemption 2 claim for environmental data because "the data do not warn employees how to perform their jobs").

Furthermore, the Navy's claim that disclosure could enable "reverse engineering" of weapons systems is a national security argument properly analyzed under Exemption 1 (classified information) or Exemption 7(F) (law enforcement records endangering safety). Congress did not place such concerns within Exemption 2. See *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989) (FOIA reflects a careful "balance between the interests of the public in greater access to information and the needs of the Government to protect certain kinds of information").

**D. The Ninth Circuit's Ruling Defeats FOIA's Purpose.**

FOIA's core purpose is "to open agency action to the light of public scrutiny." *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 372. Milner seeks information about explosive storage zones near his community—information directly relevant to public safety. The Ninth Circuit's decision allows agencies to shield any "predominantly internal" document from disclosure simply by asserting a risk of circumvention. This standard swallows FOIA's presumption of disclosure.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
