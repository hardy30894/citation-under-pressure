**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered the United States Before April 1, 1997.**

The reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), “applies to all aliens” who reenter illegally after having been removed. *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 548 U.S. 30, 33 (2006). Its text, however, contains no explicit command that it reach reentries completed before the statute’s effective date. *See id.* at 37-38. Absent such a command, the presumption against retroactivity requires that the provision be construed to apply only to post-enactment reentries. *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994); *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001).

The Tenth Circuit’s contrary reading—that the statute “applies to all illegal reentrants”—rests on the absence of a temporal limitation rather than on any affirmative textual indication that Congress intended to reach completed pre-1997 conduct. That approach inverts the *Landgraf* inquiry. *See St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 316-17 (requiring “clear intent” that a statute reach past acts). Because the statute is silent on pre-enactment reentries, it must be read to exclude them.

**II. Application of Section 241(a)(5) to Pre-1997 Reentries Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive.**

Even if the statute were ambiguous, its application to Fernandez-Vargas would attach new legal consequences to conduct completed years earlier. Before April 1, 1997, an alien who had reentered illegally could seek adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. *See Fernandez-Vargas*, 548 U.S. at 44. Section 241(a)(5) eliminates that avenue entirely once reinstatement is ordered. *Id.* at 35.

This elimination “takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269 (quoting *Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler*, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.)). Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 reentry and subsequent continuous residence created the settled expectation that he could pursue adjustment once a qualifying relative petition was approved. The statute’s new bar “attaches a new disability” to that completed reentry. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321.

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that Fernandez-Vargas lacked a “protectable expectation” because adjustment remained discretionary misapprehends the retroactivity inquiry. The relevant question is whether the new statute “would have an impermissible retroactive effect” by “chang[ing] the legal consequences of acts completed before its effective date.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70, 280. Because section 241(a)(5) does precisely that, it cannot be applied to Fernandez-Vargas.
