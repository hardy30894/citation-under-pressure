# ARGUMENT

## I. THE SCHOOL DISTRICT'S DENIAL OF ACCESS CONSTITUTED IMPERMISSIBLE VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION IN A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM

### A. Once a State Opens Its Property to Certain Speech, It May Not Discriminate on the Basis of Viewpoint

This Court has long recognized that although government need not create a public forum, once it does so, the First Amendment sharply constrains the government's ability to exclude speakers. *Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983). Even in a "limited public forum," where the State may confine access to certain subjects or speakers, it may not discriminate against speech on the basis of viewpoint. See *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) (government may restrict access "as long as the distinctions drawn are reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum" and are not "an effort to suppress expression merely because public officials oppose the speaker's view").

The District permitted use of school facilities after hours for a broad range of civic, social, and educational purposes—including discussions of childrearing and family values by the Boy Scouts and the PTA. By opening its facilities to that subject matter, the District created a limited public forum for the discussion of family issues. Having done so, it could not selectively exclude Lamb's Chapel's contribution to that discussion merely because the Chapel's perspective on family values happened to be religious.

### B. Excluding Speech Because It Is Religious in Character Is Viewpoint Discrimination, Not a Permissible Subject-Matter Limitation

The Second Circuit erred in characterizing the District's exclusion as a content-neutral limitation on the *subject matter* of the forum. The District did not exclude discussion of family values; it excluded one *viewpoint* on that subject—the religious one. This Court's precedents establish that such exclusion is the paradigmatic viewpoint discrimination the First Amendment forbids.

In *Cornelius*, this Court explained that a forum may be limited to certain groups or topics without offending the First Amendment, but the "government violates the First Amendment when it denies access to a speaker solely to suppress the point of view he espouses on an otherwise includible subject." 473 U.S. at 806. Family values was indisputably an "includible subject" in the Center Moriches forum; the Chapel was excluded solely because it proposed to address that subject from a religious perspective. That is viewpoint discrimination.

This Court's decision in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), controls. There, the University of Missouri argued that excluding religious groups from its generally open forum was necessary to maintain separation of church and state. This Court rejected that justification, holding that "an open forum in a public university does not confer any imprimatur of state approval on religious sects or practices," and that equal access "would not be incompatible with the Establishment Clause." *Id.* at 274-75. The Court further held that a state's interest in achieving greater separation of church and State than is already ensured under the Establishment Clause is not a compelling state interest sufficient to justify content-based discrimination against religious speech. *Id.* at 271-72, 276.

The Second Circuit attempted to distinguish *Widmar* on the ground that it involved students rather than outside community groups, and that the University had created a broader "generally open" forum. But nothing in *Widmar*'s reasoning turns on the status of the speaker as student versus community member. The constitutional vice identified in *Widmar*—exclusion of religious viewpoints from an otherwise open forum—is present here regardless of whether the excluded speaker is a student organization or a church.

### C. Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens Confirms That Equal Access for Religious Speech Does Not Violate the Establishment Clause

In *Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), this Court held that permitting a student religious club equal access to school facilities under the Equal Access Act did not violate the Establishment Clause. Applying the three-part test of *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602 (1971), the plurality reasoned that secondary school students "are mature enough and are likely to understand that a school does not endorse or support student speech that it merely permits on a nondiscriminatory basis." *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 250. The same logic applies with even greater force to the adult members of the Center Moriches community, who could not reasonably mistake permission to use school facilities after hours, on the same terms as any other group, for District endorsement of the Chapel's religious message.

The Second Circuit dismissed *Mergens* as resting on statutory grounds alone. But even if the *holding* was statutory, the *reasoning*—that equal access for religious speakers negates any Establishment Clause concern—applies directly to the constitutional question presented here. The District's proffered justification for excluding Lamb's Chapel was precisely the concern this Court rejected in both *Widmar* and *Mergens*: an exaggerated fear that neutral access for religious speech offends the Establishment Clause. That fear cannot justify viewpoint discrimination.

### D. The District's Justification Fails Under Any Applicable Standard

Even accepting the Second Circuit's characterization of the forum as "limited," the exclusion here cannot survive scrutiny. A restriction in a limited public forum must be reasonable and viewpoint-neutral. *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806. The District's blanket rule barring "religious purposes," Local Rule No. 7, is neither. It singles out religious viewpoints for exclusion from a forum otherwise open to discussion of the very subject—family values—that the Chapel sought to address. As this Court has held, "[t]he First Amendment's hostility to content-based regulation extends not only to restrictions on particular viewpoints, but also to prohibition of public discussion of an entire topic." *Consolidated Edison Co. v. Public Service Comm'n*, 447 U.S. 530, 537 (1980). Here, the District went further still, permitting the topic for everyone except those who would discuss it religiously.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
