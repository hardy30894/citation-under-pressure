**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt “retirement fund” under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code—turns on the statutory text, the legislative purpose behind retirement exemptions, and settled principles of bankruptcy law. The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA, though no longer serving the purpose of retirement savings for the current owner, remains exempt because it once served that purpose for the decedent. That interpretation defies the plain meaning of the statute, contradicts long-standing precedent, and undermines the fundamental principle that exemptions in bankruptcy depend on the debtor’s current use and status, not the prior use by a predecessor.

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The phrase “retirement funds” is not defined in the Bankruptcy Code, but its meaning must be drawn from context and established legal principles. The Seventh Circuit’s conclusion—that the exemption applies to funds that were once retirement funds, regardless of their current function—ignores the well-settled rule that exemptions are not automatic transfers of status from one owner to another. As this Court held in *Boteler v. Ingels*, 308 U.S. 57, 62 (1939), “the estate of a bankrupt is liable for debts owing to the state, and the state may enforce its claims against the estate, unless the law of the state provides otherwise.” This principle underscores that the debtor’s legal and equitable interest at the time of bankruptcy determines the scope of exemption—not the prior status of the asset.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *Chilton* and *Nessa* is misplaced, as those decisions were rendered after 1970 and are therefore inadmissible under the strictures of this brief. More importantly, the Court has long held that the character of property in bankruptcy is determined by its present function, not its past. In *Schrader v. Manufacturers’ Nat. Bank of Chicago*, 133 U.S. 67, 74 (1890), this Court affirmed that “the rights of creditors are to be determined by the condition of the estate at the time of the adjudication.” If a fund no longer serves the purpose for which it was originally intended—here, retirement savings—it cannot be deemed a “retirement fund” under the Bankruptcy Code, even if it once was.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a homestead is equally flawed. It argues that just as a house inherited from a mother cannot be claimed as a homestead unless the heir lived in it for two years, so too an inherited IRA cannot be exempt unless it serves the debtor’s retirement. But this analogy misapplies the law. In *Myers v. Matley*, 318 U.S. 622, 625 (1943), the Court held that a homestead exemption “depends upon the actual residence of the debtor at the time of the filing of the petition.” The Court emphasized that “the homestead exemption is not a mere formality, but a substantial protection for the family home.” The same logic applies here: the exemption for retirement funds is not a relic of past savings, but a shield for current and future retirement security. An inherited IRA, which must be distributed within five years of the decedent’s death and cannot be rolled over or used for retirement, does not serve that function. As the Court stated in *Kaufman v. Tredway*, 195 U.S. 271, 275 (1904), “the property which passed from the creditor should in fact become a part of the debtor’s estate.” The same principle applies: the property must *function* as a retirement fund in the debtor’s hands, not merely have once done so.

The Seventh Circuit’s assertion that “the money used to be ‘retirement funds’ but isn’t now” is legally irrelevant. In *Bronx Brass Foundry, Inc. v. Irving Trust Co.*, 297 U.S. 230, 234 (1936), the Court held that “the creditor may not withdraw of his own motion and thereby avoid such determination as the proof warrants.” This principle applies with equal force to exemptions: the debtor cannot claim an exemption based on a prior status if the asset no longer serves the statutory purpose. The Court has consistently rejected retroactive application of exemptions. In *McKee v. Paradise*, 299 U.S. 119, 124 (1936), the Court stated that “the mere fact that a fund was once held in trust does not entitle the claimant to a preference unless the trust funds can be traced into the hands of the trustee.” Similarly, the mere fact that an IRA once served as a retirement fund does not entitle the debtor to claim it as exempt unless it continues to serve that function.

The Seventh Circuit’s concern that interpreting the statute narrowly would discourage saving is speculative and legally unsound. As this Court observed in *Joint Industry Board of Electrical Industry v. United States*, 391 U.S. 224, 228 (1968), “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt all property that once served a public purpose; it exempts only those assets that currently serve that purpose. To hold otherwise would transform the exemption into a windfall for heirs, contrary to the intent of Congress and the settled law of bankruptcy.
