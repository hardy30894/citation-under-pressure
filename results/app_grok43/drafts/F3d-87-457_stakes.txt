ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to the State. Chapter 93-121 imposes disclosure obligations and a contingency-fee prohibition that cannot survive First Amendment scrutiny on their face.

I.

A law is facially invalid if “a substantial number of its applications are unconstitutional, judged in relation to the statute’s plainly legitimate sweep.” United States v. Stevens, 559 U.S. 460, 473 (2010) (quoting Washington State Grange v. Washington State Republican Party, 552 U.S. 442, 449 n.6 (2008)). The League need not await enforcement; when a regulation forces a choice between forgoing protected speech and risking sanctions, pre-enforcement review is available. Abbott Labs. v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 152-53 (1967).

Lobbying is “core political speech.” Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414, 422 (1988). The statute’s disclosure regime reaches every expenditure “for the purpose of lobbying,” Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a), without regard to whether the communication concerns legislation or executive action. Compelled disclosure of this breadth is subject to exacting scrutiny and must be narrowly tailored to an overriding governmental interest. Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 64-68 (1976) (per curiam). The statute’s open-ended categories—“research,” “communication,” “publications”—sweep in issue advocacy and internal strategy discussions that bear only an attenuated relationship to preventing corruption. See id. at 80. Because these provisions “burden substantially more speech than is necessary,” they fail. McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm’n, 514 U.S. 334, 357 (1995).

II.

The contingency-fee ban, Fla. Stat. § 11.047, likewise cannot be sustained. Payment for political advocacy is itself protected expression. Meyer, 486 U.S. at 422-23. A flat prohibition on success-based compensation directly restricts the “means by which” citizens “advocate” their views. Id. at 424. Although older decisions treated such arrangements as contrary to public policy, see, e.g., Trist v. Child, 88 U.S. 441, 450 (1874), those pronouncements predate modern recognition that “the First Amendment protects political association as well as political expression.” Buckley, 424 U.S. at 15. The ban therefore imposes an unconstitutional condition on the exercise of core political rights. See Perry v. Sindermann, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972).

Because both sets of provisions are substantially overbroad, the judgment should be reversed.
