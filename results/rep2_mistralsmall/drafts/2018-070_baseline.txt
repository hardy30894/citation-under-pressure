**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCSLA’S CHOICE-OF-LAW PROVISION REQUIRES A “GAP” IN FEDERAL COVERAGE BEFORE STATE LAW MAY BE ADOPTED AS SURROGATE FEDERAL LAW.

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations ... the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State ... are declared to be the law of the United States” for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. This Court has long recognized that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law only where federal law contains a “significant void or gap.” *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969); *see Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 359 (1969) (state law applies “to fill interstices” in federal scheme). The Ninth Circuit erred by dispensing with this threshold requirement.

This Court’s decision in *Rodrigue* confirms that state law fills only “interstices” in federal coverage. 395 U.S. at 359. The Supreme Court explained that the OCSLA “incorporates state law only to fill the interstices of the federal framework.” *Id.* (emphasis added). The Fifth Circuit has consistently applied this “gap-filling” requirement, holding that state law applies only where federal law is “silent or inadequate.” *PLT Eng’g, Inc. v. Union Texas Petroleum Corp.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990); *Breton Energy, L.L.C. v. Mariner Energy Res., Inc.*, 764 F.3d 394, 398 (5th Cir. 2014). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—allowing state law whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent”—ignores this settled precedent.

II. THE FLSA’S COMPREHENSIVE SCHEME LEAVES NO GAP FOR STATE WAGE AND HOUR LAWS.

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a comprehensive federal scheme governing minimum wages, overtime, and related protections. *See* 29 U.S.C. §§ 201–219. The FLSA includes an express savings clause permitting more protective state wage laws, 29 U.S.C. § 218, but this clause does not create a “gap” for state law to fill. *See, e.g., Cipriano v. City of Houston*, 723 F.3d 587, 592 (5th Cir. 2013) (savings clause preserves state laws but does not mandate their application under OCSLA).

The Department of Labor’s regulations confirm that the FLSA is a complete regulatory framework. *See* 29 C.F.R. § 784.1 (“The Act sets up its own standards and does not permit state or municipal regulation which will lessen the protection afforded by the Act.”). This Court has recognized that federal labor laws preempt state wage and hour rules where they conflict or where federal law occupies the field. *See, e.g., Gade v. Nat’l Solid Wastes Mgmt. Ass’n*, 505 U.S. 88, 98 (1992) (federal law preempts state law where it is comprehensive). The Ninth Circuit’s holding that California’s meal-period requirement may supplement the FLSA disregards this principle.

III. CALIFORNIA’S MEAL-PERIOD REQUIREMENT IS INCONSISTENT WITH THE FLSA’S STRUCTURE.

Even if state law were “applicable,” it must not be “inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). California’s meal-period requirement conflicts with the FLSA’s framework, which does not mandate meal breaks. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 207 (no meal-period provision); *see also Brigham v. Eugene Water & Elec. Bd.*, 357 F.3d 931, 940 (9th Cir. 2004) (FLSA does not require meal breaks). The Ninth Circuit erred in concluding that California’s law is not inconsistent simply because the FLSA’s savings clause preserves more protective state laws. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 218.

This Court has held that state laws are inconsistent where they “stand[] as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.” *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941). California’s meal-period rule imposes obligations beyond the FLSA’s scope, creating a direct conflict. *See, e.g., Cipriano*, 723 F.3d at 592 (state law inconsistent where it imposes additional obligations).

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that state wage and hour laws apply on the OCS without a gap in federal coverage. The OCSLA requires a “significant void or gap” before state law may be adopted as surrogate federal law, and the FLSA’s comprehensive scheme leaves no such gap. California’s meal-period requirement is also inconsistent with the FLSA. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s judgment.
