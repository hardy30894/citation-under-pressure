**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017). This “arise out of or relate to” requirement is satisfied here.

Ford has continuously and systematically served the Montana market for decades by selling thousands of vehicles to Montana residents, advertising extensively in the state, and maintaining a network of authorized dealers and repair facilities that sell and service Ford parts and vehicles. These contacts are not isolated or sporadic; they reflect Ford’s deliberate decision to “avail[] itself of the privilege of conducting business” in Montana. *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958).

The claims at issue—design defect, failure to warn, and negligence—relate directly to those contacts. The suit concerns an allegedly defective Ford vehicle that caused injury on a Montana highway to a Montana resident. Ford’s Montana activities include precisely the same types of transactions: marketing, selling, and servicing vehicles and replacement parts that are mechanically identical to the Explorer involved in the accident. The relationship between Ford’s forum contacts and the litigation is therefore not “attenuated” or “random.” *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 299 (1980).

Ford’s contrary rule—that jurisdiction exists only when the defendant’s in-state conduct literally caused the plaintiff’s injury—finds no support in this Court’s precedents. Specific jurisdiction has never required but-for causation between each forum contact and the claim. *See Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472-473 (1985) (jurisdiction proper where defendant’s contacts created a “substantial connection” with the forum). Requiring such a strict causal link would permit a manufacturer that floods a state with identical products to escape jurisdiction simply because the particular unit that injured the plaintiff entered the state through another channel. That result would undermine the “fair play and substantial justice” principles that animate the Due Process inquiry. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945).

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford’s extensive, purposeful Montana contacts render the exercise of specific jurisdiction constitutional. The judgment should be affirmed.
