The district court's denial of Riddle's petition for writ of habeas corpus was improper. Riddle's conviction and sentence were tainted by prosecutorial misconduct, as evidenced by Hill's testimony and continued prosecution of the case. This conduct deprived Riddle of a fair trial, as guaranteed by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. See Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 68-69 (1932) (holding that a fair trial is a fundamental right).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a prosecutor's role is not only to seek convictions, but also to ensure that justice is served. Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (stating that a prosecutor's "interest ... in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done"). Hill's actions, in testifying and continuing to prosecute the case, compromised his impartiality and created an appearance of impropriety. See Tumey v. Ohio, 273 U.S. 510, 532 (1927) (holding that a judge's pecuniary interest in a case renders it unfair).

Moreover, Riddle's testimony regarding Hill's alleged statement about seeking the death penalty was crucial to his defense. By rebutting this testimony and continuing to prosecute, Hill effectively vouched for the State's case and undermined Riddle's credibility. See Viereck v. United States, 318 U.S. 236, 248 (1943) (reversing a conviction due to improper prosecutorial comments). The assistant prosecutor's subsequent argument that Riddle was a liar, based on Hill's testimony, further prejudiced the jury. See United States v. Johnson, 319 U.S. 302, 305 (1943) (noting that a prosecutor's statements can be prejudicial if they are not based on evidence).

The district court's grant of summary judgment was also improper, as it denied Riddle an evidentiary hearing on his claims. See Walker v. Johnston, 312 U.S. 275, 285-86 (1941) (holding that a habeas petitioner is entitled to an evidentiary hearing if the record does not conclusively show that they are not entitled to relief). Riddle's allegations of prosecutorial misconduct and ineffective assistance of counsel require a hearing to resolve factual disputes. See Holiday v. Johnston, 313 U.S. 342, 353 (1941) (noting that a habeas court should hold a hearing if the petitioner's allegations are not clearly frivolous).

Furthermore, the district court's dismissal of Riddle's initial petition and subsequent denial of his motion to hold the proceedings in abeyance effectively forced Riddle to file a state habeas petition without the assistance of federally appointed counsel. See Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 117 (1944) (noting that a federal habeas petitioner should be allowed to exhaust state remedies with the assistance of counsel). This prejudiced Riddle's ability to effectively litigate his state habeas claims.

Additionally, the Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of ensuring that a defendant's rights are protected during the habeas process. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 465 (1938) (noting that a habeas petitioner's right to counsel is essential to a fair hearing). The district court's actions here undermined this principle.

The cumulative effect of these errors requires that Riddle's conviction and sentence be vacated. See Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 75-76 (1942) (reversing a conviction due to cumulative error). We urge this Court to reverse the district court's denial of Riddle's habeas petition and remand for an evidentiary hearing. See Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86, 91-92 (1923) (granting habeas relief due to a fundamentally unfair trial).
