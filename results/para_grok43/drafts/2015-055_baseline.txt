I. Johnson Applies Retroactively on Collateral Review.

The rule announced in Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), is a new substantive rule that applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. Under Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), new rules of constitutional law apply retroactively if they are substantive. A rule is substantive when it “narrow[s] the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms” or “place[s] particular conduct or persons covered by the statute beyond the State’s power to punish.” Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 351-52 (2004). Johnson invalidated the ACCA residual clause, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), as unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause. The decision therefore “prohibit[s] a certain category of punishment for a class of defendants because of their status or offense.” Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 330 (1989). After Johnson, no defendant may be sentenced under the residual clause; the clause “is invalid.” Johnson, 576 U.S. at 606.

Because the rule “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes,” it is substantive and must be applied retroactively. Schriro, 542 U.S. at 353; see also Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 620-21 (1998) (holding that a decision narrowing the scope of a federal offense applies retroactively on collateral attack). Procedural rules, by contrast, “regulate only the manner of determining the defendant’s culpability” and do not receive retroactive effect. Schriro, 542 U.S. at 353. Johnson did not alter sentencing procedures; it eliminated an entire category of predicate offenses.

The Eleventh Circuit’s refusal to apply Johnson retroactively therefore contravenes Teague and its progeny. Welch’s 1996 Florida robbery conviction was counted solely under the now-invalid residual clause. Because Johnson is retroactive, that conviction no longer qualifies as an ACCA predicate, and Welch is entitled to resentencing without the statutory minimum.
