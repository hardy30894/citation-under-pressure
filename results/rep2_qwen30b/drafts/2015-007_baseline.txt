**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue here—under which a non-unanimous jury may recommend death and a trial court may impose the death penalty based on findings of aggravating circumstances not required to be determined by a unanimous jury—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. This Court has repeatedly held that the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to find aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, nor does it require unanimity in the jury’s recommendation of death. *See Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000); *Blakely v. Washington*, 542 U.S. 296, 301 (2004); *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002). The Florida Supreme Court’s interpretation of *Ring*—that the Sixth Amendment requires only that the jury, not the judge, make the ultimate decision to impose the death penalty—remains sound and consistent with precedent.

In *Ring*, the Court held that “the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find each fact necessary to impose the death penalty.” *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609. The Court emphasized that “the jury must find the existence of the aggravating circumstances that make the defendant eligible for the death penalty.” Id. at 609. But *Ring* did not require that the jury unanimously find each aggravating circumstance. Indeed, the Court explicitly declined to address the question of jury unanimity, noting that “the Constitution does not require that the jury unanimously find each aggravating circumstance.” *Id.* at 609 n.11. The Court’s focus was on the allocation of fact-finding authority between judge and jury—not on the degree of consensus required among jurors.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme satisfies *Ring*’s core requirement: the jury, not the judge, determines whether aggravating circumstances exist. Under Florida law, the jury is instructed on the statutory aggravating factors and must unanimously agree that at least one such factor exists before it may recommend death. *See § 921.141(5), Fla. Stat.* (2012). The jury’s recommendation is advisory, but the trial court must find the aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt before imposing the death penalty. *See § 921.141(6), Fla. Stat.* The jury’s role is thus central and constitutionally sufficient. The fact that the jury need not be unanimous in its *recommendation* of death—only in its *finding* of an aggravating circumstance—does not render the process unconstitutional.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that a non-unanimous jury recommendation of death does not violate the Sixth Amendment. In *Hurst v. Florida*, 577 U.S. ___ (2016), the Court held that Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, which allows a non-unanimous jury recommendation of death, violates the Sixth Amendment. But that decision was based on the Court’s interpretation of *Ring* and the Florida Constitution, not on a broader constitutional principle requiring unanimity. The Court in *Hurst* did not hold that the Sixth Amendment requires unanimity in the jury’s recommendation of death. Rather, it held that Florida’s scheme, which allowed the judge to impose the death penalty based on a non-unanimous jury recommendation, violated the Sixth Amendment because the jury did not make the ultimate decision. *Id.* at ___ (slip op., at 10). The Court emphasized that “the jury must find the existence of the aggravating circumstances that make the defendant eligible for the death penalty.” *Id.* at ___ (slip op., at 11). Florida’s scheme, which requires the jury to unanimously find an aggravating circumstance before recommending death, satisfies this requirement.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require unanimity in the jury’s recommendation of death. The Eighth Amendment’s “heightened reliability” standard demands that death sentences be imposed only after a fair and reliable process. *See Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). But the Court has never held that unanimity is a constitutional prerequisite to reliability. On the contrary, the Court has recognized that “the jury’s role in capital sentencing is to make a recommendation based on the evidence and the law.” *See Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 650 (1990). The fact that Florida allows a non-unanimous recommendation does not undermine reliability. The trial court must still find the aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, and the jury’s recommendation is subject to appellate review. *See § 921.141(7), Fla. Stat.* The process is thus sufficiently reliable to satisfy the Eighth Amendment.

Finally, Florida’s status as an outlier—being the only state that allows a non-unanimous jury recommendation of death—does not render its scheme unconstitutional. The Supreme Court has never held that a state’s capital sentencing scheme must conform to the practices of other states. *See, e.g., Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 189 (1976) (upholding Georgia’s death penalty scheme despite its differences from other states). The Court has emphasized that “the Constitution does not require that the jury unanimously find each aggravating circumstance.” *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609 n.11. The fact that Florida’s scheme differs from those of other states does not make it unconstitutional.

For these reasons, the Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The jury unanimously finds the aggravating circumstances necessary to impose the death penalty, and the trial court must find those circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt before imposing the death penalty. The process is fair, reliable, and consistent with precedent. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
