**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—lies at the heart of patent law’s balance between incentivizing innovation and ensuring fair compensation. The Federal Circuit’s decision to affirm the award of Samsung’s entire profits, without regard to the componential nature of the infringed design, contravenes the statutory framework of 35 U.S.C. § 289 and this Court’s precedent, which mandates that damages be measured by the value of the patented feature, not the entire product. This Court should reverse.

### I. The Statutory Text of § 289 Requires Component-Based Damages

Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that “whoever, without authority, makes, uses, offers to sell, or sells any patented invention, within the United States… shall be liable to the patentee… for the total profit… made by the offender from the infringement.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. The key phrase—“total profit… made by the offender”—has long been interpreted to mean the profit attributable to the infringing article, not the entire product. In *Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 141, 150 (1989), this Court held that “the damages recoverable under § 289 are limited to the profit attributable to the article that embodies the patented design.” The Court emphasized that “the patentee is not entitled to the entire profit from the sale of the product,” but only “the profit attributable to the patented feature.” Id. at 151. This principle is rooted in the idea that a design patent protects only a particular ornamental feature, not the entire product.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary holding—allowing recovery of the entire profit from a smartphone based on a single design element—ignores this foundational limitation. The design at issue here, the iPhone’s rectangular bezel with rounded corners, is a component of a larger product. It is not the entire smartphone. Yet the jury awarded Apple nearly $300 million based on Samsung’s total smartphone profits. This is not “total profit… made by the offender from the infringement,” as required by § 289, but rather a windfall based on the entire product’s value.

### II. The Federal Circuit’s Decision Contradicts This Court’s Precedent

This Court has repeatedly affirmed that damages under § 289 must be tied to the value of the patented design. In *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 451, 467 (1992), the Court stated that “the measure of damages under § 289 is the profit attributable to the article that embodies the patented design.” The Court further explained that “the patentee is not entitled to the entire profit from the sale of the product,” but only “the profit attributable to the patented feature.” Id. at 467–68. This principle was reaffirmed in *Halo Electronics, Inc. v. Pulse Electronics, Inc.*, 136 S. Ct. 1923, 1930 (2016), where the Court emphasized that “the damages awarded under § 289 must be based on the value of the patented design, not the entire product.”

The Federal Circuit’s decision, however, treats the smartphone as a single unit and awards damages based on its entire profit, regardless of the componential nature of the design. This approach is inconsistent with *Kodak* and *Halo*. The Court in *Kodak* rejected the idea that “the entire profit from the sale of the product” could be recovered when the patent covered only a portion of it. 504 U.S. at 467. The same logic applies here: the design of a bezel, even if prominent, is not the entire smartphone. The Federal Circuit’s refusal to limit damages to the value of the infringing component transforms § 289 into a tool for extracting windfall profits, contrary to the statute’s purpose.

### III. The Federal Circuit’s Error Undermines the Patent System

Allowing recovery of entire product profits for componential design patents creates a perverse incentive. It encourages patentees to assert design patents for minor, non-essential features—such as a bezel or a button shape—and then seek damages based on the entire product’s value. This distorts the patent system by rewarding litigation over innovation. As this Court noted in *Kodak*, “the patentee is not entitled to the entire profit from the sale of the product.” 504 U.S. at 467. To permit otherwise is to allow a patentee to “capture the entire value of the product” based on a “minor, non-essential feature.” Id. at 468.

Moreover, the Federal Circuit’s approach fails to account for the fact that consumers purchase smartphones for their functionality, not their design. The value of the bezel is not the same as the value of the processor, camera, or operating system. Yet the jury’s award ignored this distinction. The Federal Circuit’s refusal to require a component-based damages calculation effectively allows a patentee to “piggyback” on the value of the entire product, even when the patented feature contributes only a fraction of that value.

### IV. The Federal Circuit’s Reliance on “Substantial Evidence” Is Misplaced

The Federal Circuit held that the jury’s award was supported by “substantial evidence,” citing *Consol. Edison Co. v. N.L.R.B.*, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). But that standard applies only to factual findings, not to legal conclusions about the proper measure of damages. The question of whether damages must be limited to the component’s value is a legal issue, not a factual one. *See* *Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc. v. Sandoz, Inc.*, 135 S. Ct. 831, 841 (2015) (holding that claim construction is a legal question reviewed de novo). The Federal Circuit erred in treating the legal question of damages as a factual one subject to deference.
