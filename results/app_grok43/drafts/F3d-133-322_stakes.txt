I. Mrs. Parker’s conduct fell outside the scope of any official duty.

Section 201(b)(2)(C) criminalizes a public official’s acceptance of value “in return for . . . being induced to do or omit to do any act in violation of the official duty of such official.” Mrs. Parker’s duties as a clerk to ALJ Aronson were limited to assisting at hearings, recording notes, typing decisions, and mailing opinions. She possessed no authority to approve SSI benefits or to sign the ALJ’s name. The acts for which she accepted payment—forging approval letters and reinstating benefits—were therefore not acts “which may at any time be pending, or which may by law be brought before any public official, in such official’s official capacity.” 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3).

The Supreme Court has long held that an official’s duty is measured by “settled practice.” United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914). Duties “not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice, and action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the . . . statutes against bribery.” Id. at 231. Mrs. Parker’s forgery and unauthorized reinstatement of benefits were not part of any settled practice of her clerical position; they were criminal acts committed outside the performance of her office. Because the statute reaches only acts that violate an official duty, the indictment failed to state an offense under § 201(b)(2)(C).

II. Mr. Parker’s convictions likewise cannot stand.

Mr. Parker held no public office. Section 201(b)(2) applies exclusively to “public official[s].” The government’s theory that he aided and abetted Mrs. Parker’s violations therefore collapses once her conduct is recognized as falling outside any official duty. See Nye & Nissen v. United States, 336 U.S. 613, 619 (1949) (requiring proof that the defendant “consciously share[d]” in a criminal act that itself violates federal law). The conspiracy and substantive counts against him must be reversed for the same reason.

III. Conclusion

The judgments of conviction should be reversed.
