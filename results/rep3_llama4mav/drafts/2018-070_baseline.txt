The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law as surrogate federal law on the Outer Continental Shelf (OCS) only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law. The Ninth Circuit's contrary holding, which allows state law to apply whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law, is inconsistent with the OCSLA's text and the Fifth Circuit's interpretation of the statute.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the OCS "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. The Supreme Court has not directly addressed the meaning of "applicable" in this context, but the Fifth Circuit has held that state law is "applicable" only when there is a gap or void in federal law. See Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). This interpretation is consistent with the OCSLA's purpose of providing a comprehensive body of law for the OCS.

The Ninth Circuit erred in rejecting the "gap-filling" requirement and holding that state law applies as long as it is "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. See Newton v. Parker Drilling Management Services, Ltd., (9th Cir. opinion excerpt). This interpretation would allow state law to supplant federal law on the OCS, even when federal law provides a comprehensive scheme, like the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA). The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour claims, leaving no room for state law to fill gaps. See 29 U.S.C. § 201, et seq.

The respondent's wage and hour claims under California law are preempted by the FLSA, a federal law that comprehensively regulates the same subject matter. The district court correctly held that the FLSA leaves no gap for state law to fill, and the Ninth Circuit erred in vacating that decision.

The OCSLA's legislative history and the Supreme Court's decision in Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), support the "gap-filling" interpretation. Rodrigue held that state law applies on the OCS when there is no applicable federal law, and the Court's analysis implies that state law is not applicable when federal law provides a comprehensive scheme. See id. at 355-56.

In conclusion, the OCSLA permits the application of state law on the OCS only when there is a gap in federal law. The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour claims, and California's wage and hour laws are therefore preempted. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
