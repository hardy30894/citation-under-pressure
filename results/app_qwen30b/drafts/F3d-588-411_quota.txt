**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s supplemental jury instruction on the term “shielding” violated the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment by rendering the statutory definition unconstitutionally vague and overbroad. The instruction, which adopted a non-statutory, expansive definition of “shielding” drawn from the Seventh Circuit’s own precedent in *United States v. Mendoza*, 523 F.3d 704 (7th Cir. 2008), failed to comport with the constitutional requirement that criminal statutes provide fair notice and prevent arbitrary enforcement. This Court must reverse the conviction on this ground alone, as the instruction effectively transformed a narrowly tailored statutory offense into a de facto criminalization of conduct that is constitutionally protected or at least not clearly proscribed.

The Due Process Clause requires that criminal statutes be sufficiently precise to inform individuals of the conduct that is prohibited. As this Court held in *United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. 285, 292 (2008), “a criminal statute must give fair notice of the conduct that is prohibited.” The term “shielding” in 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) is not self-defining, but the government’s reliance on a broad, non-statutory interpretation—particularly one that equates mere failure to report or document employment with active concealment—fails this test. The instruction given by the district court, which defined “shielding” as “any act that makes it more difficult for the government to detect the presence of an illegal alien,” *Mendoza*, 523 F.3d at 710, is precisely the kind of vague standard that violates due process.

This Court has repeatedly condemned such expansive definitions. In *United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259, 270 (1997), the Supreme Court emphasized that “a criminal statute must provide fair notice of the conduct that is prohibited,” and that “a statute that fails to do so is void for vagueness.” The instruction here, by equating the absence of documentation with active shielding, fails to distinguish between lawful business practices and criminal conduct. The mere failure to maintain I-9 forms, while a civil violation, does not necessarily constitute “shielding” under the statute. As the Supreme Court cautioned in *United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. at 292, “a statute must not leave the public guessing about what conduct is criminal.”

Moreover, the instruction conflated *concealing* and *shielding*, two distinct statutory terms. In *United States v. Mendoza*, the Seventh Circuit itself acknowledged that “shielding” requires more than passive inaction—it involves affirmative conduct that obstructs detection. 523 F.3d at 710. Yet the district court’s instruction failed to convey this distinction, instead treating any act that “makes it more difficult” for the government to detect an illegal alien as sufficient. This is inconsistent with the statutory text and with the principle that “a criminal statute must not be so vague that it invites arbitrary enforcement.” *United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. at 292.

The instruction also failed to incorporate the statutory requirement of *intent* to prevent detection. The Supreme Court has held that “a criminal statute must define the offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited.” *United States v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 344, 352 (2011). The instruction here, by focusing on the *effect* of conduct rather than the *intent* behind it, effectively criminalized negligence or administrative oversight. This is impermissible. In *United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. at 270, the Court rejected a definition that “would criminalize conduct that is not clearly proscribed by the statute.” The instruction here did precisely that.

The Seventh Circuit’s own precedent in *United States v. Mendoza* is not binding authority for the proposition that such a broad definition is constitutional. In *Mendoza*, the court upheld a conviction where the defendant had actively falsified documents and directed employees to lie to agents—conduct that clearly involved intent and affirmative action. 523 F.3d at 710. Here, by contrast, the evidence showed only that Ye failed to maintain I-9 forms, paid in cash, and provided housing. These acts, while potentially violative of civil law, do not rise to the level of “shielding” under a reasonable interpretation of the statute.

Furthermore, the instruction violated the principle of *fair notice* established in *United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. at 292, and *United States v. Brown*, 564 U.S. at 352. The instruction allowed the jury to convict based on conduct that is not clearly criminal under the statute. As the Supreme Court stated in *United States v. Williams*, “a statute must not leave the public guessing about what conduct is criminal.” 553 U.S. at 292. The instruction here did exactly that.

Finally, the instruction failed to incorporate the requirement of *specific intent* to prevent detection. In *United States v. Brown*, 564 U.S. at 352, the Court emphasized that “a criminal statute must define the offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited.” The instruction here, by focusing on the *effect* of conduct rather than the *intent* behind it, effectively criminalized negligence or administrative oversight. This is impermissible.

For these reasons, the district court’s supplemental instruction on “shielding” was constitutionally defective. It failed to provide fair notice, invited arbitrary enforcement, and conflated civil noncompliance with criminal conduct. The conviction must be reversed.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial, or, in the alternative, for entry of a judgment of acquittal. The instruction on “shielding” was unconstitutionally vague and overbroad, violating the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The government’s reliance on a non-statutory, expansive definition of “shielding” cannot withstand constitutional scrutiny.
