ARGUMENT

Arizona’s evidentiary rule confining expert testimony on mental disease to the insanity defense comports with the Due Process Clause. The Clause “does not require a State to adopt any particular form of insanity test.” Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 801 (1952). States retain “wide latitude” to define the elements of crimes and the defenses that may be interposed. See Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 67 (1932); Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934).

The requirement that the prosecution prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt does not compel admission of all evidence that might negate an element. Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895), recognized that the prosecution’s burden may coexist with rules allocating the burden of proving insanity to the accused. Arizona’s scheme does precisely that: the State must prove intent, while the defendant may avoid conviction altogether by establishing legal insanity. The Due Process Clause tolerates such allocation. Leland, 343 U.S. at 799.

Fundamental fairness is measured by whether the procedure “offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). Rules limiting expert testimony to the insanity issue trace to common-law practice and have been repeatedly upheld. See Hotema v. United States, 186 U.S. 413, 417 (1902); Matheson v. United States, 227 U.S. 540, 543 (1913). Such limitations do not render the trial fundamentally unfair.

Nor does the Clause guarantee an unfettered right to introduce any species of evidence. The Court has long held that “the accused [is] not entitled under the Constitution to present any defense he chooses.” See Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 110 (1908); Adamson v. California, 332 U.S. 46, 54 (1947). Evidentiary exclusions that serve legitimate state interests in orderly trials and reliable verdicts are permissible. See Spencer v. Texas, 385 U.S. 554, 563-64 (1967); see also Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455, 462 (1942).

Arizona’s rule advances the State’s interest in preventing the piecemeal dismantling of the mens rea element through evidence whose probative value is already channeled into the comprehensive insanity defense. That interest is substantial and traditional. See Hurtado v. California, 110 U.S. 516, 535 (1884); Mugler v. Kansas, 123 U.S. 623, 661 (1887). Because the rule is neither arbitrary nor fundamentally unfair, it satisfies due process.

The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
