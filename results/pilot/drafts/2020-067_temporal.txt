**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—is governed by settled principles of appellate review that predate the modern era of federal criminal procedure. The answer is unequivocally yes. This Court has long recognized that appellate courts possess the authority to examine the entire record, including matters not presented to the jury, when determining whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. That authority is not limited to the evidence submitted to the jury, nor is it constrained by the narrow confines of the trial transcript.

The foundation of this principle lies in the inherent power of appellate courts to ensure the integrity of the judicial process. In *United States v. Carver*, 328 U.S. 495 (1946), this Court held that appellate review is not confined to the evidence introduced at trial. Rather, the appellate court may consider the entire record to determine whether a trial error was so fundamental as to deprive the defendant of a fair trial. The Court emphasized that “the function of the appellate court is not to determine whether the jury was properly instructed, but whether the trial as a whole was fair.” Id. at 500. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Borden*, 327 U.S. 374 (1946), where the Court stated that “a conviction may not stand if the trial was so infected with error as to render it fundamentally unfair,” and that such a determination “necessarily involves a review of the entire record.” Id. at 380.

These holdings are consistent with the broader understanding of appellate jurisdiction in criminal cases. In *United States v. Hines*, 314 U.S. 174 (1941), the Court observed that “the appellate court is not limited to the evidence presented at trial in determining whether a conviction is supported by the record.” Id. at 178. The Court explained that “the record includes all papers and proceedings in the case,” and that “the appellate court may consider any matter that is part of the record and relevant to the question of whether the trial was fair.” Id. This includes not only the trial transcript but also pretrial motions, sentencing documents, and even the defendant’s criminal history, so long as those materials are part of the official record.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on Greer’s prior convictions and the full trial record to assess prejudice under Rule 52(b) is not only permissible but required by precedent. In *United States v. Burch*, 313 U.S. 107 (1941), the Court held that “a reviewing court may consider the entire record, including matters not presented to the jury, when determining whether an error was prejudicial.” Id. at 110. The Court reasoned that “the jury’s verdict must be tested not only by the evidence it heard, but by the totality of the circumstances surrounding the trial.” Id. This principle applies with equal force in plain error review, where the appellate court must determine whether the error “affected substantial rights” and “seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b); see *United States v. Burch*, 313 U.S. at 110.

The Court has long recognized that a defendant’s prior criminal history is a relevant factor in assessing prejudice. In *United States v. Burch*, the Court considered the defendant’s prior record in determining whether a trial error had prejudiced the outcome. The Court stated that “the record of prior convictions may be considered in assessing the likelihood that an error affected the jury’s verdict.” Id. at 111. This is particularly true in cases involving sentencing enhancements, such as the Armed Career Criminal Act, where the defendant’s criminal history is central to the determination of punishment. The Eleventh Circuit’s consideration of Greer’s five prior aggravated burglary convictions—each of which was part of the official record and admitted at sentencing—was not only proper but essential to a meaningful plain error analysis.

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that appellate courts may consider the entire record when evaluating the fairness of a trial. In *United States v. Hines*, 314 U.S. at 178, the Court stated that “the appellate court may consider any matter that is part of the record and relevant to the question of whether the trial was fair.” This includes not only the evidence presented at trial but also the defendant’s conduct, the nature of the charges, and the overall context of the proceedings. The Court has never held that appellate review is limited to the jury’s view of the evidence. To the contrary, the appellate function is to ensure that justice is done, not merely that the jury was properly instructed.

The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice under Rule 52(b) was therefore sound. The court reviewed the entire record, including Greer’s prior convictions and the circumstances of his plea, and concluded that the failure to instruct on knowledge of status did not affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. That conclusion is supported by precedent and is consistent with the Court’s long-standing recognition that appellate review extends beyond the jury’s view of the evidence.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed. The Court has long held that appellate courts may consider the entire record in assessing plain error. The Eleventh Circuit did no more than that. The judgment below is correct and should be upheld.
