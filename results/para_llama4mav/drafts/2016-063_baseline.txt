ARGUMENT

I. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit Lacks Jurisdiction to Review the Merit Systems Protection Board’s Dismissal of Perry’s Mixed Case for Lack of Jurisdiction.

The Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA) governs judicial review of Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB or Board) decisions. 5 U.S.C. § 1101 et seq. As a general rule, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit has exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from MSPB decisions. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A). However, an exception exists for "mixed cases," which involve both a challenge to an adverse employment action within the MSPB's jurisdiction and an allegation of discrimination prohibited by certain federal statutes. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1); Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41, 44 (2012). In such cases, judicial review lies in federal district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2).

The question presented is whether a mixed case dismissed by the MSPB for lack of jurisdiction falls within the exception to Federal Circuit jurisdiction. The D.C. Circuit, relying on its precedent in Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), held that it does not. However, the Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis effectively overruled Powell and requires that mixed cases dismissed on jurisdictional grounds be reviewed in district court.

In Kloeckner, the Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds, judicial review lies in district court. 568 U.S. at 56. The Court's reasoning applies with equal force to jurisdictional dismissals. The statutory text defines a mixed case as one in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" certain federal laws. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The Court in Kloeckner interpreted this language to mean that a mixed case is one that is "appealable to the MSPB." 568 U.S. at 44. Although the Court specifically addressed procedural dismissals, its reasoning is not limited to that context.

The distinction between jurisdictional and procedural dismissals is not supported by the statutory text or the Kloeckner decision. Both types of dismissals involve cases that are not ultimately "appealable to the MSPB." See Kloeckner, 568 U.S. at 44. Perry argues that the statute's language is equally applicable to appeals dismissed on jurisdictional grounds, and that Kloeckner eviscerates any effort to distinguish between jurisdictional and procedural dismissals.

Furthermore, the Board has discretion to waive procedural requirements, but it lacks authority to hear cases over which it has no jurisdiction. Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 713 F.3d 1111, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013). This distinction does not justify different treatment for jurisdictional and procedural dismissals.

The D.C. Circuit's reliance on Powell is misplaced, as Kloeckner undermines the reasoning in Powell. The Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner requires that mixed cases dismissed on jurisdictional grounds be reviewed in district court, just like those dismissed on procedural grounds.

II. The Merits of Perry’s Discrimination Claim Should Be Reviewed in District Court.

Perry's case involves a mixed claim, as he alleges that the Census Bureau's actions were discriminatory and retaliatory. The MSPB dismissed his appeal for lack of jurisdiction, finding that his retirement and suspension were voluntary. Under Kloeckner, the proper forum for judicial review of the MSPB's dismissal is district court.

The CSRA's statutory scheme contemplates that district courts will review MSPB decisions in mixed cases. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). District courts are better equipped to handle discrimination claims, as they have experience with employment discrimination cases and can consider both the MSPB's decision and the underlying discrimination claim. See Powell, 158 F.3d at 600 (noting that district courts are "more accustomed to handling discrimination claims").

In conclusion, the MSPB's dismissal of Perry's mixed case for lack of jurisdiction should be reviewed in district court, not the Federal Circuit. The Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis requires that mixed cases dismissed on jurisdictional grounds be treated similarly to those dismissed on procedural grounds, and that judicial review lies in district court.
