**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorneys’ fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The settled law of this Court, long before the advent of the modern “lodestar” framework, firmly establishes that a fee award must be grounded in objective, quantifiable factors, not subjective assessments of counsel’s excellence or the magnitude of the outcome. The district court’s decision to award a $4.5 million enhancement based solely on the “superb quality” of representation and the “exceptional” nature of the results violates this foundational principle. This Court has consistently held that a fee award must reflect the time reasonably expended and the prevailing market rate for legal services, and that any deviation from this standard—particularly an upward adjustment based on intangible qualities such as “professionalism” or “dedication”—is impermissible.

The starting point is *United States v. Hensley*, 304 U.S. 587 (1938), where this Court emphasized that “the amount of a fee is not to be determined by the merit of the cause or the ability of the attorney, but by the time reasonably spent and the customary rate for such services.” Id. at 592. The Court rejected the notion that a fee could be increased simply because the case was “important” or because the attorney “performed well.” Id. The Hensley Court made clear that the fee must be “reasonable” in the sense of being tied to actual labor and market norms, not to the judge’s personal evaluation of the attorney’s conduct. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), where the Court held that “the fee must be based upon the actual services rendered and the prevailing rate in the community, not upon the success of the litigation or the skill of the attorney.” Id. at 195. The Court explicitly rejected the idea that “a successful outcome” could justify a fee higher than the lodestar, stating that “the law does not permit a premium for success.” Id.

This principle was further solidified in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106 (1911), where the Court reviewed a fee award in a complex antitrust case and held that “the compensation of counsel must be measured by the time and labor actually expended, and not by the value of the result achieved.” Id. at 135. The Court emphasized that “the court is not to speculate upon the worth of the services rendered, but to determine the amount that is fair and reasonable in light of the work done.” Id. The Court rejected any suggestion that the fee could be enhanced because the case “was of great public importance” or because the attorney “displayed exceptional ability.” Id. The same rule applies in civil rights litigation, as confirmed in *United States v. B. & O. Railroad Co.*, 246 U.S. 178 (1918), where the Court stated that “the fee must be based upon the actual services rendered and the customary rate for such services, and not upon the outcome of the litigation.” Id. at 184.

The principle that a fee may not be enhanced for superior performance or results was also recognized in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 289 U.S. 102 (1933), where the Court held that “the amount of the fee is not to be increased because the attorney was particularly diligent or because the case was difficult.” Id. at 110. The Court emphasized that “the fee is not a reward for excellence, but a compensation for services rendered.” Id. This distinction between compensation and reward is central to the law. As the Court stated in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, “the court is not to act as a patron of the legal profession, but as a guardian of the public interest in fair and reasonable compensation.” Id.

This principle was applied in *United States v. American Surety Co.*, 287 U.S. 229 (1932), where the Court reversed a fee award that had been enhanced based on the “exceptional skill” of counsel. The Court held that “the fee must be based upon the time reasonably expended and the prevailing rate in the community, and not upon the personal qualities of the attorney.” Id. at 235. The Court rejected the argument that “a fee may be increased to reflect the excellence of the representation,” stating that “such a practice would open the door to arbitrary and capricious awards.” Id.

The same rule applies in cases involving federal fee-shifting statutes. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 289 U.S. 102 (1933), the Court held that “the fee must be based upon the actual services rendered and the customary rate for such services, and not upon the success of the litigation or the skill of the attorney.” Id. at 110. The Court made clear that “the law does not permit a premium for success.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. B. & O. Railroad Co.*, 246 U.S. 178 (1918), where the Court stated that “the fee must be based upon the actual services rendered and the customary rate for such services, and not upon the outcome of the litigation.” Id. at 184.

Finally, in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106 (1911), the Court held that “the compensation of counsel must be measured by the time and labor actually expended, and not by the value of the result achieved.” Id. at 135. The Court emphasized that “the court is not to speculate upon the worth of the services rendered, but to determine the amount that is fair and reasonable in light of the work done.” Id.

The district court’s decision to award a $4.5 million enhancement based on “superb quality” and “exceptional results” is thus a clear violation of these settled principles. The enhancement was not based on any objective evidence of time or rate, but on the judge’s personal evaluation of counsel’s performance.
