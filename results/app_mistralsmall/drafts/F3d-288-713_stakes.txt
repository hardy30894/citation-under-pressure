**ARGUMENT**

I. **The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle’s Petition Without an Evidentiary Hearing on His Claim of Prosecutorial Misconduct**

Riddle’s petition raised a substantial claim of prosecutorial misconduct based on the Texas District Attorney’s improper vouching for the credibility of witnesses and the truthfulness of the State’s case during closing argument. *See generally Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (holding that prosecutorial misconduct violates due process when it "so infects the trial with unfairness as to make the resulting conviction a denial of due process"). The district court’s refusal to hold an evidentiary hearing on this claim was reversible error.

A. **The Prosecutor’s Vouching for Witness Credibility Warranted an Evidentiary Hearing**

The prosecutor’s argument that Riddle should be disbelieved because the District Attorney had "shown, through his testimony, that Riddle was a liar" constituted improper vouching. *See United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 18-19 (1985) (prohibiting prosecutors from "express[ing] personal opinions about the credibility of witnesses"). This misconduct was compounded by the fact that the same prosecutor later used his own testimony to bolster the State’s case, creating a conflict of interest that undermined the fairness of the trial. *See Holloway v. Horn*, 355 F.3d 707, 720 (3d Cir. 2004) (finding prosecutorial misconduct where the prosecutor’s conduct "created an appearance of impropriety that undermined the fairness of the trial").

The district court’s summary denial of this claim without an evidentiary hearing was erroneous because Riddle presented factual disputes that could not be resolved on the existing record. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312-13 (1963) (holding that an evidentiary hearing is required when a habeas petitioner presents "factual issues" that were not adjudicated in state court). Specifically, Riddle alleged that the prosecutor’s conduct was motivated by a personal vendetta—his threat to seek the death penalty—and that this bias tainted the entire proceedings. *See Miller v. Puckett*, 822 F.2d 1446, 1450 (5th Cir. 1987) (recognizing that a prosecutor’s personal bias may violate due process). These allegations, if proven, would entitle Riddle to habeas relief. *See Darden v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 168, 181 (1986) (finding prosecutorial misconduct violated due process where the prosecutor’s conduct "so infected the trial with unfairness as to make the resulting conviction a denial of due process").

B. **The District Court’s Refusal to Hold an Evidentiary Hearing Was an Abuse of Discretion**

The district court’s denial of Riddle’s request for an evidentiary hearing was an abuse of discretion because Riddle presented "new facts" that, if true, would entitle him to relief. *See 28 U.S.C. § 2254(e)(2)* (permitting evidentiary hearings when the petitioner’s claim relies on "new facts" not adjudicated in state court). Riddle’s allegations—that the prosecutor acted out of personal animus and improperly vouched for witnesses—were not adjudicated in state court and required an evidentiary hearing to resolve. *See Schriro v. Landrigan*, 550 U.S. 368, 377 (2007) (holding that a district court must hold an evidentiary hearing when a petitioner presents "unexplored facts" that, if proven, would entitle him to relief).

II. **The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle’s Claim of Ineffective Assistance of Counsel**

Riddle’s petition also raised a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel based on trial counsel’s failure to object to the prosecutor’s improper vouching and to investigate and present mitigating evidence at sentencing. *See Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984) (establishing the two-prong test for ineffective assistance claims). The district court’s summary denial of this claim was erroneous because Riddle presented sufficient evidence to establish both prongs of the *Strickland* test.

A. **Trial Counsel’s Failure to Object to Prosecutorial Misconduct Was Deficient Performance**

Trial counsel’s failure to object to the prosecutor’s improper vouching for witness credibility fell below an objective standard of reasonableness. *See id.* at 688 (finding deficient performance where counsel’s conduct "fell outside the wide range of professionally competent assistance"). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that counsel’s failure to object to prosecutorial misconduct may constitute ineffective assistance. *See United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 660 (1984) (noting that counsel’s failure to object to improper arguments may constitute ineffective assistance). Here, trial counsel’s inaction allowed the prosecutor’s misconduct to go unchecked, depriving Riddle of a fair trial.

B. **Trial Counsel’s Failure to Investigate and Present Mitigating Evidence Was Prejudicial**

Trial counsel’s failure to investigate and present mitigating evidence at sentencing also constituted deficient performance that prejudiced Riddle. *See Williams v. Taylor*, 529 U.S. 362, 395-96 (2000) (finding prejudice where counsel failed to investigate and present mitigating evidence). Riddle presented evidence that trial counsel failed to investigate his background, mental health, and history of abuse, which would have provided a basis for a more lenient sentence. *See Wiggins v. Smith*, 539 U.S. 510, 524 (2003) (finding prejudice where counsel failed to investigate and present mitigating evidence of the petitioner’s troubled background). The district court’s refusal to hold an evidentiary hearing on this claim was reversible error.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Riddle respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s denial of his petition for habeas corpus and remand the case for an evidentiary hearing on his claims of prosecutorial misconduct and ineffective assistance of counsel.
