**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional principle: the government may not imprison an individual without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. The Fifth Circuit’s initial conclusion that indefinite detention under § 1231(a)(6) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) is lawful so long as the government makes “good faith efforts” to deport the alien is irreconcilable with the Constitution and this Court’s holding in *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S.Ct. 2491, 150 L.Ed.2d 653 (2001). That decision, which the Fifth Circuit now acknowledges is controlling, establishes that post-removal-period detention is not authorized beyond a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

The Fifth Circuit’s prior opinion rested on a standard that demands more than the Constitution permits: it required Zadvydas to prove that deportation is “impossible,” not merely that it is not reasonably foreseeable. This misapprehends the holding in *Zadvydas*. The Court made clear that “the statute [§ 1231(a)(6)], read in light of the Constitution’s demands, limits an alien’s post-removal-period detention to a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien’s removal.” Id. at 2498. The Court did not require an alien to demonstrate impossibility; rather, it placed the burden on the government to show that removal remains reasonably foreseeable. As the Court explained, “if removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the court should hold continued detention unreasonable and no longer authorized by statute.” Id. at 2504.

Here, Zadvydas has been in custody for over three years beyond the 90-day removal period. He has been detained not because he poses a threat to the community or is likely to flee—indeed, the INS has never alleged such a risk—but because no country will accept him. Germany and Lithuania have both declined to repatriate him, and no other country has been identified as willing to receive him. The INS has made no meaningful effort to secure a third-country agreement, and no such agreement is in sight. Under these circumstances, the government has failed to meet its burden of demonstrating that removal is reasonably foreseeable.

The Court in *Zadvydas* recognized that “the period of prior post-removal confinement grows,” the “reasonably foreseeable future” must shrink. Id. at 2505. The Court established a presumptively reasonable period of six months after the removal period, after which the government must respond to a showing of no significant likelihood of removal with evidence rebutting that showing. Id. Zadvydas has now been detained for more than three years beyond that period. The government has not presented any evidence that removal is imminent or even plausible. To the contrary, the record reflects that the INS has exhausted all known avenues for deportation. The Court’s own observation that Zadvydas’s “Lithuanian citizenship reapplication is apparently still pending” does not constitute a basis for continued detention. *Id.* at 2496. A pending application, without more, cannot justify indefinite confinement.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 73 S.Ct. 625, 97 L.Ed. 956 (1953), is misplaced. *Mezei* involved an alien denied entry at the border, whose detention was justified by national security concerns and the government’s authority to exclude. *Zadvydas*, by contrast, is a long-term resident alien who has been ordered removed after a final administrative and judicial determination. His detention is not for exclusion but for removal. The Court in *Zadvydas* explicitly rejected the applicability of *Mezei*, noting that it “dealt with aliens who had been stopped at the border, and had not effected entry into the United States.” 121 S.Ct. at 2500. The distinction is critical: *Mezei* did not involve a resident alien subject to a final removal order, nor did it address the due process limits on post-removal detention.

Moreover, the government’s argument that detention is justified because Zadvydas is removable under § 1227(a)(2) for an aggravated felony is unavailing. The fact that an alien is removable does not justify indefinite detention. The INA authorizes detention only to secure removal, not as punishment or for preventive purposes. The Court in *Zadvydas* made clear that “the choice, however, is not between imprisonment and the alien ‘living at large’... It is between imprisonment and supervision under release conditions that may not be violated.” Id. at 2502. The government has not shown that supervision is inadequate. Indeed, the district court imposed conditions—including regular reporting, employment, residence, and a $2,000 bond—that are reasonable and enforceable. The INS has not challenged these conditions, nor has it alleged any violation.

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that Zadvydas “has not shown that deportation will prove impossible” misapplies the standard. The Constitution does not require an alien to prove impossibility; it requires the government to show reasonableness. When the government cannot demonstrate that removal is reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by statute and violates due process.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Fifth Circuit must be reversed. The district court correctly concluded that Zadvydas’s continued detention beyond a reasonable period is unconstitutional. The Court should hold that once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the government may not detain the alien, even under § 1231(a)(6). The judgment of the district court ordering Zadvydas’s release, conditioned on compliance with reasonable supervision, is affirmed.
