# ARGUMENT

## THE RULE ANNOUNCED IN JOHNSON V. UNITED STATES APPLIES RETROACTIVELY ON COLLATERAL REVIEW

Petitioner is serving a fifteen-year mandatory minimum sentence that rests, in part, on a constitutional provision this Court has since held void for vagueness. Because that defect strikes at the government's very authority to impose the enhanced sentence—rather than at the fairness of the procedure used to impose it—the rule of Johnson v. United States applies retroactively to petitioner's case.

### A. Retroactivity Turns on the Nature of the Rule and Its Relationship to the Legitimacy of the Underlying Sentence.

This Court has never treated retroactivity as governed by a single mechanical formula. Instead, "the Constitution neither prohibits nor requires retrospective effect," and the answer depends on weighing "the merits and demerits in each case." Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618, 629 (1965). The framework this Court developed for that weighing examines "(a) the purpose to be served by the new standards, (b) the extent of the reliance by law enforcement authorities on the old standards, and (c) the effect on the administration of justice of a retroactive application of the new standards." Stovall v. Denno, 388 U.S. 293, 297 (1967); accord Johnson v. New Jersey, 384 U.S. 719, 728–29 (1966); Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott, 382 U.S. 406, 413–17 (1966).

That framework has always distinguished rules that merely refine trial procedure from rules that redefine the State's very authority to impose punishment. As Justice Harlan explained, new rules altering the substance of what conduct or status the State may punish stand on different footing than rules regulating how guilt is adjudicated, because the former implicate the government's power to punish at all, not merely the fairness of the process used to convict. Desist v. United States, 394 U.S. 244, 256–69 (1969) (Harlan, J., dissenting). This distinction reflects the historic office of the writ of habeas corpus, which exists to remedy fundamental defects in a prisoner's detention wherever they surface, because "conventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake and infringement of constitutional rights is alleged." Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 424 (1963).

This principle is not new to this Court. In Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), the Court held that a prisoner convicted under an unconstitutional statute was entitled to release on habeas corpus, explaining that "[a]n unconstitutional law is void, and is as no law. An offence created by it is not a crime." Id. at 376. The judgment of conviction, the Court held, was rendered by a court "without jurisdiction" to impose it. Id. at 377. Similarly, in United States v. Reese, 92 U.S. 214 (1875), the Court refused to let a criminal statute be salvaged by after-the-fact judicial narrowing, warning that "[i]t would certainly be dangerous if the legislature could set a net large enough to catch all possible offenders, and leave it to the courts to step inside and say who could be rightfully detained, and who should be set at large." Id. at 221. Both decisions rest on the insight this case requires the Court to apply: a punishment imposed without lawful authority cannot stand merely because the judgment became final before the constitutional defect was recognized.

### B. Johnson's Invalidation of the Residual Clause Is a Substantive Rule Narrowing the Category of Predicate Offenses.

Johnson v. United States held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act was unconstitutionally vague because it required courts to assess "the hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version" of an offense—an inquiry so unpredictable that it denied fair notice of the conduct being punished. That holding is a direct application of this Court's longstanding vagueness doctrine: "a statute which either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application, violates the first essential of due process of law." Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). As the Court has repeatedly emphasized, "[n]o one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes." Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939); see also United States v. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921) (invalidating a statute that failed to provide "an ascertainable standard of guilt").

Johnson's holding is therefore substantive, not procedural. It did not merely improve the method for determining whether a given offense qualifies as a "violent felony"—it eliminated an entire category of predicate offenses from the Act's reach because the standard defining that category was constitutionally void from the outset. A sentence enhanced under the residual clause is, after Johnson, indistinguishable from the void convictions condemned in Ex parte Siebold: the sentencing court lacked lawful authority to impose the enhanced punishment, regardless of when the vagueness defect was judicially recognized. See 100 U.S. at 376–77.

### C. The Traditional Factors Confirm Retroactive Application.

Even under the balancing approach of Linkletter, Stovall, and Tehan, retroactivity is warranted. The purpose of the Johnson rule is to prevent imprisonment under a standard that fails to give "men of common intelligence" fair notice of its reach, Connally, 269 U.S. at 391—a purpose implicating the legitimacy of the sentence itself, not merely the accuracy of fact-finding. Any reliance interest in the residual clause is minimal, since the clause is now understood never to have supplied a workable standard at all. And the interest in the "administration of justice," Tehan, 382 U.S. at 417, favors correcting sentences imposed under a constitutionally void provision rather than permitting prisoners to remain confined beyond the term the law actually authorizes. As this Court recognized in Fay v. Noia, finality interests must yield when "life or liberty is at stake and infringement of constitutional rights is alleged." 372 U.S. at 424.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the rule announced in Johnson v. United States applies retroactively to cases on collateral review, including petitioner's.
