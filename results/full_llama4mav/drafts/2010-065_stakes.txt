The petitioner, Carol Anne Bond, lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on the grounds that the statute exceeds the federal government's enumerated powers and is inconsistent with the Tenth Amendment. The Tenth Amendment challenge is foreclosed by the Supreme Court's decision in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), which held that private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims.

In Tennessee Electric Power Co., the Court dismissed a Tenth Amendment challenge brought by private utility companies, stating that "absent the states or their officers, [the utility companies] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]." Id. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366. This holding has been followed by several circuit courts, including the First, Second, Eighth, Ninth, and Tenth Circuits. See Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir.2005); Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir.2006); United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-527 (8th Cir.2009); Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir.2009); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir.2004).

The petitioner attempts to distinguish Tennessee Electric Power Co. by characterizing the language on standing as "textbook dicta." However, the Court's holding on standing is an alternative ground for its decision, and thus is not dicta. See Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 34. Moreover, the standing question in Tennessee Electric Power Co. concerned the constitutional jurisdiction of a federal court, and its resolution predominates any discussion of the merits. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95 (1998).

The petitioner's challenge to the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229 is therefore barred by her lack of standing to raise a Tenth Amendment claim. The judgment of the Third Circuit Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
