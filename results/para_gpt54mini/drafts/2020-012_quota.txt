**ARGUMENT**

I. A Board decision declining to reopen a prior benefits claim is not a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f).

Section 355(f) authorizes review only of “final decisions” of the Railroad Retirement Board after administrative remedies have been exhausted. But the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that an agency’s refusal to reopen a prior, finally adjudicated claim is ordinarily not a reviewable merits decision. In *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 107-09 (1977), the Court held that the Social Security Act does not confer jurisdiction to review the agency’s denial of reopening because “[c]onvincing reasons” counsel against treating such discretionary refusals as final decisions on the merits. The Court explained that reopening rules do not create a freestanding entitlement to judicial review and that allowing review of such denials would undermine finality. *Id.* at 108.

The same reasoning applies here. The Railroad Retirement Act incorporates a comparable finality structure, and the statutory phrase “final decisions” does not naturally encompass an agency’s refusal to revisit a concluded claim. As this Court held in *Roberts v. U.S. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139, 140-41 (5th Cir. 2003), “we have no jurisdiction [under section 355(f)] to review the Board’s decision not to reopen a prior claim for benefits.” *Roberts* relied on *Califano* and joined the “majority of circuits” in concluding that reopening denials are not reviewable final decisions. *Id.* at 141.

That rule is consistent with the text and structure of § 355(f). A reopening request asks the Board to exercise discretion to revisit a completed adjudication; it does not seek review of the original merits decision through the ordinary statutory review path. The Fifth Circuit correctly recognized that Salinas’s 2006 claim became final when he declined to pursue further administrative review. Once finality attached, the later refusal to reopen did not itself become a new final decision on the merits. See *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 140-41.

II. Supreme Court and circuit precedent uniformly support the conclusion that reopening denials are generally unreviewable.

The majority of circuits have followed *Califano* in rejecting judicial review of reopening denials under analogous railroad benefit statutes. The Fourth Circuit held in *Harris v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 198 F.3d 139, 142 (4th Cir. 1999), that a refusal to reopen a prior claim is not a final decision within the meaning of § 355(f). The Sixth Circuit reached the same result in *Gutierrez v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 918 F.2d 567, 570 (6th Cir. 1990), concluding that Congress did not authorize review of reopening decisions. The Seventh Circuit likewise held in *Steebe v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 708 F.2d 250, 254-55 (7th Cir. 1983), that reopening denials are not independently reviewable. And the Tenth Circuit in *Abbruzzese v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 63 F.3d 972, 974 (10th Cir. 1995), held that the statutory review provision does not extend to the Board’s refusal to reopen. This Court expressly found those decisions persuasive in *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 141.

The Fifth Circuit’s own precedents are controlling here. Under the rule of orderliness, one panel cannot overrule another. *Vaughan v. Anderson Regional Medical Center*, 849 F.3d 588, 591 (5th Cir.), cert. denied, 138 S. Ct. 101 (2017). Thus, whatever arguments Salinas may raise against *Roberts*, this panel is bound to apply it. See also *Crump v. Board of Trustees of Jackson Municipal Separate School District*, 797 F.3d 332, 339 (5th Cir. 2015) (describing the rule of orderliness as requiring adherence to prior panel decisions absent en banc or Supreme Court intervention). The Fifth Circuit faithfully applied that principle below in dismissing Salinas’s petition.

III. The minority view does not control, and it cannot displace binding Fifth Circuit precedent.

Salinas may invoke the D.C. Circuit’s contrary decision in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500, 502-04 (D.C. Cir. 2016), which held that the Railroad Retirement Act permits review of reopening denials. But *Stovic* is not controlling in this Circuit, and it expressly disagreed with *Roberts*. *Id.* at 504. The existence of a circuit split does not authorize this Court to disregard binding precedent. *Vaughan*, 849 F.3d at 591.

Nor does the earlier Eighth Circuit decision in *Sones v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991), alter the analysis. *Roberts* acknowledged that split and nonetheless adopted the majority rule, which remains the law of this Circuit. *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 141.

IV. Salinas’s petition seeks review of a discretionary reopening refusal, not a reviewable final merits decision.

Salinas’s 2006 claim was finally denied, and he allowed that denial to become final by failing to pursue further administrative review. His later request to reopen was untimely under the Board’s reopening regulations. The hearing officer’s refusal to reopen therefore addressed only whether the Board should exercise its limited authority to revisit a stale claim. That is precisely the kind of discretionary, collateral agency action that *Califano* held is not ordinarily reviewable, 430 U.S. at 108-09, and that *Roberts* held is not a “final decision” under § 355(f), 346 F.3d at 140-41.

Because this Court is bound by *Roberts*, and because the Supreme Court’s decision in *Califano* supplies the governing principle, Salinas’s petition was properly dismissed.
