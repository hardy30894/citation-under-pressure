No. Under the lodestar method, a reasonable fee under a federal fee-shifting statute is presumed to be fully compensatory, and the Court has rejected enhancements based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897-901 (1984); *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 565-68 (1986). The Court has explained that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” and that it is “unnecessary to enhance the fee for superior performance.” *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 566, 106 S. Ct. at 3098-99.

The Court’s precedents do not leave room for an enhancement here. In *Blum*, the Court held that “quality of representation” and “results obtained” ordinarily are subsumed in the lodestar and cannot justify an increase absent “specific evidence” that the lodestar does not adequately reflect those considerations. 465 U.S. at 898-901. The Court rejected an enhancement where the fee applicant had not shown that the hourly rate and hours reasonably expended failed to account for counsel’s performance. *Id.* at 899-900. Likewise, *Delaware Valley I* held that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” 478 U.S. at 566, and that upward adjustments remain limited to rare circumstances not established by mere excellence in advocacy. *Id.* at 565-68.

Later decisions confirm that the lodestar is the “centerpiece” of fee-shifting analysis and that enhancements are disfavored when the claimed factor is already built into the market rate. *City of Burlington v. Dague*, 505 U.S. 557, 562-67 (1992); *Missouri v. Jenkins*, 491 U.S. 274, 285-89 (1989). In *Dague*, the Court refused to permit risk-based enhancements because the lodestar already captures the factors relevant to a reasonable fee, and because fee-shifting statutes aim to produce a market-equivalent award, not a premium for success. 505 U.S. at 562-67. *Jenkins* likewise treated the lodestar as the principal mechanism for producing a reasonable fee and approved adjustments only where they compensated for attorney work not otherwise reflected in the hourly rate. 491 U.S. at 285-89.

Petitioner’s position would convert an “enhancement” into a second award for the same attributes that the district court already compensated through a high hourly rate. That is precisely what *Blum* forbids. 465 U.S. at 898-900. Fee-shifting statutes do not authorize paying more simply because counsel performed well or obtained a strong result; they authorize a “reasonable” fee. *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433-37 (1983). And where the lodestar already reflects the quality of counsel’s work, a further multiplier creates impermissible double counting. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901; *Dague*, 505 U.S. at 562-67.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary approach cannot be squared with this Court’s cases. Although courts of appeals have sometimes spoken in broad terms about exceptional performance, those decisions cannot override the Supreme Court’s instruction that the lodestar is presumed reasonable and enhancements for superior performance are ordinarily unnecessary. *Norman v. Housing Authority of City of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292, 1302 (11th Cir. 1988); *NAACP v. City of Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332, 1336-37 (11th Cir. 1987) (per curiam); *Shipes v. Trinity Industries*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993); *Forshee v. Waterloo Industries, Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); *Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004). To the extent those cases suggested that results alone can justify a multiplier, they conflict with *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I*.

This Court should make clear that a federal fee-shifting statute does not permit enhancing a reasonable fee award based solely on quality of performance and results obtained. The lodestar already accounts for those considerations through reasonable hours and reasonable rates. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901; *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 434-37. Because the district court here enhanced the fee only on that basis, the enhancement was improper and should be reversed.
