**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether Guam or the Navy is financially responsible for the environmental hazards arising from the Ordot Dump—turns on a single, dispositive legal issue: whether Guam’s 2004 Consent Decree with the EPA triggered its right to seek contribution under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby precluding it from pursuing a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The D.C. Circuit correctly concluded that it did. The district court erred in holding otherwise, and its decision conflicts with binding precedent and the plain text of CERCLA. We respectfully request that this Court reverse.

### I. The 2004 Consent Decree “Resolved” Guam’s Liability for a Response Action, Triggering § 113(f)(3)(B)

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who has “resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” may seek contribution from other potentially responsible parties (PRPs). 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The statute of limitations for such contribution claims is three years from the date of the settlement. Id. § 9613(g)(3). This provision is not a mere procedural rule—it is a substantive limitation on the availability of cost-recovery claims. Once a party settles with the government, it may no longer pursue a § 107(a) claim for the same response costs; instead, it must proceed under § 113(f)(3)(B), if at all.

The D.C. Circuit correctly held that the 2004 Consent Decree “resolved” Guam’s liability for the Ordot Dump cleanup. The Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the landfill, and design and install a cover system—precisely the types of actions that constitute “response actions” under CERCLA. See 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24) (defining “remedial action” as “actions consistent with permanent remedy” and including “collection of leachate and runoff”). The Decree was “judicially approved,” binding on both parties, and entered without any admission of liability. But as this Court has made clear, “the mere absence of an admission of liability does not prevent a settlement from qualifying as a ‘resolution’ under § 113(f)(3)(B).” *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 168 (2004). What matters is not the label, but the substance: “a settlement that imposes obligations to perform cleanup actions and pays for them is a resolution of liability for purposes of contribution.” *Asarco Inc. v. United States*, 866 F.3d 1118, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017).

The district court erred in focusing on the Decree’s disclaimer of liability—“without any finding or admission of liability”—as dispositive. That language, while present, is standard in consent decrees and does not negate the fact that Guam assumed full responsibility for the cleanup. As the D.C. Circuit correctly observed, “parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability.” *Asarco*, 866 F.3d at 1123. Indeed, the Supreme Court has held that “a party may settle its liability without admitting fault.” *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 60 (1998). The fact that Guam did not admit fault is irrelevant. What matters is that it agreed to perform a response action and pay for it—exactly the kind of conduct that triggers § 113(f)(3)(B).

### II. The District Court’s Interpretation of “Resolved” Contradicts CERCLA’s Structure and Purpose

The district court’s contrary conclusion—that the Decree did not “resolve” liability because it did not “admit” fault—would render § 113(f)(3)(B) meaningless. If a party could avoid the three-year statute of limitations simply by refusing to admit liability in a settlement, then Congress’s carefully crafted balance between encouraging settlements and preserving contribution rights would collapse. As this Court has warned, “Congress could not have intended such a result.” *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 170.

Moreover, the district court’s approach creates a perverse incentive: parties could delay litigation, settle without admitting fault, and then bring a § 107(a) claim years later—after the three-year window for contribution has closed. That is precisely what the D.C. Circuit rejected. The Court held that “the practical effect of allowing such a claim would be to permit a party to circumvent the statute of limitations by using a settlement to avoid liability while retaining the right to sue under § 107(a).” *Guam v. United States*, 957 F.3d 277, 288 (D.C. Cir. 2020). That cannot be the law.

### III. Guam’s Due Process and Property Interest Arguments Are Foreclosed

Guam argues that denying it the right to pursue a § 107(a) claim violates due process because it extinguishes a “property interest” in contribution. But this argument is both legally and procedurally flawed. First, Guam failed to raise it in the district court. *Keepseagle v. Vilsack*, 815 F.3d 28, 36 (D.C. Cir. 2016). Issues not raised below are forfeited. Second, even if preserved, the argument fails on the merits. The right to contribution is not a property interest that cannot be extinguished by statute. *See United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property*, 510 U.S. 43, 55 (1993) (statutes may limit or extinguish property rights). The three-year statute of limitations under § 113(g)(3) is a valid exercise of Congress’s authority. Guam’s claim expired in 2007—six years before it filed suit.
