# ARGUMENT

## THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE DOES NOT BAR RETRIAL OF A PRIOR-CONVICTION ALLEGATION IN A NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDING

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." This guarantee, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784 (1969), "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). A proceeding to determine whether a defendant suffered a prior conviction for purposes of sentence enhancement is neither a "prosecution" nor does it place the defendant twice in jeopardy for "the same offence." It is a step in the process of fixing punishment for the offense presently charged and proved. Because the prior-conviction proceeding is sentencing, not adjudication of guilt, the Double Jeopardy Clause has no application.

### A. Recidivist Proceedings Punish the Present Offense, Not the Prior One

This Court has long recognized that statutes enhancing punishment because of a defendant's prior convictions do not create a new offense or impose a second punishment for the earlier crime; they enhance the punishment for the crime currently being punished, in light of the defendant's demonstrated persistence in criminal conduct. *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616 (1912); *McDonald v. Massachusetts*, 180 U.S. 311 (1901). The prior conviction functions as a fact bearing on the appropriate sentence for the *present* felony—precisely as a defendant's age, employment history, or other background facts historically have informed sentencing determinations. Because no "offence" is retried when a court determines the truth of a sentencing allegation, the constitutional predicate for a double jeopardy claim is absent.

### B. Sentencing Determinations Are Categorically Different From Trials on Guilt

This Court's decision in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), confirms that the procedural architecture of a trial on guilt does not extend to sentencing. There, the Court upheld a death sentence imposed after the judge considered unadjudicated allegations that the defendant had committed thirty burglaries never proved at trial, holding that "[t]he due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure." *Id.* at 251. The Court explained that the procedural safeguards attending a trial on guilt—confrontation, cross-examination, and the rest—"traditionally" have not applied at sentencing. *Id.* at 245–46. *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605 (1967), later described *Williams* as holding that due process does not require a hearing at all before a court determines a sentence. *Id.* at 606. If due process itself does not compel a trial-type hearing on facts relevant to punishment, a fortiori the Double Jeopardy Clause—confined by its text to successive jeopardy for "the same offence"—cannot be read to impose the far more exacting bar against retrial that this Court has reserved for adjudications of guilt or innocence.

*Specht* is not to the contrary. There the Court required trial-type protections because Colorado's sex-offender statute effectively charged the defendant with a new substantive offense carrying separate criminal consequences. *Id.* at 608–610. No such transformation occurs here. The prior-conviction allegation does not charge a new crime; it identifies a status—the fact of an earlier conviction—relevant only to the sentence for the offense already adjudicated by the jury's guilty verdict on the substantive drug counts.

### C. This Court Has Already Permitted Repeated Sentencing Determinations Without Double Jeopardy Bar

*Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), is directly instructive. After the defendant's first conviction of murder "without capital punishment" was reversed, a second jury convicted him without that qualification, and he was sentenced to death. This Court found no double jeopardy violation, because the second proceeding did not place the defendant in jeopardy for a different offense; it merely redetermined, upon retrial of the same charge, the punishment to be imposed. *Id.* at 18. If a defendant may be exposed to a *harsher* sentence, including death, upon retrial of the very charge that generated the first sentence, a fortiori the Clause does not bar a second attempt to prove a discrete sentencing fact—a prior conviction—that never was, and never purported to be, an "offence" subject to jeopardy in the first instance.

*Pearce* confirms the point in the noncapital context most relevant here. This Court held that "neither the double jeopardy provision nor the Equal Protection Clause imposes an absolute bar to a more severe sentence upon reconviction." 395 U.S. at 723. If the Double Jeopardy Clause tolerates an increased sentence upon reconviction of the underlying offense, it necessarily tolerates a second opportunity to prove a sentencing enhancement fact where, as here, the prosecution's first attempt failed only for an evidentiary deficiency correctable on retrial—not because the fact was ever found untrue after full and fair litigation resembling a trial on guilt.

### D. Conclusion

The prior-conviction proceeding here bears none of the "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence" that would trigger double jeopardy protection. It is sentencing, governed by *Williams*, not adjudication of a second "offence" within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment. The judgment of the California Supreme Court permitting retrial of the prior-conviction allegation should be affirmed.
