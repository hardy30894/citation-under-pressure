**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) may collect unpaid partnership tax liabilities from individual partners in bankruptcy proceedings without first making individual assessments against those partners. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that it may not. The IRS’s claim is barred by the plain language of the Internal Revenue Code, settled precedent, and the fundamental principle that a taxpayer’s liability cannot be enforced without a valid assessment. We respectfully submit that the judgment below should be affirmed.

### I. THE IRS MUST INDIVIDUALLY ASSESS EACH PARTNER TO COLLECT FROM THEM; THE PARTNERSHIP ASSESSMENT DOES NOT EXTEND THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS FOR INDIVIDUALS

The statute of limitations for assessing taxes is strictly construed. Under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), the IRS must assess a tax against a taxpayer within three years after the return is filed. If it fails to do so, the government loses its right to assess that taxpayer. The IRS may not circumvent this requirement by relying on an assessment made against a partnership. The Code treats the partnership and its individual partners as separate taxpayers. See 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14) (defining “person” and “taxpayer” to include both partnerships and individuals). Each partner has a distinct taxpayer identification number and is subject to separate assessment under § 6203. The regulation implementing § 6203 requires that an assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1.

The IRS’s argument—that a partnership assessment automatically extends the limitations period for individual partners—contradicts both statutory text and precedent. Section 6502(a) permits collection only “if the levy is made or the proceeding begun—within 10 years after the assessment of the tax.” The statute refers to “the assessment of the tax,” not “an assessment of a partnership.” The plain meaning of “the assessment” is the assessment made against the taxpayer whose liability is being enforced. In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), we held that a tax lien against a partner’s property could not be enforced absent a valid assessment against that partner. The district court had correctly ruled that “the Government does not have any lien” against a partner’s property unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him. Id. at 458. We affirmed on other grounds, but the district court’s reasoning—that a valid assessment is required—remains sound and binding. The IRS’s failure to assess the Gallettis and Briguglios individually means that the three-year statute of limitations for them has expired.

The IRS attempts to distinguish *Coson* on the ground that no lien was at issue here. But the asserted right to collect in bankruptcy is no different in substance. A proof of claim in bankruptcy is a claim for a “right to payment,” 11 U.S.C. § 101(5)(A), and such a claim is unenforceable if the underlying tax liability has not been properly assessed. The bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the IRS’s claim because it was “unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor,” 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). The IRS’s claim is not merely unliquidated or contingent—it is barred by the statute of limitations.

### II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY REJECTED THE IRS’S STATE-LAW THEORY

The IRS also argues that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, and that this allows it to collect from individual partners without a separate assessment. This argument fails for the same reason: state law does not override federal procedural requirements.

California Civil Code § 16307(c) provides that “a judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner.” To collect from a partner’s personal assets, a creditor must obtain a separate judgment against the partner. *See also* 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001) (“[A] judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.”). The IRS has not obtained such a judgment. It has not even initiated a proceeding against the partners. The mere fact that partners are liable under state law does not eliminate the need for a valid federal assessment or a judgment.

The IRS cites *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), to support its position. But *Wright* is inapposite. In that case, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. The assessments were timely, and the statute of limitations was extended to both. The Seventh Circuit did not address whether a partnership assessment alone could extend the limitations period for individual partners. The court’s holding was limited to the question of whether the statute of limitations was tolled during the partnership’s bankruptcy. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562–63. The court never considered whether the IRS could collect from partners without individual assessments. Thus, *Wright* does not support the IRS’s position.

### III. THE BANKRUPTCY COURT PROPERLY DISALLOWED THE CLAIM

The bankruptcy court correctly applied the law. A claim is disallowed under § 502(b)(1) if it is “unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor.” The IRS’s claim is unenforceable because the statute of limitations has expired with respect to the individual partners. The IRS cannot collect from them without a valid assessment or judgment. The fact that the partnership was assessed does not alter this result.

The IRS’s position would allow it to bypass the three-year assessment deadline simply by naming the partnership. That would undermine the purpose of the statute of limitations: to ensure finality and prevent stale claims. See *United States v. Dalm, 494 U.S. 596, 605 (1990)* (“The statute of limitations is a procedural safeguard designed to protect taxpayers from the hazards of stale claims.”). The IRS has not shown that Congress intended to allow such a workaround.
