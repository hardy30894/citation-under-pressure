# ARGUMENT

## I. Florida's Capital Sentencing Scheme Satisfies the Sixth Amendment.

### A. Hurst's jury verdict found the fact necessary to expose him to a death sentence.

*Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), held that "[i]f a State makes an increase in a defendant's authorized punishment contingent on the finding of a fact, that fact—no matter how the State labels it—must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt." *Id.* at 602. Ring's concern was that Arizona's judge, sitting alone, found the aggravating circumstance that made the defendant eligible for death. *Id.* at 609 ("Capital defendants, no less than noncapital defendants, we conclude, are entitled to a jury determination of any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in their maximum punishment.").

That concern is absent here. Hurst's jury unanimously convicted him of first-degree murder committed in the course of a robbery. That verdict itself found, beyond a reasonable doubt, the very fact Florida law designates as an aggravating circumstance rendering Hurst eligible for death—that the murder was "committed while the defendant was engaged . . . in the commission of . . . robbery." § 921.141(5)(d), Fla. Stat. Because the jury's own guilt-phase finding supplied the eligibility fact, Florida did not, as Arizona did in *Ring*, rely on a judge's independent factfinding to expose Hurst to a punishment beyond what the jury verdict authorized. *Cf. Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000) ("[A]ny fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury, and proved beyond a reasonable doubt.").

The remaining task—weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances to arrive at a final sentence—is not a factual determination of the Apprendi/Ring variety, but a moral and predictive judgment about the appropriate punishment. This Court has consistently distinguished the jury's factfinding role from the sentencer's ultimate weighing function, and has held that the Sixth Amendment does not extend to the latter. *See Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640-41 (1989) (per curiam) ("[T]he Sixth Amendment does not require that the specific findings authorizing the imposition of the sentence of death be made by the jury."); *Harris v. Alabama*, 513 U.S. 504, 515 (1995) (reaffirming that "the Constitution permits the trial judge, acting alone, to impose a capital sentence"). Nothing in *Ring* disturbed this line of authority; *Ring* itself expressly limited its holding to the eligibility-establishing aggravator, not the ultimate sentencing calculus. 536 U.S. at 609.

### B. The Sixth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury recommendation.

Even apart from *Ring*, this Court has long held that the Sixth Amendment's jury-trial guarantee, as applied to the States, does not require unanimous verdicts. *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404 (1972) (plurality opinion) (upholding non-unanimous jury verdicts of guilt in state criminal trials). If unanimity is not constitutionally compelled for a jury's finding of guilt itself, it cannot be compelled for an advisory sentencing recommendation that, as explained above, does not itself find the fact of death-eligibility. Florida's seven-to-five recommendation therefore raises no Sixth Amendment problem distinct from the one *Ring* already addresses—and resolves in Florida's favor, given the jury's unanimous guilt-phase finding of the robbery aggravator.

## II. Florida's Treatment of Mental Retardation and Its Non-Unanimous Recommendation Do Not Violate the Eighth Amendment.

### A. Atkins committed the procedural mechanics of enforcement to the States.

*Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), held that the Eighth Amendment categorically bars execution of the mentally retarded, but the Court expressly left "to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction upon their execution of sentences." *Id.* at 317. Florida discharged that responsibility by adopting a three-pronged clinical test and assigning the ultimate determination to the trial judge under a clear-and-convincing-evidence standard. § 921.137, Fla. Stat. Nothing in *Atkins* suggests that only a jury may make that determination; to the contrary, *Atkins*'s explicit deference to state procedural choice forecloses any argument that the Eighth Amendment dictates a particular factfinder. Judges routinely resolve comparably weighty constitutional threshold questions—competency to be executed, for example—without offending the Eighth Amendment. *See Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 416-17 (1986) (leaving procedures for competency determinations to the States).

### B. This Court has repeatedly sustained Florida's advisory-jury structure against Eighth Amendment reliability challenges.

The "heightened reliability" the Eighth Amendment demands in capital sentencing, *see Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), has never been understood to require unanimity in a jury's advisory recommendation. In *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447 (1984), and again in *Harris v. Alabama*, 513 U.S. 504 (1995), this Court upheld capital sentencing schemes in which a judge, not a unanimous jury, made the final life-or-death determination, expressly rejecting the argument that the Eighth Amendment mandates jury sentencing or jury unanimity. *Harris*, 513 U.S. at 515. Likewise, in *Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242 (1976), the Court approved Florida's aggravating-and-mitigating-circumstance framework as adequately guiding sentencing discretion consistent with the Eighth Amendment. And *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 358 (2004), confirmed that *Ring*'s jury-factfinding rule is a procedural mechanism, not a freestanding Eighth Amendment command about who must find sentencing facts or by what vote. Florida's scheme, tested against these precedents, comports with the Eighth Amendment.

For these reasons, Hurst's death sentence should be affirmed.
