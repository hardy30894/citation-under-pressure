**Argument**

The Patent Act’s design-patent damages remedy is limited to “the total profit” made “by the infringer from the article of manufacture.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. That statutory phrase does not authorize an award of the infringer’s entire company-wide or product-line profits when the patented design covers only a component of a multicomponent product. This Court has long treated damages as compensatory, tied to the actual subject of the wrong, not as a windfall detached from the contribution of the protected feature. See **Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604 (1912)**; **Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641 (1915)**; **Linn v. Winona Cotton Oil Co., 59 F. 170 (8th Cir. 1893)**.

1. **Damages must be apportioned to the value actually attributable to the infringement.**  
In **Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120 (1884)**, the Court held that a patentee “must” give “evidence tending to separate or apportion” the infringer’s profits and the patentee’s damages between the patented and unpatented features. Id. at 121. The Court reiterated that a plaintiff cannot recover profits or damages attributable to unpatented aspects of a product merely because they are sold together with the patented feature. **Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 614-15 (1912)**. **Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641, 646-47 (1915)** likewise required apportionment where only part of the accused machine embodied the invention. These decisions reflect a settled principle: recovery is measured by the advantage conferred by the protected design, not by unrelated profits that happen to be realized on the finished product.

2. **Section 289 does not displace the apportionment principle when the “article of manufacture” is a component.**  
Apple’s position effectively treats the end product as the relevant “article of manufacture” whenever a patented design appears anywhere on the product. But the words “article of manufacture” historically referred to the thing to which the design is applied, not automatically the entire commercial package sold to consumers. In **Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480 (1854)**, the Court rejected a damages rule that would “make the penalty depend upon the whole value of the machine” rather than the contribution of the patented improvement. Id. at 488-89. **Burden v. Denney, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 314 (1853)** similarly refused to award damages untethered to the patented feature’s actual role in the product. And **Mowry v. Whitney, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 620 (1872)** held that profits recoverable for patent infringement are those “actually made” from the use of the invention, not speculative or unrelated gains. Id. at 649-50.

3. **Design-patent recovery must account for the limited scope of the design.**  
This Court has recognized that the value of a design patent lies in the design as perceived in the marketplace, not in the entire manufacturing enterprise. In **Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. 10 (1886)**, the Court reversed a damages award that treated the whole product value as attributable to the design, explaining that the plaintiff bore the burden to show the design’s distinct contribution. Id. at 17-18. **Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885)** reached the same result, rejecting damages based on the full article where the design feature was only part of the product. Id. at 444-45. Those cases are especially important here, because the asserted design patents concern specific ornamental features of smartphones, not the internal technology, software, or entire handset as a commercial unit.

4. **The Court should reject a rule that awards all profits from a multicomponent product based on a component design.**  
A profit award disconnected from the patented component creates the same error condemned in **Sessions v. Romadka, 145 U.S. 29 (1892)** and **Elizabeth v. Pavement Co., 97 U.S. 126 (1878)**: it converts patent damages into an unbounded levy on products that merely include the invention. When the patented feature is one element among many, the law requires proof of what the invention contributed. **Seymour, 57 U.S. (16 How.) at 488-89**; **Garretson, 111 U.S. at 121**; **Westinghouse, 225 U.S. at 614-15**.

Under these authorities, Samsung’s profits attributable to an accused smartphone cannot be presumed to equal the smartphone’s total profits merely because the phone embodies a patented ornamental component. The proper measure is limited to the profit from the relevant article of manufacture—here, at most, the component embodying the claimed design—or, at minimum, to the portion of total profit shown by competent proof to be attributable to that component. That is the rule required by **Garretson**, **Westinghouse**, **Dowagiac**, **Dobson v. Dornan**, and the Court’s broader damages precedents.

The judgment should therefore be reversed.
