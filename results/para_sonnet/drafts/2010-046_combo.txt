# ARGUMENT FOR RESPONDENTS

## I. Federal Courts Possess Broad, Flexible Equitable Power to Remedy Proven Constitutional Violations, Including Through Affirmative, Systemic Relief.

Once a constitutional violation has been proved, a federal court's remedial authority is not confined to a bare declaration of rights. This Court has long recognized that "equity has been characterized by a practical flexibility in shaping its remedies and by a facility for adjusting and reconciling public and private needs." *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955). The same principle governs here: the three-judge court, after years of failed half-measures, was entitled to fashion whatever relief the record showed was necessary to cure the Eighth Amendment violation it found.

That authority does not evaporate merely because the remedy is comprehensive or burdens the State's administrative discretion. In *Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, 377 U.S. 218 (1964), this Court upheld an order compelling local officials to levy taxes to reopen public schools, holding that equity courts may order affirmative action, not merely prohibition, when necessary to eliminate a constitutional wrong, because "the time for mere 'deliberate speed' has run out," *id.* at 234. Likewise, in *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944), the Court explained that "the essence of equity jurisdiction has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case," *id.* at 329–30. And in *Porter v. Warner Holding Co.*, 328 U.S. 395 (1946), the Court cautioned that "the comprehensiveness of this equitable jurisdiction is not to be denied or limited in the absence of a clear and valid legislative command," *id.* at 398. No such command bars the remedy here; a population limit tailored to eliminate overcrowding is simply the kind of comprehensive equitable relief these decisions contemplate.

Nor is a remedy suspect because it operates continuously and adapts to changing conditions. In *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106 (1932), Justice Cardozo observed that "[a] continuing decree of injunction directed to events to come is subject always to adaptation as events may shape the need," *id.* at 114. The population order below is precisely such a continuing decree—responsive to the ongoing constitutional injury and modifiable as conditions change.

## II. The Order Reflects the Considered Judgment, After Years of Failed Alternatives, That Nothing Less Would Cure the Violation.

The State's suggestion that a court must always begin—and end—with the least intrusive conceivable measure ignores this Court's repeated teaching that when lesser remedies have already been tried and have failed, courts must impose relief that will actually work. In *Green v. County School Board of New Kent County*, 391 U.S. 430 (1968), this Court rejected a freedom-of-choice plan that had proved ineffective, holding that "the burden on a school board today is to come forward with a plan that promises realistically to work, and promises realistically to work now," *id.* at 439. So too here: the district court did not leap to population reduction. It first approved stipulated remedial orders, then a receivership, and only after years of documented non-compliance and continuing constitutional violation did it conclude that overcrowding itself was the root cause requiring a population remedy. That escalating, record-based approach is exactly what *Green* requires—and exactly what distinguishes this order from an untested or reflexive intrusion into state prerogatives.

Just as this Court held in *Alexander v. Holmes County Board of Education*, 396 U.S. 19 (1969), that further delay could not be tolerated once it was clear existing plans would not vindicate the constitutional right—reiterating that the obligation was "to operate now and hereafter only unitary schools," *id.* at 20—the court below was entitled to conclude that continued half-measures could not vindicate the Eighth Amendment rights of California's prisoners.

## III. Administrative and Fiscal Burdens Cannot Defeat a Remedy Necessary to Cure a Proven Constitutional Violation.

This Court has never permitted a State's administrative convenience to override a federal court's duty to enforce constitutional guarantees. In *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958), the Court held that "the constitutional rights of respondents are not to be sacrificed or yielded to the violence and disorder which have followed" resistance to a remedial decree, *id.* at 16. The same principle forecloses the State's argument that fiscal or logistical difficulty in reducing population renders the remedy unlawful.

## IV. The Underlying Right Is Fully Enforceable Against the States, and the Standard Governing It Is Not Static.

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition applies with full force to the States, *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), and its content is not frozen in time but "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society," *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). A remedy adequate to eliminate present-day overcrowding-driven denial of care is fully consistent with that evolving constitutional command.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
