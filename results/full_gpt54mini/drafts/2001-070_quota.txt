**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment does not compel a categorical exemption from capital punishment for offenders with mental retardation. This Court has long held that the Amendment’s meaning is informed by “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). In capital cases, the touchstone is whether a punishment comports with contemporary standards of decency and whether it serves the legitimate purposes of punishment. *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 173, 183 (1976). Although the Court in *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), declined to recognize a per se bar, it left open the possibility that evolving standards might later support one. *Id.* at 340. That time has not arrived on this record.

As *Penry* held, the relevant inquiry is whether a national consensus has developed against the penalty at issue. *Id.* at 331. The consensus analysis looks to objective indicia such as legislative enactments and actual sentencing practices. *Id.* at 331-35; see also *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 370-71 (1989). Here, the evidence does not show the kind of settled national repudiation that would justify converting the Eighth Amendment into a categorical rule. At the time of this case, a significant number of jurisdictions still authorized the death penalty for mentally retarded offenders, and the fact that some States have elected to restrict that practice reflects policy disagreement, not a constitutional command. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 334-35; *Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 176-77.

Nor does the Constitution require this Court to short-circuit the States’ considered judgments by announcing a national rule where legislatures have been actively addressing the issue. In *Gregg*, the Court emphasized that the “basic concept underlying the Eighth Amendment is nothing less than the dignity of man.” 428 U.S. at 173. But the Court also recognized that the death penalty itself is not per se unconstitutional. *Id.* at 187. The constitutional question is thus not whether mental retardation is relevant—it plainly is—but whether it disables the States from deciding, through their own criminal laws and procedures, how to account for it. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 328-30.

The respondent’s position is also consistent with the Court’s capital-sentencing precedents. The Constitution requires that the sentencer be able to consider and give effect to mitigating evidence. *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978); *Eddings v. Oklahoma*, 455 U.S. 104, 113-15 (1982). That requirement is satisfied here so long as Atkins could present evidence of his mental condition and ask the sentencer to treat it as mitigation. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 328-30. The Eighth Amendment does not transform mitigating evidence into a complete immunity from death. *Id.* at 327-28. Indeed, the very logic of *Lockett* and *Eddings* presupposes that some defendants may be considered less culpable without becoming categorically ineligible for capital punishment. *Lockett*, 438 U.S. at 604; *Eddings*, 455 U.S. at 115.

Petitioner’s reliance on diminished culpability also does not compel a different result. This Court has recognized that death is reserved for the most culpable offenders, see *Enmund v. Florida*, 458 U.S. 782, 798 (1982), but it has never held that mental retardation alone removes an offender from the class of persons constitutionally eligible for capital punishment. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 338. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly rejected broad categorical exclusions absent a clear national consensus and a strong showing that the challenged punishment fails to serve retribution or deterrence. *Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 183, 187; *Stanford*, 492 U.S. at 377-78.

Finally, the Court should be wary of converting constitutional adjudication into a policy judgment that the States are better equipped to make. *McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279, 319 (1987), cautioned that capital sentencing involves “the difficult, and perhaps intractable, question” of how to balance individualized mitigation and punishment policy. The Constitution requires fairness, individualized consideration, and reliable procedures. *Gardner v. Florida*, 430 U.S. 349, 358 (1977). Those protections do not require this Court to announce a categorical ban where the democratic process remains engaged and where the offender’s mental condition can be fully considered by the sentencer. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 328-35.

Because the Eighth Amendment does not yet reflect a national consensus against executing mentally retarded offenders, and because existing capital-sentencing doctrine already permits consideration of mental retardation as mitigation, the judgment below should be affirmed.
