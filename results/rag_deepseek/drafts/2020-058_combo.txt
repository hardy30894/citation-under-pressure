**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The text of the Immigration and Nationality Act, this Court’s precedents, and the structure of the INA all compel the conclusion that it does. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding improperly elevates form over substance and undermines Congress’s clear design.

**I. A Grant of TPS Satisfies the Statutory Definition of “Admission.”**

The INA defines “admission” and “admitted” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). When the Secretary of Homeland Security grants TPS, the alien is inspected—the Secretary examines the alien’s application, background, and eligibility—and is authorized to remain in the United States. That authorization is a “lawful entry” in the legal sense, even if the alien physically crossed the border earlier without inspection. The term “entry” in this context is a legal term of art, not a purely physical event.

This Court has long recognized that “entry” can be a legal construct. In *Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185, 189–90 (1958), the Court held that an alien paroled into the United States has not been “admitted” because parole is a “temporary, conditional” status that does not confer lawful entry. By negative implication, a grant of TPS—which confers lawful nonimmigrant status under § 1254a(f)(4)—does constitute a lawful entry. Similarly, in *Rogers v. Quan*, 357 U.S. 193, 198 (1958), the Court reaffirmed that parole is not admission, but it did not suggest that a formal grant of lawful status could never be equivalent to admission. Here, the TPS grant is the functional equivalent of the inspection and authorization that occurs at the border for a nonimmigrant visa holder.

**II. The Purpose of § 1254a(f)(4) and the Structure of § 1255 Confirm That TPS Treats Recipients as Admitted.**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for adjustment-of-status purposes under § 1255. This language is not merely a statement about “status”; it is a directive that TPS recipients are to be treated as if they were nonimmigrants who have been lawfully admitted. The Third Circuit erred by reading § 1254a(f)(4) as solely addressing the “maintain continuously a lawful status” requirement of § 1255(c)(2), but that ignores the broader context. The same subsection expressly applies to adjustment under § 1255, and the phrase “considered as being in … lawful status as a nonimmigrant” necessarily encompasses the threshold requirement of being “inspected and admitted” because nonimmigrant status is predicated on admission.

This Court’s decision in *Cheung Sum Shee v. Nagle*, 268 U.S. 336, 345 (1925), illustrates the principle that the immigration laws must be interpreted to give effect to congressional purpose. There, the Court held that the Immigration Act of 1924 did not mandatorily exclude Chinese wives of merchants, because Congress intended to preserve the right of merchants to bring their families. Here, Congress intended TPS recipients to be able to adjust status, as evidenced by § 1254a(f)(4). The Third Circuit’s interpretation renders that provision a nullity for the many TPS recipients who entered without inspection, contrary to the canon against superfluity. *See United States ex rel. Stapf v. Corsi*, 287 U.S. 129, 133 (1932) (holding that a seaman who lands in port is deemed to be “in the United States” and subject to inspection, demonstrating that the immigration laws treat legal authorization as a form of entry).

**III. Distinguishing Between “Admission” and “Status” Does Not Preclude a Grant of TPS from Being an Admission.**

The Government argues that “admission” and “status” are distinct concepts. But this Court’s cases recognize that the two are often intertwined. In *Mrvica v. Esperdy*, 376 U.S. 560, 564 (1964), the Court addressed the requirement of continuous residence for relief under § 249, and noted that physical presence and legal status are separate inquiries. But that case does not hold that a grant of lawful status can never be an admission. To the contrary, the INA itself treats the grant of nonimmigrant status as an admission when the alien is inspected at the border. A TPS grant involves the same substantive process: inspection of the alien’s eligibility and authorization to remain. The only difference is that the alien is already present in the country. This Court has never held that a physical crossing of the border is a necessary element of “admission.”

In *United States ex rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy*, 353 U.S. 72, 75 (1957), the Court considered suspension of deportation and emphasized that eligibility depends on meeting statutory requirements. Here, the TPS statute provides that recipients are considered in lawful nonimmigrant status for adjustment purposes. That requirement is satisfied when the alien is granted TPS, which constitutes a new “lawful entry” after inspection and authorization. Similarly, in *Ceballos v. Shaughnessy*, 352 U.S. 599, 603 (1957), the Court examined eligibility for suspension and noted that the statute must be read as a whole. Reading § 1254a(f)(4) together with § 1255(a), the only logical conclusion is that Congress intended TPS to serve as an admission for those who entered without inspection.

**IV. The Third Circuit’s Interpretation Conflicts with This Court’s Precedents and Undermines Congressional Intent.**

The Third Circuit relied on a rigid distinction between “admission” and “status” that has no support in this Court’s jurisprudence. In *Chang Chan v. Nagle*, 268 U.S. 346, 350 (1925), the Court held that the immigration laws must be interpreted according to their plain language, but also recognized that the context and purpose of the statute matter. Here, the plain language of § 1254a(f)(4) treats TPS recipients as being in lawful nonimmigrant status, and the plain language of the definition of “admission” in § 1101(a)(13)(A) does not require a physical crossing. The Third Circuit’s interpretation would require TPS recipients who entered illegally to depart the United States and reenter lawfully before adjusting status—a result that Congress could not have intended, given that TPS is designed to protect aliens from unsafe conditions in their home countries.

This Court has repeatedly held that the immigration laws should be construed to avoid absurd results. *See, e.g., United States ex rel. Stapf*, 287 U.S. at 133 (interpreting the landing provisions to give effect to the statutory scheme). The Third Circuit’s interpretation would force TPS recipients to choose between remaining in lawful status and seeking permanent residence, a dilemma Congress did not create.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the holding that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.
