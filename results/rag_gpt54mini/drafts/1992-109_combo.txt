The Due Process Clause does not require a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard to competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. The controlling constitutional baseline is competency to stand trial: whether the defendant has “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him” and can consult with counsel. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam); see also Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385-86 (1966); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171-72 (1975). Nothing in this Court’s pre-1970 precedents created a separate constitutional test requiring a defendant to make a “reasoned choice” among alternatives before a guilty plea or waiver of counsel may be accepted.

This Court has long held that the relevant due process inquiry is whether the waiver was knowing, voluntary, and intelligent, not whether the defendant’s decision was optimally wise or professionally advisable. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), described waiver as “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” But Johnson did not add a heightened mental-competence test. Nor did Brookhart v. Janis, 384 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1966), which invalidated a purported waiver because the defendant did not intelligently and knowingly consent. Likewise, Carnley v. Cochran, 369 U.S. 506, 513-17 (1962), and Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 344-45 (1963), concern the state’s obligation to respect counsel rights unless validly waived; they do not announce a special competency doctrine beyond ordinary due process safeguards.

To the extent the Ninth Circuit relied on Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), that decision postdates the period relevant here and is not controlling on the constitutional question. More importantly, this Court’s pre-1970 cases cut the other way. In Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708, 723-24 (1948), the Court required a searching inquiry to ensure a defendant understands the nature of the charges and consequences of waiving counsel; it did not require proof of a special “reasoned choice” capacity. In Von Moltke, the Court emphasized the trial judge’s duty to make certain the waiver is made “with eyes open,” id. at 724, but that concern went to the adequacy of the colloquy, not to a separate constitutional competency standard.

Nor does Arsenault v. Massachusetts, 393 U.S. 5, 5-7 (1968) (per curiam), support a heightened mental standard. There, the Court addressed the use of an uncounseled plea and reiterated the importance of counsel at critical stages. The case says nothing about a special “reasoned choice” threshold for competency. Similarly, McCarthy v. United States, 394 U.S. 459, 466 (1969), required strict adherence to procedural safeguards before a guilty plea is accepted, but that rule was supervisory and statutory in character, not a constitutional enlargement of competency doctrine.

The correct constitutional rule is also consistent with this Court’s competency cases involving self-representation and criminal proceedings generally. Rees v. Peyton, 384 U.S. 312 (1966) (per curiam), ordered inquiry into whether the petitioner “has capacity to appreciate his position and make a rational choice with respect to continuing or abandoning further litigation,” but that was a case about whether to continue appellate proceedings, not a holding that guilty-plea or counsel waivers require a heightened constitutional standard. The Court’s pre-1970 criminal competency cases—especially Dusky, 362 U.S. at 402, Pate, 383 U.S. at 385-86, and Drope, 420 U.S. at 171-72—establish a due process floor, not a more demanding “reasoned choice” test.

That distinction matters. The Constitution requires a defendant to be competent and the waiver to be knowing and voluntary. It does not require the trial court to find that the defendant made the best or most rational choice available. See Johnson, 304 U.S. at 464; Brookhart, 384 U.S. at 4-5; Von Moltke, 332 U.S. at 723-24. Nevada therefore satisfied due process when it applied the ordinary competency standard and accepted Moran’s plea and waiver after inquiry. The Ninth Circuit erred in constitutionalizing a heightened “reasoned choice” test that this Court had never adopted.
