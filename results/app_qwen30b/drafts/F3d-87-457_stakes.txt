**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS OF CHAPTER 93-121 ARE FACIALLY UNCONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE THEY IMPRISON THE FIRST AMENDMENT RIGHTS OF PROTECTED SPEECH AND ASSOCIATION WITHOUT A NECESSARY AND PROPORTIONAL RELATION TO A SUBSTANTIAL GOVERNMENTAL INTEREST.

The First Amendment protects the right of individuals and organizations to engage in political expression, including the advocacy of legislative and executive action through lobbying. *See Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 14, 96 S.Ct. 612, 632, 46 L.Ed.2d 659 (1976)* (“The right to engage in political expression is fundamental to the operation of our democratic system.”). Florida’s disclosure regime under Chapter 93-121, as applied to professional lobbyists, imposes a sweeping requirement that compels the public dissemination of detailed financial information about lobbying activities—expenditures on food, travel, media advertising, research, and other categories—regardless of whether such expenditures are directly related to the content of the speech. *Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a)*. This mandatory disclosure is not limited to expenditures that are themselves expressive; it includes routine operational costs that are incidental to the lobbying process. Such a regime constitutes a content-neutral regulation of speech, but one that is not narrowly tailored to serve a compelling state interest.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that disclosure requirements, while potentially permissible, must be carefully calibrated to avoid chilling protected speech. *See *Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310, 345, 130 S.Ct. 876, 899, 175 L.Ed.2d 753 (2010) (plurality opinion) (“Disclosure requirements may be justified by a compelling state interest, but only if they are narrowly tailored to serve that interest.”). In *McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm’n*, 514 U.S. 334, 347, 115 S.Ct. 1511, 1520, 131 L.Ed.2d 426 (1995), the Court struck down a state law requiring the disclosure of the names of individuals who distributed anonymous political literature, holding that “the First Amendment protects the right to remain anonymous in political discourse.” The Court emphasized that “the right to speak anonymously is a vital component of the freedom of expression.” *Id.* at 348, 115 S.Ct. at 1520. The Florida statute, by contrast, compels the disclosure of not only the identity of the lobbyist and principal but also the precise nature and source of every expenditure—information that may reveal the identity of donors, the scope of influence, and the strategic focus of lobbying efforts. This level of detail goes far beyond what is necessary to prevent corruption or fraud.

Moreover, the statute’s requirement that all expenditures—regardless of their expressive content—be disclosed creates a substantial risk of chilling protected advocacy. *See *Buckley, 424 U.S. at 25, 96 S.Ct. at 637 (“The threat of disclosure may deter individuals from engaging in political speech, even when the speech is constitutionally protected.”). The Florida statute provides no mechanism to distinguish between expenditures that are expressive and those that are merely administrative. As a result, a lobbyist may be deterred from engaging in legitimate advocacy out of fear that the disclosure of routine travel or research expenses will expose the identity of a donor or reveal the substance of a confidential negotiation. This chilling effect is particularly acute for organizations like the Florida League of Professional Lobbyists, which represent clients with sensitive or controversial policy interests.

The state’s asserted interest in preventing corruption and ensuring transparency is not sufficient to justify such a broad disclosure mandate. While the prevention of corruption is a substantial governmental interest, *see *Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce, 494 U.S. 652, 665, 110 S.Ct. 1391, 1400, 108 L.Ed.2d 652 (1990), the means chosen must be narrowly tailored. *See *United States v. O’Brien, 391 U.S. 367, 377, 88 S.Ct. 1673, 1679, 20 L.Ed.2d 672 (1968). The Florida statute fails this test. It applies to all lobbying expenditures, including those that are purely incidental to advocacy, and it does not require a showing of actual corruption or quid pro quo. The statute’s broad reach renders it overbroad and therefore facially invalid under the First Amendment.

II. THE BAN ON CONTINGENCY-FEE LOBBYING VIOLATES THE FIRST AMENDMENT BY UNREASONABLY RESTRICTING THE MANNER OF POLITICAL EXPRESSION.

The Second Circuit’s decision in *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), established that the government may not prohibit political speech based on the speaker’s motive or the method of financing, so long as the speech itself is not corrupting. *Id.* at 19, 96 S.Ct. at 634. The Court held that “the First Amendment protects the right to engage in political expression, including the right to spend money to influence elections and legislation.” *Id.* at 25, 96 S.Ct. at 637. The Florida statute’s prohibition on contingency-fee arrangements—where a lobbyist is paid only if a desired legislative outcome is achieved—directly targets the method of compensation for political advocacy. This restriction is not justified by a compelling state interest and is not narrowly tailored.
