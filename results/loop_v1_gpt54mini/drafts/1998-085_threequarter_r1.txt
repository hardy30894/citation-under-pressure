Respondent’s punitive-damages claim fails because Title VII’s text and this Court’s punitive-damages precedents require more than a finding of intentional discrimination. Congress limited punitive damages to cases in which the employer acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights” of the plaintiff. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That standard focuses on state of mind, not on whether the underlying discrimination was independently “egregious” or “outrageous.” But the Court of Appeals correctly recognized that, in the ordinary civil-rights setting, punitive damages have historically been reserved for conduct of a far more aggravated sort than simple disparate treatment.

At common law, punitive damages were never automatic upon proof of a wrong. They were reserved for conduct marked by “evil motive” or “reckless indifference” and were treated as an extraordinary remedy. Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1851); Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893); The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546 (1818). This Court repeatedly described punitive awards as appropriate only when the defendant’s conduct was especially culpable, not merely unlawful. Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), though later than the period respondent relies on, reflects that settled understanding. The older authorities are the relevant guide here.

The Court’s early civil-rights cases likewise confirm that a punitive award requires more than proof of discrimination alone. In Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303 (1880), the Court condemned racial exclusion from juries as unconstitutional state action, but the decision did not suggest that every constitutional violation warranted punitive sanctions. Similarly, Virginia v. Rives, 100 U.S. 313 (1880), and Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), treated discriminatory administration of law as unlawful, but they did not convert every violation into a punitive-damages case. The same is true of Ex parte Virginia, 100 U.S. 339 (1880), where the Court addressed discriminatory jury selection by a state judge; the case established unlawfulness, not an automatic entitlement to exemplary damages.

Title VII’s remedial structure also counsels against collapsing liability and punishment. Back pay and equitable relief are available upon proof of discrimination; punitive damages are separate and require a heightened showing. That distinction is consistent with the Court’s treatment of damages generally. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555 (1931), and Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251 (1946), explain that damages remedies depend on the character and proof of the wrong, while the availability of enhanced damages remains exceptional. Punitive damages, in particular, have long depended on aggravating circumstances. Day, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363; Lake Shore, 147 U.S. 101.

Petitioner’s proposed rule would erase Congress’s choice to confine punitive damages to cases of malice or reckless indifference. Under her approach, any intentional Title VII violation could support punishment, because every intentional discrimination case would be “egregious” enough. But this Court has never equated every intentional statutory violation with the kind of aggravated misconduct that justifies exemplary damages. See The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546; Lake Shore, 147 U.S. 101. Nor does a bare discriminatory motive automatically establish the sort of culpability historically required for punishment. Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30.

The better reading, and the one the court below effectively adopted, is that punitive damages under Title VII require proof that the employer acted with the requisite culpable state of mind and that the conduct was sufficiently aggravated to justify punishment. That approach harmonizes the statutory text with the traditional rule that punitive damages are reserved for especially blameworthy conduct. Day, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363; Lake Shore, 147 U.S. 101; The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546. Because petitioner proved, at most, intentional discrimination in a promotion decision, but not the level of aggravated wrongdoing necessary for punitive relief, the judgment below should be affirmed.
