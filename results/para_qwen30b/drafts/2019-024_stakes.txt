**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations under ERISA, 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), bars a claim where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” in the context of a fiduciary duty claim under § 1104. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that a plaintiff may avoid the statute of limitations by asserting ignorance of information that was readily available and disclosed, misreads the law and undermines the statutory purpose of finality in ERISA litigation. This Court should reverse.

### I. The Ninth Circuit Misapplied the “Actual Knowledge” Standard by Requiring Knowledge of Imprudence, Not Just the Transaction

The Ninth Circuit held that “actual knowledge” under § 1113(2) requires knowledge not only of the transaction but also of its imprudence. *Sulyma v. Intel Corp.*, 937 F.3d 1188, 1195 (9th Cir. 2019). This interpretation is contrary to binding precedent and creates a standard that is unworkable and inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 135 S. Ct. 1823 (2015), and this Court’s long-standing interpretation of ERISA’s limitations period.

In *Tibble*, the Court emphasized that the statute of limitations “is not triggered by a plaintiff’s discovery of the law, but by the plaintiff’s actual knowledge of the breach.” 135 S. Ct. at 1831. The Court further clarified that “a fiduciary’s breach of duty may be ongoing, and the limitations period may run from the date of the last act constituting the breach.” Id. at 1832. This framework rests on the principle that the statute of limitations begins when the plaintiff knows of the *facts* constituting the breach—not when he understands the legal significance of those facts.

The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that Sulyma must have known the investments were “imprudent” before the limitations period began effectively imports a legal conclusion into the factual inquiry. But *Tibble* explicitly rejected such a reading. The Court stated that “a plaintiff need not know that a fiduciary has violated ERISA to trigger the limitations period.” Id. at 1831. Instead, “actual knowledge” means knowledge of the *facts* that constitute the breach—such as the existence and nature of the investment decisions. Id. at 1832.

This Court has long held that “actual knowledge” does not require a plaintiff to understand the legal implications of a transaction. In *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that trustees were barred from bringing a claim more than three years after they had “actual knowledge of the transaction” even though they did not know it was unlawful. Id. at 991–92. The court reasoned that “the statute of limitations is triggered by [a claimant’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law.” Id. at 992. The same logic applies here: Sulyma’s knowledge of the investment allocations—regardless of whether he understood their prudence—should have triggered the limitations period.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding in *Waller v. Blue Cross of California*, 32 F.3d 1337 (9th Cir. 1994), is not binding on this Court and was wrongly applied. *Waller* involved a plaintiff who had received a notice of plan termination and annuity purchase, and the court held that the limitations period began when the plaintiff received the notice. Id. at 1341. The court did not address whether knowledge of imprudence was required. The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Waller* to support a new rule requiring knowledge of imprudence is a misreading of that precedent.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s Rule Encourages Forum Shopping and Undermines ERISA’s Purpose of Finality

The Ninth Circuit’s decision creates a dangerous precedent: a plaintiff may avoid the statute of limitations by simply claiming not to have read or recall having read documents that were provided. This invites plaintiffs to ignore disclosures and then assert ignorance years later, undermining the predictability and finality that ERISA’s limitations period is designed to ensure.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that ERISA’s limitations periods serve a critical function: “to prevent stale claims and to ensure that fiduciaries are not subjected to litigation based on events that occurred long ago.” *Tibble*, 135 S. Ct. at 1832. The statute of limitations is not a mere technicality—it is a “safeguard against the dangers of stale claims.” Id. at 1831. If a plaintiff can avoid the limitations period by claiming ignorance of information that was disclosed and accessible, the statute becomes meaningless.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule also conflicts with the principle that “a plaintiff cannot avoid the statute of limitations by his own inaction.” *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988). In *Meagher*, the court held that a plaintiff who received reduced pension checks had actual knowledge of the transaction—regardless of whether he knew it was unlawful—because he received the checks and was aware of the reduction. Id. The same logic applies here: Sulyma had access to the Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and Annual Disclosures that detailed the allocation to hedge funds and private equity. He was not prevented from reading them. His failure to do so does not excuse the limitations period.
