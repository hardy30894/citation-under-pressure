# ARGUMENT

## I. Missouri's Judges Selected Under the Missouri Plan Are Not "Employees" Protected by the ADEA

Congress does not lightly displace a State's control over the structure and composition of its own judiciary. When Congress legislates in an area traditionally reserved to the States, courts must be assured that Congress "clearly and manifestly" intended that result. This Court held in *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218 (1947), that "we start with the assumption that the historic police powers of the States were not to be superseded by the Federal Act unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress." *Id.* at 230. Few state functions are more "historic" than a State's authority to determine who shall serve as its judges and on what terms. As this Court recognized in *Coyle v. Smith*, 221 U.S. 559 (1911), matters concerning "the character of [a State's] internal government," including the composition of its own institutions, are reserved to the State's own sovereign choice, free from federal interference unless Congress unmistakably says otherwise. *Id.* at 565.

The ADEA contains no such clear statement. Section 630(f) excludes from the definition of "employee" any "appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Judges appointed under the Missouri Plan plainly fall within that exclusion. Judging is not merely mechanical application of settled rules; it necessarily involves the exercise of judgment, discretion, and the resolution of unsettled questions that bind future litigants and shape the development of the law for the entire State. Judges occupying supervisory positions—chief justices, presiding and chief judges—additionally exercise direct rule-making and administrative authority over the courts. That function is indistinguishable in substance from the discretionary, policy-laden work Congress meant to exempt when it carved out appointees "on the policymaking level."

Nor does the placement of that exception among provisions addressing the executive and legislative branches narrow its reach. Where Congress excludes a category of officials from a comprehensive scheme, courts should not assume Congress silently intended to leave an entire branch of state government—the judiciary—wholly unprotected by any exemption while carving one out for every other branch. The better reading, consistent with the presumption against intrusions on core state governmental choices articulated in *Rice*, is that Congress meant appointed judges, like elected ones, to remain outside the Act. Missouri's constitutional retirement age for its judiciary is not preempted.

## II. Missouri's Mandatory Judicial Retirement Age Satisfies Rational-Basis Review

The Equal Protection Clause does not require Missouri to treat elderly judges identically to younger ones. Age classifications, like other classifications that do not burden a suspect class or fundamental right, are tested only for whether they bear "some reasonable basis." *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911). Under that deferential standard, a classification "does not offend against that clause merely because it is not made with mathematical nicety or because in practice it results in some inequality." *Id.*

This Court's decisions confirm that legislatures—and here, the people of Missouri acting through their constitution—may draw lines based on generalized, even imperfect, correlations between age and capacity. In *Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm'rs*, 330 U.S. 552 (1947), the Court upheld a state pilotage system that rested on generalized assumptions about experience and fitness, recognizing the State's broad latitude to regulate qualifications for positions demanding exacting skill and judgment. Judicial office likewise "makes unique and exacting demands" on mental faculties that may erode with age, and the State is entitled to act on that judgment without individualized proof in every case. A State need not wait for demonstrated incapacity in each judge; it may instead adopt a categorical rule to avoid the "unpleasantness" and institutional harm of case-by-case removal.

Petitioners' insistence that Missouri could have chosen a more precise or more individualized mechanism does not render the mandatory age irrational. As this Court explained in *Williamson v. Lee Optical Co.*, 348 U.S. 483 (1955), reform "may take one step at a time," and a legislature may address "the phase of the problem which seems most acute" without remedying every related concern simultaneously. *Id.* at 489. Similarly, in *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), the Court confirmed that "it is no requirement of equal protection that all evils of the same genus be eradicated or none at all." *Id.* at 110. And under *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961), the burden falls on the challenger "to negative every conceivable basis which might support" the classification. *Id.* at 425–26. Petitioners cannot meet that burden here.

Finally, this Court has long recognized that a State may organize its judicial system as it sees fit, even where that produces variation in how judicial power is structured and exercised. *See Missouri v. Lewis*, 101 U.S. 22 (1880). Missouri's mandatory retirement age reduces delay from death or incapacity, eases generational turnover, and preserves public confidence in the judiciary. Because these are rational, legitimate objectives, the provision does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment. The judgment below should be affirmed.
