**ARGUMENT**

The decision below rests on the mistaken premise that the Coast Guard’s mere statutory authority over uninspected vessels ousts OSHA from any role in regulating occupational safety aboard such vessels. Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act, 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1), preempts OSHA only where another federal agency exercises statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health. The Coast Guard has not exercised such authority over the working conditions of employees on uninspected drilling barges like Rig 52. The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion to the contrary cannot be squared with this Court’s pre-1970 precedents.

**I. The Coast Guard Has Not Occupied the Field of Occupational Safety for Uninspected Vessels**

Section 4(b)(1) requires actual exercise of regulatory authority, not mere jurisdiction. The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels are limited to specific equipment—fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and the like. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. They do not address the comprehensive occupational safety standards that OSHA applied here. *Huron Portland Cement Co. v. City of Detroit*, 362 U.S. 440, 443 (1960), held that a local ordinance could supplement federal vessel equipment regulations because the federal scheme did not stand as an obstacle to local laws that were “complementary to it.” The Coast Guard’s limited equipment rules are similarly complementary to, not preemptive of, OSHA’s broader safety mandate. *See Sherlock v. Alling*, 93 U.S. 99, 103 (1876) (state law not conflicting with federal maritime law is valid). The Coast Guard’s failure to regulate occupational safety on uninspected vessels leaves room for OSHA.

**II. Rig 52 Is Not a Vessel “in Navigation” for Purposes of Seamen’s Working Conditions**

The Fifth Circuit treated Rig 52 as a vessel in navigation subject to exclusive Coast Guard authority over seamen’s working conditions. But this Court has consistently distinguished between traditional vessels and stationary platforms. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355–356 (1969), held that artificial island drilling rigs are not “vessels” under the Death on the High Seas Act; they are “like fixed platforms.” *Id.* at 355. Rig 52, a barge that was moored and drilling a well, was similarly not a vessel in navigation. *Roper v. United States*, 368 U.S. 20, 21–22 (1961), held that a ship deactivated and stripped of gear was not “in navigation” and thus had no warranty of seaworthiness. *Id.* at 22. Rig 52’s employees were drilling workers, not seamen primarily engaged in navigation. *See Street v. Shipowners’ Ass’n of the Pacific Coast*, 263 U.S. 334, 337 (1923) (defining seamen as those “serving on board” vessels). The barge’s stationary work function brings it within OSHA’s “workplace” definition, 29 U.S.C. § 653(a).

**III. The Coast Guard’s Authority Is Not Exclusive Over All Working Conditions on Uninspected Vessels**

The Fifth Circuit erroneously inferred exclusivity from the Coast Guard’s general authority to promote safety of life and property. But this Court has repeatedly limited the Coast Guard’s reach. *Maul v. United States*, 274 U.S. 501, 506 (1927), held that Coast Guard officers could not seize a vessel on the high seas beyond 12 miles absent statutory authority; jurisdiction is not plenary. *Cook v. United States*, 288 U.S. 102, 107 (1933), similarly limited Coast Guard boarding and search authority to inbound vessels. The Coast Guard’s jurisdiction over uninspected vessels is no more exclusive. *Blamberg Brothers v. United States*, 260 U.S. 452, 455 (1923), recognized that a barge could be a “vessel” under the Suits in Admiralty Act, but that does not confer exclusive regulatory authority over all working conditions. The OSH Act fills a gap the Coast Guard has not addressed.

**IV. Precedent Requires Actual Conflict, Not Speculative Overlap**

Section 4(b)(1) preempts only where another agency’s exercise of authority creates a conflict. *Sherlock*, 93 U.S. at 103, held that state law may apply to maritime torts unless it created a burden or impediment upon commerce. No such interference exists here. The Coast Guard’s equipment regulations for uninspected vessels do not address the safety standards OSHA cited. *Huron*, 362 U.S. at 443, upheld local law that complemented federal regulation. OSHA’s standards are complementary, not conflicting. The Fifth Circuit’s fear of “overlapping regulation” is not grounded in this Court’s pre-1970 cases, which require actual conflict.

**V. Conclusion**

Because the Coast Guard has not exercised statutory authority over occupational safety on uninspected vessels, and because Rig 52 is not a vessel in navigation, OSHA’s citation is valid. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
