The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution, found in Article I, Sections 2 and 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress. U.S. Term Limits, Inc.'s attempt to impose additional term limits on Arkansas's congressional delegation through Amendment 73 is unconstitutional because it adds a qualification not enumerated in the Constitution.

The Constitution sets forth specific qualifications for congressional service, and the Framers deliberately chose not to include term limits among them. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969). In Powell, this Court held that the Constitution's Qualifications Clauses are exclusive and that Congress may not add to or alter them. The Court reasoned that the Framers' decision to specify certain qualifications implied that they intended to preclude additional ones.

Similarly, in this case, Arkansas's attempt to impose term limits on its congressional delegation through Amendment 73 is an impermissible addition to the constitutional qualifications. The Qualifications Clauses are a comprehensive and exclusive list of the requirements for congressional service, and states may not supplement them. See Newberry v. United States, 256 U.S. 232 (1921) (recognizing that the Constitution's enumeration of qualifications is exclusive).

The fact that Amendment 73 is couched in terms of "ballot access" rather than an outright bar to congressional service does not save it from constitutional infirmity. The practical effect of the Amendment is to prevent certain individuals from serving in Congress, which is tantamount to adding a qualification. The Constitution prohibits states from achieving this result, just as it prohibits Congress from doing so. Powell v. McCormack, supra.

Furthermore, the severability clause in Amendment 73 cannot salvage the state term limits provisions (Sections 1 and 2) because the Amendment was presented to voters as an indivisible package. The ballot title and summary described the Amendment as imposing term limits on "elected officials," lumping state and federal officeholders together. As a result, it is impossible to determine whether voters would have approved term limits for state officeholders without the provision limiting congressional terms. Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). The lack of a clear, separate vote on state term limits renders the entire Amendment invalid.

In conclusion, Amendment 73's attempt to impose term limits on Arkansas's congressional delegation is unconstitutional, and the severability clause cannot save the state term limits provisions. The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution are exclusive, and states may not add to or alter them.
