**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether a federal fee-shifting statute permits enhancement of a reasonable attorneys’ fee award based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is no. This Court has never authorized such an enhancement, and the text, history, and purpose of fee-shifting statutes foreclose it.

**I. The Plain Text of Fee-Shifting Statutes Authorizes Only a “Reasonable” Fee, Not a Bonus for Superior Performance.**

Federal fee-shifting statutes, such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988, provide that a prevailing party may recover “a reasonable attorney’s fee.” The term “reasonable” denotes a fee that is adequate to attract competent counsel, not a premium for exceptional results. *See Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 430 (1983) (noting that “reasonable fee” is one “sufficient to induce a capable attorney to undertake the representation”). Nothing in the statutory text suggests that Congress intended to authorize a windfall for counsel who perform admirably. *See Alyeska Pipeline Service Co. v. Wilderness Society*, 421 U.S. 240, 260 (1975) (holding that courts may not award fees absent explicit statutory authority). The lodestar method—multiplying hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate—already accounts for the quality of representation, because more skilled attorneys command higher rates. *See Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 898 (1984) (holding that “the quality of representation” is reflected in the hourly rate). An enhancement for superior performance would thus double-count a factor already embedded in the lodestar.

**II. This Court’s Precedent Forecloses Enhancement for Quality of Performance or Results Obtained.**

This Court has consistently held that the lodestar is presumptively the reasonable fee and that enhancements are permissible only in rare and exceptional circumstances, and never for factors already subsumed in the lodestar. *See Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986) (plurality opinion) (stating that “the lodestar figure includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee”). In *Blum*, the Court rejected an enhancement based on the quality of representation and results obtained, because those factors are “fully reflected in the lodestar amount.” *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 900. The Court explained that an upward adjustment is justified only in the “rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged.” *Id.* at 899. But the Court did not hold that such evidence could support an enhancement; it merely left the question open. *See id.* at 901 (stating that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified,” but reversing the enhancement at issue). The dicta in *Blum* cannot override the clear holding that the lodestar already accounts for quality and results.

**III. The District Court’s Enhancement Violated Due Process and Exceeded Its Discretion.**

The district court enhanced the fee based on its own “observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel” and its “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006). This was not “specific evidence” as *Blum* requires; it was the judge’s personal opinion, offered without notice to the defendants or an opportunity to respond. Such a procedure violates due process. *See Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950) (holding that due process requires “notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to present their objections”). The district court effectively became a witness for the plaintiffs, testifying ex parte in its own order. This is impermissible. *See United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409, 421 (1941) (holding that a judge may not rely on private knowledge not in the record).

**IV. The Text and History of Fee-Shifting Statutes Confirm That Enhancements Are Not Authorized.**

Congress enacted fee-shifting statutes to ensure that plaintiffs with meritorious claims could obtain counsel, not to reward counsel for exceptional performance. *See Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968) (per curiam) (stating that a plaintiff “should ordinarily recover an attorney’s fee” to encourage private enforcement of civil rights laws). The purpose is to make the plaintiff whole, not to enrich counsel. *See Alyeska*, 421 U.S. at 247 (noting that the “American Rule” generally prohibits fee shifting absent statutory authorization). An enhancement for superior performance would transform fee awards into punitive sanctions against defendants, contrary to the compensatory purpose of fee-shifting statutes. *See Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 695 (1978) (holding that fee awards are “not intended to punish” but to compensate).

**V. The Lower Courts’ Reliance on Dicta and Misreading of Precedent Cannot Justify the Enhancement.**

The Eleventh Circuit relied on dicta in *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* to affirm the enhancement. But dicta is not binding. *See Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399 (1821) (Marshall, C.J.) (stating that “general expressions” in an opinion “are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used”). The holding of *Blum* is that the lodestar is presumptively reasonable and that quality of representation and results obtained are not independent bases for enhancement. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 900. The holding of *Delaware Valley I* is that “the lodestar figure includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee.” *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 566. Neither case authorizes the enhancement at issue here.

**VI. The District Court’s Enhancement Is Unreviewable and Therefore Invalid.**

The district court relied on its own personal experience and observations, which are not part of the record. This makes the enhancement unreviewable on appeal. *See SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 318 U.S. 80, 87 (1943) (holding that a reviewing court must judge the propriety of agency action “solely by the grounds invoked by the agency”). If the basis for the enhancement is not in the record, the appellate court cannot determine whether the district court abused its discretion. *See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951) (holding that reviewing courts must consider “the whole record” to determine if findings are supported by substantial evidence). The enhancement must be vacated.

**VII. The Purported Consensus Among the Circuits Is Irrelevant Because This Court’s Precedent Controls.**

The Eleventh Circuit noted that other circuits have permitted enhancements for exceptional performance. But those decisions are inconsistent with this Court’s holdings in *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I*. *See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004) (misreading *Delaware Valley I* as permitting enhancements). This Court has the final word on the meaning of federal fee-shifting statutes. *See Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803) (holding that “it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is”). The lower courts’ error cannot bind this Court.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to vacate the fee enhancement.
