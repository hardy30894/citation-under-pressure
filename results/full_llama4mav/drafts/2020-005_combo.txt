The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff's claims "arise out of or relate to" the defendant's activities within the state. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), established that a defendant's activities within a state can subject it to jurisdiction there, even if it is not incorporated or headquartered in that state. The Court has since clarified that the "arise out of or relate to" standard is met when there is a substantial connection between the defendant's in-state activities and the plaintiff's claims. See McGee v. International Life Ins. Co., 355 U.S. 220 (1957).

In this case, Ford Motor Company's activities within Montana, including advertising and selling parts, demonstrate a substantial connection to the claims brought by the personal representative of Markkaya Jean Gullett's estate. The Court has long recognized that a defendant's voluntary activities within a state can subject it to jurisdiction there. See Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352 (1927). By availing itself of the privilege of doing business in Montana, Ford Motor Company has subjected itself to the jurisdiction of Montana's courts. See International Harvester Co. v. Kentucky, 234 U.S. 579 (1914).

The exercise of personal jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company in Montana is consistent with the principles of due process. The Court has held that a state may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when it has "minimum contacts" with the state. See Pennoyer v. Neff, 95 U.S. 714 (1877); International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945). In this case, Ford Motor Company's activities within Montana, including its advertising and sales, demonstrate sufficient minimum contacts to support the exercise of personal jurisdiction.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that a defendant's activities within a state can subject it to jurisdiction there, even if those activities are not directly related to the plaintiff's claims. See Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437 (1952). In Perkins, the Court held that a foreign corporation's activities within Ohio, including maintaining an office and conducting business, were sufficient to subject it to jurisdiction there, even though the plaintiff's claims did not arise out of those activities. See Hutchinson v. Chase & Gilbert, 45 F.2d 139 (2d Cir. 1930) (L. Hand, J.).

In addition, the Court has held that a state may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when it has a significant interest in the litigation. See Blackmer v. United States, 284 U.S. 421 (1932). In this case, Montana has a significant interest in protecting its residents and ensuring that companies that do business within the state are held accountable for their actions.

The Montana Supreme Court's decision to exercise personal jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company is consistent with these principles.
