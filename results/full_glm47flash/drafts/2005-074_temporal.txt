**Argument**

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Is Prospective Only and Cannot Be Applied Retroactively to Pre-Enactment Re-Entries.**

The Tenth Circuit erred in applying the reinstatement statute to Fernandez-Vargas’ 1982 re-entry. A statute is not to be construed as retroactive unless the language of the act clearly indicates such intent. *Bennett v. Proprietary Laundry Ass'n*, 342 U.S. 138, 143 (1952). The language of § 241(a)(5) does not clearly indicate an intent to apply to conduct occurring before the statute’s effective date. The statute is a remedial measure designed to address the "catch and release" phenomenon that arose after the 1996 amendments. Applying it to a 1982 re-entry would punish conduct that was not a violation of the law at the time it was committed. *Klein v. United States*, 80 U.S. 128, 133 (1871). Because the statute does not explicitly state that it applies to re-entries occurring prior to its enactment, it must be construed as prospective only.

**II. The Term "Prior Order" Refers to the Order Prior to Re-Entry, Not the Order Prior to the Statute’s Enactment.**

The Tenth Circuit interpreted "prior order" to mean any order issued before 1997. This interpretation ignores the general rule of statutory construction that a law is prospective unless it explicitly says otherwise. *Klein v. United States*, 80 U.S. 128, 133 (1871). "A statute is not to be construed as retroactive unless the language of the act clearly indicates such intent." *Id.* The statute’s text does not distinguish between orders issued before or after the statute’s effective date. Therefore, the term "prior" must be read in the context of the alien’s re-entry, not the statute’s enactment. To read the statute otherwise would be to apply a new law to an old act without clear legislative intent, which is impermissible. *United States v. Rauscher*, 119 U.S. 407, 410 (1886).

**III. Fernandez-Vargas Had a Protectable Expectation That the Law in Effect at the Time of His Re-Entry Would Govern His Status.**

The Tenth Circuit held that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation" of relief because the 1996 amendments changed the law. However, the application of a new law to old conduct violates due process. *United States v. Rauscher*, 119 U.S. 407, 410 (1886). Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States in 1982, when the law allowed him to apply for status. He had a reasonable expectation that the law in effect at the time of his re-entry would govern his status. The reinstatement statute cannot retroactively strip him of this expectation. *Bennett v. Proprietary Laundry Ass'n*, 342 U.S. 138, 143 (1952).
