**ARGUMENT**

**I. A violation of Rule 32(a)(2) is subject to harmless-error analysis and does not automatically entitle a defendant to collateral relief.**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) provides that "[a]ny error, defect, irregularity, or variance which does not affect substantial rights shall be disregarded." This rule applies with equal force to violations of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure occurring at sentencing. As this Court held in *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), an error warrants relief only when a court cannot say "with fair assurance, after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole, that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error." *Id.* at 765. Rule 32(a)(2)'s requirement that a sentencing court advise a defendant of his right to appeal is a procedural safeguard, not a jurisdictional prerequisite to a valid judgment. A violation of that rule, like violations of other sentencing-related rules, is therefore properly evaluated for its actual effect on the defendant, not treated as per se grounds for vacatur.

This Court's decision in *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), confirms that not every departure from the formal requirements of Rule 32 is cognizable on collateral review. There, the Court held that a district court's failure to comply with the Rule 32(a) allocution requirement did not warrant relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255, because the omission was "neither jurisdictional nor constitutional" and was not "a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice." *Id.* at 428. The same reasoning governs a Rule 32(a)(2) violation: the failure to give the required advice is a technical procedural lapse, remediable only where it actually deprived the defendant of the right the Rule was designed to protect.

*United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780 (1979), reinforces this principle in the closely related context of Rule 11. There, a defendant sought collateral relief because the district court failed to advise him, as Rule 11 then required, of a mandatory special parole term. This Court rejected the claim, explaining that "a defendant who is not entitled to challenge his guilty plea in a direct appeal because he did not raise the issue there should not be able to raise the claim on collateral attack without a showing that the error resulted in a complete miscarriage of justice." *Id.* at 783–85. The Court emphasized that finality interests are especially strong where the defect is technical and unaccompanied by any showing of actual harm. The same finality concerns apply here: a district court's oral omission of appellate advice, standing alone, does not undermine the integrity of the proceeding when the defendant already possessed the substance of that information.

**II. Because Peguero had actual knowledge of his right to appeal, the District Court's omission caused no prejudice and affords no basis for relief.**

The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that a defendant is aware of his right to appeal so that he may exercise or waive it knowingly. Where, as here, the defendant already possesses that knowledge through other means, the purpose of the Rule is fully served notwithstanding the court's failure to repeat the advice at sentencing. The District Court, after an evidentiary hearing, found as fact that Peguero "knew of his right" to appeal at the time of sentencing. That factual finding is decisive under the harmless-error framework articulated in *Kotteakos* and applied to sentencing-rule violations in *Hill* and *Timmreck*: an error that does not affect a defendant's actual awareness of, or ability to exercise, a right cannot be said to have "substantially swayed" the outcome, 328 U.S. at 765, nor to constitute a "fundamental defect" resulting in "a complete miscarriage of justice," 368 U.S. at 428.

Petitioner cannot show that the District Court's omission caused him to forgo an appeal he otherwise would have taken. To the contrary, the record establishes that Peguero, fully cognizant of his appellate rights, made a considered decision not to appeal. Allowing him to obtain collateral relief under these circumstances would convert Rule 32(a)(2) from a rule designed to protect a defendant's informed choice into a mechanical trap for the unwary sentencing court—precisely the sort of "inroad on the concept of finality" that this Court cautioned against in *Timmreck*, 441 U.S. at 784.

**III. Sound policy confirms that habeas relief is unwarranted absent a showing of prejudice.**

Extending automatic relief for every Rule 32(a)(2) omission, regardless of the defendant's actual knowledge, would ill serve the interests of finality that animate this Court's collateral-review jurisprudence. As *Hill* and *Timmreck* recognize, § 2255 relief is reserved for defects that undermine the fairness or validity of the proceeding itself, not for harmless procedural oversights. Petitioner's proposed rule—vacatur without regard to prejudice—would encourage defendants who knowingly declined to appeal to manufacture a right to collateral review years later, based solely on a court reporter's omission rather than any actual deprivation of rights. Nothing in Rule 32, in § 2255, or in this Court's precedents compels so anomalous a result.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
