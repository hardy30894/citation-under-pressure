**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the plain language and legislative history of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that state wage and hour laws may apply on the OCS “as long as they are ‘applicable and not inconsistent’ with existing federal law,” without requiring a showing of a gap or void in federal coverage. That interpretation misreads the statute, contradicts this Court’s precedent, and undermines the comprehensive nature of the federal regulatory scheme governing the OCS.

**I. THE OCSLA DOES NOT AUTOMATICALLY APPLY STATE LAW TO THE OCS; IT REQUIRES A GAP IN FEDERAL LAW BEFORE STATE LAW MAY BE ASSIMILATED.**

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of § 1333(a)(2)(A) as a broad, open-ended mandate to apply state law whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law ignores the foundational principle that the OCSLA was designed to *supplement* federal law, not to *replace* it. The statute’s text, legislative history, and this Court’s decisions make clear that state law is not automatically operative on the OCS. Rather, it is adopted as “surrogate federal law” only when federal law is silent or inadequate—i.e., when there is a gap.

This principle was established in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), which held that state law applies on the OCS “only to the extent necessary to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. The Ninth Circuit rejected this test, but the Court has never overruled *Continental Oil*, and the principle remains sound. Indeed, in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 366, 89 S.Ct. 1835, 23 L.Ed.2d 360 (1969), the Court emphasized that the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision “was not intended to create a general rule of state law application,” but rather to “remove any obstacle to the application of state law” *where federal law does not otherwise provide a remedy*. The Court explained that the High Seas Act did not apply to the accidents on the fixed platform because they were not “attributable to traditional maritime activity,” and thus “any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]” was “remove[d].” Id. at 366, 89 S.Ct. 1835. This reasoning confirms that state law is not automatically applicable—it is only adopted when federal law fails to cover the situation.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading would render the “gap” requirement superfluous. If state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent,” then the phrase “to the extent that they are applicable” becomes meaningless. The statute does not say “all applicable state laws apply,” but rather “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent.” The word “to the extent” implies limitation—state law applies only *insofar as* federal law does not already govern. As this Court has repeatedly held, “Congress is presumed to use words in a manner that gives meaning to every clause.” *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538, 75 S.Ct. 513, 99 L.Ed. 615 (1955). The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation violates that principle.

**II. THE FAIR LABOR STANDARDS ACT IS A COMPREHENSIVE AND EXCLUSIVE FEDERAL SCHEME THAT LEAVES NO ROOM FOR STATE LAW TO FILL A GAP.**

The Ninth Circuit held that California’s wage and hour laws are not inconsistent with the FLSA because the FLSA contains a savings clause allowing more protective state laws. See 29 U.S.C. § 218. But the savings clause does not mean that state law is automatically applicable on the OCS. It only means that *if* federal law is silent or inadequate, state law may apply. Here, federal law is not silent. The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs minimum wage, overtime, and meal periods for all employees engaged in interstate commerce, including those on the OCS.

The FLSA’s regulatory framework, including Department of Labor (DOL) regulations, provides detailed guidance on when meal periods are required, how they are to be compensated, and how to calculate overtime. See 29 C.F.R. § 516.2. These regulations are binding and have the force of law. *Chrysler Corp. v. Brown*, 441 U.S. 281, 295–96, 99 S.Ct. 1705, 60 L.Ed.2d 208 (1979). The FLSA’s structure—its definitions, exemptions, and enforcement mechanisms—demonstrates that Congress intended it to be the exclusive federal remedy for wage and hour violations. As this Court has recognized, “the FLSA is a comprehensive statute designed to protect workers from exploitation.” *Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight System, Inc.*, 450 U.S. 728, 739, 101 S.Ct. 1491, 67 L.Ed.2d 641 (1981).

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that California’s meal period law is “not inconsistent” with the FLSA because it is preserved by the savings clause is legally flawed. Inconsistency does not require a direct conflict; it includes situations where state law attempts to impose obligations that are already governed by federal law. The FLSA already defines when meal periods are required and how they must be compensated. California’s law, which mandates a 30-minute meal period for every five hours worked, is not merely “more protective”—it is a *different* rule.
