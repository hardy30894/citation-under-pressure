ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Denying Winbush a Fingerprint Expert and a Continuance to Prepare His Defense.

A criminal defendant's right to present a defense is "a fundamental element of due process of law." Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967). That right necessarily includes a fair opportunity to test the government's forensic case. Here, the district court's eleventh-hour denial of Winbush's request for his own fingerprint expert deprived him of any meaningful ability to challenge the inferences the government invited the jury to draw—or not draw—from the absence of his fingerprints on the recovered contraband.

This Court reviews the denial of a continuance for abuse of discretion, asking whether the ruling was "so arbitrary as to violate due process." Morris v. Slappy, 461 U.S. 1, 11–12 (1983). The Supreme Court has cautioned that "not every denial of a request for more time . . . violates due process," Ungar v. Sarafite, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964), but that inquiry requires courts to weigh the reasons for the delay against the prejudice to the defendant. The magistrate judge here did neither. Rather than examining whether Winbush's need for expert assistance was substantial, the court denied the motion solely because it arrived "woefully late," without any inquiry into whether the government's own reliance on the absence of fingerprints—through Special Agent Shay's stipulated testimony and Deputy Commander Reilly's testimony explaining away that absence—created exactly the kind of one-sided forensic narrative that due process forbids.

The government did not merely decline to rely on fingerprint evidence; it affirmatively used the lack of prints as a sword, offering Reilly to testify about "the difficulties in obtaining latent fingerprints from different materials and surfaces" so that the jury would not draw an exculpatory inference from the absence of Winbush's prints on the jacket, gun, and drugs recovered along the flight path. Winbush was entitled to rebut that testimony with his own expert, particularly where the physical evidence connecting him to the Tennessee and Kentucky crack rested entirely on location and circumstance rather than direct forensic proof. Denying him any expert of his own, on the eve of trial, while permitting the government's expert to explain away the very evidence that might have exonerated him, rendered the trial fundamentally unfair. See Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973) (the right to present evidence in one's defense is "far too dear" to be sacrificed to procedural formalism).

II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Establish That Winbush Constructively Possessed the Tennessee Crack, the Kentucky Crack, or the Firearm.

Even crediting every reasonable inference in the government's favor, as this Court must, Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979), the evidence did not permit a rational jury to find beyond a reasonable doubt that Winbush possessed the drugs and firearm recovered along his flight path rather than merely having fled near them.

Constructive possession requires the government to establish "a nexus between the accused and the item to demonstrate that the accused had the power and intention to exercise dominion and control" over it. United States v. Kitchen, 57 F.3d 516, 520–21 (7th Cir. 1995). Mere proximity to contraband, without more, is insufficient. United States v. Griffin, 493 F.3d 856, 863 (7th Cir. 2007). Here, the Tennessee crack was found on a different block than the leather jacket, gun, and Kentucky crack, separated by distance and time during an active foot chase in the snow, at night, through a residential area. No witness saw Winbush drop the Tennessee bags; no fingerprint or other forensic evidence tied him to that particular cache. The jury was left to infer possession solely from proximity to a set of footprints that officers themselves were tracking only because they hoped—and could not confirm—that the prints belonged to Winbush alone.

The same infirmity infects the Kentucky crack and firearm. While the jacket was distinctively Winbush's, and officers observed him holding a firearm before he fled, the jacket was recovered separately from Winbush's person, in an area accessible to others, after an unsupervised interval during the chase. This Court has been clear that "presence . . . without more, does not establish knowing possession," Morris, 349 F.3d at 1013 (7th Cir. 2003) (constructive possession requires proof of knowledge and control, not mere accessibility). The government offered no direct evidence—no fingerprints, no eyewitness identification of Winbush discarding the jacket at that specific location, no admission—that Winbush, rather than an unknown third party moving through the same area that night, was responsible for placing the jacket, gun, and Kentucky crack where they were found.

Because the government's proof of possession rested on inference stacked upon inference—proximity in time, proximity in space, and ownership of a jacket recovered apart from Winbush's person—rather than direct evidence of dominion and control, no rational jury could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt on the § 841(a)(1) and § 924(c) counts tied to the Tennessee and Kentucky crack and the firearm. Jackson, 443 U.S. at 319.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Winbush respectfully requests that this Court reverse his convictions on Counts II, III, and V, and remand for a new trial with the assistance of a defense fingerprint expert.
