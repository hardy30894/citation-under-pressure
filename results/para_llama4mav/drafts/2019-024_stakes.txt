The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case undermines the statutory purpose of ERISA's three-year statute of limitations, 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), by holding that a plaintiff's "actual knowledge" of a breach or violation requires more than just awareness of the underlying transaction. The court below erred in requiring that the plaintiff also have knowledge that the transaction was imprudent.

ERISA's statute of limitations is triggered by the plaintiff's "actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This Court has not directly addressed the meaning of "actual knowledge" in this context, but the Ninth Circuit's interpretation is inconsistent with its own precedent. In Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that "actual knowledge" is triggered by knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by knowledge of the law. Id. at 991-92. The court reaffirmed this holding in Meagher v. Int'l Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), stating that the "statute of limitations is triggered by [a claimant's] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law." Id. at 1423 (alterations in original).

The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case, however, adds an additional layer to the "actual knowledge" requirement, holding that a plaintiff must have knowledge not only of the transaction but also that the transaction was imprudent. This interpretation is not supported by the statute or the court's own precedent. In Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co., 916 F.2d 548 (9th Cir. 1990), the court held that the plaintiff had "actual knowledge" of the breach when it was informed of the transaction, even though it did not know that the transaction was illegal under ERISA. Id. at 552.

Requiring a plaintiff to have knowledge that the transaction was imprudent would effectively read the "actual knowledge" standard out of the statute, as it would allow plaintiffs to claim that they did not know the transaction was imprudent, even if they had knowledge of the underlying facts. This interpretation is inconsistent with the statutory purpose of providing a clear and certain limitations period for ERISA claims.

The decision below should be reversed, and the court should hold that a plaintiff's "actual knowledge" of a breach or violation is triggered by knowledge of the underlying transaction, not by knowledge that the transaction was imprudent. This interpretation is consistent with the Ninth Circuit's own precedent, including Blanton, Meagher, and Ziegler, and with the statutory purpose of ERISA's statute of limitations.
