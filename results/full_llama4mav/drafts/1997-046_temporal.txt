The respondent, Eugene Woodard, does not have a protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment is implicated only when a state creates a liberty interest through its laws or regulations. As the Supreme Court held in Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. 87, 120-21 (1925), "a pardon in our days is not a private act of grace from an individual happening to possess power. It is a part of the Constitutional scheme." However, this does not imply that clemency proceedings are subject to the same due process requirements as judicial proceedings.

The Court has long recognized that clemency decisions are a matter of executive discretion, and as such, are not subject to judicial review. In United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. 128, 147 (1871), the Court stated that "pardon ... is an act of grace, proceeding from the power intrusted with the execution of the laws." The Ohio Constitution grants the governor the power to grant reprieves, commutations, and pardons, and the Ohio courts have recognized that this power is not limited by statute. See State ex rel. Atty. Gen. v. Peters, 43 Ohio St. 629, 648 (1885).

The respondent's argument that Ohio's clemency procedure is an integral part of the adjudicatory process is unpersuasive. The Court has distinguished between judicial proceedings and clemency decisions, holding that the latter are not subject to the same due process requirements. In Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 601 (1896), the Court noted that "the granting of a pardon is not a judicial function, but is vested in the executive."

Furthermore, the respondent's Fifth Amendment claim is without merit. The Ohio clemency regulation does not compel the respondent to incriminate himself. As the regulation states, the prisoner "shall be free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes." This is consistent with the holding in Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 111-12 (1908), that the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination is not implicated when a person is not compelled to testify.

In conclusion, the respondent does not have a protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings, and the Fifth Amendment is not implicated by the clemency regulation. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
