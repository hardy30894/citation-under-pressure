**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. Congress has provided an “intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of delegated authority, and the Attorney General’s delegation of that authority to the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) is both authorized and consistent with constitutional separation of powers. This Court should affirm the judgment of the Third Circuit.

**I. THE DELEGATION TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL SATISFIES THE “INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE” TEST**

Article I, § 1 of the Constitution vests all legislative power in Congress, but the Constitution does not prohibit Congress from delegating authority to other branches so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegate. *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress sets forth clear standards, even when those standards are broad. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 377 (1989); *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935). The key inquiry is not whether the standards are precise, but whether they are “sufficiently definite to guide the exercise of the delegated authority.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 377.

Section 811(h) provides such a principle. It authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance if he determines that “an imminent hazard to the public safety” exists. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is not vague or unbounded. The statute explicitly identifies the factors the Attorney General must consider: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. Id. § 811(h)(3). These are not mere suggestions—they are mandatory criteria. The Attorney General must also provide notice in the Federal Register and consider the views of the Secretary of Health and Human Services. Id. § 811(h)(4). These procedural safeguards ensure that the decision is not arbitrary and that the delegatee acts within a defined framework.

The Toubys do not dispute that Euphoria met these criteria. The DEA Administrator concluded that 4-methylaminorex was a potent stimulant clandestinely produced, capable of causing seizures, respiratory depression, and death. 52 Fed. Reg. 30,174 (1987). The substance was already being manufactured in underground labs, and its abuse was spreading rapidly. These facts satisfy the statutory standard of “imminent hazard to the public safety.” The delegation is not invalid simply because the standard is not mathematically precise. As this Court has held, “Congress may delegate authority to an administrative agency to make rules and regulations, even though the standards are broad, so long as they are not so vague as to permit arbitrary action.” *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952).

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S SUB-DELEGATION TO THE DEA IS AUTHORIZED BY CONGRESS**

The Toubys also challenge the Attorney General’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator. But Congress has expressly authorized such sub-delegation. Section 811(h) does not purport to vest the power solely in the Attorney General; it authorizes the Attorney General to “take such action as may be necessary” to implement the temporary scheduling process. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). The Attorney General, in turn, has long exercised the authority to delegate functions under the CSA. See 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989). This regulation, which was amended in 1987 to include the temporary scheduling power, is a valid exercise of the Attorney General’s authority under the statute.

The Supreme Court has consistently upheld such sub-delegations when Congress has authorized them. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 377–78 (upholding delegation of sentencing authority to the Federal Sentencing Commission); *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409, 422 (1941) (upholding delegation of enforcement authority to the Attorney General). The Court has recognized that “the complexity of modern regulatory systems” requires Congress to rely on administrative expertise. *Mistretta*, 109 S.Ct. at 654. The DEA is the agency with the specialized knowledge and resources to assess emerging drug threats and respond swiftly. The Attorney General’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA is not only reasonable but necessary to fulfill Congress’s intent to protect public safety.

**III. THE PROVISION IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE IT CREATES CRIMES**

The Toubys argue that the delegation is unconstitutional because it allows the creation of new crimes. But this argument misapprehends the nature of the power at issue. The temporary scheduling provision does not create new criminal conduct; it merely classifies an existing substance as a controlled substance for purposes of enforcement. The underlying criminal prohibitions—prohibiting manufacture, distribution, and possession of controlled substances—are already established by Congress in 21 U.S.C. § 841. The temporary schedule simply determines which substances fall within the scope of those prohibitions.

The Supreme Court has rejected the notion that delegations involving criminal penalties are categorically invalid. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 377. In *Panama Refining*, the Court struck down a delegation because it lacked any standard at all. 293 U.S. at 415. Here, by contrast, Congress has provided multiple, specific criteria. The delegation is not “a mere delegation of legislative power to the Executive to make law at will.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 377. Rather, it is a carefully structured mechanism to respond to rapidly evolving drug threats.
