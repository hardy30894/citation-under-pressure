# ARGUMENT

## THE THREE-JUDGE COURT'S POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER SATISFIES EVERY REQUIREMENT OF THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT

### A. The PLRA Permits Prisoner-Release Orders Where Necessary to Remedy Constitutional Violations

The Prison Litigation Reform Act does not categorically forbid population caps; it channels them through a rigorous procedural gate that the three-judge court satisfied in full. Congress required that any "prisoner release order" be entered only by a three-judge panel, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(B), and only after the panel finds that "crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right" and that "no other relief will remedy the violation," *id.* § 3626(a)(3)(E). The three-judge court below made precisely these findings after fourteen days of trial testimony from medical and correctional experts. Nothing in the PLRA's text bars relief once these predicates are met; to the contrary, the statute's structure presupposes that population reduction remains an available remedy, subject to heightened findings, not an option Congress eliminated altogether.

This Court has already recognized that the PLRA's remedial constraints operate as limits on *how* courts must proceed, not as a bar on system-wide relief for system-wide violations. In *Lewis v. Casey*, 518 U.S. 343 (1996), the Court cautioned against "individual remedies for individual violations of policies," 518 U.S. at 349, but did not suggest that constitutional violations produced by overcrowding lie beyond judicial remedy. Where, as here, the constitutional injury is systemic—inadequate medical and mental health care flowing directly from a population that the correctional system was never designed to hold—the remedy must be systemic as well.

### B. The Order Is Narrowly Drawn to Remedy the Violation and Extends No Further Than Necessary

The PLRA requires that relief "extend no further than necessary to correct the violation," be "narrowly drawn," and constitute "the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court's order satisfies each requirement. The court did not mandate the release of any specific prisoner or dictate precisely how California must reduce its population; it left the State multiple methods of compliance, including parole reform, sentencing modifications, and construction of new capacity. This flexibility is the hallmark of a narrowly tailored remedy.

The underlying receivership litigation illustrates the restraint district courts in this case have consistently exercised. As the Ninth Circuit observed in the companion proceedings below, the Receiver's construction and funding orders "clearly follow[ed] from" the district court's earlier, unappealed remedial orders and the State's own repeated non-objection to successive iterations of the Receiver's Plans of Action. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d 1088 (9th Cir. 2009) (unpublished disposition incorporating panel opinion); *see* Ninth Circuit Op. at 3591–92 (noting California "made no objections" to the Turnaround Plan of Action and "did not object to or appeal from" the order approving it). This history of incremental, consent-based remedial steps—not judicial overreach—produced the population order now before this Court.

### C. The District Court Gave the State Every Required Opportunity Before Ordering Relief

The PLRA mandates that a court "give substantial weight to any adverse impact on public safety" before ordering release, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(ii), and requires that the State first be given a "reasonable amount of time" to comply with prior orders, *id.* § 3626(a)(3)(A)(i). The three-judge court's record demonstrates exhaustive compliance with these procedural safeguards, built upon nearly a decade of remedial efforts predating the final order. California itself does not dispute that it stipulated to remedial orders in 2002 and 2004, that it "admitted that it was unable to comply with the injunctive relief to which it had stipulated," and that six days of evidentiary hearings in 2005 established "a long history of constitutional violations and a failure of California to comply with remedial orders." Ninth Circuit Op. at 3590–91. Only after this sustained failure—and after a receivership proved insufficient to cure the underlying overcrowding—did the panel turn to population reduction.

This procedural history matters because it forecloses any claim that the three-judge court acted precipitously or without first affording California the "reasonable time to comply" the statute demands. The State had years, not months, to remedy the violation through less intrusive means. It failed. The PLRA does not require district courts to repeat measures already proven futile; it requires only that lesser remedies be tried and given a fair opportunity to succeed before population caps are imposed.

### D. Deference to the District Court's Institutional Findings Is Warranted

Finally, this Court has long recognized that federal courts overseeing complex institutional remedies retain substantial discretion to fashion relief responsive to entrenched constitutional violations, particularly where, as here, the State's own conduct—including its consent to the receivership and its non-opposition to successive remedial plans—shaped the remedial trajectory. *Cf. Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay*, 437 U.S. 463, 468 (1978) (recognizing the settled principle that orders "fully adjudicated" and integrated into a continuing remedial scheme merit different appellate treatment than freestanding rulings). The State cannot now disown the consequences of a remedial structure it helped construct and repeatedly declined to challenge at each earlier opportunity.

### CONCLUSION

The three-judge court's order rests on exhaustive findings, procedural regularity, and a remedial history to which California acquiesced at every prior stage. The order should be affirmed.
