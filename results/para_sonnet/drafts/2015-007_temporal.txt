# ARGUMENT

## I. FLORIDA'S CAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEDURE DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT

The Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, protects the right to a jury determination of guilt in "serious criminal cases." *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145, 149 (1968). Nothing in that guarantee, however, extends to the selection of a particular punishment once guilt has been lawfully established. This Court has consistently distinguished the adjudicative function of the jury from the discretionary, individualized task of sentencing, which has traditionally been committed to the trial judge.

In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), this Court upheld a judge's authority to impose a death sentence based on information never presented to, or found by, the jury. The Court explained that a sentencing judge's task requires "a wide discretion in the sources and types of evidence used to assist him in determining the kind and extent of punishment," *id.* at 246, and that confining the judge to the "narrow issue of guilt" would be inconsistent with the individualized nature of sentencing. *Id.* at 247. The Court reaffirmed this principle a decade later in the capital context itself, holding that a sentencing court's consideration of facts never submitted to the jury was constitutionally sound because "the due-process clause" does not require that "the sentencing procedure . . . be a duplicate of the trial." *Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576, 584 (1959).

This Court's decision in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), confirms that States retain broad latitude to allocate sentencing-related fact-finding between judge and jury. The Court held that due process did not require submission of recidivism facts to the jury under the same procedural safeguards governing the trial of guilt, because the Constitution leaves States "wide leeway" in devising sentencing procedures suited to their own penal philosophies. *Id.* at 564. Florida's determination that a capital defendant's sentence—including subsidiary determinations bearing on that sentence—may be finalized by the trial judge upon the jury's advisory recommendation falls squarely within this tradition.

Nor does the jury trial right require unanimity in an advisory sentencing recommendation. Although unanimity has long been recognized as an incident of the common-law jury, this Court has never held it to be an indispensable feature of every jury function the States choose to create. In *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930), the Court described unanimity and a panel of twelve as "essential" features of the jury *as known to the common law*, but recognized that such features could be waived or modified by consent and were not rigid constitutional commands in every proceeding. Similarly, in *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581, 602 (1900), the Court held that the jury trial guarantee does not "prevent the States from decreasing" the historic incidents of jury trial when a State elects to depart from common-law forms in matters not going to guilt itself. Because Florida's jury renders only an advisory sentencing recommendation—with the ultimate sentencing determination and responsibility resting on the trial judge—the unanimity concerns animating the common-law jury tradition are not implicated in the same manner as they are in the adjudication of guilt.

## II. FLORIDA'S PROCEDURE FOR DETERMINING MENTAL RETARDATION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment, incorporated against the States, *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), draws its meaning from "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). But this Court has never held that the Eighth Amendment dictates *which factfinder*—judge or jury—must resolve a defendant's claim of mental incapacity. To the contrary, this Court has repeatedly left the allocation of such determinations to the States' own procedural judgments.

In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld a state procedure placing the burden of proving insanity on the defendant, explaining that questions concerning a defendant's mental condition and its legal consequences are matters "of state law" that States may resolve through their own evidentiary and procedural mechanisms without offending the Constitution. *Id.* at 799–801. The Court reaffirmed this deference in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), declining to constitutionalize any particular procedure for adjudicating whether a defendant's mental or physical condition should excuse or mitigate punishment, and emphasizing that such determinations are properly left "to the political process" and the "informed judgment" of the States. *Id.* at 533 (plurality opinion).

Just as *Leland* and *Powell* confirm that a State may commit the adjudication of a defendant's mental condition to a judge following an adversarial hearing, Florida's procedure—permitting the trial judge to determine mental retardation after full evidentiary presentation, while allowing the jury to weigh the same evidence as mitigation—reflects a constitutionally permissible allocation of factfinding responsibility. The reliability concerns underlying the Eighth Amendment, as this Court recognized in *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459 (1947), are satisfied where, as here, the defendant receives a full and fair opportunity to present his evidence to a neutral factfinder; the Constitution does not additionally require that a jury, rather than a judge, render the ultimate determination.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Florida should be affirmed.
