**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STATUTORY TEXT OF § 1981a(b)(1) DOES NOT IMPOSE AN INDEPENDENT "EGREGIOUSNESS" REQUIREMENT.

The plain text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) authorizes punitive damages where the plaintiff demonstrates that the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." Congress expressly declined to add any additional textual requirement such as "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct. *See* United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921) ("Where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning the duty of interpretation does not arise."). This Court has consistently refused to engraft additional conditions onto a statute where Congress has not done so. *See* Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 490 (1917); United States v. Shreveport Ry. & Nav. Co., 240 U.S. 333, 342 (1916).

II. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY OF THE 1991 CIVIL RIGHTS ACT DOES NOT SUPPORT AN "EGREGIOUSNESS" REQUIREMENT.

Petitioner's reliance on isolated remarks in the legislative history suggesting punitive damages should be reserved for "extraordinarily egregious cases" is misplaced. *See* 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (statement of Sen. Dole). Such remarks were not adopted as part of the statute and cannot override the clear statutory text. *See* United States v. Pub. Utils. Comm'n, 345 U.S. 295, 315 (1953); United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 549 (1940). Moreover, other statements in the legislative history confirm Congress intended punitive damages to be available upon a showing of malice or reckless indifference, without an additional egregiousness requirement. *See* 137 Cong. Rec. H9527 (Nov. 7, 1991) (statement of Rep. Edwards). This Court has long held that where legislative history is conflicting, the plain text of the statute controls. *See* United States v. Fisher, 28 U.S. 358, 384 (1830).

III. THIS COURT'S PUNITIVE DAMAGES PRECEDENTS REJECT REQUIREMENTS OF "EGREGIOUSNESS" OR "OUTRAGEOUSNESS" INDEPENDENT OF MALICE OR RECKLESS INDIFFERENCE.

This Court has repeatedly held that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant's conduct is malicious or recklessly indifferent, without any additional showing of egregiousness or outrageousness. In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363 (1851), this Court upheld a punitive damages award where the defendant's conduct was willful and malicious, stating that punitive damages are appropriate where the defendant acts "with fraudulent or malicious intent." *Id.* at 371. In *Randall v. Brigham*, 74 U.S. 523 (1868), the Court affirmed an award of punitive damages for oppressive and malicious conduct, explaining that such damages are justified where the defendant acts "with a bad motive or with reckless indifference." *Id.* at 540. These principles were reaffirmed in *Berry v. Branner*, 87 U.S. 274 (1874), where the Court upheld punitive damages for wanton and reckless conduct. *Id.* at 280.

The Court has also rejected attempts to impose an "egregiousness" requirement in analogous contexts. In *United States v. Chouteau*, 102 U.S. 603 (1880), the Court rejected the argument that punitive damages require a showing of "aggravated" or "egregious" conduct, holding instead that malice or reckless indifference suffices. *Id.* at 611. In *Hill v. Kimball*, 76 U.S. 597 (1869), the Court upheld punitive damages for fraudulent misrepresentation, rejecting the argument that the conduct must be "outrageous" or "shocking to the moral sense." *Id.* at 608.

IV. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN IMPOSING AN ADDITIONAL "EGREGIOUSNESS" REQUIREMENT.

The lower court's decision erroneously grafted an "egregiousness" requirement onto § 1981a(b)(1), contrary to the statute's plain text and this Court's precedents. As this Court has held, courts may not impose additional requirements where Congress has not done so. *See* Caminetti, 242 U.S. at 490; Shreveport Ry., 240 U.S. at 342. The court below also misapplied this Court's punitive damages jurisprudence by requiring an independent showing of egregiousness, despite clear authority to the contrary. *See* Day, 54 U.S. at 371; Randall, 74 U.S. at 540; Berry, 87 U.S. at 280. This error warrants reversal.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed. The text of § 1981a(b)(1) does not impose an "egregiousness" or "outrageousness" requirement, and this Court's precedents confirm that punitive damages may be awarded upon a showing of malice or reckless indifference. The decision below erroneously engrafted an additional requirement onto the statute and misapplied this Court's punitive damages jurisprudence.
