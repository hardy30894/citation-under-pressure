# ARGUMENT

## I. THE CLEMENCY PROCESS ITSELF CREATES NO LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST PROTECTED BY THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE

Clemency is fundamentally distinct from the judicial determination of guilt and sentence. It "has not been treated as a part of the judicial function" but rather is "a matter of grace" belonging to the executive. Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). Once a defendant has been afforded a full and fair trial, the resulting sentence "is a valid judgment," and "the fact that a pardon may be granted for meritorious reasons does not mean that its denial is arbitrary" or otherwise subject to judicial oversight. Id. at 464–65. This Court has been explicit: "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." Id. at 464 (citing Meachum v. Fano, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976)).

Dumschat forecloses respondent's claim to a constitutionally protected interest in Ohio's clemency procedure. There, a prisoner argued that repeated, unexplained denials of commutation applications violated due process. This Court held that "no legitimate claim of entitlement" to clemency exists merely because a state has created a clemency mechanism, since the decision to grant or deny clemency "is committed, as is the pardoning power, to the unfettered discretion" of the executive. Id. at 464–65 & n.7. The same is true here: Article III, § 11 of the Ohio Constitution vests the Governor with "almost unfettered discretion in determining whether to grant or deny clemency." No statute may bind that discretion, and the state courts have confirmed that even the Adult Parole Authority's own recommendation carries no binding force. See State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward, 71 Ohio St. 3d 513, 520 (1994) ("the General Assembly could not…enact a statute requiring the Governor to accept the recommendation of the [Authority]").

Because clemency is an act of grace rather than an entitlement flowing from any judicial or quasi-judicial adjudication, it does not implicate the "life" or "liberty" interests protected by the Fourteenth Amendment. This Court has consistently distinguished between rights that flow from the conviction and sentence themselves—already extinguished through the ordinary criminal process—and the wholly discretionary possibility of executive mercy that may or may not be extended afterward. See Meachum, 427 U.S. at 224 (due process protects against government action altering the conditions of confinement only where a cognizable liberty interest is at stake). No such interest exists in the "unfettered" discretion to commute a lawfully imposed sentence.

Nor does the "great distance" between trial and clemency support extending Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), to this context. Evitts addressed appellate proceedings that form "an integral part of the…system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant." Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956), quoted in Evitts, 469 U.S. at 393. Clemency, by contrast, operates outside and after that adjudicatory system is complete; it is, as this Court has recognized, "a matter of grace," not adjudication. Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 464. Even this Court's willingness in Evitts to extend due process protections to appellate proceedings did not extend as far as habeas review, "notwithstanding" its far closer connection to trial-stage adjudication. See Evitts, 469 U.S. at 394 (citing Ross v. Moffitt, 417 U.S. 600 (1974)). If habeas corpus—unquestionably closer to the "adjudicative system" than clemency—receives no such extension, clemency proceedings, further removed still, warrant none either.

## II. VOLUNTARY PARTICIPATION IN A CLEMENCY INTERVIEW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIFTH AMENDMENT

Respondent's Fifth Amendment challenge fails because Ohio's procedure imposes no compulsion whatsoever. The regulation "does not require a waiver of the Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination"; a prisoner who requests an interview "remains free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes." The interview is available only if the prisoner *requests* it—a purely voluntary election that carries no penalty for declining and no compulsion to answer any particular question once undertaken.

This Court's decision in Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), controls. There, this Court held that speculative, hypothetical risks of future self-incrimination do not warrant relief when no actual compulsion is shown. Relief premised on such contingent harm is "premature." Id. at 324. The same logic applies here with even greater force: respondent has not yet even requested an interview, has not answered any question, and by his own pleading "does not contemplate that this action will have any effect on his state convictions and/or sentences." Any risk of self-incrimination is thus wholly hypothetical, resting on speculation about proceedings that may never occur.

The voluntary nature of the interview distinguishes this case from those involving true compulsion. Where "the state has left the option of remaining silent fully available," no Fifth Amendment violation can be shown merely because choosing to speak might have consequences. See Baxter, 425 U.S. at 316–17. The Authority does not compel an answer to "a single question," nor does it penalize a prisoner who declines the interview altogether or who terminates it mid-course. The absence of compulsion is fatal to any Fifth Amendment claim.

## III. THE UNCONSTITUTIONAL-CONDITIONS DOCTRINE IS INAPPLICABLE

Respondent's suggestion that the interview option presents an unconstitutional "Hobson's choice" misapprehends the doctrine. Unconstitutional conditions analysis presupposes that the government has attached a penalty to the exercise of a constitutional right. Here, no benefit is withheld and no penalty imposed based on the prisoner's silence. The clemency decision remains committed to the Governor's "unfettered discretion," Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 465 n.7, uninfluenced by whether the prisoner speaks or remains silent during a voluntary interview.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be reversed insofar as it recognizes any due-process interest in clemency proceedings and remands for further Fifth Amendment findings.
