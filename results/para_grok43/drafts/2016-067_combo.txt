The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates only those who collect debts “owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). Its text therefore excludes a purchaser that collects debts owed to itself. Statutory language must be given its ordinary import; “where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning the duty of interpretation does not arise.” Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The same rule of literal construction appears in United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95-96 (1820), and Lake County v. Rollins, 130 U.S. 662, 670 (1889).

When Santander purchased the defaulted loans, it succeeded to the rights of the original creditor. An assignee stands in the shoes of its assignor and may enforce the obligation in its own name. Bate Refrigerating Co. v. Sulzberger, 157 U.S. 1, 18 (1895); see also Schooner Paulina’s Cargo v. United States, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 52, 60 (1812). Thereafter the debts were owed to Santander, not “another.” The Act’s definitional structure confirms the distinction: only one who regularly collects debts “owed or due another” qualifies as a debt collector. The phrase “owed or due another” refers to the state of the obligation at the moment of collection. United States v. Hartwell, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 385, 396 (1868).

Congress did not intend to subject every purchaser of receivables to the Act’s restrictions. “It is the duty of the court to give effect to the intention of the legislature, and that intention must be ascertained from the words employed.” Hawaii v. Mankichi, 190 U.S. 197, 212 (1903). Had Congress wished to regulate all post-default purchasers, it would have said so expressly. The statute’s exclusions reinforce the point; they protect entities that service debts “not in default when obtained,” confirming that the critical inquiry is the identity of the obligee at the time of collection, not the debt’s prior status. The Emily, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 381, 388 (1824).

Because the complaint alleges only that Santander collected debts it owned, the pleaded facts fall outside the statutory definition. The judgment of the court of appeals should therefore be affirmed.
