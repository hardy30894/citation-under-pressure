**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that Congress possessed constitutional authority under the Commerce Clause to enact 18 U.S.C. § 1951, the Hobbs Act, as applied to Dominic Pearson’s prosecution. The Hobbs Act criminalizes robbery or extortion that “in any way or degree” affects interstate commerce. While this Court has long upheld the Act’s constitutionality in the context of violent crimes with a tenuous connection to commerce, *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 555 (1995), the application of the Hobbs Act to a robbery that occurred in a local restaurant—where the only interstate nexus is the purchase of goods from out-of-state suppliers—exceeds the limits of Congress’s enumerated powers and violates the structural principles of federalism. The government’s theory of jurisdiction here rests on a speculative chain of commerce, which cannot sustain a federal criminal statute under the Supreme Court’s post-*Lopez* jurisprudence.

The Hobbs Act, as applied in this case, criminalizes conduct that is neither inherently commercial nor substantially related to interstate commerce. The robbery of Mr. Goodcents Subs & Pastas—though tragic—was a localized, intrastate crime. The victim was a nineteen-year-old employee killed during a robbery of a single restaurant. The stolen funds were used to purchase goods from local and regional suppliers, not to finance a national enterprise. The government’s sole argument for jurisdiction is that Mr. Goodcents purchases goods from out-of-state vendors, and that the stolen money would have been used to pay for those goods. But this is precisely the kind of “de minimis” or “attenuated” connection to interstate commerce that *Lopez* rejected as insufficient to justify federal regulation.

In *United States v. Lopez*, the Supreme Court struck down the Gun-Free School Zones Act, holding that “the possession of a firearm in a school zone is in no sense an economic activity that might, through repetition, substantially affect any sort of interstate commerce.” 514 U.S. at 558. The Court emphasized that “Congress may regulate only those activities having a substantial relation to interstate commerce,” id. at 559, and that “the Constitution limits Congress’ power to regulate only those activities that are themselves economic in nature.” Id. at 561. The Court further warned that “if Congress could regulate any activity that might have some effect on interstate commerce, then the Commerce Clause would be a limitless grant of power.” Id. at 562.

Here, the government seeks to regulate a violent crime—robbery and murder—based on the speculative future use of stolen funds to purchase goods from out-of-state suppliers. This is no different from the Court’s rejection of the Gun-Free School Zones Act, where the Court found that the connection between gun possession and interstate commerce was “too attenuated” to justify federal regulation. Id. at 562. The same is true here: the mere fact that a business purchases goods from out-of-state suppliers does not transform a local robbery into a matter of national concern. As the Court stated in *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 617 (2000), “Congress may not regulate non-economic, violent crime simply because it may have some aggregate effect on interstate commerce.” The murder of Amie Montgomery was not an economic activity, nor was it part of a larger commercial enterprise. It was a violent act committed in a single location, with no direct or substantial impact on interstate commerce.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336 (1971), and *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336 (1971), is misplaced. In *Bass*, the Court upheld the Hobbs Act’s application to a robbery that disrupted a business’s ability to conduct interstate transactions. 404 U.S. at 340. But in that case, the robbery occurred at a business that was “engaged in interstate commerce,” and the disruption of its operations had a direct and immediate effect on its ability to ship goods across state lines. Id. at 341. Here, no such disruption occurred. Mr. Goodcents continued to operate after the robbery, and the stolen funds were not used to purchase goods that were shipped across state lines. The government’s theory is not that the robbery disrupted interstate commerce, but that the stolen money *might* have been used to do so. This speculative, future use of funds cannot satisfy the “substantial relation” test.

Moreover, the Court in *Gonzales v. Raich*, 545 U.S. 1, 17 (2005), reaffirmed that “the Commerce Clause does not empower Congress to regulate purely local, non-commercial activity.” The Court emphasized that “the distinction between commercial and non-commercial activity is central to the Commerce Clause analysis.” Id. at 18. The robbery in this case was not commercial in nature. It was a violent crime committed by individuals for personal gain. The fact that the victim’s employer purchases goods from out-of-state suppliers does not transform the robbery into a commercial activity.

Finally, the Court in *United States v. Morrison* held that Congress cannot use the Commerce Clause to regulate “non-economic, violent crime” simply because it may have “aggregate effects” on commerce. 529 U.S. at 617. The Court warned that “if Congress could regulate any activity that might have some effect on interstate commerce, then the Commerce Clause would be a limitless grant of power.” Id. at 618. The same danger exists here. If Congress can criminalize a local robbery based on the speculative future use of stolen funds to purchase goods from out-of-state suppliers, then virtually any crime could be federalized under the Commerce Clause.

For these reasons, the application of the Hobbs Act to Dominic Pearson’s case exceeds Congress’s constitutional authority under the Commerce Clause. The robbery was a local, non-economic crime with no substantial relation to interstate commerce. The government’s theory of jurisdiction is based on a speculative, attenuated connection that violates the principles of federalism and the structural limits of federal power established in *Lopez*, *Morrison*, and *Raich*. The conviction must be reversed.

**WHEREFORE**, appellant Dominic G. Pearson respectfully requests that this Court reverse his conviction on all counts, vacate his sentence, and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
