**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DEBT IS DISCHARGEABLE BECAUSE PETITIONER LACKED THE REQUISITE INTENT TO DECEIVE.**

The Bankruptcy Code is designed to provide a "fresh start" for the "honest but unfortunate debtor." *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286 (1991). However, this policy does not extend to those who obtain money or property through actual fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). To render a debt nondischargeable under this provision, a creditor must prove the debtor obtained the funds through a material misrepresentation, with knowledge of its falsity or reckless indifference to the truth, and with intent to deceive. *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118 (3d Cir. 1995). The Third Circuit erred in finding that Petitioner acted with the requisite "reckless indifference" to the truth.

Petitioner was unaware of the specific rent control ordinance regarding vacant units. He believed he was permitted to charge fair market value and could only increase existing tenants' rent by six percent. This belief, while mistaken, was not the product of a scheme to defraud. The Third Circuit’s decision conflates negligence with fraud. *In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr. E.D. Va. 1991), held that "reckless conduct" requires a pattern of conduct that demonstrates a conscious indifference to the consequences. Here, Petitioner’s conduct was isolated and based on a misunderstanding of the law, not a pattern of deceit. Consequently, the debt arising from the overcharge is dischargeable.

**II. STATUTORY TREBLE DAMAGES ARE DISCHARGEABLE AS PUNITIVE DAMAGES.**

The Third Circuit held that the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are nondischargeable because they are "entirely foreseeable" and thus part of the debt obtained by fraud. This reasoning conflicts with the Ninth Circuit’s holding in *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 198 (9th Cir. 1991). In *Levy*, the court held that punitive damages are dischargeable because they are not "obtained by" fraud. Id. at 199. The court reasoned that while the compensatory damages were obtained by fraud, the punitive damages were not. Id. The Third Circuit’s distinction—that statutory treble damages are dischargeable because they are "codified"—is unpersuasive.

As the Supreme Court defined in *Molzof v. United States*, 502 U.S. 301 (1992), punitive damages are intended to punish and deter, not to compensate. Id. at 305. Treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act serve a punitive purpose. Therefore, they should be treated as punitive damages for the purposes of dischargeability. The Third Circuit’s reliance on *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), is misplaced because *St. Laurent* involved discretionary jury awards, whereas here the damages were mandated by statute. Because the treble damages are punitive in nature, they are dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code.
