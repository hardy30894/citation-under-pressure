The respondent, United States, respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on grounds that the statute is beyond the federal government's enumerated powers and inconsistent with the Tenth Amendment.

The Tenth Amendment challenge is foreclosed by the Supreme Court's decision in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). In Tennessee Electric, the Court held that private parties, like Bond, lack standing to raise a Tenth Amendment claim because the Amendment is designed to protect the states from federal overreach, not to confer rights on individual citizens. Id. at 143-144. The Court stated that "the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]." Id. at 144.

This Court should follow the reasoning in Tennessee Electric and hold that Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction on Tenth Amendment grounds. The Tenth Amendment is intended to protect the states, not individual litigants like Bond. As the Supreme Court noted in Tennessee Electric, the absence of a state or its officers as a party to the litigation is fatal to a Tenth Amendment claim brought by a private party. Id.

Furthermore, the Necessary and Proper Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18, grants Congress the authority to enact laws necessary to effectuate its treaty obligations, as established in Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416 (1920). In Holland, the Court upheld a federal statute enacted to implement a treaty, holding that "if [a] treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government." Id. at 432. The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and 18 U.S.C. § 229 is a necessary and proper means to implement its provisions.

The respondent respectfully submits that the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed, as Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction on Tenth Amendment grounds, and the statute under which she was convicted is a valid exercise of Congress's authority under the Necessary and Proper Clause.
