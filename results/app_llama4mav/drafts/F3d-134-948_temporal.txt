ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Denying Selam's Habeas Corpus Petition on the Grounds that He Was Not Denied Compulsory Process.

The Indian Civil Rights Act ("ICRA") guarantees tribal court defendants the right "to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor." 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). The right to compulsory process is a fundamental right essential to a fair trial. As the Supreme Court held in Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967), "the right to compulsory process is a fundamental element of due process of law." The Court further emphasized that this right is "so fundamental and essential to a fair trial" that it is "incorporated into the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment." Id. at 18-19.

In this case, Selam provided his attorney, Patricia Leno-Baker, with a list of five potential character witnesses. Although Leno-Baker requested Selam's assistance in contacting these witnesses, he failed to assist her. Nevertheless, Leno-Baker located three of the witnesses on her own. The fact that two of them informed her that their testimony would be damaging to Selam does not negate the fact that Selam was aware of his right to compel the appearance of witnesses on his behalf. The tribal judge and Leno-Baker both advised Selam of this right before the trials, but Selam did not ask the judge to subpoena anyone.

The Supreme Court has held that a defendant's failure to request a subpoena does not necessarily constitute a waiver of the right to compulsory process. In Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400 is not available, but  we can look at an earlier case,  Jencks v. United States, 353 U.S. 657 (1957), where the Court emphasized the importance of the defendant's right to obtain witnesses in his favor. The Court stated that "the Government's failure to produce [witnesses] was not cured by the defendant's failure to subpoena them." Id. at 668 (quoting  Gordon v. United States, 344 U.S. 414, 423 (1953)). Similarly, in this case, Selam's failure to request a subpoena does not necessarily mean that he waived his right to compulsory process.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has also held that the right to compulsory process includes the right to effective assistance of counsel in securing witnesses. In Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), the Court emphasized that "the right to have counsel is not limited to the presence of counsel; it includes the right to effective assistance of counsel." Id. at 71. In this case, Leno-Baker's failure to secure the attendance of Selam's witnesses, despite her efforts to locate them, raises questions about the effectiveness of her assistance.

Therefore, the district court erred in denying Selam's habeas corpus petition on the grounds that he was not denied compulsory process.

II. The District Court Erred in Holding that Selam Waived His Confrontation Clause Claim.

The district court held that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by failing to raise it in the tribal court of appeals. However, the Supreme Court has held that a defendant's failure to raise a constitutional claim at trial does not necessarily constitute a waiver of that claim. In Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), overruled on other grounds is not available, the Court held that "a federal habeas judge may deny relief to an applicant who has deliberately bypassed the orderly procedure of the state courts." Id. at 438. However, the Court also emphasized that "the federal habeas judge's inquiry should be directed to whether the petitioner has deliberately by-passed the orderly procedure of the state courts." Id. In this case, there is no evidence that Selam deliberately bypassed the tribal court of appeals.

Moreover, the Court has also held that exhaustion of state remedies is not a jurisdictional requirement, but rather a matter of comity. In Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), the Court stated that "ordinarily an application for habeas corpus by one detained under a state court judgment of conviction for crime will be entertained by a federal court only after all state remedies available, including all appellate remedies in the state courts and in this Court by appeal or writ of certiorari, have been exhausted." Id. at 116-17. However, the Court also emphasized that "the rule of exhaustion is not one defining power but one which relates to the appropriate exercise of power." Id. at 117-18.

In this case, Selam raised his Confrontation Clause claim for the first time in his federal habeas corpus petition. While it is true that he did not raise this claim in the tribal court of appeals, there is no evidence that he deliberately bypassed the tribal court of appeals. Therefore, the district court erred in holding that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.
