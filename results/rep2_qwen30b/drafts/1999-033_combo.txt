**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not afford a cause of action to a plaintiff who alleges only that a governmental entity acted out of personal spite or animus toward him, without alleging membership in a protected class or that he was treated differently from others similarly situated. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that a “class of one” claim may be maintained under the Equal Protection Clause when the plaintiff alleges no factual basis for comparison with others or any rational relationship between the treatment and a legitimate governmental objective. This Court has long held that equal protection requires more than mere individualized treatment; it demands a showing of classification based on a cognizable criterion. The judgment below must be reversed.

The foundation of equal protection jurisprudence is that the Clause protects against arbitrary or irrational distinctions drawn by the State. As this Court stated in *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373 (1886), “all persons within the territorial jurisdiction of a State are entitled to the equal protection of the laws.” But that guarantee does not extend to every instance of perceived unfairness. The Clause “does not require that all persons be treated alike, but that all persons similarly situated be treated alike.” *Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36, 70 (1873). The absence of a class or group with which to compare one’s treatment renders any claim of unequal treatment legally insufficient. To hold otherwise would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a general due process right against arbitrary action, a result this Court has consistently rejected.

In *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537, 559 (1896), the Court emphasized that the Constitution does not prohibit all distinctions, only those that are “arbitrary” or “unreasonable.” The Court upheld a Louisiana law requiring separate railway cars for white and Black passengers, not because the law was desirable, but because it did not violate the Equal Protection Clause so long as the facilities were “equal.” The decision rested on the principle that the law was not irrational or arbitrary in its application to a class. The absence of a class, however, would have rendered the claim untenable. Indeed, the Court’s opinion in *Plessy* assumed the existence of a class—Black persons—and the legitimacy of the State’s classification of them.

Similarly, in *Buchanan v. Warley*, 245 U.S. 60, 76 (1917), the Court struck down a municipal ordinance that prohibited Black persons from occupying homes in white neighborhoods. The Court held that the law violated the Equal Protection Clause because it “discriminated against a class of persons” based on race. The decision turned on the existence of a class and the irrationality of the classification. The Court did not suggest that a single individual could challenge a law merely because he was personally aggrieved. The principle remains: equal protection is not a remedy for individual grievances, but a safeguard against invidious classification.

This Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that a plaintiff may assert an equal protection claim based solely on personal animus. In *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 n.4 (1938), the Court noted that the Equal Protection Clause “is not a general command against arbitrary action,” but applies primarily to classifications that affect fundamental rights or involve suspect classes. The Court made clear that “the Constitution does not require that the State treat all persons identically,” but only that it not “treat persons differently without a rational basis.” *Baldwin v. G.A.F. Sales, Inc.*, 372 U.S. 513, 520 (1963), reaffirmed that “the Equal Protection Clause does not require that all persons be treated alike, but that all persons similarly situated be treated alike.” The absence of a similarly situated group renders the claim legally deficient.

In *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911), the Court held that a plaintiff could not challenge a state law on equal protection grounds merely because he was personally injured by it. The Court stated: “The Constitution does not guarantee to every individual the right to engage in a particular business or to do a particular act, unless the law is arbitrary or capricious.” The Court emphasized that “the equal protection of the laws” does not mean “equal treatment of all persons in all circumstances,” but “equal treatment of all persons in like circumstances.” The same principle applies here: without a class of similarly situated individuals, there can be no violation of equal protection.

The Court has also rejected the idea that personal spite or ill will can form the basis of an equal protection claim. In *Barron v. Baltimore*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 243, 250 (1833), the Court held that the Bill of Rights did not apply to the States, and by extension, that the Fourteenth Amendment’s equal protection guarantee was not a license for individuals to challenge every governmental decision they found personally offensive. The Court has consistently held that “the Constitution does not create a right to be free from personal animosity.” *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962), recognized that “the Equal Protection Clause is not a general command against arbitrary action,” but a limitation on the State’s power to classify persons.

Finally, in *Meyer v. Nebraska*, 262 U.S. 390, 399 (1923), the Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment protects certain fundamental rights, but only when those rights are “so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” The Court did not suggest that the Equal Protection Clause could be invoked to challenge individualized treatment without a class or group. The absence of such a group renders the claim legally insupportable.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals must be reversed. The Equal Protection Clause does not create a cause of action for a “class of one” where the plaintiff alleges no membership in a class or group similarly situated. The law is clear: equal protection requires a classification, not a grievance. Without such a classification, there is no violation of the Constitution. The judgment below is contrary to settled precedent and must be reversed.
