**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court’s adoption of a broad “predominantly internal” standard, divorced from the statutory text and Supreme Court precedent, improperly expands Exemption 2 beyond its narrow scope and undermines the foundational principle that FOIA exemptions must be “narrowly construed.” See *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361, 96 S.Ct. 1592. The ESQD documents at issue are not “internal personnel rules and practices” within the meaning of § 552(b)(2), nor do they meet the threshold for exemption under any other FOIA provision. The Navy’s failure to classify these documents, coupled with the absence of any credible evidence of national security harm, compels reversal.

**I. The ESQD Documents Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices” Under Exemption 2**

Exemption 2 applies only to information “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has made clear that this exemption is not a catch-all for sensitive government information. In *Rose*, the Court emphasized that Exemption 2 “is not a general exemption for all information that might be sensitive or potentially harmful if disclosed,” but rather “a narrow exception” designed to protect only “the most mundane and routine internal administrative matters.” 425 U.S. at 363, 96 S.Ct. 1592. The Court further cautioned that the exemption “must be narrowly construed” and is “explicitly exclusive.” *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151, 109 S.Ct. 2841 (quoting *Robertson*, 422 U.S. at 262, 95 S.Ct. 2140).

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard—under which any information that serves an internal function, even if it governs operational safety or facility design, may be exempt—contravenes this narrow construction. The Court in *Rose* rejected the notion that “internal” could be interpreted to include “any information that is not directly related to the public’s business with the agency.” 425 U.S. at 369, 96 S.Ct. 1592. The ESQD arc maps are not internal personnel rules; they are technical safety calculations used to determine safe storage distances for explosives. As the Navy itself acknowledges, these maps are “designed to be a long term planning tool for the Navy” and are used to “design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities.” They are not rules governing employee conduct, training, or internal operations. Rather, they are engineering data that inform the physical layout of a military installation and the risk management of hazardous materials.

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that these documents are “predominantly internal” is unsupported by the record. The Navy has not demonstrated that the ESQD maps are used to regulate employee behavior, manage internal workflows, or govern personnel decisions. To the contrary, the maps are used to comply with safety regulations—specifically, the OP-5 manual—which are themselves publicly available in part. The fact that the Navy uses the maps to plan and construct facilities does not transform them into “internal personnel rules and practices.” The Supreme Court has consistently held that operational or technical data, even if used internally, do not fall within Exemption 2 unless they are “related solely” to internal personnel matters. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369, 96 S.Ct. 1592. The ESQD maps are not such data.

**II. The Risk of Circumvention Is Not Established, and the Navy Has Not Met Its Burden**

Even if the documents were internal in nature, Exemption 2 requires a showing that disclosure would “present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation.” *Morley*, 508 F.3d at 1124. The Navy’s claim of circumvention rests entirely on speculative assertions in affidavits, particularly Commander Whitbred’s statement that “a person may be able to reverse engineer” the arc maps to discover “particular ammunition, explosive[s and] weapons systems.” But this is not a risk of circumvention—it is a risk of discovery. The distinction is critical. Circumvention implies that disclosure would enable someone to evade or undermine a regulatory scheme. Here, the Navy has not shown that the maps are used to enforce any regulation, nor that their disclosure would allow an adversary to bypass safety protocols or operational controls.

Moreover, the Navy has not classified the ESQD maps under Executive Order 13526, which governs national security information. If the maps truly posed a threat to national defense, the Navy would have classified them under Exemption 1. The fact that they remain unclassified—despite the Navy’s own assertions of danger—undermines the claim of risk. As the dissent correctly observes, “I have trouble reconciling Commander Whitbred’s statement about the national security risks of releasing the ESQD arc maps for NMII with the Navy’s failure to classify them.” The Navy’s failure to classify the documents is itself evidence that they do not meet the threshold for national security protection.

**III. The Majority’s Expansion of Exemption 2 Violates FOIA’s Presumption of Disclosure**

FOIA is “a declaration of policy that the public is entitled to know what the government is doing.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 360, 96 S.Ct. 1592. The burden is on the agency to justify withholding information, and affidavits alone are insufficient if they are speculative or contradicted by the record. *Ray*, 502 U.S. at 173, 112 S.Ct. 541. Here, the Navy has not provided any evidence that the maps have ever been used to circumvent safety rules, nor that their disclosure has ever led to an incident. The public interest in knowing the potential blast ranges of explosives stored near populated communities—especially in a densely populated area like Puget Sound—far outweighs any speculative risk.
