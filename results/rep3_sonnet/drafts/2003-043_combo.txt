# ARGUMENT

## I. THE STATUTE UNAMBIGUOUSLY REQUIRES ASSESSMENT OF THE INDIVIDUAL TAXPAYER BEFORE THE TEN-YEAR EXTENDED LIMITATIONS PERIOD APPLIES TO THAT TAXPAYER

### A. The Text of the Internal Revenue Code Defines "Taxpayer" and "Person" to Treat Partners and Partnerships as Separate Entities

Section 6501(a) requires that "the amount of any tax imposed by this title shall be assessed within 3 years after the return was filed." An assessment, in turn, "shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer." 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The definitional provisions of the Code make clear that a partnership and its individual partners are distinct taxpayers. A "person" includes "an individual" and "a partnership" as separate categories. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1). A "taxpayer" is "any person subject to any internal revenue tax." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14). Congress's decision to list "individual" and "partnership" disjunctively demonstrates that an assessment against one is not an assessment against the other.

This Court has long recognized that the plain language of the taxing statutes controls. In *Commissioner v. Acker*, 361 U.S. 87 (1959), the Court refused to extend a penalty provision beyond its precise statutory terms, explaining that tax penalties, like limitations provisions, "must be strictly construed" and courts may not "read into the [Code] provisions which are not there." *Id.* at 91. That principle applies with full force here: nothing in § 6501 or § 6502 permits an assessment against a partnership to substitute for the assessment the statute requires against the individual "taxpayer" whose liability is at issue.

### B. Assessment Functions as the Equivalent of a Judgment and Must Be Made Against the Party to Be Bound

An assessment is not a mere bookkeeping formality; it is the procedural mechanism by which the government reduces a claim to something functionally equivalent to a judgment, entitling it to proceed directly against the taxpayer's property without first litigating liability. *See Bull v. United States*, 295 U.S. 247, 260 (1935) (assessment "is given the force of a judgment," and "if the amount due is not paid a lien attaches to all the property of the taxpayer"). Because assessment carries such significant legal consequences, due process principles require that assessment be made against the specific taxpayer against whom the government intends to enforce collection. This Court has consistently required that a party be given the formal notice attendant to being named the object of a legal proceeding before its property may be reached. *See Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714, 733 (1877) (a proceeding to bind a defendant's property requires that the defendant be a party to the action).

The Ninth Circuit's decision below correctly applied this principle, holding that "the individual or entity assessed must be a separately identified 'taxpayer.'" The Partnership was assessed; Debtors were not. Each Debtor, however, is "a separate 'taxpayer'" with "its, his, or her own taxpayer identification number." An assessment against the Partnership therefore extended the statute of limitations "only with respect to the Partnership," leaving "unaltered the limitations period applicable to Debtors."

### C. This Court's and the Circuits' Pre-Existing Case Law Confirms That Assessment Against an Entity Does Not Bind Individual Members Absent Separate Assessment

The Seventh Circuit's decision in *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), on which the government relied below, is not to the contrary; indeed, it confirms respondents' position. In *Wright*, the IRS separately assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). It was only because of that dual assessment that the six-year statute of limitations extended to the individual partners at all. The Seventh Circuit was never presented with, and did not decide, whether an assessment against the partnership alone suffices to bind the partners. As the Ninth Circuit correctly recognized, *Wright* "had no opportunity to address the question" presented here.

This Court's own precedent in the partnership-taxation context supports treating the partnership and partners as distinct for assessment purposes. In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the court of appeals held that a tax lien could not attach to a purported partner's property where the assessment named only the partnership and "an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers." The district court in that case, whose reasoning the Ninth Circuit did not disturb, explained that a lien cannot exist against a particular individual's property under §§ 6321 and 6322 "unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203." *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). The same logic controls here: without an assessment against Debtors individually, no extended limitations period runs against them, and the IRS's proof of claim is time-barred.

Similarly, in *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), this Court's sister panel confronted a partner who had voluntarily paid a tax assessed against his partnership and later sought a refund. The court held he was not entitled to a refund because, under state law, he was substantively liable for the partnership's debts. *Id.* at 910. But critically, the district court whose judgment was affirmed made clear that this substantive liability did not eliminate the government's *procedural* burden to either assess the partner individually or obtain a judgment against him in order to *collect* the tax by compulsion: "the only reason for naming such individual ... is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action." *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959), *aff'd*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). Absent a voluntary payment, individual assessment, or judgment, the government cannot force collection—precisely the circumstance presented here, where the IRS attempts to enforce an unassessed, unadjudicated claim through a bankruptcy proof of claim.

### D. State Partnership Law Confirms That a Judgment Against the Entity Is Not a Judgment Against the Individual Partner

Even under substantive partnership law, joint and several liability of partners for partnership debts does not eliminate the procedural requirement that the creditor obtain a judgment against the partner individually before reaching the partner's separate assets. This principle finds support in the traditional common-law approach reflected in *Liverpool & London & Globe Insurance Co. v. Bolling*, 176 F.2d 91 (4th Cir. 1949), which recognized that liability among joint obligors does not obviate the requirement of individualized process to bind each obligor's separate estate. The Ninth Circuit properly relied on the codification of this principle in California Corporations Code § 16307(c), which the panel quoted for the proposition that "[a] judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner."

Because the IRS never assessed Debtors individually, never obtained a judgment against them, and the three-year limitations period of § 6501(a) has long since expired, the bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the IRS's claims under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). This Court should affirm.
