**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON APPELLANT’S CONSPIRACY CLAIM UNDER THE RAILWAY LABOR ACT.

The Railway Labor Act (“RLA”) provides a comprehensive scheme for the resolution of labor disputes in the railroad industry, including the enforcement of collective bargaining agreements through private arbitration. *Union Pacific R.R. Co. v. Price*, 360 U.S. 601, 604 (1959). To state a claim for conspiracy under the RLA, a plaintiff must allege a combination or agreement between a union and an employer to deprive the plaintiff of a right guaranteed by the Act. *Texas & N.O.R. Co. v. Brotherhood of Ry. & S.S. Clerks*, 281 U.S. 548, 568 (1930). Here, Appellant alleged that UTU and CSX conspired to deny him the CSX stock by manipulating the arbitration process. The district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine issues of material fact remain as to whether such an agreement existed.

The Supreme Court has recognized that unions and employers may be held liable for conspiring to violate the RLA when their actions are undertaken with a common design to injure an employee’s rights under the Act. *United Rys. & Elec. Co. v. Pennsylvania R.R. Co.*, 266 U.S. 157, 164 (1924). In *Pennsylvania R.R. Co. v. Rychlik*, 268 U.S. 271, 275 (1925), the Court held that a union’s refusal to process a grievance in good faith, combined with an employer’s participation in that refusal, could constitute a conspiracy under the RLA. Similarly, Appellant’s evidence—including the timing of the executive session, the exclusion of neutral parties, and the subsequent denial of his claim—supports an inference of concerted action. The district court improperly resolved these factual disputes in favor of the moving parties. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) (summary judgment is inappropriate where reasonable minds could differ on the inferences to be drawn from the evidence).

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN AWARDING DAMAGES AND ATTORNEYS’ FEES ON UTU’S AND CSX’S COUNTERCLAIMS UNDER THE FEDERAL WIRETAP ACT.

The Federal Wiretap Act, 18 U.S.C. § 2510 *et seq.*, prohibits the interception of oral communications where the parties have a reasonable expectation of privacy. *United States v. Jones*, 542 F.2d 661, 666 (6th Cir. 1976) (citing *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967)). However, the Act contains an exception for interceptions made with the consent of one of the parties to the communication. *Id.* (citing *United States v. Caceres*, 440 U.S. 741 (1979)). The district court erred in concluding that Appellant’s recording of the executive session violated the Act because the executive session was not a “private” communication within the meaning of the statute.

The Supreme Court has held that the Fourth Amendment’s protection against unreasonable searches does not extend to conversations held in public or semi-public settings where there is no reasonable expectation of privacy. *Katz*, 389 U.S. at 351. Similarly, courts interpreting the Wiretap Act have distinguished between truly private communications and those conducted in settings where participants have no legitimate expectation that their words will remain unrecorded. *United States v. Harpel*, 412 F.2d 354, 356 (5th Cir. 1969) (holding that a conversation in a police station interrogation room was not private where the defendant was aware that the conversation was being recorded). Here, the executive session was a procedural meeting of the PLB, a statutorily authorized arbitration panel, and was not a private conversation. *See 45 U.S.C. § 153* (establishing the PLB as a quasi-judicial body). The district court’s contrary conclusion misapplies the Wiretap Act and warrants reversal.

Moreover, even if the recording violated the Act, the district court erred in awarding damages and attorneys’ fees to UTU and CSX. The Act provides for statutory damages only where the violation was willful or intentional. *18 U.S.C. § 2520*. The district court’s finding that Appellant acted willfully was clearly erroneous. Appellant testified that he believed the executive session was part of the public arbitration process and that he did not intend to violate any law. *See United States v. Murdock*, 290 U.S. 389, 394 (1933) (holding that willfulness requires a voluntary, intentional violation of a known legal duty). The district court’s award of punitive damages and attorneys’ fees was therefore unsupported by the evidence.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed. Appellant’s conspiracy claim under the RLA presents genuine issues of material fact that preclude summary judgment. Further, the district court erred in awarding damages and attorneys’ fees on UTU’s and CSX’s counterclaims under the Federal Wiretap Act.
