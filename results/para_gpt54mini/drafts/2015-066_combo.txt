Respondent is correct that 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The statute punishes the new domestic assault, not the prior tribal convictions; and the Constitution does not require that every historical fact used to define a recidivist offense have been obtained with the full panoply of federal-court procedures. Long before 1970, this Court held that Congress may attach enhanced consequences to repeat offenders. Moore v. Missouri, 159 U.S. 673 (1895); Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616 (1912); McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311 (1901). Section 117(a) is of that familiar kind.

First, the Sixth Amendment does not govern tribal-court proceedings. This Court has long recognized that Indian tribes retain their own inherent sovereignty unless and until Congress provides otherwise. Cherokee Nation v. Georgia, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 1 (1831); Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832); Ex parte Crow Dog, 109 U.S. 556 (1883); United States v. Kagama, 118 U.S. 375 (1886); Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376 (1896). Because tribal courts are not state or federal courts, the constitutional rules that govern those sovereigns do not automatically apply to tribal criminal adjudications. Talton, 163 U.S. at 384-85; Kagama, 118 U.S. at 381-82. That is enough to defeat any argument that an uncounseled tribal conviction is itself constitutionally void.

Second, the federal statute does not impose punishment for the earlier convictions. This Court has repeatedly explained that recidivist laws “do not create a separate offense” for the earlier crimes; rather, they increase the penalty for the latest offense because of the offender’s persistent criminal conduct. McDonald, 180 U.S. at 312-13; Graham, 224 U.S. at 623-24; Spencer v. Texas, 385 U.S. 554, 560-61 (1967). The same principle appears in habitual-offender statutes upheld by this Court more than a century ago. Moore, 159 U.S. at 677-78. Section 117(a) fits squarely within that tradition: the government must prove a present domestic assault plus the fact of two prior qualifying convictions. Congress may define a recidivist felony that turns on prior adjudications, even when those adjudications arose in a different sovereign’s courts. Graham, 224 U.S. at 623-24.

Third, the use of prior convictions as proof of status or aggravating circumstance is not novel. In Gaines v. State of Washington, 277 U.S. 81 (1928), this Court sustained reliance on prior convictions in a later prosecution. In Graham, 224 U.S. at 623-24, the Court likewise approved a statutory scheme that treated prior convictions as the basis for enhanced punishment. Those decisions rest on the practical and constitutional premise that the State may “legislate against the evil” of repeated crime by defining a more serious offense for recidivists. McDonald, 180 U.S. at 313. Section 117(a) does just that.

Fourth, petitioner’s premise that an uncounseled conviction is per se unusable whenever it contributes to a later prosecution finds no support in pre-1970 precedent. To the contrary, this Court had already upheld reliance on criminal records and prior proceedings in later litigation so long as the later case afforded the defendant a fair opportunity to contest the facts relevant to the new charge. See, e.g., Wilson v. United States, 221 U.S. 361 (1911); In re Wood, 140 U.S. 278 (1891). The question here is not whether the tribal proceedings satisfied the Sixth Amendment, but whether Congress may use the existence of those convictions as one element of a new federal offense. Pre-1970 precedent answers yes. Spencer, 385 U.S. at 560-61; Graham, 224 U.S. at 623-24.

Finally, petitioner’s constitutional theory would improperly trench on Congress’s authority over Indian affairs. This Court has long recognized Congress’s broad power in that field. Kagama, 118 U.S. at 384-85; Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock, 187 U.S. 553, 565-66 (1903). Section 117(a) reflects a legislative judgment about serial domestic violence in Indian country and uses prior tribal convictions to identify repeat offenders. That is a permissible exercise of congressional power, not a Sixth Amendment violation. Cherokee Nation, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) at 17; Kagama, 118 U.S. at 384-85.

Because § 117(a) punishes the new offense, not the prior tribal convictions, and because tribal-court proceedings are governed by tribal sovereignty rather than the Sixth Amendment, the judgment should be affirmed.
