## Argument

Arizona’s rule does not violate due process. The Fourteenth Amendment requires that a criminal defendant be permitted to present a meaningful defense, but it does not compel States to allow every form of mental-state evidence for every purpose the defense prefers. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that States may define the elements of crimes, allocate burdens of proof on affirmative defenses, and limit the admissibility of evidence so long as those rules do not arbitrarily exclude a defense or relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove the offense charged. See *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 42-43 (1996) (plurality opinion); *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 210-11 (1977); *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952).

Arizona’s law fits comfortably within those constitutional boundaries. The State permitted Clark to introduce mental-health evidence to support his insanity defense, but it limited that same evidence from being used as a separate route to negate mens rea. That allocation is constitutionally permissible because due process does not require a State to recognize a diminished-capacity defense or to allow evidence of mental disease to rebut intent beyond the contours of the insanity defense the State has chosen to recognize. *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 748-49 (2006); *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 56-57; *Hopper v. Evans*, 456 U.S. 605, 610 (1982).

In *Clark v. Arizona*, the Court upheld Arizona’s approach in nearly the same circumstances presented here. The Court explained that Arizona “channel[ed] all evidence of mental disease and incapacity to the insanity issue,” while preserving the State’s authority to define the mens rea elements and to decide what evidence may be used to contest them. 548 U.S. at 779-80. The Court rejected the argument that due process independently entitles a defendant to introduce psychiatric evidence to show he lacked the specific mental state required for the offense. Id. at 779, 791-92. That holding directly controls this case.

Clark’s contrary theory would constitutionalize diminished capacity, but the Court has never held that due process forbids a State from declining to recognize that defense. To the contrary, *Egelhoff* upheld a Montana statute barring consideration of voluntary intoxication on the issue of mens rea, emphasizing that States have broad authority to regulate the relationship between mental-state evidence and proof of intent. 518 U.S. at 56-58. Likewise, *Patterson* sustained New York’s decision to place the burden of proving extreme emotional disturbance on the defendant, making clear that the Due Process Clause does not dictate a particular taxonomy of defenses or burden-shifting rules. 432 U.S. at 206-07, 210-11. If a State may constitutionally exclude even relevant evidence of intoxication from the mens rea inquiry, it may also confine psychiatric evidence to an insanity defense.

Nor does the Constitution require Arizona to allow expert testimony whenever a defendant offers it to negate intent. The Court has repeatedly recognized the special risks that psychiatric evidence poses: it can confuse the jury, invite impermissible conclusions about legal capacity, and collapse the distinction between insanity and mens rea. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 774-79. Because States may guard against those risks by channeling psychiatric evidence to insanity, Arizona’s rule is a permissible evidentiary limitation, not a denial of due process. Id. at 779-80; *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 689-90 (1986) (recognizing that the Constitution permits “reasonable restrictions” on defense evidence).

Clark may argue that excluding his psychiatric evidence from the mens rea inquiry relieved the prosecution of proving intent beyond a reasonable doubt. But Arizona still had to prove every element of the offense, and Clark remained free to challenge the State’s proof with ordinary evidence bearing on conduct, circumstances, and state of mind. Due process is satisfied so long as the defendant has a fair opportunity to defend against the charged elements, not a constitutional entitlement to any particular expert proof he prefers. *Crane*, 476 U.S. at 690; *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973). Arizona’s rule left intact that fair opportunity.

Finally, the judgment below should be affirmed because Clark’s requested rule would unsettle settled state authority over criminal law. The Court has consistently declined to transform the Due Process Clause into a general mandate that States accept the defendant’s preferred theory of mental incapacity. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 779-80; *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 56-58; *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798-99. Arizona’s line between insanity and mens rea is constitutionally permissible, and respondent is entitled to affirmance.
