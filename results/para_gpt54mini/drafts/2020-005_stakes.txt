Ford is subject to specific jurisdiction in Montana because this suit “arise[s] out of or relate[s] to” Ford’s deliberate forum conduct. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316-17 (1945); *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472-73 (1985). The Due Process Clause does not require a strict causal link between the defendant’s forum contacts and the plaintiff’s injury. Rather, a defendant may be sued where its contacts create a substantial connection to the forum and the claims relate to those contacts. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of California, San Francisco County*, 582 U.S. 255, 262-63 (2017); *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court*, 592 U.S. 351, 359-60 (2021).

Ford’s contrary rule would collapse specific jurisdiction into a proximate-cause test, but the Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected that view. In *Ford*, the Court explained that “arise out of or relate to” disjunctively permits jurisdiction where the defendant’s forum conduct has a meaningful relationship to the litigation, even if the precise product that injured the plaintiff was not first sold, designed, or manufactured in the forum. 592 U.S. at 359-62. The Court emphasized that Ford “systematically served” the forum markets for the very vehicles at issue and thus “deliberately exploited” those markets. *Id.* at 355, 364. That is enough for due process because specific jurisdiction turns on “the defendant’s relationship with the forum State,” not on a rigid causation requirement. *Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277, 284-85 (2014); *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980).

Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction fits comfortably within that framework. Ford did not merely place products into the stream of commerce; it maintained an extensive, purposeful presence in Montana by advertising, selling, servicing, and supporting vehicles and parts there. That sustained course of conduct created the same kind of “continuing obligations” and forum-directed market exploitation the Court found decisive in *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475-76, and *Ford*, 592 U.S. at 363-64. The vehicle involved here was a Ford Explorer, the exact model Ford extensively marketed in Montana. The decedent was injured and killed in Montana, and the claims allege defects and failures tied to the safety of that Montana-market vehicle. That is a sufficient “connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue” under *Ford*, 592 U.S. at 358-60.

Ford’s insistence that none of its Montana contacts “caused” this particular Explorer’s path to Montana cannot defeat jurisdiction. The Supreme Court rejected the same argument in *Ford*, holding that “causation” is not the “sole source of specific jurisdiction.” 592 U.S. at 360. Instead, the question is whether the lawsuit relates to the defendant’s forum conduct in a way that makes suit there foreseeable and fair. *Id.* at 360-62; *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 474-77. Here, Ford chose to cultivate the Montana market for the very type of vehicle involved in the crash. Having done so, it cannot invoke due process to avoid defending a Montana suit about the safety of that vehicle in Montana.

Nor does *Bristol-Myers* help Ford. There, the Court found no specific jurisdiction because the nonresident plaintiffs’ claims lacked any connection to California beyond the defendant’s general in-state sales activity. 582 U.S. at 264-65. The Court expressly distinguished cases like *Ford*, where the defendant’s forum activities concerned the same type of product and the same alleged defect as the plaintiff’s claims. *Ford*, 592 U.S. at 362-64. Unlike *Bristol-Myers*, Montana is not being asked to adjudicate a suit based on unrelated forum contacts. It is adjudicating claims arising from Ford’s marketing and support of the same vehicle line in the very state where the fatal accident occurred.

The Due Process Clause requires only that maintaining suit in the forum be reasonable in light of the defendant’s deliberate forum affiliation. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 476-77. Ford’s pervasive Montana contacts, the Montana crash, and the forum-specific safety claims make this case paradigmatic specific jurisdiction. The judgment below should be affirmed.
