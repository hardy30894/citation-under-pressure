The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the unconstitutional policy is revised during litigation. The mootness doctrine is rooted in Article III's requirement that federal courts only decide live cases or controversies. As the Supreme Court has explained, "a case is moot when the issues presented are no longer 'live' or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome." Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc., 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013) (quoting Murphy v. Hunt, 455 U.S. 478, 481 (1982)).

In Flanigan's Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), this Court held that a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law. The Court reasoned that nominal damages are not sufficient to establish a live controversy when the plaintiff has not alleged any actual injury. Id. at 1265-66. Here, Uzuegbunam and Bradford's claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot because GGC revised its policies, rendering the challenged provisions no longer in effect. See id. at 1255-56.

The district court correctly concluded that Uzuegbunam and Bradford's complaint did not request compensatory damages. The complaint's allegations of "monetary damages" and "damages in an amount to be determined by the evidence and this Court" are ambiguous, but when viewed in context, they refer to nominal damages. As this Court has held, "compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit [must] be based on actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the 'abstract value' of the constitutional rights that may have been violated." Slicker v. Jackson, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000). Uzuegbunam and Bradford did not allege any actual injury, and their complaint does not support a claim for compensatory damages.

The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying Uzuegbunam and Bradford leave to amend their complaint. They requested leave to amend in response to GGC's motion to dismiss, which is not a proper procedural mechanism. See Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018). Moreover, Uzuegbunam and Bradford did not specify the substance of their proposed amendment or attach a copy of the proposed amendment to their motion. See Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018).

In conclusion, the Eleventh Circuit correctly held that Uzuegbunam and Bradford's constitutional challenge to GGC's policies is moot, and the district court did not err in dismissing their complaint. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
