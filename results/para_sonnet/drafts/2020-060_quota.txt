**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 113(f)(3)(B)'s contribution remedy is triggered only by a settlement that resolves CERCLA liability, not by any settlement touching environmental contamination.**

CERCLA offers two distinct remedial paths for recovering cleanup costs: a six-year cost-recovery action under section 107(a), and a three-year contribution action under section 113(f). See 42 U.S.C. §§ 9607, 9613(f); *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 161 (2004). Congress created section 113 to fill a gap in the original statute, which "contained no provision expressly providing for a right of action for contribution." *Id.* at 162. Section 113(f)(3)(B) is one of "two express avenues for contribution," *id.* at 167, and it applies only where a party "has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement," 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B).

That statutory text demands resolution of *CERCLA* liability—liability for a "response action" as CERCLA defines that term—not merely liability under some other environmental statute. This Court has repeatedly insisted that CERCLA's cost-allocation mechanisms be read according to their precise terms, because the statute's dual remedial structure reflects a considered congressional design allocating cleanup costs among responsible parties. *See United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128 (2007) (distinguishing the separate functions of sections 107 and 113); *Key Tronic Corp. v. United States*, 511 U.S. 809 (1994) (construing CERCLA's cost provisions narrowly according to their text). CERCLA's remedial purpose is to "ensure that responsible parties foot the bill," *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010), but that purpose is served only when the party invoking section 113(f)(3)(B) has actually settled *CERCLA* liability—not liability arising under an entirely different regulatory regime such as the Clean Water Act.

**II. The 2004 Consent Decree resolved Guam's liability under the Clean Water Act, not CERCLA, and therefore did not trigger section 113(f)(3)(B).**

EPA sued Guam in 2002 solely "under the Clean Water Act," 33 U.S.C. §§ 1251 *et seq.*, alleging that Guam discharged pollutants "into waters of the United States without obtaining a permit." The resulting Consent Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty and design a landfill cover system to remedy that Clean Water Act violation—nothing in the Decree references CERCLA, designates the Ordot Dump as a CERCLA "response action," or purports to resolve liability under section 107(a). The Decree even states expressly that it was entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam."

Courts examining similarly ambiguous settlements have recognized that a disclaimer of liability, combined with the absence of any CERCLA reference, weighs against finding that the agreement resolved CERCLA liability. *See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (concluding that a consent decree did not resolve CERCLA liability where "the plaintiff had not conceded the question of its liability"). While the court below relied on *Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017), and *Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries, Inc.*, 937 F.3d 928, 931 (7th Cir. 2019), for the proposition that a disclaimer alone cannot defeat section 113(f)(3)(B), those cases involved settlements that *did* reference CERCLA response costs or otherwise arose out of CERCLA enforcement. Guam's Decree did neither. It arose from a Clean Water Act enforcement action concerning unpermitted discharges—an entirely distinct statutory violation from the CERCLA "removal" or "remedial action" definitions found in 42 U.S.C. § 9601(23)–(24).

Reading section 113(f)(3)(B) to encompass any settlement that happens to require environmental cleanup, regardless of the statute under which it was obtained, would stretch the provision far beyond its text and would ambush unwary state and territorial governments that settle environmental disputes without any inkling that they are simultaneously starting a CERCLA contribution clock. Such a result cannot be squared with Congress's careful, party-protective design in *Cooper* and *Atlantic Research*, nor with this Court's consistent refusal to expand CERCLA liability beyond what the statutory text supports. *See Meghrig v. KFC Western, Inc.*, 516 U.S. 479 (1996) (declining to read remedies into environmental statutes beyond their express terms); *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States*, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009) (CERCLA liability rules must track the statute's actual allocation scheme, which "did not mandate 'joint and several' liability in every case").

**III. Because the Consent Decree never resolved CERCLA liability, Guam's section 107(a) cost-recovery action remains timely.**

Because section 113(f)(3)(B) was never triggered, Guam's section 107(a) claim—subject to a six-year limitations period, 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B)—remains available. Guam should be permitted to pursue the Navy for the costs of remediating contamination the Navy caused during decades of military operation of the Ordot Dump. The D.C. Circuit's contrary holding conflates two distinct statutory schemes and forecloses relief that Congress intended CERCLA's cost-recovery provision to provide. This Court should reverse.
