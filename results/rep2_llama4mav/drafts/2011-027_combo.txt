The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood. The equal footing doctrine, which grants states title to the beds of navigable waters upon admission to the Union, is rooted in the principle that newly admitted states are entitled to the same rights and sovereignty as the original thirteen states. United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935); Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212, 228-229 (1845). To determine whether a river is navigable for title purposes, courts have long applied the test of whether the river was navigable in fact at the time of statehood. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870); United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49, 55-56 (1926).

The navigability test is not a static or abstract inquiry, but rather a fact-specific determination that requires consideration of the river's condition at the time of statehood. As the Court held in United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931), "the test of navigability...involves...the character of the river...at the time of statehood." This inquiry is necessary to determine whether the river was capable of being used for commerce, trade, or travel at the time the state was admitted to the Union. The Montello, 87 U.S. 430, 441-442 (1874).

PPL's argument that the trial court erred in relying on historical evidence to determine navigability is unfounded. Courts have long recognized that historical evidence is relevant and probative in determining navigability at the time of statehood. See, e.g., Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 591 (1922) (considering historical evidence, including early maps and accounts of explorers, to determine navigability). The use of such evidence is not only permissible but necessary, as it provides the most reliable means of reconstructing the river's condition at the time of statehood.

Furthermore, the Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the trial court's consideration of the State's evidence on summary judgment was proper. The court relied on Smith v. Barrett, 194 Mont. 332, 635 P.2d 1284 (1981), which permits the consideration of exhibits attached to an affidavit if a proper foundation can be laid. The State's expert, Foley, provided a proper foundation for the historical documents she submitted, and the documents themselves were self-authenticating under M. R. Evid. 902.

The trial court's determination that the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of statehood is supported by the evidence and consistent with the principles of the equal footing doctrine. As the Court held in Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894), "the title and dominion over navigable waters and the lands beneath them are held by the United States...for the benefit of the people, and...upon the admission of a state into the Union, the title to such lands and waters passes to the state." The Montana Supreme Court's decision is consistent with this principle and should be affirmed.

The decision below is also consistent with the long-standing precedent established in cases such as The Genesee Chief v. Fitzhugh, 53 U.S. 443 (1851), which held that the navigability of a river is determined by its capacity for use in commerce. The trial court's finding that the relevant stretches of the rivers were navigable at the time of statehood is a factual determination that is entitled to deference. United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 404 (1940).

In conclusion, the Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood. The trial court's determination is supported by the evidence and consistent with the principles of the equal footing doctrine. The decision below should be affirmed.
