**ARGUMENT**

**I. This Case Is Moot Because the Challenged Policies Were Repealed and Appellant Uzuegbunam Graduated, Eliminating Any Ongoing Controversy.**

Article III requires a live case or controversy “at all stages of review, not merely at the time the complaint is filed.” *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 90–91 (2013) (quoting *Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona*, 520 U.S. 43, 67 (1997)). When a challenged policy is repealed and the plaintiff graduates, claims for injunctive and declaratory relief become moot unless the defendant is “reasonably expected” to resume the challenged practice. *Los Angeles County v. Davis*, 440 U.S. 625, 631 (1979). Here, Georgia Gwinnett College replaced its speech-zone and code-of-conduct policies with ones permitting expressive activity anywhere on campus. That revision was “unambiguous” and there is “no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to” the prior policies. Eleventh Circuit Op. 7. Uzuegbunam’s graduation further eliminated any possibility of future injury. The district court correctly found, and appellants do not contest, that their requests for prospective relief are moot. *Id.* at 7 n.1.

**II. A Claim for Nominal Damages Alone Cannot Resuscitate an Otherwise Moot Controversy.**

Appellants’ sole remaining request is for nominal damages. But a prayer for nominal damages does not “resurrect” a moot case. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 107 (1998). In *Steel Co.*, the plaintiff sought only declaratory and injunctive relief, not damages, so the Court did not reach the question of nominal damages. *Id.* at 107. However, the Court has consistently held that a federal court lacks jurisdiction to award “a remedy for a past violation” when the underlying controversy is no longer live. *See, e.g., Lewis v. Continental Bank Corp.*, 494 U.S. 472, 478 (1990) (mootness requires dismissal of entire case unless “some collateral consequence” remains). Nominal damages are purely symbolic—they “recognize the violation of a right” without providing “any compensatory relief.” *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978). They cannot substitute for the concrete injury necessary to maintain a justiciable controversy. *See Spencer v. Kemna*, 523 U.S. 1, 18 (1998) (Ginsburg, J., concurring) (noting that nominal damages “would not meet the ‘case or controversy’ requirement of Article III” if the underlying claim is moot).

This Court has never held that nominal damages alone can keep a case alive after the challenged conduct has ceased and the plaintiff suffers no ongoing harm. Indeed, in *City of Erie v. Pap’s A.M.*, 529 U.S. 277, 287–88 (2000), the Court dismissed as moot a challenge to a nude-dancing ordinance after the defendant’s lease expired, even though the plaintiff had sought nominal damages. The Court’s precedents thus reinforce that nominal damages are insufficient to create a live controversy when the underlying dispute is over. *See also Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 192 (2000) (distinguishing civil penalties, which partially redress past harm and deter future violations, from nominal damages, which serve no such purpose).

**III. Appellants’ Argument That Their Claim for Compensatory Damages Saves the Case Is Unavailing.**

Appellants belatedly contend that their complaint sought compensatory damages, not merely nominal damages. The district court correctly rejected this reading. The First Amended Complaint’s “prayer for relief” requested only “nominal damages,” and the factual allegations described no “actual injury” such as monetary loss, physical pain, or emotional distress. *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000). Under *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007), bare legal conclusions do not suffice. Appellants’ failure to allege compensable injuries means they cannot salvage mootness by invoking compensatory damages. The district court also properly denied leave to amend because appellants sought amendment only through a response to a motion to dismiss, which does not comply with Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15. *See Cita Trust Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018). That decision was not an abuse of discretion.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
