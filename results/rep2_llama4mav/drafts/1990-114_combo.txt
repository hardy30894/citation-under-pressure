The petitioners' challenge to Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges is without merit. The Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) does not apply to appointed state court judges because they are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f).

As the court below correctly held, appointed state court judges in Missouri are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" within the meaning of the ADEA. The policymaking level exception is not limited to the executive and legislative branches, but rather applies to any appointee who exercises significant policymaking authority. See United States v. Lovett, 328 U.S. 303, 316 (1946) (recognizing that the term "policymaking" is not limited to a specific branch of government). Judges, in deciding cases, often create law and establish general standards or specific rules of decision that may be relied upon in the future. See Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938) (recognizing the lawmaking role of judges). Moreover, Missouri's appointed judges exercise considerable policymaking responsibility in the supervision of the Missouri court system. See In re Sawyer, 260 U.S. 622, 631 (1923) (recognizing the administrative role of judges).

The legislative history of the ADEA exemptions supports this interpretation. The conference report accompanying the 1972 amendments to the ADEA indicates that the exemption for "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" is intended to apply to high-level appointees, such as cabinet officers. See Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180. This exemption is not limited to the executive branch, but rather applies to any appointee who exercises significant policymaking authority.

Furthermore, the mandatory retirement provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The rational basis test applies to age-based classifications, and Missouri's mandatory retirement provision is rationally related to a legitimate state interest. See Williamson v. Lee Optical Co., 348 U.S. 483, 488 (1955) (holding that a state law is constitutional if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest). The state has a legitimate interest in ensuring that its judges are capable of performing their duties, and a mandatory retirement age is a rational means of achieving this goal. See Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1960) (citing Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1959)).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that age is not a suspect classification, and that states have broad discretion to establish age-based classifications. See McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420, 425-26 (1961) (holding that a state law is constitutional if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest). In this case, Missouri's mandatory retirement provision is rationally related to the state's interest in ensuring that its judges are capable of performing their duties. See Goesaert v. Cleary, 335 U.S. 464, 467 (1948) (holding that a state law is constitutional if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest).

The Court has also recognized that judges are different from other government employees, and that states have a legitimate interest in ensuring that their judges are capable of performing their duties. See Tumey v. Ohio, 273 U.S. 510, 532 (1927) (recognizing the importance of judicial independence and competence). A mandatory retirement age is a rational means of achieving this goal, as it eliminates the need for individualized determinations of a judge's fitness for office. See Adler v. Board of Education, 342 U.S. 485, 493 (1952) (recognizing the state's interest in ensuring the fitness of its employees).

In conclusion, the ADEA does not apply to appointed state court judges in Missouri, and the mandatory retirement provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. The judgment of the court below should be affirmed.
