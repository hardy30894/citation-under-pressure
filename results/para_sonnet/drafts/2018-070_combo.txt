**ARGUMENT**

**I. OCSLA Extends Federal Enclave Law to the Outer Continental Shelf, and Enclave Doctrine Confines State Law to Filling Gaps in Federal Regulation**

Congress declared that federal law governs the Shelf and its fixed structures "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). This Court has already construed that choice: "the purpose of the [OCSLA] was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf." Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). Rodrigue treated fixed platforms as the functional equivalent of federal enclaves, not as ordinary state territory in which state and federal law coexist as they do onshore. Id. at 355–56, 365–66.

That characterization matters because this Court's enclave precedents establish that state law imported into a federal enclave occupies a subordinate, interstitial role—it operates only until, and only to the extent that, Congress legislates on the same subject. In Chicago, Rock Island & Pacific Railway Co. v. McGlinn, 114 U.S. 542 (1885), the Court held that when territory passes to exclusive federal jurisdiction, the laws of the ceding state continue in force only until altered by Congress, because such laws "are needed for the protection of the rights of the inhabitants" and continue "except as far as such laws are inconsistent with the [federal government]." Id. at 546. The doctrine is not one of dual, coextensive regulation; it is a doctrine of gap-filling continuity, displaced the moment federal legislative will occupies the field. James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94 (1940), reaffirmed that principle, holding that a state safety statute continued to operate on a federal enclave only because Congress had not yet enacted a comprehensive substitute; the Court made clear that state law persists "until Congress ... substitutes ... its own regulations." Id. at 99–100.

This Court's Assimilative Crimes Act cases confirm the same limiting principle in a closely analogous statutory structure. That Act borrows state criminal law for federal enclaves, but only for conduct "not made punishable by any enactment of Congress." United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286, 289 (1958). In sustaining the Act's prospective borrowing of state law, this Court emphasized that the assimilated law exists to supply what federal law omits, not to supplement or duplicate an existing federal scheme. Id. at 293–94. Franklin v. United States, 216 U.S. 559 (1910), applied the predecessor statute on the same understanding, assimilating state law only where "no act of Congress" addressed the relevant conduct. Id. at 568.

The same limitation governs regulatory, non-criminal assimilation. In Pacific Coast Dairy, Inc. v. Department of Agriculture, 318 U.S. 285 (1943), this Court held that a State's milk-price regulation could not reach a federal enclave because state law has no independent force there; it enters an enclave only by congressional sufferance, and then only within the space Congress leaves open. Id. at 294–95. And in Johnson v. Yellow Cab Transit Co., 321 U.S. 383 (1944), the Court held that state law could not be applied within an area of exclusive federal jurisdiction where doing so would intrude upon a domain federal authority already occupied. Id. at 390–91. Each decision teaches the same lesson: within federal enclaves, state law survives only in the interstices of federal regulation, not alongside a comprehensive federal scheme addressing the same subject matter.

**II. The FLSA's Comprehensive Wage-and-Hour Scheme Leaves No Gap for State Law to Fill on the Shelf**

Applying that settled enclave principle here, the question under § 1333(a)(2)(A) is not whether California law is merely "not inconsistent" with the FLSA in the sense of imposing no directly conflicting obligation—that is the ordinary preemption inquiry this Court has applied to state regulation of private conduct generally. The question, consistent with McGlinn, Sadrakula, Sharpnack, and Pacific Coast Dairy, is whether federal law already occupies the field, leaving no "void or gap" for assimilated state law to fill. Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). The FLSA comprehensively regulates minimum wages, overtime, and the calculation of compensable working time for employees like respondent. Congress's decision to occupy that field with a detailed federal scheme is itself the reason no assimilated state law is needed or permitted; the enclave analogy this Court drew in Rodrigue would be meaningless if state law could freely supplement a subject Congress has already regulated comprehensively.

**III. The Ninth Circuit's "Applicable and Not Inconsistent" Standard Ignores the Enclave Framework This Court Adopted in Rodrigue**

The court below treated § 1333(a)(2)(A) as an ordinary savings-clause preemption provision, asking only whether state and federal law directly conflict. That approach cannot be reconciled with Rodrigue's holding that the Shelf's fixed structures are to be governed "as if" they were areas of exclusive federal jurisdiction, 395 U.S. at 355, nor with the unbroken line of enclave decisions—McGlinn, Sadrakula, Sharpnack, Franklin, Pacific Coast Dairy, and Johnson—holding that assimilated state law operates only where federal law is silent. The gap-filling standard, not mere non-conflict, is the correct rule, and the judgment below should be reversed.
