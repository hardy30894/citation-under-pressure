## Argument

The district court erred in denying Floyd Perkins's petition for a writ of habeas corpus by requiring him to demonstrate reasonable diligence in addition to making a credible claim of actual innocence. This Court has previously held that a credible claim of actual innocence is sufficient to warrant equitable tolling of AEDPA's statute of limitations. The Warden's argument that the Supreme Court's decision in Pace v. DiGuglielmo imposes an additional burden of reasonable diligence is unpersuasive.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a claim of actual innocence can overcome procedural barriers to habeas relief. In Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), the Court held that a habeas petitioner could overcome a procedural default by showing that he was actually innocent. Similarly, in Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), the Court granted habeas relief to petitioners who were convicted in a mob-dominated trial, noting that the petitioners' claims of innocence were supported by new evidence.

The principle that a credible claim of actual innocence can warrant habeas relief has been consistently applied by the courts. In Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court held that a habeas petitioner who claimed that he had not waived his right to counsel could obtain relief if he could show that he was actually innocent. More recently, this principle was reaffirmed in Walker v. Johnston, 312 U.S. 275 (1941), where the Court held that a habeas petitioner could challenge the constitutionality of his conviction if he could show that he was actually innocent.

The district court's reliance on Pace v. DiGuglielmo is misplaced. While Pace does require a petitioner to demonstrate reasonable diligence in order to equitably toll a statute of limitations, it does not involve a claim of actual innocence. In fact, the Supreme Court has long distinguished between claims of actual innocence and other types of habeas claims. In Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309 (1915), the Court noted that a claim of actual innocence is a fundamental challenge to the validity of a conviction, and that it can overcome procedural barriers to habeas relief.

Other cases have also recognized the importance of actual innocence in habeas proceedings. In Price v. Johnston, 334 U.S. 266 (1948), the Court held that a habeas petitioner could obtain relief if he could show that he was actually innocent, even if he had not exhausted his state remedies. Similarly, in Holiday v. Johnston, 313 U.S. 342 (1941), the Court granted habeas relief to a petitioner who claimed that he was actually innocent, noting that the petitioner's claim was supported by new evidence.

Furthermore, in Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60 (1942), the Court emphasized the importance of ensuring that the guilty are not wrongly imprisoned, stating that "justice, though due to the accused, is due to the accuser also." Id. at 88 (quoting Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 122 (1934)). This principle is particularly relevant in cases like Perkins's, where new evidence has come to light that supports the petitioner's claim of actual innocence.

In United States ex rel. McCann v. Adams, 320 U.S. 220 (1943), the Court also recognized that a claim of actual innocence can be a basis for habeas relief, even if the petitioner has not shown cause for his procedural default. The Court noted that "the history of liberty has largely been the history of observance of procedural safeguards." Id. at 225 (quoting McNabb v. United States, 318 U.S. 332, 347 (1943)).

Finally, in Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), the Court held that a habeas petitioner who was denied effective assistance of counsel could obtain relief if he could show that he was prejudiced by the denial. The Court's decision in Powell emphasizes the importance of ensuring that petitioners receive a fair trial, and that actual innocence claims are given due consideration.

In light of these precedents, it is clear that a credible claim of actual innocence is sufficient to warrant equitable tolling of AEDPA's statute of limitations. The district court erred in requiring Perkins to demonstrate reasonable diligence in addition to making a credible claim of actual innocence. This Court should reverse the district court's decision and remand the case for further proceedings.
