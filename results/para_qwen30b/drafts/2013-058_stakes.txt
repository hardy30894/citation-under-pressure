**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt “retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)—is governed by the plain language of the Bankruptcy Code and settled precedent interpreting its exemptions. The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt because it is not “held for the debtor’s retirement.” That interpretation misreads the statute’s text, ignores the legislative purpose behind retirement fund exemptions, and contradicts binding circuit precedent. The correct rule is that an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt retirement fund under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) so long as it was originally established as a retirement vehicle under the Internal Revenue Code and remains within the statutory framework of tax-deferred retirement savings.

**I. The Bankruptcy Code’s Exemption for Retirement Funds Is Not Limited to Funds Held for the Debtor’s Own Retirement**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt from creditors’ claims “any retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986.” The statute does not condition exemption on the current owner’s intent or future retirement plans. It focuses on the *nature* of the account, not the *use* to which the funds are put by the current holder. The Seventh Circuit’s insistence that the funds must be “held for the owner’s retirement” to qualify as “retirement funds” invents a requirement absent from the text.

The Supreme Court has long held that “the meaning of a statute is to be found in its language, read in context and in light of its purpose.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). Here, the language is unambiguous: the exemption applies to funds in accounts “exempt from taxation” under the Internal Revenue Code. An inherited IRA is unquestionably such an account. It is governed by §408 of the IRC, which provides for tax-deferred growth and is explicitly listed in §522(d)(12). The fact that the beneficiary must begin distributions within a fixed time frame—often within five years—does not strip the account of its tax-deferred status or its character as a retirement savings vehicle. The requirement to distribute funds earlier than the beneficiary’s retirement age is a *feature* of the inherited IRA regime, not a disqualifying flaw.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a withdrawal followed by a gift is inapt. In that hypothetical, the funds are no longer in an IRA and are no longer subject to the tax-deferral regime. But in the case of an inherited IRA, the funds remain in a qualified retirement account under §408(d)(3)(C), subject to the same tax-deferral rules as the original account. The fact that the beneficiary must withdraw the funds within a set period does not transform the account into a general-purpose savings account. As the Fifth Circuit correctly observed in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486, 490 (5th Cir. 2012), “the fact that the funds are not held until retirement does not mean they are not retirement funds.” The account’s purpose—deferred taxation for retirement savings—remains intact.

**II. The Seventh Circuit’s Interpretation Contradicts the Purpose of the Bankruptcy Code’s Exemption**

The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption of retirement funds reflects a fundamental policy: to protect the debtor’s future financial security. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “Congress intended to allow debtors to retain a ‘fresh start’ after bankruptcy, and to do so, it exempted certain assets from the reach of creditors.” *Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 500, 505 (2000). This includes retirement savings, which are essential to a debtor’s long-term economic stability.

The Seventh Circuit’s rule undermines this policy by allowing creditors to seize funds that were originally intended for retirement, even though they remain within the same tax-deferred framework. If an inherited IRA is not exempt simply because the beneficiary must withdraw funds before retirement, then a debtor who inherits a large IRA—perhaps the only substantial asset—can be stripped of that resource, even though it was never intended for current consumption. This outcome is inconsistent with the Code’s goal of preserving a debtor’s ability to rebuild financial independence.

Moreover, the Seventh Circuit’s approach creates a perverse incentive: the more a parent saves in an IRA, the more vulnerable their child’s inheritance becomes in bankruptcy. This contradicts the legislative intent to encourage retirement savings. As the Fifth Circuit noted, “Congress did not intend to penalize families for saving for retirement by making inherited IRAs vulnerable to creditors.” *Chilton*, 674 F.3d at 491. The Seventh Circuit’s rule does precisely that.

**III. The Seventh Circuit’s Decision Creates a Circuit Split and Misapplies Precedent**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision in *Clark* directly conflicts with the Fifth Circuit’s holding in *Chilton*, which has been followed by the Eighth Circuit in *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), and the Ninth Circuit in *In re Hines*, 578 B.R. 888 (9th Cir. BAP 2017). These courts have uniformly held that an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt retirement fund under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it is a tax-deferred retirement account under the IRC.
