**Argument**

Section 241(a)(5) does not reach Fernandez-Vargas because Congress did not clearly make the reinstatement statute apply to aliens who had already reentered and remained in the United States before its effective date. Absent a clear statement, statutes are presumed to operate prospectively only. Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 265-73 (1994); INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 316-20 (2001). That presumption is especially strong where applying the statute would attach new legal consequences to completed conduct. Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-70.

Before IIRIRA, an alien who reentered unlawfully after deportation could, at least in some circumstances, seek discretionary relief from deportation, including adjustment of status. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 293-94, 325. Section 241(a)(5), by contrast, commands that the prior order “is reinstated from its original date” and that the alien “is not eligible and may not apply for any relief” under the INA. 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). But Congress did not say that this new bar would apply to aliens who had already completed their unlawful reentry and had been living here for years before the statute took effect. Where Congress wishes to upset settled expectations, it must do so clearly. Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 272-73; St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 316-20.

That principle controls here. Fernandez-Vargas reentered in 1982 and remained in the United States for fifteen years before IIRIRA became effective. During that time, he could, under then-existing law, pursue adjustment of status through marriage to a United States citizen. See St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 293-94. Congress’s later decision to eliminate eligibility for “any relief” cannot retroactively extinguish the legal consequences of his pre-enactment residence and relationship. The retroactivity inquiry asks whether the statute “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 270. It does so here by converting a long-standing unlawful presence that had not yet been acted upon into a categorical disability against all relief. That is the sort of “new disability” Landgraf forbids. Id. at 269-70.

The Government’s likely reliance on the fact that Fernandez-Vargas remained illegally present after IIRIRA does not defeat the retroactivity objection. A statute is not made prospective simply because it is triggered by continued presence when the operative facts arose before enactment. In St. Cyr, the Court rejected the argument that an alien’s plea-based conviction could be treated as a merely future event because the deportation proceeding occurred later; what mattered was that the alien had relied on the availability of relief when he acted. 533 U.S. at 321-24. Likewise, here, Fernandez-Vargas’s pre-IIRIRA decision to remain in the country and later seek adjustment occurred in a legal regime that did not impose section 241(a)(5)’s absolute bar. Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-70.

Nor can the statute be saved by characterizing the reinstatement order as merely procedural. A law is retroactive when it “takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability.” Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269 (internal quotation marks omitted). Section 241(a)(5) does exactly that: it strips eligibility for relief that existed when Fernandez-Vargas reentered and lived here, and it does so without any individualized hearing on the equities Congress previously allowed immigration authorities to consider. See St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 325-26.

Finally, the Tenth Circuit’s approach conflicts with the Court’s retroactivity cases because it focuses on the absence of a “protectable expectation” rather than on whether Congress clearly prescribed retroactive application. The inquiry begins with congressional intent; only if Congress has not spoken clearly does the presumption against retroactivity apply. Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 280; St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 316-20. Congress did not clearly provide that section 241(a)(5) would apply to aliens who had already reentered and remained here before April 1, 1997. The statute therefore should not be read to bar Fernandez-Vargas’s application for adjustment of status, and its application here is impermissibly retroactive. 

The judgment should be reversed.
