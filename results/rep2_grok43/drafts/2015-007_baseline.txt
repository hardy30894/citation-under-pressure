I. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Satisfies the Sixth Amendment.

Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find any fact that increases the maximum authorized penalty, because Arizona’s aggravating factors operated “as the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense.” Id. at 609 (quoting Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 494 n.19 (2000)). Florida’s scheme differs in structure. The jury is instructed on the statutory aggravating circumstances, deliberates, and returns an advisory verdict that death is appropriate. That verdict necessarily embodies the jury’s determination that at least one aggravator exists and outweighs mitigation. The trial judge then independently reviews the same evidence and issues written findings. Because the jury’s recommendation supplies the essential factual predicate for a death sentence, the scheme complies with Ring.

This Court has repeatedly upheld non-unanimous jury determinations in capital cases. See, e.g., Schad v. Arizona, 501 U.S. 624, 630-31 (1991) (plurality opinion) (no constitutional requirement of unanimity on alternative theories of first-degree murder); Apodaca v. Oregon, 406 U.S. 404, 411 (1972) (Sixth Amendment does not mandate unanimous verdicts in state criminal trials). Florida’s requirement of a majority vote for a death recommendation is therefore consistent with these precedents.

II. Neither the Sixth nor the Eighth Amendment Requires a Jury Determination of Mental Retardation.

Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits execution of intellectually disabled offenders but expressly “le[ft] to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” Id. at 317. Florida has assigned that determination to the trial court, subject to appellate review, and permits the defendant to present evidence of intellectual disability both as a bar to execution and as mitigation. Nothing in Atkins or Ring converts the factual question of intellectual disability into an element that must be found by a jury.

The Eighth Amendment likewise imposes no requirement of jury unanimity. See McKoy v. North Carolina, 494 U.S. 433, 443 (1990) (Eighth Amendment concerns focus on individualized sentencing, not procedural uniformity among states). Florida’s outlier status among capital jurisdictions does not itself render its procedures cruel and unusual. See Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957, 990 (1991) (plurality opinion) (“Diversity in the States’ criminal procedures is one of the virtues of federalism.”).

Because the Florida Supreme Court correctly applied these settled principles, the judgment should be affirmed.
