**ARGUMENT**

The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction over this dispute. The Pressls’ complaint asks a state court to interpret a privately negotiated easement under Virginia property law. Nothing on the face of that complaint invokes federal law, and neither 28 U.S.C. § 1331 nor 16 U.S.C. § 825p supplies the missing federal question. Because “the party seeking removal bears the burden of demonstrating that removal jurisdiction is proper,” and “[i]f federal jurisdiction is doubtful, a remand is necessary,” *Mulcahey v. Columbia Organic Chemicals Co.*, 29 F.3d 148, 151 (4th Cir. 1994), this Court must vacate the judgment below and remand the case to state court.

**I. The well-pleaded complaint asserts only state law claims.**

Federal question jurisdiction exists only when a federal question appears on the face of a properly pleaded complaint, “unaided by anything alleged in anticipation of avoidance of defenses.” *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). “[A] case may not be removed to federal court on the basis of a federal defense … even if the defense is anticipated in the plaintiff’s complaint.” *Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams*, 482 U.S. 386, 393 (1987). The Pressls’ complaint seeks a declaration of their rights under a 1960 flowage easement and asks the court to construe language such as “possess and use said premises in any manner not inconsistent with” APCO’s rights. That is a question of Virginia contract and property law. APCO’s only response is that FERC’s licensing scheme governs the property—a federal defense that, under *Caterpillar*, cannot manufacture jurisdiction that the complaint itself does not create.

**II. Because this is a declaratory judgment action, the Court must look to the character of the threatened coercive suit, which likewise sounds in state law.**

Declaratory relief does not enlarge federal jurisdiction. “Federal courts have regularly taken original jurisdiction over declaratory judgment suits in which, if the declaratory judgment defendant brought a coercive action to enforce its rights, that suit would necessarily present a federal question.” *Franchise Tax Bd. v. Construction Laborers Vacation Trust*, 463 U.S. 1, 19 (1983); *see also Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.*, 339 U.S. 667, 673 (1950). Here, the coercive action that the Pressls’ suit forestalls is not a federal enforcement action but a state law claim—APCO’s assertion of a right to remove the Pressls’ dock under the terms of its easement, sounding in Virginia property law, not the Federal Power Act. Because the anticipated coercive suit itself would not “necessarily present a federal question,” *Franchise Tax Bd.*, 463 U.S. at 19, the declaratory posture of this case supplies no independent basis for federal jurisdiction.

**III. The narrow *Grable* exception for state claims embedded with a substantial federal issue does not apply.**

The mere presence of a federal issue in a state law claim does not automatically confer federal question jurisdiction. *Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 813 (1986). Jurisdiction lies only in the “special and small category” of cases where the state law claim necessarily raises a federal issue, actually disputed, substantial, and capable of resolution in federal court without disturbing the congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005); *Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh*, 547 U.S. 677, 699 (2006) (describing *Grable* as covering only a “special and small category” of cases).

This case does not fit that category. Resolving the Pressls’ claim requires nothing more than applying ordinary Virginia rules of contract and easement construction to the 1960 grant’s text. The FERC license is, at most, background context for why the easement was created; the complaint does not require the court to interpret any FERC order, license condition, or provision of the Federal Power Act. Because the federal issue APCO invokes is neither necessarily raised nor substantial, and because federalizing ordinary easement disputes between private landowners and hydroelectric licensees would disturb the state-federal balance that *Grable* was careful to preserve, jurisdiction cannot rest on this theory. *See Grable*, 545 U.S. at 319–20.

**IV. Section 825p does not supply jurisdiction because this is not a suit to enforce a liability created by the Federal Power Act.**

Section 825p grants district courts jurisdiction over “violations of this chapter or the rules, regulations, and orders thereunder” and suits “to enforce any liability or duty created by” the Act. 16 U.S.C. § 825p. The Pressls do not allege that APCO violated the Federal Power Act, a FERC regulation, or any license order; they allege that APCO misconstrued a private easement. Courts have recognized that disputes over rights and duties arising from private agreements connected to a hydroelectric project, rather than from the Act itself, do not arise under § 825p. *See Sayles Hydro Assocs. v. Maughan*, 985 F.2d 451, 454–55 (9th Cir. 1993) (state law claims concerning rights under agreements related to a FERC-licensed project do not arise under the Federal Power Act). The same is true here.

**V. Any doubt about jurisdiction must be resolved in favor of remand.**

Removal statutes are strictly construed against removal, and “if federal jurisdiction is doubtful, a remand is necessary.” *Mulcahey*, 29 F.3d at 151; *see also Dixon v. Coburg Dairy, Inc.*, 369 F.3d 811, 816 (4th Cir. 2004) (en banc) (removal jurisdiction is strictly construed). At minimum, serious doubt surrounds APCO’s jurisdictional theories. That doubt compels remand.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should vacate the judgment of the district court and remand the case with instructions to remand to the Circuit Court for Franklin County, Virginia.
