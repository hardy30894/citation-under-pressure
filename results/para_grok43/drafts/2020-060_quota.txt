**ARGUMENT**

The 2004 consent decree did not resolve Guam’s liability within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Guam therefore retains its right to pursue cost recovery under § 107(a). See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 161 (2004) (explaining that § 107 permits a PRP that has incurred response costs to recover from other PRPs).

Section 113(f)(3)(B) authorizes contribution only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action.” The decree’s substantive terms and express disclaimer demonstrate that no such resolution occurred. The decree states it was entered “without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3, J.A. 92. This Court has recognized that a PRP’s refusal to concede liability weighs against finding resolution under § 113(f)(3)(B). Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015). Other circuits agree that “the mere fact that [a party] refused to admit liability is not enough to exempt [a consent] [d]ecree from the reach of section 113(f)(3)(B)” only when substantive obligations otherwise compel that conclusion; the disclaimer remains relevant. Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries, Inc., 937 F.3d 928, 931 (7th Cir. 2019); see also Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co., 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017).

The decree addressed Clean Water Act violations, not CERCLA response costs. United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (CERCLA targets hazardous-substance releases). Its cover-system requirement does not transform the settlement into a CERCLA resolution. Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009) (CERCLA liability turns on specific statutory predicates).

Even if the decree had triggered § 113(f)(3)(B), Guam could still maintain its timely § 107 claim. The statute of limitations for § 107 actions is six years, CERCLA § 113(g)(2)(B), while contribution actions are limited to three, § 113(g)(3). General Electric Co. v. Jackson, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010) (CERCLA encourages prompt cleanup). Forcing Guam into an expired contribution action would frustrate that purpose. O’Neil v. Picillo, 883 F.2d 176, 178–79 (1st Cir. 1989) (joint-and-several liability under § 107 ensures responsible parties bear cleanup costs).

Guam’s suit therefore remains timely under § 107(a). The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
