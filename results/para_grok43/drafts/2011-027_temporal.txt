The equal footing doctrine vests in the State, upon its admission to the Union, title to the beds of all waters then navigable in fact. Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230 (1845). That title is determined by the condition of the waters at the precise date of statehood. Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894).

Navigability for title turns on whether the river "was used, or was susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce" at that historic moment. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870). Evidence of actual use before and after statehood is competent to establish the river's capacity at the critical date, provided natural conditions remained unchanged. United States v. Cress, 243 U.S. 316, 321 (1917); Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 586 (1922).

A river that is navigable in its natural state throughout its course within the State passes in its entirety; separate proof of navigability for each discrete reach is unnecessary when the evidence shows continuous susceptibility to commercial use. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 77 (1931); see also Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 77, 85 (1922). The trial court therefore properly received historical records, explorer accounts, and period testimony demonstrating that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were highways for trade in 1889. Such materials, authenticated under settled rules governing ancient documents and official publications, establish the requisite navigability at statehood without further segmentation.

Because title vested in the State under these principles, the Hydroelectric Resources Act authorizes compensation for the occupation of those beds. The judgment below rests on a correct application of the constitutional test and should be affirmed.
