The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that it could review the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury, when determining whether the plain error in Greer's trial affected his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. Under plain-error review, an appellate court may reverse a lower court's decision only if there is an error that is plain, affects the defendant's substantial rights, and seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993).

When assessing whether an error affected a defendant's substantial rights, the court must determine whether the error was prejudicial, i.e., whether it affected the outcome of the trial. Id. To make this determination, the Eleventh Circuit properly considered the entire record, including Greer's prior convictions, which were not presented to the jury. This approach is consistent with the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), where the Court held that an appellate court may review the entire record, including matters outside the trial transcript, when assessing the effect of plain error on a defendant's substantial rights. Id. at 74-75.

Greer's argument that the Eleventh Circuit erred by looking beyond the evidence presented to the jury is unpersuasive. The plain-error standard does not limit the appellate court's review to only the evidence presented to the jury. Rather, it allows the court to consider all relevant information, including the entire record, to determine whether the error was prejudicial. See United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004) (explaining that the plain-error standard requires the court to consider "all the circumstances" when assessing prejudice).

Furthermore, the Eleventh Circuit's consideration of Greer's prior convictions was not an improper expansion of the record, but rather a necessary examination of the relevant facts to determine whether the error was prejudicial. As the Supreme Court has recognized, an appellate court's review of the entire record is essential to ensuring that a defendant's substantial rights are protected. See Vonn, 535 U.S. at 74-75.

In this case, the Eleventh Circuit's review of the entire record, including Greer's prior convictions, was proper and necessary to determine whether the plain error in his trial affected his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The court correctly applied the plain-error standard, and its decision should be affirmed.
