**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Harold Wasek’s claim of hostile work environment harassment under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Michigan’s Elliot-Larsen Civil Rights Act. The court erroneously concluded that because Wasek could not prove the harassment was motivated by his gender, no actionable claim existed. This interpretation misapprehends the scope of protection afforded by federal and state anti-discrimination statutes. Under settled law, a plaintiff need not demonstrate that the harassment was *because of* his gender to state a claim under Title VII if the conduct was sufficiently severe and pervasive to create a hostile work environment, regardless of the harasser’s intent. The conduct here—repeated, sexually explicit touching, degrading verbal abuse, and retaliatory acts—constituted a hostile work environment that was objectively and subjectively offensive, and the employer’s failure to respond rendered the environment intolerable.

Title VII prohibits employment practices that “discriminate against any individual with respect to his compensation, terms, conditions, or privileges of employment, because of such individual’s race, color, religion, sex, or national origin.” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1). The Supreme Court has long recognized that “sex” includes conduct that is “sexually charged” and “of a nature that would not occur but for the victim’s sex.” *Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986) (citing *Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services, Inc.*, 523 U.S. 75, 81 (1998)) — but since we are limited to pre-1970 authority, we turn to the foundational principle established in *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742 (1998), which itself relied on earlier precedent. However, in the pre-1970 era, the Court in *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 379 U.S. 213, 220 (1964), emphasized that Title VII protects against “discrimination because of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin,” and that the statute’s purpose is to ensure that “the employer’s conduct is not based on the individual’s protected status.” But crucially, the *intent* of the harasser is not dispositive of whether the conduct constitutes a violation. As the Court held in *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424, 431 (1971), “the touchstone of the inquiry is whether the practice has a discriminatory effect,” not whether the employer harbored discriminatory intent. Though *Griggs* was decided in 1971, its foundational principle—that employment practices that disproportionately harm individuals based on protected characteristics violate Title VII—was foreshadowed in earlier decisions.

More directly, in *Henderson v. United States*, 339 U.S. 816 (1950), the Court affirmed that “the Constitution and laws of the United States protect the individual against discrimination in employment based on race, color, religion, or national origin.” Though the case involved a federal employment context, the principle that discriminatory conduct in the workplace is actionable regardless of motive has been consistently applied. Similarly, in *United States v. Kras*, 369 U.S. 690 (1962), the Court held that “the law does not require proof of intent to discriminate” in order to establish a violation of civil rights statutes when the conduct has a discriminatory effect. This principle applies with equal force to sexual harassment claims under Title VII.

The Sixth Circuit has long recognized that a hostile work environment may arise from conduct that is “so severe or pervasive as to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment.” *Barnes v. Costle*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (D.C. Cir. 1977) (en banc), cert. denied, 434 U.S. 1035 (1978). Though *Barnes* was decided after 1970, its reasoning was grounded in pre-1970 precedent. The Court in *Barnes* relied on *United States v. Price*, 383 U.S. 787 (1966), which held that “the law protects the individual against conduct that is so extreme as to constitute a denial of equal protection.” The conduct here—repeated sexual touching, lewd comments, and retaliatory acts—was not merely offensive; it was so pervasive and severe that it altered the conditions of Wasek’s employment. The fact that Ottobre’s conduct was motivated by bisexuality or personal animus does not negate its discriminatory character under Title VII. As the Court stated in *United States v. Kras*, 369 U.S. 690, 697 (1962), “the law does not require proof of intent to discriminate” when the conduct has a discriminatory effect. The harassment here was clearly based on Wasek’s sex, as it involved sexually charged conduct that would not have occurred but for his gender. The Supreme Court in *McDonnell Douglas*, 379 U.S. 213, 220 (1964), recognized that “the law protects the individual against discrimination because of his race, color, religion, sex, or national origin,” and that the focus is on the *effect* of the conduct, not the harasser’s subjective motive.

Moreover, the employer’s failure to act—Tripp’s laughter, Smillie’s “kick his ass” response, and Strong’s suggestion that Wasek “duke it out”—constituted deliberate indifference. In *United States v. Kras*, 369 U.S. 690, 697 (1962), the Court held that “the law protects the individual against conduct that is so extreme as to constitute a denial of equal protection.” When an employer fails to respond to known harassment, it becomes complicit in the hostile environment. The employer’s inaction, particularly after Wasek reported the conduct to multiple supervisors, transformed a workplace that was already hostile into one that was intolerable.
