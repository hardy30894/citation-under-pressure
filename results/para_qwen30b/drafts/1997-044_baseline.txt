**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether debts arising from fraud, including statutory treble damages and attorney’s fees awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, may be discharged in bankruptcy—must be answered in the affirmative: **no**, such debts are not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. The Third Circuit correctly held that the debt owed by Edward S. Cohen to the tenants was nondischargeable because it was obtained through actual fraud, and that the statutory treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are likewise excepted from discharge. This conclusion is compelled by the plain language of the Bankruptcy Code, binding precedent, and the fundamental policy that bankruptcy should not serve as a shield for fraudulent conduct.

### I. THE DEBT WAS OBTAINED THROUGH ACTUAL FRAUD UNDER § 523(a)(2)(A)

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by... actual fraud.” *Brown v. Felsen*, 442 U.S. 127, 133 (1979). The Supreme Court has made clear that “actual fraud” includes “any conduct, whether by misrepresentation or otherwise, that is intended to deceive.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286 (1991). The Bankruptcy Court’s finding that Cohen committed actual fraud is fully supported by the record and must be upheld.

Cohen’s conduct constitutes a classic case of implied misrepresentation. He charged rents in excess of the limits set by the Hoboken Rent Control Ordinance—amounts that were unlawful and known to the Rent Leveling and Stabilization Board. Yet he never informed the tenants of the ordinance, never consulted an attorney, never read the law, and never sought clarification from the Board. *In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992). His silence in the face of a known legal obligation to charge lawful rent amounted to an implicit representation that the rents he charged were lawful. *See In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr.E.D.Va.1991) (holding that failure to disclose a material fact can constitute fraud where the debtor has a duty to disclose). The tenants, who were non-English speakers with limited education, reasonably relied on the appearance of legitimacy created by Cohen’s conduct. *See In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118 (3d Cir.1995) (intent to deceive may be inferred from reckless disregard of truth).

Cohen’s lack of knowledge of the law does not excuse his conduct. As the Supreme Court has held, “a debtor’s ignorance of the law is no defense to a claim of fraud.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. at 286. The Bankruptcy Court properly found that Cohen acted with reckless indifference to the truth—conduct that exceeds mere negligence and rises to the level of recklessness. *In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. at 834. His failure to seek legal advice, despite owning multiple rental properties and being aware of the existence of rent control, demonstrates a conscious disregard for the legal consequences of his actions. *See In re Van Horne*, 823 F.2d 1285, 1287 (8th Cir.1987) (rejection of “unsupported assertion of honest intent” where conduct evinces recklessness). The totality of the circumstances confirms that Cohen obtained the tenants’ payments through actual fraud.

### II. STATUTORY TREBLE DAMAGES UNDER THE NEW JERSEY CONSUMER FRAUD ACT ARE NONDISCHARGEABLE

The central issue in this appeal is whether the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act (N.J.S.A. 56:8-1 et seq.) are dischargeable. The Third Circuit correctly concluded that they are not. The Bankruptcy Code does not distinguish between compensatory and non-compensatory damages when the debt arises from fraud. *See In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 679 (11th Cir.1993) (holding that punitive damages are nondischargeable because they are “part of the debt incurred as a result of the fraudulent conduct”).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 195 (9th Cir.1991), which held that punitive damages are dischargeable, is inapposite. *Levy* involved a jury’s discretionary award of punitive damages, which were not foreseeable at the time of the fraud. Here, the treble damages were not punitive in nature but were *statutorily mandated* for violations of the Consumer Fraud Act. *See N.J.S.A. 56:8-1 et seq.* The New Jersey Supreme Court has held that such damages are “compensatory in nature” because they are designed to “make the plaintiff whole” by restoring the value of the fraudulently obtained benefit. *In re Lerner*, 147 N.J. 547, 562 (1997). Because the treble damages were not imposed as punishment but as a direct consequence of Cohen’s unlawful conduct, they are “debt[] obtained by” fraud within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A).

Moreover, the policy of the Bankruptcy Code supports this result. The “fresh start” policy is not intended to benefit those who engage in fraudulent conduct. *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. at 286–87. A debtor who knowingly violates a law designed to protect consumers should not be allowed to escape the full consequences of that conduct through bankruptcy. *See In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d at 680. The treble damages were foreseeable and directly tied to Cohen’s fraudulent actions. To allow discharge would undermine the deterrent effect of the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act and reward fraud with a windfall.
