# ARGUMENT

## I. Nichols v. United States Establishes the Governing Rule: A Valid Uncounseled Conviction May Be Used in a Later Proceeding.

In *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), this Court held that a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid when entered because no sentence of imprisonment was imposed, may be used in a subsequent proceeding "to enhance punishment," even though "the fact of the prior conviction" was thereby used to increase the defendant's sentence. *Id.* at 748–49. The Court expressly reaffirmed *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367 (1979), which held that "the Sixth Amendment does not require the appointment of counsel in any misdemeanor case in which the defendant is not sentenced to a term of imprisonment." *Id.* at 373–74. Because Bryant's tribal convictions did not violate the Sixth Amendment—the Amendment simply does not apply to tribal proceedings—the convictions are, under *Nichols*, valid convictions that the government may use.

The Ninth Circuit's attempt to cabin *Nichols* to the sentencing context, distinguishing it because § 117(a) uses the convictions as an "element" rather than an enhancement, finds no footing in this Court's precedent. This Court has repeatedly explained that recidivist statutes do not reach back and punish the predicate conduct at all. As the Court stated in *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008), "[w]hen a defendant is given a higher sentence under a recidivism statute . . . 100% of the punishment is for the offense of conviction. None is for the prior convictions." *Nichols* itself recognized that recidivist statutes "penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant." 511 U.S. at 747. That principle applies with equal force whether the recidivism finding operates as a sentencing factor or, as in § 117(a), as an element that transforms a misdemeanor into a felony. In either posture, the prior convictions serve the identical function—establishing status—and the reliability calculus under *Nichols* does not change.

## II. Bryant's Tribal Court Convictions Were Constitutionally Valid When Entered.

It is axiomatic that the Bill of Rights, including the Sixth Amendment, does not constrain tribal governments. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896). This Court has reaffirmed that principle in the modern era, explaining that it is "beyond dispute" that the "Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes." *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). Tribal courts instead operate under the procedural protections Congress prescribed in the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1303, which affords a right to counsel narrower than the Sixth Amendment. Because Bryant's tribal proceedings complied with the law actually applicable to them, his convictions were not infirm, unlawful, or unreliable in any constitutional sense. They were valid exercises of what this Court has called the tribes' "retained tribal sovereignty" to punish tribal offenders. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978).

Congress's plenary authority "to legislate in respect to tribal affairs," *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004), squarely permits it to treat those valid convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a), just as Congress may recognize state and federal convictions obtained under those jurisdictions' respective procedural rules. There is no constitutional anomaly in a federal recidivist statute drawing on convictions rendered under a different, but lawful, procedural regime.

## III. Principles of Comity and Respect for Tribal Sovereignty Foreclose Treating Tribal Convictions as Constitutionally Suspect.

This Court has long instructed that federal courts must extend comity to tribal court judgments. *Iowa Mutual Insurance Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987). Federal courts may not use their own procedural expectations as a yardstick to delegitimize valid tribal adjudications, for doing so would "intrude[] needlessly on tribal self-government." *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978). The court below, by conditioning the use of tribal convictions on a showing that tribal courts provided "a right to counsel that is, at minimum, coextensive with the Sixth Amendment," effectively demanded that tribal courts mirror federal constitutional procedure as a precondition to federal recognition of their judgments—an approach in tension with this Court's repeated admonitions against imposing "judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts" as a condition of respecting tribal adjudications.

## IV. The Presumption of Regularity Attaching to Prior Convictions Applies with Full Force Here.

Finally, this Court has recognized a presumption of regularity that attaches to final convictions, placing the burden on a defendant to show the conviction was unconstitutionally obtained under the law applicable to it. *Parke v. Raley*, 506 U.S. 20, 29–30 (1992). Bryant cannot make that showing, because no error occurred: the tribal court had no obligation to appoint counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment, and it violated no law—tribal, federal, or constitutional—in convicting him without doing so.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, an uncounseled tribal court conviction, valid when rendered because the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal proceedings, may serve as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) without offending the Sixth Amendment. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
