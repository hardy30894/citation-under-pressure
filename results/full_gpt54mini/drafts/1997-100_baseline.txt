The Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second attempt to prove a prior conviction allegation after an appellate court has found the evidence insufficient. The Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). When a sentencing proceeding “has the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence,” an adverse determination based on insufficient evidence is an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes. Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981); Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203, 209-210 (1984).

California’s prior-conviction proceeding fits that model. The State chose to litigate the allegation in a separate, bifurcated hearing; Monge waived a jury only as California law permitted; the prosecution was required to prove the allegation beyond a reasonable doubt; the rules of evidence applied; and Monge enjoyed confrontation and cross-examination rights. See In re Yurko, 10 Cal. 3d 857, 862-863 & n.5 (1974); People v. Tenner, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993); People v. Reed, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 224, 228 n.6 (1996). That is not an informal sentencing inquiry of the kind approved in Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949); it is an adversarial adjudication of a discrete factual issue that determines whether Monge’s punishment will be doubled. See Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967).

This Court has already recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause is implicated where the State gets only one full and fair opportunity to prove the facts necessary for an increased punishment. In Burks v. United States, 437 U.S. 1, 16-17 (1978), the Court held that retrial is barred after reversal for evidentiary insufficiency because “the prosecution cannot complain of prejudice, for it has been given one fair opportunity to offer whatever proof it could assemble.” That principle applies with equal force here. The State had its chance to prove that Monge’s prior assault qualified as a serious felony. The Court of Appeal found the proof insufficient. A second proceeding would give the prosecution exactly what Burks forbids: another opportunity to cure evidentiary failure.

Bullington and Rumsey supply the controlling analogy. In Bullington, the Court held that a capital sentencing hearing that “resembled and, indeed, functioned like a trial” triggers double jeopardy protections. Bullington, 451 U.S. at 438. Rumsey reiterated that a sentencing determination reached after an adversarial proceeding on specific factual predicates can be an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes. Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 211-212. The operative question is whether the proceeding is sufficiently trial-like, not whether the label “sentencing” appears on the docket. Id. at 209-210.

The State will argue that Bullington and Rumsey were capital cases, but the Double Jeopardy Clause does not vanish outside the death penalty context. The Constitution protects against multiple prosecutions for the same issue of fact, and the Court has applied double jeopardy principles in a broad range of criminal settings. Pearce, 395 U.S. at 717; Burks, 437 U.S. at 11. Moreover, the Court’s capital/noncapital distinction reflects the unique severity of death, not a categorical rule that sentencing factfinding in noncapital cases is immune from double jeopardy scrutiny. Bullington, 451 U.S. at 438-439; Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 209-210.

The California Supreme Court relied heavily on Williams and on the proposition that a state need not provide trial-like procedures at sentencing. But once California chose to create a formal, adversarial prior-conviction trial, Williams no longer controls. Williams, 337 U.S. at 251, expressly concerned the scope of due process at ordinary sentencing, not a statutory enhancement proceeding with proof beyond a reasonable doubt, evidentiary rules, and confrontation rights. See Specht, 386 U.S. at 610. Having made the allegation a litigated factual issue, the State cannot deny the constitutional consequences of losing on sufficiency.

Finally, treating the prior-conviction allegation as beyond double jeopardy would undermine the rule of Burks and invite repeated prosecutions whenever the State’s proof is inadequate. The Constitution does not permit the government to “hone its trial strategies and evidence” after failing once to prove the facts necessary to increase punishment. Lockhart v. Nelson, 488 U.S. 33, 42 (1988). Because the Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient, retrial of Monge’s prior-conviction allegation is barred.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
