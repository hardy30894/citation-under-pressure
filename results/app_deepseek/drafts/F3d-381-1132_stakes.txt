# ARGUMENT

## I. The Court of Federal Claims erred when it held that the scope of the easement is irrelevant to a physical taking claim.

The Fifth Amendment demands just compensation when the government physically occupies private property. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 421 (1982). Where the government’s right to occupy land derives from an easement, the easement’s terms define the scope of that right. When the government exceeds those terms, its physical presence is unauthorized, and a compensable taking occurs. *Hendler v. United States*, 952 F.2d 1364, 1374–75 (Fed. Cir. 1991).

The Court of Federal Claims dismissed Tuthill’s claim with the assertion that “[t]he scope of the easement is not relevant in this court because it cannot give rise to a physical taking.” That is legal error. The scope of the easement is the very thing that distinguishes authorized occupation from unauthorized occupation. Where a government physical occupation exceeds the scope of an easement, the occupation is a taking, not merely a trespass. This principle has long been recognized. In *St. Louis v. Western Union Telegraph Co.*, 148 U.S. 92, 98–99 (1893), the Supreme Court held that a telegraph company’s use of its street easement to provide general commercial telegraph service exceeded the scope of the easement and constituted a taking requiring compensation. The physical poles and wires remained within the easement’s boundaries; but the unauthorized use of those poles and wires for an unrelated commercial purpose was an additional taking. The same logic applies here.

## II. The BPA’s installation of fiber optic cables with capacity far exceeding its current needs, and its lease of unused fibers for a commercial telecommunications business, exceeds the scope of the easement.

The 1953 easement grants the BPA the right to erect “appurtenant signal lines . . . necessary in connection with” its power transmission lines. The words “appurtenant” and “necessary” limit the privilege to lines that serve the power transmission function. Such an easement “must be construed strictly against the grantee,” *see* *City of St. Louis*, 148 U.S. at 98, and may not be used for a substantially different purpose.

The undisputed facts establish that the BPA installed either 36- or 72-fiber cables across Tuthill’s property while its current needs require only a fraction of those fibers. The BPA admits it installed the excess capacity to meet projected load growth through 2025. It also admits that it currently leases the unused fibers to an unrelated telecommunications company for profit.

That lease is a separate, commercial use. It is not “appurtenant” to the transmission of power. It is not “necessary in connection with” the power lines. The BPA is using Tuthill’s land as a commercial telecommunications corridor—a purpose wholly foreign to the easement that Tuthill’s predecessor granted in 1953. As in *City of St. Louis*, the government cannot convert an easement granted for one purpose into a vehicle for a different business enterprise. 148 U.S. at 98–99.

The trial court observed that “[p]hysical presence of the fiber optic cable over the easement is not a taking.” That analysis is incomplete. The relevant inquiry is whether the *entire* physical presence is authorized. The original power and signal lines were authorized and compensated in 1953. The additional fiber-optic capacity, installed for future needs and third-party leasing, is a new and expanded occupation of Tuthill’s property. The government may not rely on the easement to justify an installation that is larger than necessary for the easement’s purpose and that is used to generate revenue from third parties.

## III. The Court of Federal Claims erred by focusing on the absence of an additional physical intrusion rather than on the unauthorized character of the government’s occupation.

The trial court reasoned that “no additional physical intrusion to [Tuthill’s] land has resulted from the lease to the third parties.” That reasoning mistakes the issue. A physical taking occurs when the government occupies property without authorization, regardless of whether the physical intrusion is large or small, or whether it is a new cable or an expanded use of an existing cable. In *Loretto*, the Supreme Court held that even a small cable box on the exterior of an apartment building constituted a permanent physical occupation and a per se taking. 458 U.S. at 438. Here, the government has occupied a substantial portion of Tuthill’s land with fiber optic cables whose capacity and commercial use go beyond the easement’s grant. The trial court’s focus on the fact that the cable lies within the same corridor as the power lines misses the point: the taking is the use of that corridor for an unauthorized purpose.

The trial court also suggested that Tuthill’s remedy lies in a trespass suit, not a takings action. But a physical occupation by the government that is permanent and unauthorized is a taking, not merely a trespass. *Hendler*, 952 F.2d at 1374–75. Tuthill has alleged a permanent, unauthorized occupation of its land—the very paradigm of a physical taking. Because the government has occupied Tuthill’s property beyond the scope of its easement, and because that occupation is permanent, Tuthill is entitled to just compensation under the Fifth Amendment.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Federal Claims should be reversed and the case remanded for proceedings consistent with this Court’s determination that the government’s use of the easement may constitute a physical taking.
