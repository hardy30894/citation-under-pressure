## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because a reasonable jury could find that the ship breached the vessel’s “active operations” duty under § 905(b) of the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C. § 905(b). In *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos*, the Supreme Court held that a vessel remaining in control of an area or operation must exercise ordinary care and may be liable when its own conduct creates or leaves a hazard during cargo operations. 451 U.S. 156, 167-78 (1981). The Third Circuit has recognized that the vessel’s duties under *Scindia* are not confined to latent defects; when the ship’s crew continues to operate ship’s equipment involved in stevedoring work, the vessel may be liable for negligence in the manner of that active participation. *Kirsch v. Plovidba, 971 F.2d 1026, 1030-33 (3d Cir. 1992)*.

### I. A reasonable jury could find that the ship retained active control over the snatch blocks and hatch-cover system.

The undisputed record shows that the hatch-cover and snatch-block apparatus was ship’s gear, operated by the crew. The crew used the block-and-pulley system to open and close hatch covers, and the snatch blocks themselves were moved by the crew. Unlike ordinary portable gear supplied to the stevedore, these blocks were fixed to the hatches and had to be moved by the ship’s personnel. That makes this case materially different from one in which the danger is wholly created by the stevedore’s own work methods. Under *Scindia*, the vessel owes a duty of reasonable care where it actively involves itself in cargo operations or retains control over the instrumentalities of the work. 451 U.S. at 167-68; *Kirsch*, 971 F.2d at 1030-31.

Here, the hazard Serbin encountered was not merely a condition of the cargo; it was the position of a ship-operated moving part that the crew was responsible to reposition. The snatch block was left in the down position on top of a cargo unit, exactly where the crew’s own procedure required it to be moved before lower-deck cargo could be removed. A jury could conclude that the vessel, through its crew, was actively engaged in the very operation that created the condition Serbin encountered. See *Kirsch*, 971 F.2d at 1031-33 (affirming that a vessel may be liable where it “actively participates” in cargo operations or retains control over the operation that causes injury).

### II. The vessel’s negligence is triable because the crew left a dangerous condition in the work area and Serbin’s response was foreseeable.

The record supports a finding that the ship’s crew left the snatch block in an unsafe position in the longshoremen’s work area. Serbin testified that the block was “where the hookup man would normally stand in the wing,” leaving “no place to run” if anything fell. That testimony, if credited, permits a finding that the ship’s crew created a danger in the course of a ship-controlled operation and left it for the longshoremen to confront. *Scindia* does not permit a vessel to remain passive when it knows, or should know, that its own operation is exposing stevedores to an unreasonable hazard. 451 U.S. at 170-72.

The foreseeability of Serbin’s conduct also matters. Serbin did not invent an unusual or extraordinary response; he attempted to restore the block to the position in which the crew was expected to place it so unloading could continue. He testified that he chose to act because waiting for the crew would waste time and because he had moved blocks before. A jury could find that the ship should have foreseen that longshoremen, under pressure to continue cargo operations, would attempt to move the block themselves rather than stop work indefinitely. Negligence under § 905(b) is a jury question when reasonable minds could differ on whether the vessel’s conduct created an unreasonable risk and whether the longshoreman’s response was within the range of foreseeable reactions. See *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 176-78; *Kirsch*, 971 F.2d at 1032-33.

### III. Summary judgment was improper because comparative negligence, if any, does not bar recovery.

Even if a jury were to find that Serbin acted imprudently in attempting to move the block himself, that would not entitle the ship to judgment as a matter of law. Section 905(b) incorporates comparative fault principles; a longshoreman’s negligence reduces, but does not eliminate, recovery. *Edmonds v. Compagnie Generale Transatlantique*, 443 U.S. 256, 271-72 (1979). Thus, Serbin’s decision to move the block himself could at most diminish damages; it cannot negate the ship’s own negligence where the vessel’s crew left ship gear in a hazardous position during ongoing cargo operations. Id.; *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 165-66.

The district court therefore erred by resolving factual issues that should have been left to the jury: whether the crew’s conduct constituted active negligence, whether the condition was unreasonably dangerous, and whether Serbin’s response was foreseeable. On this record, a rational factfinder could find the ship liable under § 905(b). The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
