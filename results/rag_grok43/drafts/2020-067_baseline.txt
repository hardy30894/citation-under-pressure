**ARGUMENT**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a reviewing court conducting plain-error analysis under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) may consult the entire record—not merely the evidence presented to the jury—to determine whether a forfeited *Rehaif* error affected substantial rights or seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.

Rule 52(b) authorizes correction of a plain error only if it “affect[ed] substantial rights” and “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732, 736 (1993) (quoting *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936)). The first of these inquiries requires the defendant to show “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different.” *Molina-Martinez v. United States*, 578 U.S. 189, 194 (2016). That probability is assessed by examining “the entire record,” because an appellate court “must consider the error in the context of the entire trial.” *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 470 (1997); see also *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 632-633 (2002) (reviewing indictment and sentencing findings outside the jury’s verdict to conclude that the fourth prong was not satisfied).

This Court has repeatedly applied that principle to omitted-element errors. In *Johnson*, the Court looked to the defendant’s own testimony admitting the element the jury had not been instructed to find. 520 U.S. at 470. In *Cotton*, the Court examined the indictment and the presentence report to determine that the drug-quantity error did not seriously affect the fairness or integrity of the proceedings. 535 U.S. at 632-633. Nothing in *Rehaif v. United States*, 588 U.S. 255 (2019), altered this settled practice; the decision addressed only the substantive elements of § 922(g), not the scope of appellate review under Rule 52(b).

Petitioner’s contrary rule—that an appellate court is confined to the trial transcript—would produce anomalous results. It would require vacatur even when the defendant’s prior felony convictions are undisputed and the record conclusively shows he knew of his prohibited status. Such a limitation finds no support in the text of Rule 52(b) or in this Court’s decisions applying it. The Eleventh Circuit therefore acted well within its authority when it consulted Greer’s criminal history to conclude that he had not satisfied the third or fourth prongs of plain-error review.
