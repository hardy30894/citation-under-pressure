**ARGUMENT**

I. THE SEARCH WARRANT FAILED TO ESTABLISH A NECESSARY NEXUS BETWEEN THE PLACE TO BE SEARCHED AND THE EVIDENCE SOUGHT, VIOLATING THE FOURTH AMENDMENT.

The search warrant authorizing the seizure of evidence from Joubert’s parents’ home was constitutionally defective because it failed to establish a sufficient nexus between the premises and the evidence sought. The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant “particularly describ[e] the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized.” *Jones v. United States*, 362 U.S. 257, 261 (1960). This requirement ensures that law enforcement does not conduct general searches under the guise of a warrant. *Winston v. United States*, 336 U.S. 635, 640 (1949).

Here, the affidavit relied solely on speculative assertions about Joubert’s past conduct and his alleged intent to destroy evidence—without any direct link between those actions and the specific residence. While the affiant noted that Joubert had moved into his parents’ home in late May or early June 2012, he offered no evidence that Joubert resided there at the time of the search, nor did he establish that Joubert had ever used the home as a base for storing digital media or photographs of minors. The mere fact that Joubert lived with his parents during the investigation does not justify a warrant for their home absent more concrete indicia of possession or control over the items sought.

Moreover, the affiant’s conclusion that “persons engaged in the molestation and exploitation of minors often maintain possession and/or control of physical or electronic documents pertaining to their victims” is insufficient to satisfy the particularity requirement. *United States v. Ventresca*, 380 U.S. 102, 108 (1965), held that a warrant must be supported by facts showing that the evidence is likely to be found at the specified location. Here, the affidavit contained no such facts. The only connection between the home and the evidence was the claim that Joubert “tore apart the computer tower” and “stacked the removed components inside his parents’ home”—a statement made by SJ, an unreliable informant whose credibility was never tested under cross-examination. *United States v. Rizzo*, 337 F.2d 100, 103 (2d Cir. 1964) (holding that hearsay statements cannot alone support probable cause where they are uncorroborated and lack independent verification).

Even if the court were to accept the possibility that Joubert stored evidence at his parents’ home, the warrant still failed to meet constitutional standards. *Ker v. California*, 374 U.S. 23, 37 (1963), emphasized that a warrant must not authorize a fishing expedition. The warrant here authorized the search of “any and all computers or related storage devices,” “cameras,” and “photographs, electronic images, and videos of minors” without limitation. Such a broad sweep violates the principle that warrants must be narrowly tailored to the evidence sought. *Brinegar v. United States*, 338 U.S. 160, 175–76 (1949), recognized that the scope of a search must be reasonable in light of the circumstances, and that general warrants are unconstitutional.

The absence of a nexus renders the entire search invalid. *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452, 463 (1932), established that a warrant must describe the place to be searched with precision so that officers do not exceed their authority. The warrant here described the premises as “the residence of Robert Joubert’s parents,” but provided no factual basis linking Joubert’s criminal activity to that residence. Without such a foundation, the warrant violated the Fourth Amendment.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN ADMITTING TESTIMONY OF UNCHARGED CHILD MOLESTATION.

The district court erred in admitting testimony regarding Joubert’s alleged abuse of SJ, a non-biological son, which occurred decades earlier and was not charged. Under *Hawkins v. United States*, 356 U.S. 260, 264 (1958), evidence of other crimes is generally inadmissible unless it serves a legitimate purpose such as proving motive, intent, or identity. Even then, its admission must be strictly limited. *United States v. Berrigan*, 378 F.2d 141, 145 (3d Cir. 1967), clarified that evidence of prior misconduct may be admitted only when it is relevant to a material issue and not merely cumulative.

Here, the testimony about SJ was irrelevant to the charges against Joubert. The indictment concerned sexual exploitation of children in 2002–2004 involving KC, a different victim. The alleged abuse of SJ occurred in the mid-1980s, nearly two decades before the charged conduct. The temporal distance and lack of similarity in modus operandi render the evidence highly prejudicial and substantially more damaging than probative. *United States v. Hines*, 332 F.2d 522, 525 (5th Cir. 1964), held that evidence of prior offenses is inadmissible when it tends to suggest a character trait rather than a specific intent or plan.

Furthermore, the testimony was introduced not to prove identity or pattern but to establish a propensity for abuse. *United States v. Douds*, 332 F.2d 517, 520 (2d Cir. 1964), warned that allowing such evidence risks transforming the trial into a moral judgment rather than a legal inquiry. The jury was thus misled into believing that Joubert was a habitual offender simply because he allegedly abused one individual long ago.
