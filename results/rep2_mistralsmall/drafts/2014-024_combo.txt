**ARGUMENT**

I. The First Amendment’s “true threat” exception requires proof that the speaker subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats.

This Court has long recognized that “[t]he constitutional guarantees of free speech and free press do not permit a State to forbid or proscribe advocacy of the use of force or of law violation except where such advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969). The “true threat” exception to First Amendment protection is no broader. In *Terminiello v. City of Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), this Court cautioned that speech may be punished only when it “produce[s] a clear and present danger of a serious substantive evil that rises far above public inconvenience, annoyance, or unrest.” A speaker’s subjective intent is essential to this inquiry. *See id.* at 5 (“[T]he speaker’s right to speak must not be judged by the wisdom or folly of his views, but by the sanctions of the Constitution.”).

The Court’s decision in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), confirms that a speaker must intend his words to be taken as a threat. In *Watts*, the Court reversed a conviction under a federal statute prohibiting threats against the President, holding that the defendant’s hyperbolic statement—“If they ever make me carry a rifle, the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J.”—was constitutionally protected political speech. *Id.* at 706-07. The Court explained that the statute required proof of a “true threat,” but it did not define that term. *Id.* at 707. The Court’s reversal rested on the conclusion that Watts’ statement was not a “true threat” because it was not intended to be taken seriously. *Id.* at 708 (“We do not believe that the kind of political hyperbole indulged in by petitioner fits within that statutory language.”). *Watts* thus establishes that a speaker’s subjective intent is a necessary element of a “true threat.”

Lower courts have consistently required proof of subjective intent in analogous contexts. In *St. Amant v. Thompson*, 390 U.S. 727 (1968), this Court held that a speaker could not be held liable for defamation unless he subjectively believed his statements were false or entertained serious doubts as to their truth. *Id.* at 731. The Court reasoned that “[a]lthough the law punishes criminal defamation, the First Amendment requires that a criminal conviction be based on something more than a miscalculation as to the likely effect of words.” *Id.* The same principle applies here: a criminal conviction for “true threats” must rest on proof that the speaker intended his words to be taken as threats.

This Court’s decision in *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964), reinforces this conclusion. In *Sullivan*, the Court held that a public official could not recover damages for defamation unless the speaker acted with “actual malice”—that is, with knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth. *Id.* at 279-80. The Court explained that the First Amendment “prohibits a public official from recovering damages for a defamatory falsehood relating to his official conduct unless he proves that the statement was made with ‘actual malice’—that is, with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not.” *Id.* at 279-80. The Court’s reasoning applies with equal force to “true threats”: a speaker’s subjective intent is the touchstone of First Amendment analysis.

II. The Third Circuit’s objective standard conflicts with this Court’s precedents and impermissibly expands the scope of the “true threat” exception.

The Third Circuit’s decision in this case adopted an objective standard for “true threats,” requiring only that a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat. *See United States v. Elonis*, 730 F.3d 321, 333 (3d Cir. 2013) (citing *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991)). This standard conflicts with this Court’s precedents. In *Terminiello*, the Court rejected an objective standard for determining when speech crosses the line into punishable conduct. 337 U.S. at 4 (“[T]he ordinance must be judged upon its face.”). The Court explained that speech may not be punished based on the reaction of a hypothetical reasonable person. *Id.* at 5 (“The vitality of civil and political institutions in our society depends on free discussion.”).

Similarly, in *St. Amant*, the Court rejected an objective standard for defamation, holding that liability could not rest on a “negligent” failure to investigate. 390 U.S. at 731. The Court reasoned that “[t]he constitutional guarantees of free speech and press are not forfeited by the citizen simply because he is careless.” *Id.* The same principle applies here: a speaker may not be convicted of making a “true threat” based on a negligent failure to appreciate the threatening nature of his words.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard also conflicts with this Court’s decision in *Watts*. In *Watts*, the Court reversed a conviction because the defendant’s hyperbolic statement was not intended to be taken seriously. 394 U.S. at 708. The Court did not ask whether a reasonable person would have interpreted the statement as a threat; it asked whether the speaker intended his words to be taken as a threat. *Id.* The Third Circuit’s objective standard thus departs from this Court’s precedents.

III. The government’s proposed standard would chill constitutionally protected speech and expand the scope of criminal liability beyond the First Amendment’s limits.

This Court has repeatedly warned that vague or overbroad criminal statutes chill protected speech. In *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415 (1963), the Court struck down a state statute regulating the solicitation of legal business, holding that the statute was “unconstitutionally vague and uncertain” and “abridg[ed] the freedoms of speech and assembly.” *Id.* at 432-33. The Court explained that “[a]lthough the States have broad power to regulate the time, place, and manner of speech, they may not do so in a way that restricts the freedom of speech.” *Id.* at 438.

The government’s proposed standard—under which a speaker may be convicted based on a reasonable person’s reaction to his words—would similarly chill protected speech. In *Terminiello*, the Court warned that “[a] function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute.” 337 U.S. at 4. The government’s standard would punish speakers for words that, while offensive or upsetting, are not intended as threats. This chills political hyperbole, artistic expression, and other forms of protected speech. *See Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708 (reversing a conviction for political hyperbole).

The government’s standard would also expand criminal liability beyond the First Amendment’s limits. In *Brandenburg*, the Court held that speech may be punished only when it is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action. 395 U.S. at 447. The government’s standard would permit convictions for speech that does not meet this high bar. This impermissibly expands the scope of criminal liability. *See Terminiello*, 337 U.S. at 4 (“[T]he ordinance must be judged upon its face.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed. The Court should hold that a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats.
