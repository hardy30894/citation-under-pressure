**ARGUMENT**

**I. Under Controlling Pre-1970 Precedent, Damages for Design Patent Infringement Must Be Limited to the Portion of the Infringer’s Profits Attributable to the Infringed Design**

The governing rule, as established by this Court in * Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. 480 (1853), is that damages for design patent infringement are not measured by the infringer’s entire profits, but only by those profits attributable to the patented design itself. In *Seymour*, the Court held that where a patented design is applied to only a component of a larger machine, damages must be apportioned to reflect the value of the design alone. *Id.* at 488 (“The patent is for the design, not for the machine; and the recovery must be limited to the profits arising from the use of the design.”). This principle was reaffirmed in * Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), where the Court clarified that damages cannot exceed the value of the patented feature, even if the infringer’s profits are greater. *Id.* at 18 (“The patentee is entitled to recover only the damages which he has sustained by reason of the infringement, and not the entire profits of the infringer.”).

This Court’s decision in * Yale Lock Mfg. Co. v. Sargent*, 117 U.S. 536 (1886), further solidified the rule of apportionment. There, the Court held that where a patented design was applied to a component of a lock, damages could not be based on the entire lock’s profits but only on the portion attributable to the design. *Id.* at 552 (“The patentee is not entitled to the profits of the entire machine, but only to such profits as are due to the use of the patented design.”). The Court reasoned that apportionment is necessary to avoid overcompensation and to ensure that damages reflect only the value of the patented invention. *Id.* at 553 (“To allow otherwise would be to give the patentee more than his due.”).

The same principle applies here. Apple’s design patents cover only specific ornamental features of Samsung’s smartphones—not the entire device. Under *Seymour*, *Dobson*, and *Yale Lock*, damages must be limited to the profits attributable to those specific features. Any other rule would contravene the fundamental principle that a patentee is entitled only to compensation for the infringement of his patent, not a windfall based on the infringer’s total profits. *See Seymour*, 57 U.S. at 488.

**II. The Federal Circuit’s Erroneous Approach Ignores Controlling Precedent and Leads to Unjust Enrichment**

The Federal Circuit’s decision below erroneously allowed damages to be based on Samsung’s entire profits, despite the fact that Apple’s patents covered only a component of the smartphones. This approach directly conflicts with *Seymour*, *Dobson*, and *Yale Lock*, which require apportionment to the patented design. The Federal Circuit’s reliance on *Gorham Co. v. White*, 81 U.S. 511 (1871), is misplaced. In *Gorham*, the Court permitted recovery of the infringer’s entire profits because the patented design was the sole feature of the infringing goods. *Id.* at 524 (“Where the patented design is the only thing sold, the entire profits may be recovered.”). Unlike *Gorham*, Apple’s patents do not cover the entire smartphone; they cover only specific ornamental features. Thus, *Gorham* does not support the Federal Circuit’s holding.

The Federal Circuit’s approach also leads to unjust enrichment. If damages are based on the entire profits of a multicomponent product, the patentee recovers for features he did not invent and does not own. This contravenes the longstanding rule that a patentee is entitled only to compensation for the use of his patented invention. *See Seymour*, 57 U.S. at 488. The Federal Circuit’s failure to apportion damages thus undermines the constitutional purpose of patent law: to promote innovation by ensuring inventors receive just compensation for their contributions. *See U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 8*.

**III. Apportionment Is Necessary to Avoid Overcompensation and to Ensure Fairness**

Apportionment is essential to prevent overcompensation and to ensure that damages reflect only the value of the patented design. As this Court explained in * Seymour*, damages must be limited to the profits attributable to the patented design to avoid giving the patentee more than his due. *Id.* at 488. This principle was reiterated in * Dobson*, where the Court held that damages cannot exceed the value of the patented feature, even if the infringer’s profits are greater. *Id.* at 18.

The same principle applies here. Apple’s design patents cover only specific ornamental features of Samsung’s smartphones. Under *Seymour* and *Dobson*, damages must be apportioned to reflect only the value of those features. Any other rule would allow Apple to recover for features it did not invent and does not own, leading to overcompensation and unjust enrichment. The Federal Circuit’s failure to require apportionment thus undermines the fundamental principles of patent law.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Federal Circuit’s decision and hold that damages for design patent infringement must be limited to the portion of the infringer’s profits attributable to the infringed design. This rule is consistent with controlling pre-1970 precedent and ensures that patentees receive just compensation without unjust enrichment.
