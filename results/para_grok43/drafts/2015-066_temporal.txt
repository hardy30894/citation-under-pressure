The Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies only in federal prosecutions. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 463 (1938). It has no application to proceedings conducted by Indian tribes, which are distinct sovereigns whose powers of self-government predate the Constitution. Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376, 382-384 (1896); see also Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. 515, 559 (1832). Consequently, a conviction obtained in tribal court without appointed counsel does not offend the Sixth Amendment.

Section 117(a) does not prosecute the defendant anew for the tribal offenses. It punishes only the subsequent federal offense, using the prior convictions solely to measure the penalty. McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311, 313 (1901); Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616, 623 (1912). Because the tribal judgments remain valid under the only law that governed their rendition, their use as predicates raises no constitutional infirmity. See Moore v. Missouri, 159 U.S. 673, 677 (1895).

The same principle governs recidivist statutes that rely on judgments rendered in other jurisdictions. In each instance the prior conviction is accepted as a historical fact establishing the defendant's status, not as an element requiring relitigation under federal procedural rules. Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448, 452 (1962); see also Chandler v. Fretag, 348 U.S. 3, 8 (1954). No authority requires that every predicate conviction satisfy the full panoply of federal constitutional protections that would apply had it been obtained in a federal court.

The statute therefore comports with the Sixth Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
