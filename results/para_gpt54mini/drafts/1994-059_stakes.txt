**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Constitution’s enumerated qualifications for Members of Congress are exclusive, and states may not add to them.**

Article I sets forth the sole qualifications for the House and Senate. A Representative must be at least 25 years old, have been seven years a citizen, and be an inhabitant of the state when elected. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2. A Senator must be at least 30 years old, have been nine years a citizen, and be an inhabitant of the state when elected. U.S. Const. art. I, § 3, cl. 3. The Constitution nowhere authorizes the States to impose additional qualifications. To the contrary, the Supreme Court has repeatedly treated these qualifications as exclusive.

In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that the House may not exclude a duly elected Member who satisfies the Constitution’s express qualifications, explaining that the Framers intended those qualifications to be the exclusive conditions for service. Id. at 522-48. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution’s text and structure” foreclose adding qualifications by ordinary political actors. Id. at 548. That principle controls here: if the House itself may not supplement Article I qualifications, neither may a State.

More recently, *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779 (1995), squarely held that Arkansas’s Amendment 73 is unconstitutional insofar as it sought to impose term-limit ballot disqualifications on federal candidates. The Court explained that “the Framers intended the Constitution to be the exclusive source of qualifications for Members of Congress.” Id. at 800. It further held that allowing states to add qualifications would defeat the Constitution’s “uniform national character” for Congress. Id. at 802-03. The Court rejected the argument that the States retain a residual “reserved” power to prescribe additional qualifications, concluding that the power to add qualifications “was not ‘reserved’ by the States.” Id. at 804. Under *Thornton*, the federal-office provisions of Amendment 73 cannot stand.

The Arkansas Supreme Court reached the same conclusion below, correctly recognizing that “uniformity of qualifications is paramount, and individual states are not free to engraft variations.” *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill*, 316 Ark. 251, 268, 872 S.W.2d 349, 358 (1994). That reasoning accords with the Supreme Court’s later decision in *Thornton* and should be affirmed.

II. **The ballot-access label does not save Amendment 73, because the operative effect is to bar otherwise qualified candidates from office.**

The State cannot evade Article I by recasting a qualification as a ballot-access rule. In *Thornton*, the Supreme Court rejected Arkansas’s argument that its measure merely controlled ballot placement, holding that a state may not accomplish indirectly what it is forbidden to do directly. 514 U.S. at 829-31. The Court reasoned that a candidate disqualified from the ballot is, for constitutional purposes, being subjected to an additional qualification for office. Id. at 831. Amendment 73 therefore violates Article I whether described as a ballot restriction or an eligibility rule.

This Court has likewise recognized that constitutional analysis turns on effect, not label. *Nixon v. Administrator of General Services*, 433 U.S. 425, 470 (1977). Here the effect is plain: a person who has already served the prescribed number of terms is barred from continued candidacy for Congress, even if otherwise fully qualified under Article I. That is an additional qualification.

III. **Sections 1 and 2 are severable from Section 3, and the invalidity of the federal-office provisions does not require invalidating state term limits.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that state term limits for state officers are within the State’s authority. *Hill*, 316 Ark. at 275-77, 872 S.W.2d at 362-63. The invalidity of Section 3 therefore does not infect Sections 1 and 2 unless the provisions are inseparable. Severability is a matter of legislative intent, and a severability clause is strong evidence that valid portions should survive. *Alaska Airlines, Inc. v. Brock*, 480 U.S. 678, 686-87 (1987). The question is whether the remaining provisions can function independently and whether the legislature or electorate would have enacted them absent the invalid part. Id. at 684-86.

Amendment 73 contains an express severability clause. It is also textually divided: Sections 1 and 2 regulate Arkansas executive and legislative offices, while Section 3 separately addresses federal congressional offices. The state provisions are complete and operative on their own. Under *Alaska Airlines*, they should be preserved. See also *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, 108-09 (1976) (applying severability principles to preserve valid portions of a statute where possible).

IV. **Conclusion**

Because Article I’s qualifications for Members of Congress are exclusive, Arkansas may not add a term-limit qualification, even in ballot-access form. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969); *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779 (1995). The Court should therefore affirm the invalidation of Section 3, while preserving Sections 1 and 2 as severable provisions governing state offices.
