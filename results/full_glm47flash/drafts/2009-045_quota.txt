**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Supreme Court Has Held That Fee Enhancements Based on Quality of Performance and Results Are Permissible Only in the “Rare” and “Exceptional” Case, Requiring Specific Evidence.**

The Supreme Court has established that the “quality of representation” and the “results obtained” cannot serve as an independent basis for increasing the fee absent specific evidence. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 899, 104 S.Ct. 1541, 1549 (1984). In *Blum*, the Court rejected the argument that an upward adjustment is never appropriate, but emphasized that such adjustments are limited to the “rare” case where the applicant offers specific evidence to demonstrate that the quality of service rendered was superior to that reasonably expected in light of the hourly rates charged. *Id.* at 899–900, 104 S.Ct. at 1549. This standard was reiterated in *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 565, 106 S.Ct. 3088, 3098 (1986), where the Court held that upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are permissible only in “certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” *Id.* at 565, 106 S.Ct. at 3098. The Third Circuit has similarly noted that the “rare” case exception is a “high bar” that must be met with concrete evidence. *Klein v. City of Philadelphia*, 285 F.3d 747, 754 (3d Cir. 2002).

**II. The District Court Failed to Provide the Specific Evidence Required by *Blum* and *Delaware Valley*.**

Here, the district court enhanced the fee based on the judge’s “own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta” and the court’s “observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1286 (11th Cir. 2008). This reasoning is insufficient under *Blum*, which requires that the record contain specific evidence of superior performance. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549. The district court’s findings were conclusory and failed to identify specific instances of performance that justified a rate above the market standard. *See Applegate v. United States*, 52 Fed. Cl. 751, 773 (Fed. Cl. 2002) (holding that enhancement is inappropriate where the record demonstrates the case was a “cause celebre” and the firm embraced it for public relations, rather than suffering for the client). Because the district court did not point to specific evidence in the record to support the enhancement, the award was an abuse of discretion.

**III. The District Court’s Reliance on Its Own Testimony Violated Due Process and the Standard of Review.**

The district court effectively testified ex parte in the order granting the enhancement, comparing counsel’s performance to unnamed other cases. *Kenny A.*, 532 F.3d at 1236. This conduct is fundamentally flawed. The Supreme Court has instructed that the lodestar figure is presumed to be the reasonable fee, and the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar. *Delaware Valley*, 478 U.S. at 566, 106 S.Ct. at 3098. The Eleventh Circuit’s prior decisions in *NAACP v. Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332 (11th Cir. 1987), and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292 (11th Cir. 1988), did not mandate the enhancement here. *NAACP* and *Norman* merely vacated district court decisions that failed to address the plaintiffs’ arguments for enhancement. *Id.* at 1336–37; *Norman*, 836 F.2d at 1306. They did not establish that a district court may enhance a fee based on the judge’s personal observations of “stellar performance” without specific record evidence. *See Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433, 103 S.Ct. 1935, 75 L.Ed.2d 703 (1983) (lodestar is presumed reasonable). Accordingly, the district court abused its discretion by enhancing the fee without the requisite specific evidence.
