**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively to invalidate predicate convictions used under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA)—is one of profound constitutional significance. The Court has long recognized that when a new rule of constitutional law “clarifies” a prior ambiguity in the law, it is generally applied retroactively to cases on collateral review. *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989). *Johnson* did precisely that: it held that the ACCA’s residual clause—defining a “violent felony” as one that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another”—was unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. The Court emphasized that the clause failed to provide “fair notice” of the conduct it punished and allowed for “arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement.” Id. at 598. This was not a mere refinement of existing doctrine; it was a fundamental clarification that the clause was void for vagueness. As such, *Johnson* qualifies as a “watershed rule of criminal procedure” and must be applied retroactively.

The Eleventh Circuit erred in concluding otherwise. It held that *Johnson* does not apply retroactively because the rule “does not announce a new substantive rule” but rather “clarifies” the meaning of an existing statute. *Welch v. United States*, 797 F.3d 1227, 1232 (11th Cir. 2015). This reasoning misapprehends the nature of *Johnson*. The Court did not merely interpret the residual clause—it invalidated it as a violation of due process. The clause had long been used to classify crimes based on hypothetical risk, a practice the Court found “unpredictable and arbitrary.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 598. The Court explicitly rejected the idea that such a clause could be saved by “a generic definition of the crime.” Id. at 599. The rule announced in *Johnson* is not a new interpretation of an old law; it is a declaration that the law, as applied, was unconstitutional. That is the essence of a retroactive rule.

The Court has repeatedly held that when a rule “clarifies” that a statute is unconstitutionally vague, it is retroactive. *Dawson v. Delaware*, 503 U.S. 159, 165 (1992) (holding that a rule invalidating a statute for vagueness is retroactive); *United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537, 555 (1982) (same). In *Johnson*, the Court stated that the residual clause “fails to give fair notice” and “allows for arbitrary enforcement,” both hallmarks of a void-for-vagueness challenge. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 598. These are not mere interpretive concerns—they are constitutional defects. The rule announced in *Johnson* is therefore not a new rule of criminal procedure in the narrow sense, but a rule that “clarifies” the constitutional limits of legislative power. Such rules are retroactive under *Teague*.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion rests on a flawed application of *Teague*. It relied on *Lockley*, 632 F.3d 1238 (11th Cir. 2011), to argue that *Johnson* does not apply retroactively because it “does not announce a new rule of criminal procedure.” But *Lockley* involved a non-vagueness issue—whether a state conviction qualified as a “crime of violence” under the Sentencing Guidelines. The court in *Lockley* did not address the constitutional implications of vagueness. *Johnson*, by contrast, was a constitutional decision. The Court did not merely reinterpret a statute; it held that the statute, as applied, violated the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. The distinction is critical. As the Court has held, “a decision that a statute is unconstitutionally vague is not a new rule of criminal procedure for purposes of *Teague*.” *Dawson*, 503 U.S. at 165.

Moreover, the Court has recognized that the retroactive application of a rule invalidating a vague statute is essential to the integrity of the criminal justice system. In *United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537 (1982), the Court held that a rule invalidating a statute for vagueness was retroactive because “the rule is not a new one, but a clarification of the constitutional limits of legislative power.” Id. at 555. The same is true here. *Johnson* did not create a new rule; it reaffirmed that the Constitution requires fair notice and predictable application of criminal laws. The residual clause, by allowing courts to assess hypothetical risk, failed that test. The rule announced in *Johnson* is therefore retroactive.

Finally, the Court has emphasized that “the rule of law must be stable, but not at the cost of justice.” *United States v. Haynes*, 407 U.S. 147, 154 (1972). Denying retroactivity in this case would allow the government to enforce a statute that the Court has now declared unconstitutional. That would be a profound injustice. The petitioner, Gregory Welch, was sentenced under a law that the Supreme Court has now held to be void for vagueness. To deny him the benefit of that holding would undermine the very principle of due process the Constitution protects.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. The rule announced in *Johnson* is a watershed rule that clarifies a constitutional defect in the ACCA’s residual clause. It must be applied to ensure that the Constitution’s guarantee of fair notice is honored. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed.
