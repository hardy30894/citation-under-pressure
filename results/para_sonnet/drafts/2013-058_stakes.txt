# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF § 522(b)(3)(C) AND (d)(12) LIMITS THE EXEMPTION TO FUNDS SET ASIDE FOR THE DEBTOR'S OWN RETIREMENT

The Bankruptcy Code exempts "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under" specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). This Court has repeatedly instructed that statutory analysis begins and ends with the text where that text is unambiguous. *Hartford Underwriters Ins. Co. v. Union Planters Bank, N.A.*, 530 U.S. 1, 6 (2000). The word "retirement" is not surplusage; it modifies "funds" and requires that the money in question be dedicated to the debtor's own post-employment years.

An inherited IRA fails this test. As the Seventh Circuit correctly recognized, once Ruth Heffron died, the funds in her IRA "became no one's retirement funds." Pet. App. 4. The tax code's special rules for inherited, non-spousal IRAs strip the account of every attribute that makes retirement savings what they are: the beneficiary may not make new contributions, 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); may not roll the account into her own IRA; and—critically—must draw down the entire balance within a statutorily fixed period, as short as five years, regardless of her age or proximity to retirement. 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B). Heidi Heffron-Clark could withdraw the entire $300,000 the day after she inherited it, spend it on a vacation home, and suffer no early-withdrawal penalty. That is the opposite of retirement savings; it is, in the Seventh Circuit's words, "an opportunity for current consumption, not a fund of retirement savings." Pet. App. 8.

## II. THE STATUTE ASKS WHETHER THE FUNDS ARE CURRENTLY RETIREMENT FUNDS, NOT WHETHER THEY ONCE WERE

Petitioners' position—and that of the Fifth Circuit in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012)—rests on the notion that because the funds were retirement funds in Ruth's hands, they remain "retirement funds" forever, regardless of subsequent transformation. That reading collapses when tested against ordinary usage and against parallel provisions of the Code itself.

Section 522(b)(3)(A) exempts a debtor's "domicile" only if the debtor has resided there for the requisite statutory period before filing. If a debtor inherits a parent's house and immediately rents it out, the house does not remain an exempt "homestead" merely because it once served that function for the parent. Exemption turns on the property's current character in the debtor's hands, not its history in someone else's. The retirement-fund exemption operates identically. As the court below explained, "[e]xemption would depend on how Heidi used the property, not how her mother used it. Just so with retirement funds." Pet. App. 7.

This is not merely an analogy; it reflects how this Court has approached exemption statutes generally—by asking what the asset *is*, functionally, in the debtor's estate at the time of filing. *See Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320, 325 (2005) (examining whether IRA funds served "the purpose of retirement savings" in the debtor's own hands). *Rousey* involved a traditional IRA maintained by the debtor for the debtor's own retirement—precisely the situation Congress meant to protect. Nothing in *Rousey* suggests that any account bearing the letters "IRA," regardless of its actual function, qualifies.

## III. TREATING INHERITED IRAs AS EXEMPT WOULD CONVERT A LIMITED TAX-DEFERRAL MECHANISM INTO AN UNBOUNDED BANKRUPTCY SHIELD

Congress's retirement-fund exemption exists to prevent debtors from being stripped of savings needed for their own old age. Inherited IRAs serve no such function and, if exempted, would allow debtors to shield unlimited inherited wealth from creditors while that wealth remains fully accessible for present spending. The Seventh Circuit's hypothetical illustrates the point precisely: had Ruth withdrawn the $300,000 during her lifetime and gifted it to Heidi a month before death, no one would suggest the funds retained their "retirement" character in Heidi's checking account. Pet. App. 4–5. The timing of the transfer—at death rather than shortly before—cannot logically alter that conclusion, because in both scenarios the funds have ceased to be reserved for anyone's retirement and become instead unrestricted assets available for current use.

Petitioners' amici argue that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage intergenerational savings. Whatever the policy merits of that argument, this Court has cautioned against stretching a statute's text to serve a perceived purpose at the expense of its actual words. As the Court explained in *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522 (1987), "no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs," and "it frustrates rather than effectuates legislative intent simplistically to assume that whatever furthers the statute's primary objective must be the law." *Id.* at 525–26 (emphasis in original). Congress could have written § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) to exempt any account that was *ever* someone's retirement fund; it did not. It exempted "retirement funds"—present tense, functionally defined—and inherited IRAs, stripped of every retirement-oriented feature by operation of the tax code itself, do not qualify.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
