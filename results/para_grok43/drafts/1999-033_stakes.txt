The Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a “class of one” where the plaintiff alleges neither membership in a class or group nor discrimination on the basis of a suspect classification or fundamental right.

The Clause “is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc., 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985). This command presupposes the existence of a class whose members are treated differently from others similarly situated. See Plyler v. Doe, 457 U.S. 202, 216 (1982) (“The Equal Protection Clause directs that all persons similarly circumstanced shall be treated alike.”). A plaintiff therefore must plead that the State “selected or reaffirmed a particular course of action at least in part ‘because of,’ not merely ‘in spite of,’ its adverse effects upon an identifiable group.” Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney, 442 U.S. 256, 279 (1979).

Absent such a class-based allegation, the claim collapses into an assertion that government action was arbitrary or motivated by personal animus. That contention finds no textual or doctrinal home in the Equal Protection Clause. See Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976) (“The central purpose of the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment is the prevention of official conduct discriminating on the basis of race.”). Claims of wholly irrational or spite-driven official conduct are instead governed by the Due Process Clause. See Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co., 272 U.S. 365, 395 (1926).

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary holding—that a plaintiff may prevail by showing state action “motivated solely by a ‘spiteful effort to “get” him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective’”—therefore rests on a misapplication of equal-protection principles. Because respondent pleaded neither class membership nor any prohibited classification, the complaint fails to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
