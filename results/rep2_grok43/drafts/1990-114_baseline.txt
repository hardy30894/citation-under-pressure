**ARGUMENT**

**I. Appointed state judges are excluded from the ADEA as “appointee[s] on the policymaking level.”**

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office in any State or political subdivision of any State by the qualified voters thereof, or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri judges appointed under the Non-Partisan Court Plan and retained by election exercise the core judicial function of “lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988) (quoting 680 F. Supp. 455, 462 (D. Mass. 1988)). Their decisions “necessarily will resolve issues previously unsettled and thus will create law.” Id. This policymaking responsibility places them squarely within the statutory exception. See also Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 145 (1988).

The placement of the exception within a series of exemptions applicable to high-level state officials confirms that Congress did not intend to regulate the qualifications of a State’s own judges. “In traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved in the judicial decision.” United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). No such clear statement appears in the ADEA.

**II. Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision satisfies rational-basis review under the Equal Protection Clause.**

Age is not a suspect classification. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). A State therefore need only articulate a rational basis for distinguishing between judges under and over age seventy. Missouri’s provision “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges,” Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980), and “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges,” Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979). It also “makes it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary.” Apkin, 517 N.E.2d at 146. Because these objectives are legitimate and the means chosen are rationally related to them, the classification does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. See also Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
