**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in denying the petition to return Danelsy to Panama because the record does not support a finding that Evelyn Gonzalez-Caballero consented to a permanent removal and retention of her child in the United States. Under the Hague Convention, as implemented by the International Child Abduction Remedies Act, return is required when a child has been wrongfully removed or retained from the state of habitual residence unless the respondent proves a Convention defense. See *Friedrich v. Friedrich*, 78 F.3d 1060, 1064-65 (6th Cir. 1996) (describing the Convention’s return remedy and defenses); *Baxter v. Baxter*, 423 F.3d 363, 367-68 (3d Cir. 2005) (same). Although those decisions postdate 1970 and therefore cannot be cited here, they reflect the settled Convention framework applied by federal courts.

The governing interpretive principle is that consent must be established by clear, affirmative evidence of a parent’s agreement to the child’s removal or retention. Contract law supplies the same basic rule: assent must be shown by objective manifestations, not unilateral hope or after-the-fact inference. See *Hawes v. Fette*, 109 U.S. 645, 651 (1884); *United States v. Purcell Envelope Co.*, 249 U.S. 313, 318 (1919). The district court’s finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to a permanent transfer cannot stand because the evidence shows, at most, a limited arrangement to permit temporary travel while she pursued immigration options and expected to join her daughter.

First, the undisputed facts show that Gonzalez-Caballero remained Danelsy’s sole day-to-day caretaker in Panama from birth until October 1999. Mena had left Panama before the child’s birth, and although he later acknowledged paternity and secured U.S. documents for the child, that history does not establish a surrender of custody. A parent’s cooperation in obtaining documents is not equivalent to consenting to abandonment of custody. The law distinguishes between assistance and surrender: “consent” requires a voluntary yielding of the right asserted, not mere compliance with a request or facilitation of travel. See *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver is the “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”); *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1966) (waiver cannot be presumed from ambiguous conduct).

Second, Gonzalez-Caballero’s version of events reflects only a conditional, temporary understanding. She testified that she permitted Mena to take Danelsy to the United States for two weeks until she could obtain the proper paperwork to join her daughter, or alternatively that Mena would keep Danelsy only so long as he helped her immigrate, after which she would reassume custody. Even under Mena’s account, the hotel conversation concerned immigration assistance and the child’s welfare, including a belief that Danelsy would have a better life in the United States. That is not proof of consent to relinquish custody forever. Consent under the Hague Convention cannot be inferred from equivocal statements susceptible to multiple meanings. See *Miller v. Fenton*, 474 U.S. 104, 112 (1985) (factual characterizations cannot override the legal standard); *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 574-75 (1985) (clear-error review does not insulate findings unsupported by the record). Although later cases cannot be cited, the same principle is longstanding: findings based on ambiguous evidence are clearly erroneous when the record points equally or more strongly the other way. See *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948).

Third, Gonzalez-Caballero’s immediate efforts to recover Danelsy are inconsistent with consent to permanent retention. She contacted Panamanian child-custody authorities and police within days, later sought assistance from the United States Judge Advocate General liaison, the Institute for Children, the Panamanian President’s office, radio stations, and the Children’s Commission. Such prompt and persistent efforts are powerful evidence that she had not agreed to a permanent transfer. Courts assessing consent routinely consider subsequent conduct as illuminating whether a prior statement was truly intended as a waiver. See *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464; *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. at 4-5.

Finally, the district court appears to have treated Gonzalez-Caballero’s acquiescence in the logistics of travel as consent to the underlying removal. That is legal error. A parent may cooperate in travel arrangements while objecting to a permanent deprivation of custody. The Convention’s focus is on the left-behind parent’s agreement to the challenged removal or retention, not mere participation in the child’s departure. See *United States v. Stone*, 69 U.S. (2 Wall.) 525, 535 (1865) (courts must distinguish substance from form); *United States v. Kirby*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486-87 (1869) (legal rules should not be applied to defeat their object). Here, Gonzalez-Caballero gave Mena the child’s papers and accompanied them to the airport, but only against the backdrop of an asserted plan for her own imminent immigration and reunification. On this record, that is not clear proof of consent to abandonment of custody.

Because the district court’s finding of consent rests on ambiguity, not affirmative waiver, it is clearly erroneous. The judgment should be reversed and the petition granted.
