**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is one of statutory interpretation, and the answer must be grounded in the text, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Third Circuit erred in holding that a TPS grant satisfies the “inspected and admitted or paroled” requirement of § 1255(a). That interpretation distorts the meaning of “admission,” conflates status with entry, and undermines the carefully calibrated distinctions Congress drew between lawful presence and lawful entry. We respectfully submit that a grant of TPS does not constitute an admission, and that the District Court’s contrary conclusion must be reversed.

**I. The Text of the INA Defines “Admission” as a Physical, Procedural Event, Not a Status Grant**

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely descriptive—it is dispositive. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “admission” is a “procedural” and “factual” event tied to the moment of physical entry, not a status conferred by administrative action. *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012); *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013). The distinction between “admission” and “status” is not merely semantic—it is foundational. As we have held, “the date of gaining a new status is not the same as the date of the physical event of entering the country.” *Hanif*, 694 F.3d at 485. To equate a grant of TPS with an admission would collapse two distinct legal concepts into one, contrary to the plain language of the statute.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954 (9th Cir. 2017), is misplaced. *Ramirez* held that “a grant of lawful nonimmigrant status may be considered an admission” *only where the alien was previously inspected and admitted*. Id. at 960. The Ninth Circuit did not hold that a grant of status *creates* an admission; it held that status *follows* from admission. The Third Circuit’s interpretation, by contrast, would allow an alien who entered without inspection to become “admitted” simply by receiving TPS—precisely the kind of result the INA’s text and structure are designed to prevent.

**II. Congress Distinguished “Status” from “Admission” Throughout the INA**

Congress did not use the term “admission” in § 1254a(f)(4), which provides that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). Instead, Congress used the term “status,” which is distinct from “admission.” This deliberate choice confirms that Congress understood the difference. As the Fifth Circuit has held, “admission and status are fundamentally distinct concepts.” *Gomez v. Lynch*, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016). Admission is a one-time procedural event; status is a continuing condition of presence. To treat TPS as an admission would render § 1254a(f)(4) superfluous, because it would make the “lawful status” provision redundant with the “admission” requirement.

Moreover, Congress provided specific exceptions to the admission requirement in other parts of § 1255—such as for special immigrants under § 1255(h) and for certain visa-eligible aliens under § 1255(i)—but did not extend the same exception to TPS recipients. *Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. at 432. The rule of statutory construction that “where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion” applies with full force here. *Id.* Congress could have made TPS recipients eligible for adjustment of status without an admission, but it did not. That omission is not accidental—it is intentional.

**III. The Purpose of TPS Is Temporary Relief, Not a Pathway to Permanent Residence**

The purpose of the TPS program is to provide temporary protection from removal during extraordinary conditions in a home country. *8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1)*. It is not a mechanism for adjusting status. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.” *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004). Interpreting TPS as an admission would render § 1254a(h)(2)—which requires a supermajority of the Senate to pass legislation adjusting the status of TPS recipients—meaningless. If TPS recipients could adjust status under § 1255(a) simply by virtue of their status, Congress would have no need for the supermajority requirement. That result is inconsistent with the statute’s structure and purpose.

The Ninth Circuit’s concern that requiring TPS recipients to reenter legally would “undermine the purpose of TPS” is unfounded. *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964. TPS recipients may remain in the United States indefinitely so long as the Secretary extends TPS for their country. *8 U.S.C. § 1254a(c)(1)*. They are not required to leave. The fact that they may not adjust status does not undermine the program’s purpose—it simply reflects Congress’s decision to limit TPS to temporary relief, not permanent residence.
