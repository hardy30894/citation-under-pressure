**ARGUMENT**

The conviction of Anthony Elonis must be reversed because 18 U.S.C. §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be a threat. The district court's objective "reasonable speaker" standard—asking only whether a reasonable person would foresee the statement as threatening—eliminates the mens rea that the statute and the First Amendment demand.

**I. Section 875(c) Incorporates a Subjective Intent Requirement**

The word "threat" in §875(c) necessarily implies a communication made with the purpose of intimidating or coercing. This Court has long held that criminal statutes are presumed to require a culpable mental state. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 252 (1922) (recognizing that "there is a presumption that a statute does not impose strict liability"). That presumption applies with full force here. Section 875(c) is not a public welfare offense; it targets core First Amendment activity and carries serious penalties. *Cf. Balint*, 258 U.S. at 252 (recognizing narrow category of strict-liability regulatory offenses). The statute's silence on mens rea does not license courts to dispense with intent. A "threat" is not a threat unless the speaker means it as one.

**II. The First Amendment Requires Subjective Intent to Threaten**

The First Amendment shields speech unless it falls within a narrowly defined exception. The "true threat" exception covers only statements that the speaker intentionally communicates as a serious expression of an intent to commit unlawful violence. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (reversing conviction under §871(a) because the defendant's statement was "political hyperbole" and not a "true threat"). In *Watts*, the Court examined the context and the speaker's apparent intent, not merely how a reasonable listener would react. *Id.* at 707–08. An objective standard would sweep in protected speech—jests, artistic expression, emotional outbursts—that the speaker never intended to be threatening.

This Court's incitement cases reinforce the principle that speech cannot be punished without proof of the speaker's intent to cause harm. *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (requiring intent to incite "imminent lawless action"); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940) (reversing breach-of-peace conviction because there was "no evidence that the defendants' purpose was to incite" a breach of the peace); *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (speech may not be punished unless it "is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action"). The same logic governs threats: the government must prove that the defendant intended his words to be taken as a threat. *See Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919) (clear and present danger test considers "the intent with which the act was done").

The objective standard adopted below—whether a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement as threatening—is constitutionally insufficient. It punishes speech based on the reaction of a hypothetical listener, not the speaker's purpose. That approach chills protected expression and invites juries to convict based on the offensiveness of the words rather than the speaker's culpable intent. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708.

**III. The Objective Standard Is Void for Vagueness**

A statute that criminalizes speech without requiring subjective intent fails to give fair warning of what conduct is prohibited. *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921) (striking down statute that "forbids no specific or definite act" and leaves "to the courts and juries the duty of fixing its meaning"). Under the objective standard, a speaker cannot know whether his words will be deemed threatening until a jury decides how a reasonable person would interpret them. That uncertainty is intolerable when speech is at stake. *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 220 (1876) ("It would certainly be dangerous if the legislature could set a net large enough to catch all possible offenders, and leave it to the courts to step inside and say who could be rightfully detained."). The rule of lenity therefore requires that §875(c) be construed to require subjective intent. *Reese*, 92 U.S. at 221.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury be required to find that Elonis subjectively intended his communications to be threats.
