ARGUMENT

The district court erred in holding that an unaccepted Rule 68 offer for the full amount of an individual claim divests the court of subject-matter jurisdiction over a putative class action before a certification ruling. In Sosna v. Iowa, 419 U.S. 393 (1975), the Supreme Court recognized that a properly pleaded class action presents a live controversy even after the named plaintiff’s personal stake ends, because the class possesses an independent interest in the litigation. Id. at 399-401. That principle was reaffirmed in U.S. Parole Commission v. Geraghty, 445 U.S. 388 (1980), which held that a named plaintiff retains a personal stake sufficient to appeal a denial of certification notwithstanding mootness of individual claims. Id. at 404.

This Court’s decision in Clark v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co., 590 F.3d 1134 (10th Cir. 2009), does not compel dismissal. Clark addressed only the ordinary rule that “a suit brought as a class action must be dismissed for mootness when the personal claims of the named plaintiffs are satisfied and no class has been properly certified.” Id. at 1138. It did not consider a Rule 68 offer tendered before the certification motion deadline set by the district court’s scheduling order, nor did it address the relation-back doctrine applied when a defendant’s strategic timing prevents timely certification. See Deposit Guaranty National Bank v. Roper, 445 U.S. 326, 339 (1980) (holding that an offer of judgment does not extinguish the named plaintiff’s interest in class-wide relief).

The same concern for preventing defendants from “picking off” named plaintiffs before certification has led other circuits to apply relation back. Weiss v. Regal Collections, 385 F.3d 337, 348 (3d Cir. 2004); Zeidman v. J. Ray McDermott & Co., 651 F.2d 1030, 1050 (5th Cir. 1981); Pitts v. Terrible Herbst, Inc., 653 F.3d 1081, 1091 (9th Cir. 2011). These decisions rest on the same equitable considerations recognized in Franks v. Bowman Transportation Co., 424 U.S. 747, 756 n.8 (1976), and County of Riverside v. McLaughlin, 500 U.S. 44, 51-52 (1991).

Finally, the district court’s refusal to enter judgment on the unaccepted offer distinguishes this case from United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36 (1950), and its progeny. Because no judgment was entered and the class-certification motion was filed within the court-ordered deadline, the action never lost the “concrete adverseness” required by Article III. See Camreta v. Greene, 563 U.S. 692, 701 (2011). The complaint therefore should be reinstated.
