**ARGUMENT**

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The Internal Revenue Code requires that an assessment be made against each taxpayer individually before the ten-year statute of limitations for collection applies. A partnership and its partners are separate taxpayers, and an assessment against the partnership does not constitute an assessment against the partners.

**I. The statutory scheme distinguishes partnerships from partners as separate taxpayers.**

Section 6203 provides that an “assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The term “taxpayer” includes both partnerships and individuals. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The implementing regulation requires that a valid assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. Thus, the IRS must identify the specific taxpayer against whom it makes an assessment. An assessment against a partnership identifies only the partnership, not the individual partners.

This Court has long recognized that a partnership is a distinct entity from its partners for federal tax purposes. In *Forsyth v. Woods*, 78 U.S. 484, 487 (1871), the Court stated that “the partnership is a distinct thing from the partners themselves.” See also *Heiner v. Mellon*, 304 U.S. 271 (1938) (treating partnership as separate for income tax purposes); *Guaranty Trust Co. v. Commissioner*, 303 U.S. 493 (1938) (same). The separate identity of the partnership is also reflected in the bankruptcy context. *Liberty Nat. Bank v. Bear*, 276 U.S. 215, 219 (1928) (requiring that the trustee “keep separate accounts of the partnership property and of the property belonging to the individual partners”). In *Francis v. McNeal*, 228 U.S. 695, 700 (1913), the Court observed that it would be “an anomaly to allow proceedings in bankruptcy against joint debtors from some of whom, at any time before, pending, or after the proceeding, the debt could be collected in full.” That anomaly would arise if the IRS could collect from partners without assessing them individually.

**II. The statute of limitations for collection runs from the assessment against the particular taxpayer.**

Section 6502(a) provides that a tax may be collected “within 10 years after the assessment of the tax.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). The “tax” referred to is the tax assessed against the taxpayer. If the IRS assesses only the partnership, the ten-year period runs only as to the partnership. The partners remain subject to the general three-year limitations period for assessment under § 6501(a). The IRS did not assess the partners within three years, and it did not bring a collection action within that period. Therefore, the statute of limitations bars collection from the partners.

The IRS argues that state law makes partners liable for partnership debts, but state law cannot override federal procedural requirements for tax collection. In *Commissioner v. Tower*, 327 U.S. 280, 286 (1946), the Court emphasized that the determination of who is a partner for tax purposes depends on the substance of the arrangement, not merely state law labels. Similarly, in *Commissioner v. Culbertson*, 337 U.S. 733, 740 (1949), the Court stated that the question is whether the partnership is “real within the meaning of the federal revenue laws.” Here, the federal revenue laws require an assessment against each taxpayer. State law may create substantive liability, but it does not dispense with the federal requirement of an assessment.

**III. The cases relied upon by the IRS do not support its position.**

The IRS cites *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), but that case involved a partner who voluntarily paid the tax and then sought a refund. The court held only that the partner could not recover a refund because he was liable under state law. The district court in *Young* acknowledged that to collect without a voluntary payment, the IRS would need to “resort to court action” or make an individual assessment. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). The Ninth Circuit’s affirmance did not adopt a broader rule.

In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the court affirmed the invalidity of a tax lien against a partner where no assessment had been made against him individually. The district court had held that “such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property … unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). That reasoning supports the respondent’s position.

**IV. The bankruptcy context reinforces the need for individual assessments.**

In bankruptcy, claims are allowed only if enforceable against the debtor. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). The IRS’s claim against the partners is unenforceable because the statute of limitations has run. The partnership’s bankruptcy does not extend the limitations period for the partners. See *Meek v. Centre County Banking Co.*, 268 U.S. 426 (1925) (partnership bankruptcy does not automatically bring in partners). The separate treatment of partnership and individual estates in bankruptcy, as recognized in *Liberty Nat. Bank*, 276 U.S. at 219, underscores that the IRS must pursue each taxpayer separately.

**Conclusion**

The IRS failed to assess the individual partners within the three-year limitations period. The assessment against the partnership does not extend the ten-year collection period to the partners. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
