**ARGUMENT**

The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, and this Court should vacate the dismissal and remand with instructions to return the case to state court. A federal court is “one of limited jurisdiction,” and “the party asserting jurisdiction bears the burden of proving that the case is properly in federal court.” *Dixon v. Coburg Dairy, Inc.*, 369 F.3d 811, 816 (4th Cir. 2004) (en banc). Because removal jurisdiction must be strictly construed, “if federal jurisdiction is doubtful, a remand to state court is necessary.” *Mulcahey v. Columbia Organic Chems. Co.*, 29 F.3d 148, 151 (4th Cir. 1994).

I. **The Pressls’ complaint does not arise under federal law.**

Federal-question jurisdiction exists only when a federal issue appears on the face of the well-pleaded complaint. *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908); *Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams*, 482 U.S. 386, 392 (1987). A defendant may not create federal jurisdiction by raising a federal defense. *Caterpillar*, 482 U.S. at 393.

That rule controls here. The Pressls filed a state declaratory-judgment action asking a Virginia court to construe a private flowage easement and determine whether APCO may impose dock-related restrictions beyond the easement’s terms. Their claim turns on the meaning of the easement under Virginia property law. The fact that APCO may contend that federal law or a FERC license gives it broader authority is, at most, a defense. But “a case may not be removed to federal court on the basis of a federal defense.” *Caterpillar*, 482 U.S. at 393; see also *Franchise Tax Bd. v. Constr. Laborers Vacation Tr.*, 463 U.S. 1, 14 (1983).

APCO relied below on the fact that the property lies within a federally licensed project boundary. That does not itself convert a state-law easement dispute into a federal case. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “the mere presence of a federal issue in a state cause of action does not automatically confer federal-question jurisdiction.” *Merrell Dow Pharms. Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 813 (1986). Rather, federal jurisdiction exists only in the “special and small category” of state-law claims that necessarily raise a substantial, disputed federal issue. *Gunn v. Minton*, 568 U.S. 251, 258 (2013). This case does not fit that narrow category. The district court could resolve the Pressls’ requested declaration by interpreting the easement under Virginia law without deciding any disputed federal issue.

II. **Section 825p does not confer jurisdiction over this private easement dispute.**

The district court also erred in invoking 16 U.S.C. § 825p. That statute provides jurisdiction over “all suits and actions at law or in equity brought by or against the [FPC],” now FERC’s predecessor authority. 16 U.S.C. § 825p. The statute does not create general federal jurisdiction over every dispute that touches a licensed hydroelectric project. Courts must read jurisdictional statutes according to their text, and § 825p is limited to actions involving the Commission. See *Arkansas Power & Light Co. v. FPC*, 137 F.2d 769, 771 (8th Cir. 1943) (construing the predecessor statute to confer jurisdiction over suits “brought by or against” the agency).

This case is between private landowners and a power company. FERC is not a party. The Pressls seek no relief against the agency and ask only for construction of a private easement. Because § 825p does not extend to private-state law property disputes merely because they relate to a federally licensed project, it cannot support jurisdiction here. See *Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.*, 339 U.S. 667, 672–74 (1950) (the Declaratory Judgment Act does not enlarge federal jurisdiction); *Franchise Tax Bd.*, 463 U.S. at 21 (federal jurisdiction cannot be manufactured through artful pleading in declaratory actions).

III. **The district court should have remanded instead of reaching the merits.**

When subject matter jurisdiction is absent, the proper course is remand. 28 U.S.C. § 1447(c); *Ellenburg v. Spartan Motors Chassis, Inc.*, 519 F.3d 192, 196–97 (4th Cir. 2008). A federal court “may not assume jurisdiction for the purpose of deciding the merits.” *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 94 (1998). Because the district court lacked jurisdiction, its merits ruling dismissing the complaint must be vacated. *Hertz Corp. v. Friend*, 559 U.S. 77, 94 (2010) (federal courts must assure themselves of jurisdiction before proceeding); *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 94.

The Pressls’ suit belongs in state court, where it began. The judgment should therefore be vacated and the case remanded with instructions to remand to the Circuit Court for Franklin County, Virginia.
