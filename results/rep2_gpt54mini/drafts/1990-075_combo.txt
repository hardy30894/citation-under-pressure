The Controlled Substances Act does not offend the nondelegation doctrine. Congress supplied an intelligible principle: the Attorney General may temporarily schedule a drug only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” and only after considering specified factors. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3), (4). The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld far broader delegations so long as Congress “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle.” J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). Congress may act through another branch when it establishes policy and limits the delegate’s discretion. Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 426-27 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). Here, Congress did exactly that.

Touby’s effort to constitutionalize a heightened rule for criminal statutes fails. The Court has upheld delegations that carry criminal consequences where Congress provided standards. In Yakus, the Court sustained a wartime pricing statute whose violation could be punished criminally, emphasizing that Congress had fixed the governing policy and standards. 321 U.S. at 427-30. Likewise, in Lichter v. United States, 334 U.S. 742, 785-86 (1948), the Court upheld a statute authorizing contract renegotiation even though noncompliance exposed parties to liability, explaining that Congress may delegate authority when it states the objective and the criteria. The same principle governs here. Congress specified the end—preventing an imminent hazard to public safety—and the factors the Attorney General must evaluate. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3); see also United States v. Rock Royal Co-op., Inc., 307 U.S. 533, 574-75 (1939).

The temporary-scheduling provision is especially appropriate because Congress acted in a technically complex, fast-moving regulatory field. The Court has long recognized that delegation is proper where the subject matter requires specialized judgment and prompt adaptation. Sunshine Anthracite Coal Co. v. Adkins, 310 U.S. 381, 398 (1940); Opp Cotton Mills, Inc. v. Administrator of Wage and Hour Division, 312 U.S. 126, 145-46 (1941). Congress found that designer drugs could evade slower permanent scheduling and therefore authorized temporary action to meet an immediate public-safety threat. That is precisely the kind of practical administrative discretion the Court approved in Hampton and Yakus. 276 U.S. at 406-09; 321 U.S. at 425-27.

Touby’s reliance on Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved open-ended grants of power unmoored from any meaningful standard. Panama Refining invalidated a delegation because Congress had supplied no policy “to govern the President’s course.” 293 U.S. at 415. Schechter Poultry struck down a code-making regime that effectively permitted private groups and the Executive to define criminal rules without congressional direction. 295 U.S. at 541-42. By contrast, the CSA cabins the Attorney General with express substantive criteria, a defined public-safety trigger, a short duration, and consultation requirements. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2)-(4).

Nor is there any constitutional defect in the Attorney General’s delegation of temporary-scheduling authority to the DEA. Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate functions under the drug laws. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that, where Congress entrusts authority to the Executive, the head of an Executive Department may assign tasks to subordinates absent a statutory prohibition. United States v. Germaine, 99 U.S. 508, 510 (1879); Butterworth v. United States ex rel. Hoe, 112 U.S. 50, 59-60 (1884); In re Neagle, 135 U.S. 1, 63-65 (1890). The same principle applies here: the Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA is an internal allocation of executive responsibility, not an unconstitutional transfer of legislative power.

The Court should therefore reject both challenges. Congress made the policy judgment, fixed the standards, and imposed limits. The Attorney General merely executed that policy, and lawfully entrusted its administration to the DEA. The convictions should be affirmed.
