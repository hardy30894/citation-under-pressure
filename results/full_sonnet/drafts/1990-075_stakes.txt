# ARGUMENT

## I. CONGRESS'S DELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL SATISFIES THE INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE STANDARD

Article I vests "all legislative powers" in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. But this Court has long recognized that Congress does not violate this provision merely by obtaining assistance from the coordinate branches in filling in the details of a regulatory scheme. As this Court held in *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394 (1928), Congress may delegate authority so long as it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *Id.* at 409. This standard, reaffirmed just two Terms before the decision below in *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), reflects "a practical understanding that in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives." *Id.* at 372.

Section 811(h) readily satisfies this standard. Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. It specified that temporary scheduling is available only to "avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety," 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), and it required the Attorney General to consider three enumerated statutory factors bearing on the substance's history, current pattern of abuse, and risk to public health, *id.* § 811(c)(4)-(6), (h)(3). Congress further cabined the delegation temporally: temporary scheduling lasts only one year, extendable by six months, *id.* § 811(h)(2), and procedurally: the Attorney General must publish notice in the Federal Register and consider the views of the Secretary of Health and Human Services before acting, *id.* § 811(h)(4).

This Court has upheld delegations far less circumscribed. In *Mistretta*, the Court sustained Congress's delegation to the Sentencing Commission of authority to promulgate binding sentencing guidelines—a delegation the Court found adequate because Congress specified the goals the Commission must pursue, the product it must create, and the factors it must weigh. 488 U.S. at 374-79. Indeed, outside the two 1935 cases—*Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935)—this Court has upheld every delegation challenge presented to it, "even where Congress has provided negligible standards for the delegatee to follow." Pet. App. 12 (Third Circuit opinion).

*Schechter Poultry* and *Panama Refining* are inapposite. In *Panama Refining*, Congress gave the President authority to prohibit interstate transport of "hot oil" without declaring "any policy," qualifying the President's authority in any way, or establishing "any criterion to govern the President's course." 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, Congress authorized the President to approve industry-drafted codes of "fair competition" with no standard beyond that open-ended phrase. 295 U.S. at 521-23. Section 811(h) contains precisely what those statutes lacked: an imminent-hazard trigger, enumerated substantive factors, and firm temporal limits.

Petitioners' contention that a heightened non-delegation standard applies because temporary scheduling has criminal consequences finds no support in this Court's precedent. The delegations struck down in *Panama Refining* and *Schechter Poultry* also carried criminal penalties, yet the Court's analysis in both cases turned on the absence of any intelligible principle—not on the criminal character of the regulated conduct. Nothing in *Hampton* or *Mistretta* suggests that the "intelligible principle" test varies with the nature of the sanction attached to noncompliance.

## II. CONGRESS AUTHORIZED THE ATTORNEY GENERAL TO SUBDELEGATE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA

Petitioners' subdelegation argument fares no better. Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Act. Section 871(a) provides that "[t]he Attorney General may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice." 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This broad grant plainly encompasses the temporary scheduling function conferred by § 811(h), which is itself housed within the same subchapter.

The Attorney General's subdelegation to the DEA Administrator is consistent with longstanding practice under the Act. As early as 1973, pursuant to his § 871(a) authority, the Attorney General promulgated a regulation subdelegating his scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator. See 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1986). When Congress added the temporary scheduling provision in 1984, it did so against this settled backdrop of subdelegation, and gave no indication that the new authority was to be treated differently from the Attorney General's other scheduling functions. The Attorney General's 1987 amendment extending the subdelegation regulation to cover "[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended," 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), thus falls squarely within the authority Congress conferred in § 871(a).

## III. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY EXERCISED JURISDICTION OVER PETITIONERS' CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGE

Although § 811(h)(6) provides that a temporary scheduling order "is not subject to judicial review," this bar does not extend to constitutional challenges raised in a criminal prosecution. As the court below recognized, "the availability of judicial review is presumed" and courts "will not read a statutory scheme to take the 'extraordinary' step of foreclosing jurisdiction unless Congress' intent to do so is manifested by 'clear and convincing' evidence." *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). Nothing in § 811(h)(6) or its legislative history clearly evinces an intent to foreclose criminal defendants from challenging the constitutionality of the delegation itself. Every court of appeals to consider the question has agreed. See *United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426 (10th Cir. 1987).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
