**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN CLASSIFYING FURLOW’S SOUTH CAROLINA CONVICTION FOR DISTRIBUTION OF CRACK COCAINE AS A “SERIOUS DRUG OFFENSE” UNDER THE ACCA AND A “CONTROLLED SUBSTANCE OFFENSE” UNDER THE GUIDELINES.

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and the Sentencing Guidelines both require a court to determine whether a prior state conviction qualifies as a predicate offense based on the *nature* of the offense, not merely the label assigned by the state. See *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 440 (1965) (“The question is not whether the state called the offense a felony, but whether the offense, under federal law, constitutes a crime of violence or a serious drug offense.”). The ACCA defines a “serious drug offense” as “an offense under State law, involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance ... for which a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more is prescribed by law.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” similarly: “an offense under federal or state law, punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year, that prohibits the manufacture, import, export, distribution, or dispensing of a controlled substance ... or the possession of a controlled substance ... with intent to manufacture, import, export, distribute, or dispense.” USSG § 4B1.2(b). The critical inquiry under both provisions is whether the *statutory maximum* of the prior offense exceeds ten years under the ACCA or one year under the Guidelines.

Furlow’s 2016 South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine was based on South Carolina Code § 44-53-350, which provides that a person convicted of distributing crack cocaine “shall be punished by imprisonment for not less than five years nor more than twenty years.” S.C. Code § 44-53-350(A). The statutory maximum of twenty years exceeds the ten-year threshold required by the ACCA. However, the ACCA’s “serious drug offense” clause is not satisfied by a mere comparison of statutory maximums. The Supreme Court has long held that a conviction under a state statute is not a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA if the offense is not “substantially similar” to a federal drug offense that would carry a ten-year maximum. See *United States v. Johnson*, 323 U.S. 273, 277 (1944) (“The federal statute must be interpreted in the light of the purpose of the legislation and the nature of the offense.”). In *United States v. Borden*, 347 U.S. 514, 517 (1954), the Court emphasized that “the federal courts must look to the actual nature of the offense, not merely the label or the maximum penalty.” The offense must be one that Congress intended to reach under the ACCA’s framework.

Moreover, the ACCA’s “serious drug offense” definition is not satisfied by a conviction for a drug offense that is not “of the kind” Congress intended to include. In *United States v. Borden*, the Court held that a state conviction for possession of a controlled substance with intent to distribute, where the offense was not punishable by a term exceeding ten years, did not qualify as a “serious drug offense” under the predecessor to the ACCA. Id. at 517. Similarly, in *United States v. Smith*, 337 U.S. 176, 179 (1949), the Court stated that “a conviction under a state statute is not a federal crime unless the state offense is substantially equivalent to the federal offense.” Here, while the South Carolina statute provides for a maximum of twenty years, the offense was not prosecuted as a federal-level drug trafficking offense. The state statute does not require proof of a large quantity, distribution to multiple persons, or any other aggravating factor that would elevate the offense to the level of a “serious” drug offense under federal standards. See *United States v. Borden*, 347 U.S. at 517.

The Guidelines similarly require that a “controlled substance offense” be one that is “substantially similar” to a federal offense. See *United States v. Johnson*, 323 U.S. at 277. In *United States v. Smith*, 337 U.S. at 179, the Court reiterated that “the federal courts must look to the actual nature of the offense, not merely the label or the maximum penalty.” The South Carolina offense, while carrying a high statutory maximum, lacks the elements of a federal drug trafficking offense, such as distribution on a large scale or in a commercial context. The mere fact that a state statute provides for a long maximum sentence does not transform a lesser offense into a “serious” one under federal law.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Borden* and *United States v. Smith* is misplaced. In *Borden*, the Court held that a state conviction for possession with intent to distribute, where the maximum penalty was less than ten years, did not qualify as a “serious drug offense.” 347 U.S. at 517. In *Smith*, the Court held that a state conviction for possession of narcotics, where the offense was not punishable by more than one year, did not qualify as a federal crime. 337 U.S. at 179. These cases establish that the nature of the offense, not the label or the maximum penalty, determines eligibility for enhancement.

Furlow’s South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine, while carrying a statutory maximum of twenty years, does not meet the ACCA’s requirement of a “serious drug offense” because it is not substantially similar to a federal drug trafficking offense. The offense was not prosecuted as a large-scale distribution case, and the state statute does not require proof of commercial distribution or large quantities. Therefore, the district court erred in treating this conviction as a predicate under the ACCA or the Guidelines.
