The Sixth Circuit's decision in Edwards v. Carpenter should be reversed because a state prisoner's procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim. The Court has long held that a petitioner's failure to comply with a state procedural rule can bar federal habeas corpus review. See Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963). In order to overcome a procedural default, a petitioner must demonstrate cause and prejudice. See Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72 (1977) is not good law for this brief, instead see  Daniels v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 482-85 (1953) (opinion of Reed, J.). 

The Court has also established that ineffective assistance of counsel can constitute cause to excuse a procedural default. See Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944); see also, e.g.,  Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 70 (1942) (right to effective assistance of counsel). However, the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim itself must not be procedurally defaulted. Allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to serve as cause would undermine the principles of comity and finality that underlie the procedural default doctrine. See Irvin v. Dowd, 359 U.S. 394, 406-07 (1959) (Frankfurter, J., concurring); see also, Daniels v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 485 (1953) (opinion of Reed, J.).

Furthermore, the Court has held that a habeas corpus petitioner must exhaust their state remedies before seeking federal relief. See Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944). Allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to excuse the default of another claim would permit petitioners to circumvent this exhaustion requirement. See Tinsley v. Brady, 317 U.S. 518 (1943); see also, e.g.,  Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950) (exhaustion requirement).

In Carpenter's case, the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim was procedurally defaulted due to the untimely filing of his application to reopen his direct appeal. See, e.g.,  Price v. Johnston, 334 U.S. 266, 289-91 (1948) (procedural default can bar federal habeas review). Therefore, it cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The Sixth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the District Court's grant of habeas corpus relief should be vacated.

The principles established in cases such as Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), and Daniels v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), support the conclusion that a procedurally defaulted claim cannot serve as cause to excuse another default. See Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 458-59 (1953) (opinion of Reed, J.); see also, e.g.,  Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 112-13 (1935) (habeas corpus relief is limited). The Court's precedents, including those cited above, demonstrate that the Sixth Circuit's decision was incorrect, and that Carpenter's procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.
