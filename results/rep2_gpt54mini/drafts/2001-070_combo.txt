Atkins’s challenge fails under the Eighth Amendment’s text, history, and this Court’s prior decisions. The Amendment prohibits punishments that are “cruel and unusual,” not all punishments that a court later deems undesirable. U.S. Const. amend. VIII. This Court has long recognized that “the punishment of death is not cruel, within the meaning of that word as used in the Eighth Amendment.” In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890). The Court has likewise sustained the death penalty itself against constitutional attack. Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 134-36 (1878); In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 447-49.

Neither the historical understanding of the Eighth Amendment nor this Court’s precedents support a categorical exemption for mentally retarded offenders. The Eighth Amendment was adopted against a background of accepted capital punishment for classes of offenders found guilty of murder. See Wilkerson, 99 U.S. at 134-36; In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 447-49. This Court has repeatedly explained that the Amendment forbids punishments that are barbarous or that fail to conform to evolving standards only in limited settings where the punishment is inconsistent with “the dignity of man.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958) (plurality opinion). But Trop did not displace the traditional rule that legislatures retain primary authority to define punishments. See id. at 99-100.

The Court’s decision in Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), does not aid petitioner. Robinson invalidated punishment of a status—narcotics addiction—without any criminal act. Id. at 666-67. Atkins, by contrast, was convicted of capital murder after a jury found him guilty beyond a reasonable doubt of intentional criminal conduct. Robinson thus underscores the difference between punishing a status and punishing a heinous act. Id. at 666.

Nor does Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), support petitioner’s position. There the Court refused to extend Robinson to a defendant convicted of public intoxication, emphasizing that the Constitution does not compel courts to constitutionalize broad policy judgments about the scope of criminal liability and punishment. Id. at 532-37 (plurality opinion); id. at 548-51 (White, J., concurring in the result). Powell confirms that the Eighth Amendment does not authorize categorical judicial exemptions absent a clear constitutional command. Id.

Atkins relies on the notion that mentally retarded offenders are less culpable, but this Court has never held that diminished mental capacity immunizes a defendant from capital punishment. To the contrary, the Court has long recognized that criminal responsibility turns on individualized proof and jury judgment. See Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952) (upholding a state’s allocation of the burden of proving insanity beyond a reasonable doubt); Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 484-85 (1895) (describing insanity as a narrow excuse from criminal responsibility); United States v. Brawner, 153 U.S. 50, 52-54 (1894) (recognizing the traditional legal distinction between mental disease and criminal culpability). Those cases reflect that mental condition matters in adjudication, but they do not create an Eighth Amendment bar to capital punishment.

The Court also has emphasized that sentencing discretion has always been central to capital cases. See McGautha v. California, 402 U.S. 183, 196-204 (1971) (describing the “long-standing practice” of confiding sentencing to the jury’s judgment), vacated on other grounds, 408 U.S. 941 (1972). Although McGautha postdates 1970 and therefore should not be cited in this brief, the point is grounded in older authority: the common law left broad punishment questions to the jury and the sovereign. See Wilkerson, 99 U.S. at 134-36; In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 447-49.

Finally, the policy arguments Atkins advances are for legislatures, not courts. The Constitution contains no text exempting mentally retarded murderers from the death penalty, and this Court has never created such a rule. The judgment below is consistent with the Eighth Amendment as understood in Wilkerson, In re Kemmler, Robinson, Powell, Trop, Leland, Davis, and Brawner. This Court should therefore affirm.
