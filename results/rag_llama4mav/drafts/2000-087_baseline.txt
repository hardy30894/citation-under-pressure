The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. 

The statute's language is ambiguous, and when faced with such ambiguity, courts must construe the statute to avoid serious constitutional concerns, as established in United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194, 202 (1957). Indefinite detention of aliens who have been admitted to the United States but subsequently ordered removed raises significant due process concerns. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Due Process Clause applies to aliens within the United States, including those who have been lawfully admitted (Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896)).

In Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678 (2001), the Court acknowledged that § 1231(a)(6) "does not apply narrowly to 'a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals,' ... but broadly to aliens ordered removed for many and various reasons, including tourist visa violations" (id. at 2499). The Court held that this broad application raises serious constitutional concerns, particularly with respect to the detention of aliens who are not a threat to national security or public safety. To avoid these concerns, the Court construed § 1231(a)(6) to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation (id. at 2498).

The Court further held that "once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute" (id. at 2503). In determining reasonableness, the habeas court should "measure reasonableness primarily in terms of the statute's basic purpose, namely assuring the alien's presence at the moment of removal" (id. at 2504). The Court established a presumptively reasonable period of detention of six months, after which the Government must respond with evidence sufficient to rebut the alien's showing that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future (id. at 2505).

In this case, the petitioner, Kestutis Zadvydas, has been detained for an extended period beyond the 90-day removal period. The Government has failed to demonstrate a significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. Under the Supreme Court's interpretation of § 1231(a)(6) in Zadvydas, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute. Therefore, the Attorney General is not authorized to detain Zadvydas indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period.

The Fifth Circuit's prior opinion, which held that Zadvydas' detention was lawful as long as "good faith efforts to effectuate ... deportation continue" and Zadvydas failed to show that deportation will prove impossible (Zadvydas v. Underdown, 185 F.3d 279, 297 (5th Cir. 1999)), is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's holding. The Supreme Court's decision requires a more nuanced assessment of the likelihood of removal, rather than simply considering the Government's good faith efforts.

In conclusion, the post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The statute contains an implicit "reasonable time" limitation, and continued detention is only authorized when removal is reasonably foreseeable. As the Supreme Court held in Zadvydas, once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.
