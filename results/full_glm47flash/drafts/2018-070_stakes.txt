**Argument**

**I. OCSLA’s Choice-of-Law Provision Requires a Gap in Federal Law**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when federal law is not exclusive. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 366 (1969). In *Rodrigue*, the Supreme Court held that the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision applies state law only when the federal law governing the OCS is inapplicable. The Court explained that the federal admiralty action under the High Seas Act “no more applies to these accidents actually occurring on the islands than it would to accidents occurring in an upland federal enclave.” *Id.* at 366. Consequently, the OCSLA does not create a general right to apply state law; it merely ensures that state law fills the void when federal law is silent or inapplicable. Here, the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is the exclusive federal scheme governing wage and hour claims on the OCS. Therefore, California law cannot apply.

**II. The FLSA Constitutes a Comprehensive Statutory Scheme That Leaves No Room for State Law**

The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no significant void for state law to fill. See Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). The Fifth Circuit has long held that the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision requires a “necessity to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law before state law may be adopted. *Id.* at 1036. The FLSA provides a complete set of regulations regarding minimum wage, overtime, and record-keeping. It is designed to be a self-contained body of law. As the Fifth Circuit noted, “the FLSA is a comprehensive statutory and regulatory scheme that leaves no voids or gaps for state law to fill.” *Id.* at 1036. Because the FLSA comprehensively addresses the subject matter of Newton’s claims, there is no gap for California’s wage and hour laws to fill.

**III. The FLSA’s Savings Clause Is a Narrow Exception, Not a General Invitation for State Law**

While the FLSA contains a savings clause that allows for more protective state laws, 29 U.S.C. § 218, this provision is a narrow exception to the Act’s comprehensive scheme, not a general rule permitting state law to apply whenever it is not inconsistent. The Supreme Court has noted that the purpose of the OCSLA was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. See Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19, 26 (1988). Congress intended federal law to be the primary regulator of OCS activities, with state law serving only as a supplement to fill gaps. *Id.* at 26. The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of the OCSLA as allowing state law to apply whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law effectively eviscerates the comprehensive nature of the FLSA. This interpretation is inconsistent with the text of the OCSLA and the Supreme Court’s holding in *Rodrigue*. Accordingly, the district court correctly concluded that the FLSA preempts Newton’s state wage and hour claims.
