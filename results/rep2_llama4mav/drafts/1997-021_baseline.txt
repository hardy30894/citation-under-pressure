The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). The NLRB's standard, as articulated in Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057 (1989), requires an employer to have "objective indications raising a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status" before conducting a poll. However, this standard is too stringent and renders employer polls "useless and illegitimate" (Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984)).

The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have rejected the NLRB's standard, holding that an employer may poll its employees if it has "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support" (A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984)). This standard is more reasonable, as it allows employers to test employee sentiment while protecting employees from coercive polling practices.

The NLRB's justification for its standard is that polling can be "potentially disruptive and unsettling" (Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061-62). However, this concern can be addressed through procedural safeguards, such as those outlined in Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967). The NLRB's standard is not entitled to deference, as it is not a reasonable interpretation of the NLRA (NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990)).

Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence. The record shows that several employees expressed dissatisfaction with the union, and the company's poll was conducted in a fair and non-coercive manner. The NLRB's decision to discount certain employee statements and rely on an overly stringent standard was arbitrary and capricious.

The Court should hold that the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard is not rational and consistent with the NLRA and that the NLRB's factual determination was not supported by substantial evidence. The Court should adopt the "substantial, objective evidence" standard and remand the case to the NLRB for further proceedings.

As the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have recognized, the "substantial, objective evidence" standard strikes a reasonable balance between the employer's interest in testing employee sentiment and the statutory goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships (A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d at 1145; Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d at 867; Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d at 1299). By adopting this standard, the Court can ensure that employers are able to make informed decisions about union representation while protecting employees from coercive polling practices.
