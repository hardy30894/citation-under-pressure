# ARGUMENT

The question presented assumes a false premise: that a "reasonable" attorney's fee under a federal fee-shifting statute is nothing more than a mechanical product of hours and a market rate. That assumption cannot be reconciled with nearly a century of this Court's equitable fee jurisprudence—the very body of law that fee-shifting statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988 were enacted to draw upon and extend. Equity has never confined attorney compensation to a bare hourly calculation; it has always asked what the attorney's work actually achieved and how well it was done. The district court's enhancement here rested on exactly those traditional considerations, supported by detailed findings, and is therefore entitled to the deference this Court has long accorded to the tribunal that observed the litigation firsthand.

## I. Equity Has Long Tied Attorney Compensation to the Quality of Representation and the Results Achieved

In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1881), this Court recognized the equitable authority of a court to compensate an attorney whose efforts preserved or created a fund for the benefit of others, tying the amount of compensation to the value the attorney's work produced rather than to a fixed formula. *Id.* at 532–37. *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), confirmed that an attorney's entitlement to compensation from a common fund depends on the benefit his efforts conferred on those who share it, not merely on the hours he recorded. *Id.* at 125–26. This Court applied the same principle in *Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co. of New York*, 259 U.S. 101 (1922), examining a fee award against the results actually secured for the beneficiaries of the litigation, *id.* at 105, and again in *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), which grounded the power to award fees in the equitable authority of federal courts to do justice between the litigants based on what the litigation accomplished. *Id.* at 164–67. *Woods v. City National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago*, 312 U.S. 262 (1941), likewise measured a contested fee award against both the benefit conferred on the estate and the caliber of the legal work performed to produce it. *Id.* at 268–69.

Read together, these decisions establish a settled principle: from the earliest exercises of federal equity jurisdiction over attorney compensation, courts have treated the quality of representation and the results obtained as legitimate—and at times decisive—components of a reasonable fee. Nothing in the text or history of § 1988 suggests that Congress intended to strip courts of that traditional equitable authority when it authorized fee awards to prevailing civil rights plaintiffs. To the contrary, Congress legislated against this equitable backdrop, and this Court should not presume that Congress silently abrogated it.

## II. Fee-Shifting Statutes Exist to Secure Counsel Capable of Producing Exceptional Results, Not Merely to Reimburse Hours

This Court's decision in *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400 (1968), construing the materially identical fee provision of Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, confirms that fee-shifting statutes serve a purpose broader than simple reimbursement. The Court explained that "if successful plaintiffs were routinely forced to bear their own attorneys' fees, few aggrieved parties would be in a position to advance the public interest by invoking the injunctive powers of the federal courts." *Id.* at 402. That reasoning presupposes fee awards adequate to attract and reward the kind of skilled, sustained advocacy that actually vindicates the statutory right at stake—not advocacy that merely clears a minimum hourly threshold.

*Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714 (1967), reinforces the point from the opposite direction. There, this Court declined to fashion a fee-shifting remedy absent statutory authorization, recognizing that Congress deliberately chooses when and how to displace the American Rule in order to advance particular statutory policies. *Id.* at 719–20. Where Congress has made that choice specifically to enable enforcement of civil rights, as it did in § 1988, courts must give that choice practical effect. A regime that caps every fee award at a mechanical hourly product—no matter how extraordinary the result obtained—would frustrate rather than serve the very purpose *Piggie Park* and *Fleischmann* identify: ensuring that counsel capable of achieving exceptional results are available to plaintiffs who could not otherwise obtain them.

## III. The District Court's Firsthand Assessment of Counsel's Performance Warrants Deference

No court is better positioned than the district court to judge whether counsel's performance and the result obtained were exceptional. This Court has long recognized that the tribunal that observes proceedings firsthand deserves deference precisely because a reviewing court cannot recreate that vantage point from a cold record. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), this Court held that a trial court's findings should be disturbed only when the reviewing court, examining the entire record, "is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *Id.* at 395. *Graver Tank & Manufacturing Co. v. Linde Air Products Co.*, 336 U.S. 271 (1949), reaffirmed that same standard, emphasizing that a trial judge's evaluation of the evidence before him is not to be set aside lightly by an appellate court operating at a remove from the proceedings. *Id.* at 274–75. That deference applies with full force to a district judge's assessment—informed by years presiding over difficult, protracted litigation—of whether counsel's advocacy and the result achieved were extraordinary.

## IV. This Is the Rare Case in Which Enhancement Was Warranted

The district court did not rest on generalized praise. It made detailed, record-based findings concerning the severity of the crisis facing Georgia's foster children, the comprehensive relief secured for the plaintiff class, and counsel's sustained skill and commitment over years of demanding litigation. Those findings are precisely the kind of evidence that equity has always treated as relevant to a reasonable fee. Consistent with the equitable principles reflected in *Greenough*, *Pettus*, *Newton*, *Sprague*, and *Woods*; the statutory purpose confirmed in *Piggie Park* and *Fleischmann*; and the deference commanded by *Gypsum* and *Graver Tank*, the enhancement below should be affirmed.
