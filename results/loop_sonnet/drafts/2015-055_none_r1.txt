# ARGUMENT

## THE RULE ANNOUNCED IN JOHNSON V. UNITED STATES APPLIES RETROACTIVELY ON COLLATERAL REVIEW

### A. Retroactivity Turns on the Nature of the Rule and Its Relationship to the Legitimacy of the Underlying Sentence.

This Court has never treated retroactivity as governed by a single mechanical rule. Rather, "the Constitution neither prohibits nor requires retrospective effect," and the answer depends on weighing "the merits and demerits in each case." Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618, 629 (1965). The framework this Court has applied examines "(a) the purpose to be served by the new standards, (b) the extent of the reliance by law enforcement authorities on the old standards, and (c) the effect on the administration of justice of a retroactive application of the new standards." Stovall v. Denno, 388 U.S. 293, 297 (1967); accord Johnson v. New Jersey, 384 U.S. 719, 728–29 (1966); Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott, 382 U.S. 406, 413–17 (1966).

That framework, however, has always distinguished between rules that merely refine the procedures used to adjudicate guilt and rules that redefine the very authority of the State to impose punishment at all. As Justice Harlan explained, new rules that alter the substance of what conduct or status the State may punish stand on a different footing from rules that regulate trial procedure, because the former call into question "the government's power to bring a defendant to trial" for the conduct at issue, not merely the fairness of the process used to convict him. Desist v. United States, 394 U.S. 244, 262 (1969) (Harlan, J., dissenting). When a rule falls into the first category, retroactive application follows not from a discretionary balancing of interests but from the basic principle that no court ever had lawful authority to impose the punishment in the first place.

That principle is not new. In Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), this Court held that a prisoner convicted under an unconstitutional statute was entitled to release on habeas corpus, explaining that "[a]n unconstitutional law is void, and is as no law. An offence created by it is not a crime." Id. at 376. The judgment of conviction, the Court held, was rendered by a court "without jurisdiction" to impose it. Id. at 377. Relatedly, in United States v. Reese, 92 U.S. 214 (1875), the Court refused to permit a criminal statute to be salvaged by after-the-fact judicial narrowing, warning that "[i]t would certainly be dangerous if the legislature could set a net large enough to catch all possible offenders, and leave it to the courts to step inside and say who could be rightfully detained, and who should be set at large." Id. at 221. Both decisions rest on the same insight this case requires the Court to apply: a punishment imposed without lawful authority cannot stand merely because the judgment convicting or sentencing the prisoner became final before the constitutional defect was recognized.

### B. Johnson's Invalidation of the Residual Clause Is a Substantive Rule Narrowing the Category of Predicate Offenses.

Johnson v. United States held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act was unconstitutionally vague because it required courts to assess "the hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version" of an offense, an inquiry so unpredictable that it denied fair notice of the conduct being punished. That holding is a direct application of this Court's longstanding vagueness doctrine, under which "a statute which either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application, violates the first essential of due process of law." Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, "[n]o one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes." Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939); see also United States v. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921) (invalidating a criminal statute that failed to provide "an ascertainable standard of guilt").

Johnson's holding is therefore substantive, not procedural. It did not merely improve the method by which courts determine whether a given offense qualifies as a "violent felony"—it eliminated an entire category of predicate offenses from the Act's reach because the standard defining that category was constitutionally void from the start. A sentence enhanced under the residual clause is, after Johnson, indistinguishable from the void convictions in Ex parte Siebold: the sentencing court lacked lawful authority to impose the enhanced punishment, regardless of when the vagueness defect was judicially recognized. See Siebold, 100 U.S. at 376–77.

### C. The Traditional Factors Confirm Retroactive Application.

Even under the balancing approach of Linkletter, Stovall, and Tehan, retroactivity is warranted. The purpose of the Johnson rule is to prevent imprisonment under a standard that fails to give "men of common intelligence" fair notice of its reach, Connally, 269 U.S. at 391—a purpose implicating the very legitimacy of the sentence, not merely the accuracy of fact-finding. Any reliance interest in the residual clause is minimal, since the clause is now understood never to have supplied a workable standard at all. And the interest in the "administration of justice," Tehan, 382 U.S. at 417, favors correcting sentences imposed under a constitutionally void provision rather than permitting prisoners to remain confined beyond the term the law actually authorizes.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the rule announced in Johnson v. United States applies retroactively to cases on collateral review, including petitioner's.
