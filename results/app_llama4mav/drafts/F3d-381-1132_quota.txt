ARGUMENT

I. The Court of Federal Claims Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to the Government Because the Installation of Excess Fiber Optic Capacity and Leasing to Third Parties Constitutes a Physical Taking.

The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits the government from taking private property without just compensation. U.S. Const. amend. V. A physical taking occurs when the government physically occupies or invades private property, thereby depriving the owner of its rights to exclude others and to use the property as it sees fit. Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419, 435-36 (1982). The government's actions in this case, specifically the installation of excess fiber optic capacity and leasing it to third parties, constitute a physical taking.

The government's easement, granted in 1953, permits the installation of "signal lines... necessary in connection therewith" the power transmission lines. However, the BPA's decision to install far more fiber optic capacity than necessary for its current needs, and to lease the excess capacity to third parties, exceeds the scope of the easement. See, e.g., City of Manhattan Beach v. Superior Court, 906 P.2d 987, 994 (Cal. 1995) (holding that an easement's scope is limited to the purpose for which it was granted). The government's actions are not merely an incidental or de minimis invasion of Tuthill's property rights, but rather a significant and permanent physical occupation. See, e.g., Kaiser Aetna v. United States, 444 U.S. 164, 179-80 (1979) (holding that a physical invasion, no matter how small, can constitute a taking).

The Court of Federal Claims erred in holding that the scope of the easement is irrelevant to the takings analysis. The scope of the easement is crucial in determining whether the government's actions constitute a physical taking. See, e.g., United States v. Causby, 328 U.S. 256, 262-63 (1946) (holding that the government's use of airspace above a property owner's land constituted a taking because it interfered with the owner's rights to use and enjoy the property). The government's argument that the installation of excess capacity was done to accommodate future growth is not dispositive, as the fact remains that the excess capacity is being used for purposes outside the scope of the easement.

Furthermore, the government's leasing of excess capacity to third parties constitutes a separate and distinct physical taking. See, e.g., Yuba Natural Resources, Inc. v. United States, 904 F.2d 1577, 1581 (Fed. Cir. 1990) (holding that the government's flooding of private property constituted a taking). The fact that the physical presence of the fiber optic cable was initially authorized by the easement does not immunize the government's subsequent actions from takings liability. See, e.g., Hendler v. United States, 952 F.2d 1364, 1376 (Fed. Cir. 1991) (holding that the government's installation of monitoring wells on private property constituted a taking).

The government's reliance on cases such as Nollan v. California Coastal Commission, 483 U.S. 825 (1987), is misplaced. While Nollan held that a condition imposed on a landowner as a condition of development did not constitute a taking, it did not address the issue of a physical occupation or invasion. Id. at 834-35. In contrast, the government's actions in this case involve a direct physical invasion of Tuthill's property rights.

In sum, the government's installation of excess fiber optic capacity and leasing it to third parties constitutes a physical taking, and the Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government. See, e.g., Palm Beach Isles Associates v. United States, 231 F.3d 1354, 1364-65 (Fed. Cir. 2000) (holding that a physical taking can occur even if the government's actions are not necessarily permanent).

II. The Court of Federal Claims Erred in Failing to Consider Tuthill's Property Interests.

The Court of Federal Claims also erred in holding that Tuthill had not identified a property interest that could have been taken. Tuthill has a clear property interest in the land burdened by the easement, and the government's actions have interfered with that interest. See, e.g., Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council, 505 U.S. 1003, 1019 (1992) (holding that a property owner's rights to use and enjoy their property are fundamental property interests). The government's physical occupation of Tuthill's property has deprived Tuthill of its rights to exclude others and to use the property as it sees fit.

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the summary judgment granted by the Court of Federal Claims and remand for further proceedings.
