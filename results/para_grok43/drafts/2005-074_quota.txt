**ARGUMENT**

I. Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to reentries completed before its April 1, 1997 effective date.

The statute’s text addresses only an alien who “has reentered the United States illegally” after the prior order. 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). Because Mr. Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 reentry predated the provision, ordinary principles of statutory construction require that the new consequences not attach. Landgraf v. USI Film Prods., 511 U.S. 244, 265 (1994) (“[C]ongressional enactments and administrative rules will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result.”). The presumption against retroactivity is “deeply rooted in our jurisprudence,” id. at 265, and applies with full force to immigration statutes that enlarge removal consequences. INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001).

II. Even if the statute reaches pre-1997 reentries, its application here is impermissibly retroactive.

A statute operates retroactively when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-70; see also Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer, 520 U.S. 939, 947 (1997). Mr. Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 reentry and subsequent continuous residence were lawful under then-existing law; the 1997 statute stripped him of any opportunity to seek adjustment of status. This “new disability” with respect to past conduct triggers the presumption. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 321; Martin v. Hadix, 527 U.S. 343, 357-58 (1999).

The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on the 2001 adjustment application as a post-enactment “trigger” misapprehends the analysis. Retroactivity turns on whether the statute “increase[s] a party’s liability for past conduct,” Lindh v. Murphy, 521 U.S. 320, 326 (1997), not on later attempts to mitigate that liability. See also Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp., 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988) (“[A] statutory grant of legislative rulemaking authority will not, as a general matter, be understood to encompass the power to promulgate retroactive rules.”). The relevant completed event remains the 1982 reentry.

III. The presumption is not overcome.

Nothing in the statute’s text or structure supplies the “clear intent” required to overcome the presumption. General Motors Corp. v. Romein, 503 U.S. 181, 191 (1992); Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386, 390 (1798). Immigration provisions that enlarge removal grounds have consistently been held to require explicit retroactive language. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 317-18; see also United States v. Mendoza-Lopez, 481 U.S. 828, 837-38 (1987). Absent such language, Section 241(a)(5) cannot be applied to Mr. Fernandez-Vargas.
