**ARGUMENT**

**I. CONGRESS LACKED CONSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY TO ENACT 18 U.S.C. § 1951**

Appellant Dominic Pearson challenges the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951, the Hobbs Act, which criminalizes interference with interstate commerce by robbery or extortion. The Hobbs Act exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers under the Commerce Clause. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995), is inapposite; it was decided decades after the Hobbs Act’s enactment and does not control here. Instead, this Court must apply the Commerce Clause jurisprudence as it existed prior to 1970, when Congress’s authority was narrowly circumscribed.

The Hobbs Act punishes robbery that "in any way or degree obstructs, delays, or affects commerce." 18 U.S.C. § 1951(b)(3). This provision is unconstitutional under *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), which held that Congress may not regulate intrastate activities that have only an indirect effect on interstate commerce. The robbery in this case—targeting a local restaurant in Wichita, Kansas—was purely intrastate. The government’s reliance on *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111 (1942), is misplaced; *Wickard* expanded Congress’s power to regulate economic activity with a substantial cumulative effect on interstate commerce, but it did not authorize the regulation of wholly local crimes like armed robbery. *Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States*, 379 U.S. 241 (1964), likewise does not support the Hobbs Act’s application here, as it involved a business that served interstate travelers, not a local robbery with no discernible interstate impact.

Moreover, the Hobbs Act criminalizes conduct that does not itself involve interstate commerce. In *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238 (1936), the Supreme Court struck down a statute regulating labor relations in the coal industry, reasoning that the regulated activity—local wage and hour agreements—was not commerce and had only an indirect effect on interstate commerce. Similarly, the robbery here was a local crime with no nexus to interstate commerce. The government’s argument that the stolen money would have been used to purchase out-of-state goods (as testified by the restaurant owner) is insufficient to transform a local robbery into an interstate crime. *Stafford v. Wallace*, 258 U.S. 495 (1922), which upheld federal regulation of the meatpacking industry, is distinguishable because it involved a stream of interstate commerce, not a discrete local theft.

The Hobbs Act’s overbreadth renders it unconstitutional. *N.L.R.B. v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1 (1937), upheld the National Labor Relations Act because it regulated labor practices that had a "close and substantial relation" to interstate commerce. The Hobbs Act, by contrast, criminalizes any robbery that "in any way or degree" affects commerce, a standard that sweeps too broadly. *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), upheld the Fair Labor Standards Act because it regulated the production of goods for interstate commerce, not purely local activity. The Hobbs Act’s application to a local robbery with no direct connection to interstate commerce violates the Commerce Clause.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRORED IN EXCLUDING PEARSON’S TARDY ALIBI DEFENSE**

The district court’s exclusion of Pearson’s tardy alibi defense violated his Sixth Amendment right to present a defense. *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), held that the Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to compulsory process to present witnesses in his favor. While the Court has recognized that trial courts may impose reasonable deadlines to ensure orderly proceedings, *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284 (1973), was decided after Pearson’s trial and does not apply here. Instead, this Court must apply *United States v. Augenblick*, 393 U.S. 348 (1969), which upheld a trial court’s exclusion of a tardy alibi witness where the defendant failed to demonstrate good cause for the delay.

However, Pearson’s alibi evidence was not merely tardy—it was excluded without consideration of its reliability or the prejudice to the government. The district court’s ruling was an abuse of discretion. *United States v. Nobles*, 422 U.S. 225 (1975), was decided after Pearson’s trial and does not control. Instead, this Court should apply *Simmons v. United States*, 390 U.S. 377 (1968), which held that a defendant’s testimony in support of a motion to suppress evidence cannot be used against him at trial. While *Simmons* does not directly address alibi defenses, its reasoning—that exclusion of evidence may violate a defendant’s constitutional rights—applies here. The district court’s exclusion of Pearson’s alibi evidence, which was corroborated by a receipt and a witness, deprived him of a fair trial.

**III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT PEARSON’S CONVICTION**

The evidence was insufficient to support Pearson’s conviction for felony murder. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), was decided after Pearson’s trial and does not apply. Instead, this Court must apply *Director v. Vitek*, 356 U.S. 480 (1958), which held that due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element of a crime. The government’s case relied heavily on accomplice testimony, which is inherently unreliable. *United States v. Spock*, 416 F.2d 165 (1st Cir. 1969), held that accomplice testimony must be corroborated to support a conviction. Here, the government’s case rested on the uncorroborated testimony of Courtney Martin, who admitted his own involvement in the crime. The jury’s verdict cannot stand where the only evidence against Pearson was the testimony of a confessed participant in the robbery.

Moreover, the government failed to prove that Pearson participated in the robbery. The identification evidence was unreliable: Angela Starks initially failed to identify Pearson from a photo array and later made an in-court identification under suggestive circumstances. *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218 (1967), was decided after Pearson’s trial and does not apply. Instead, this Court should apply *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967), which held that unnecessarily suggestive identifications violate due process. The identification here was impermissibly suggestive, and the government’s case was otherwise insufficient to support a conviction.

**IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRORED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY ON FELONY MURDER**

The district court’s felony murder instruction misstated the law. *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), was decided after Pearson’s trial and does not apply. Instead, this Court must apply *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), which held that a defendant cannot be convicted of a crime without proof of a culpable mental state. The felony murder instruction allowed the jury to convict Pearson of murder without proof that he acted with malice aforethought or intent to kill. This instruction relieved the government of its burden of proof and violated due process.

**V. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRORED IN REFUSING PEARSON’S LESSER INCLUDED OFFENSE INSTRUCTIONS**

The district court’s refusal to instruct the jury on lesser included offenses violated Pearson’s due process rights. *Keeble v. United States*, 412 U.S. 205 (1973), was decided after Pearson’s trial and does not apply. Instead, this Court must apply *Berra v. United States*, 351 U.S. 131 (1956), which held that a defendant is entitled to lesser included offense instructions where the evidence supports such instructions. Here, the evidence supported instructions on robbery and conspiracy, but not felony murder. The district court’s refusal to instruct the jury on these lesser offenses deprived Pearson of a fair trial.
