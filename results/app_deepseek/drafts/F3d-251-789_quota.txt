ARGUMENT

**Standard of Review**

The district court’s denial of a Hague Convention petition is reviewed de novo as to legal conclusions, but its factual findings—including whether a parent consented to removal—are reviewed for clear error. *Gonzalez v. Gutierrez*, 311 F.3d 942, 947 (9th Cir. 2002). A finding is clearly erroneous when, after reviewing the entire record, we are left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made. *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985).

**The District Court’s Finding of Consent Was Clearly Erroneous**

The Convention permits a court to deny return if the removing parent proves by a preponderance of the evidence that the left-behind parent consented to the child’s removal or retention. 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(2)(B); *Mozes v. Mozes*, 239 F.3d 1067, 1077 (9th Cir. 2001). Consent must be “clear and unequivocal,” *Furnes v. Reeves*, 362 F.3d 702, 723 (11th Cir. 2004), and “voluntary, deliberate, and unequivocal.” *Baxter v. Baxter*, 423 F.3d 363, 373 (3d Cir. 2005). The record demonstrates that Gonzalez-Caballero did not give such consent.

First, Gonzalez-Caballero’s testimony consistently showed she allowed Mena to take Danelsy only for a short period—two weeks—until she could immigrate and reassume custody. A limited, temporary permission does not amount to consent to permanent retention. *In re Lozano*, 809 F.3d 665, 670 (D.C. Cir. 2016) (consent must be “actual” and not based on misapprehension of duration). The district court disregarded this unambiguous evidence, relying instead on isolated statements that Gonzalez-Caballero “agreed” to Danelsy’s removal. But context matters: she gave possession only as part of a larger plan for her own immigration. Under *Mozes*, 239 F.3d at 1078, consent to a temporary visit is not consent to permanent relocation.

Second, Mena’s own account undercuts his claim. He testified that Gonzalez-Caballero told him she could no longer care for Danelsy because she was pregnant. Yet he simultaneously claims she agreed to permanent removal. These contradictory narratives do not meet the heightened standard of “clear and unequivocal” consent. *Furnes*, 362 F.3d at 723. Moreover, Mena—not Gonzalez-Caballero—purchased a one-way ticket for Danelsy and obtained her passport in advance, suggesting he planned to retain her regardless of any consent. The burden was on Mena to prove consent, *Shealy v. Shealy*, 295 F.3d 1117, 1122 (10th Cir. 2002), and he failed to do so.

Third, Gonzalez-Caballero’s immediate and persistent efforts to recover Danelsy after their departure are inconsistent with consent. She contacted Panamanian authorities, police, the U.S. Judge Advocate General, the Institute for Children, the Panamanian President’s office, radio stations, and the Children’s Commission—all within days of Mena’s failure to call upon arrival. A parent who has consented to permanent removal does not immediately launch an international campaign to recover the child. *Norinder v. Fuentes*, 657 F.3d 526, 531 (7th Cir. 2011) (subsequent actions are relevant to whether consent existed). The district court erroneously minimized this evidence.

**Even if Gonzalez-Caballero Initially Consented, She Effectively Withdrew Consent**

Consent, once given, may be withdrawn before the removal becomes wrongful. *See Friedrich v. Friedrich*, 78 F.3d 1060, 1068 (6th Cir. 1996) (noting that consent can be revoked before the child’s removal or retention becomes permanent). Here, Gonzalez-Caballero’s first contact with authorities occurred “the day after” Mena and Danelsy left Panama, when Mena failed to call as promised. That prompt action demonstrates that any temporary permission she gave was conditioned on Mena’s promise to contact her immediately. When that condition was broken, she withdrew her consent. The district court erred by failing to consider this revocation.

Because the district court’s consent finding is clearly erroneous, this Court should reverse and order Danelsy’s return to Panama.
