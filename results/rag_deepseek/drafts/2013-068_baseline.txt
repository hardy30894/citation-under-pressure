**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE MASSACHUSETTS BUFFER ZONE IS A VALID CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME, PLACE, MANNER RESTRICTION.**

The Act is a content-neutral regulation of speech in a traditional public forum, subject to intermediate scrutiny. *See Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 715 (2000). It does not regulate speech based on its message or viewpoint; it applies equally to all persons, regardless of the content of their expression. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 722. The statute’s legislative history—focusing on “ensuring safe access” to reproductive health care facilities—is devoid of any intent to suppress particular viewpoints. *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 176.

The Act serves significant governmental interests: protecting public safety, ensuring unimpeded access to health care, and preventing obstruction of entrances. *See Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 767–68 (1994) (recognizing “the State’s interest in protecting the health and safety of its citizens” and “ensuring public safety and order”). The record shows that prior to the Act, patients and staff faced harassment, physical blocking, and intimidation. Buffer zones of 35 feet are a modest response to a demonstrated problem.

The Act is narrowly tailored. It does not ban speech; it merely restricts the location of speech within a small zone adjacent to clinic entrances. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 726 (upholding a 100-foot buffer zone). Unlike the floating buffer zone invalidated in *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 377 (1997), the fixed 35-foot zone allows petitioners to stand on the sidewalk, display signs, leaflet, and engage in “sidewalk counseling” from a distance that does not impede patient access. The Act includes exemptions for those entering or leaving the facility, employees, emergency personnel, and persons using the sidewalk to reach other destinations. *See* Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½.

The Act leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. Petitioners can still speak, pray, distribute literature, and display signs from beyond the buffer zone. They can also engage in residential picketing, media campaigns, and other off-site advocacy. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 727 (finding “ample alternative channels” for communication even with a 100-foot zone). Mere reduction in effectiveness does not render a restriction unconstitutional. *See id.* at 726.

**II. *HILL V. COLORADO* CONTROLS AND SHOULD NOT BE OVERRULED.**

*Hill* upheld a Colorado statute that criminalized “knowingly approach[ing]” within eight feet of another person for “oral protest, education, or counseling” within 100 feet of a health care facility. 530 U.S. at 707. The Court held the statute was content-neutral, served the significant interest in “protecting the health and safety of citizens,” and was narrowly tailored to avoid “unnecessary interference with First Amendment freedoms.” *Id.* at 715, 726. The Massachusetts Act is even more protective of speech: it does not restrict approaching individuals; it only creates a fixed 35-foot zone around clinic entrances. Petitioners can still stand at the perimeter and communicate with anyone willing to listen.

*Hill* is consistent with this Court’s precedents. *See Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768 (upholding a 36-foot buffer zone around an abortion clinic); *see also Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 377 (invalidating a floating buffer zone but affirming that fixed buffer zones are permissible). To overrule *Hill* would unsettle reliance interests of states and municipalities that have enacted similar laws. *See* *Hilton v. South Carolina Public Railways Comm’n*, 502 U.S. 197, 202 (1991) (stare decisis “has added force” when “the legislature has acted in reliance upon” the decision). Congress and state legislatures have relied on *Hill* in drafting buffer-zone laws, and abortion clinics have structured their operations accordingly.

Nothing in subsequent decisions “has so dramatically changed the legal landscape” as to justify overruling *Hill*. *See* *Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pa. v. Casey*, 505 U.S. 833, 854 (1992) (joint opinion). *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), and *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), addressed content-based laws that discriminated against disfavored speakers; they do not undermine the content-neutral analysis in *Hill*. The Act here is manifestly content-neutral.

The Court should reaffirm *Hill* and uphold the Massachusetts buffer zone as a constitutional exercise of the State’s police power to protect public health, safety, and access to medical care.
