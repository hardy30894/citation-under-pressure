## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to the ship, as there are genuine issues of material fact regarding the ship's negligence under section 5(b) of the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C. § 905(b). Specifically, the ship breached its "active operations" duty to exercise reasonable care to avoid injuries to longshoremen.

The "active operations" duty, as established in pre-1970 case law, requires a shipowner to exercise reasonable care to avoid injuries to longshoremen when the ship is actively involved in the cargo operations. In The Max Morris, 281 U.S. 412 (1930), the Supreme Court held that a shipowner has a duty to exercise reasonable care to avoid injuries to longshoremen. The Court emphasized that this duty is not limited to warning longshoremen of hidden dangers, but also extends to taking affirmative steps to prevent injuries.

In this case, the ship's crew was responsible for moving the snatch blocks, including the one that Serbin attempted to move. However, the crew had left the block in a down position, resting on top of a cargo unit, creating a hazardous condition. Serbin, in an effort to avoid delaying the unloading process, decided to move the block himself. The ship argues that Serbin's decision to move the block was an assumption of risk, but this argument ignores the fact that the ship's crew had a duty to exercise reasonable care to avoid creating hazardous conditions.

The ship's failure to return the snatch block to the up position and secure it properly created a hazardous condition that contributed to Serbin's injury. As the Court held in The Admiral Peoples, 295 U.S. 649 (1935), a shipowner's negligence can be established by showing that the ship failed to exercise reasonable care to avoid creating hazardous conditions.

Furthermore, the ship's argument that Serbin was contributorily negligent is not dispositive, as the Court held in Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn, 346 U.S. 406 (1953), that a longshoreman's contributory negligence does not necessarily bar recovery under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act. Rather, the Court emphasized that the shipowner's negligence is the primary consideration in determining liability.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the ship had a duty to exercise reasonable care to avoid injuries to longshoremen, including Serbin. The ship's failure to properly secure the snatch block created a hazardous condition that contributed to Serbin's injury. Therefore, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to the ship, and this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.
