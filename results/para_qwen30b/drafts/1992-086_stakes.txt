**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment guarantees that “Congress shall make no law…abridging the freedom of speech.” U.S. Const. amend. I. This protection extends to private citizens’ expression on public property when the government has created a forum for such speech. Here, the Center Moriches School District has, through policy and practice, opened its school facilities to a wide range of community groups for non-curricular, after-hours use. By doing so, it has established a *limited public forum*—a category of public property where the government, having opened the space for expressive activity, may not discriminate on the basis of viewpoint or content. The District’s blanket exclusion of religious speech, including Lamb’s Chapel’s request to show a family-values film series, violates the First Amendment’s Free Speech Clause. The Second Circuit erred in concluding otherwise.

### I. The School District’s Facilities Are a Limited Public Forum Open to Non-Religious Groups

The Supreme Court has long recognized that when a government creates a forum for public expression, it must do so without discrimination based on the speaker’s viewpoint. *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983). A “limited public forum” arises when a government “by policy or practice” opens its property to certain types of speech or speakers. *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985). Once such a forum is created, the government may not exclude speech based on its content or viewpoint, even if the speech is religious in nature.

The record here is clear: the Center Moriches School District has permitted numerous non-religious community groups to use its facilities after school hours. The District allowed the Southern Harmonizers, a secular a cappella group, to perform in the high school auditorium. The performance was described as “musical and cultural,” not religious, and occurred in a non-religious context. Similarly, the District permitted the Center Moriches Free Public Library to sponsor a four-night lecture series on parapsychology, transpersonal psychology, and quantum physics. The lecturer, Jerry Huck, testified that his talks were grounded in science and that any references to spiritual or religious ideas were “a fascinating sideline” and not the purpose of the lectures. These uses were not incidental or isolated; they were part of a consistent pattern of allowing non-religious, non-curricular groups to use school facilities.

The Second Circuit’s conclusion that the District had not opened its facilities to religious use by policy or practice is factually and legally flawed. The District’s own rules and practices demonstrate that it has permitted expressive activity on its premises—so long as it is not religious. The District’s refusal to allow religious groups to use the facilities, while permitting secular groups to do so, is not neutral. It is a content-based exclusion that violates the First Amendment.

### II. The District’s Exclusion of Religious Speech Is Viewpoint Discrimination

The First Amendment does not permit the government to exclude speech simply because it is religious. *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 392 (1993) (plurality opinion). In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Court held that a state university could not deny access to student religious groups when it had opened its campus to other student organizations. The Court emphasized that “the University has created a forum generally open for use by student groups,” and that “the First Amendment prohibits the exclusion of religious speech from such a forum.” *Id.* at 267, 273. The same principle applies here: the District has opened its facilities to non-religious groups, and thus cannot exclude religious groups based on the content of their speech.

The District’s argument that religious speech is inherently different and thus subject to special scrutiny is foreclosed by *Mergens*. In *Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), the Court held that a public high school could not deny a student religious club access to school facilities when it had created a limited public forum for non-curricular student groups. The Court stated that “the Equal Access Act prohibits discrimination based on the content of the students’ speech,” and that “the First Amendment requires the same result.” *Id.* at 247, 250. The Court made clear that “the government may not deny access to a speaker simply because the speaker’s message is religious.” *Id.* at 249.

The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a film series on family values—designed to promote Christian values but otherwise indistinguishable in content and purpose from secular programs—constitutes viewpoint discrimination. The film series was not a religious service; it was a community educational event. The District’s own rules allowed secular groups to use the facilities for similar purposes. The only difference was the religious perspective. That difference is constitutionally impermissible.

### III. The District’s Policy Violates the First Amendment’s Guarantee of Equal Access

The District’s policy of excluding all religious groups from its facilities, while allowing secular groups to use them, is not neutral. It treats religious speech as inherently different and less worthy of inclusion. This violates the principle that “the government may not suppress speech merely because it opposes the speaker’s view.” *Paulsen v. County of Nassau*, 925 F.2d 65, 69 (2d Cir. 1991). The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use the facilities, despite its willingness to allow other groups to do so, is not a reasonable regulation—it is a content-based exclusion.

The Second Circuit’s reliance on *Deeper Life* cases is misplaced. Those cases involved different factual contexts and did not address the creation of a limited public forum. In contrast, here, the District has repeatedly allowed non-religious groups to use its facilities. The government cannot create a forum and then exclude religious speakers from it. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 392 (plurality opinion). The District’s policy is not “reasonably related to a legitimate government interest” but is instead a blanket ban on religious expression in a forum that is otherwise open.
