**ARGUMENT**

**I. Petitioner possesses a protected liberty interest in the clemency process.**

The Supreme Court has recognized that clemency is a "regular and critical component of the decisionmaking process employed by the State to determine the magnitude of its deprivation of the prisoner's liberty." *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956). While the state may create statutory procedures, the Constitution protects liberty interests that are independent of those procedures. *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 469 (1981) (Stevens, J., dissenting). Because clemency is the final step in the state's adjudicative system, Petitioner is entitled to due process protections. The state cannot treat clemency as a mere formality or a "statutory creation" devoid of constitutional significance. *Id.* at 469. Therefore, the clemency process itself implicates a protected liberty interest.

**II. The clemency interview regulation imposes an unconstitutional condition on the exercise of that liberty.**

The regulation provides that any statement given at the interview "shall be considered at the clemency hearing." This compels Petitioner to choose between asserting a Fifth Amendment privilege and risking the denial of clemency. This creates a "Hobson's choice" between participating in the process and remaining silent. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 324 (1976). The state cannot force a waiver of constitutional rights to access a state benefit. Furthermore, this creates a disincentive for the state to offer interviews, contrary to the principles in *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 499 (1995). The regulation effectively conditions the opportunity for clemency on the relinquishment of a fundamental right, which is impermissible under the Fifth Amendment.
