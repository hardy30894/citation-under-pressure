**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that the reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 241(a)(5), applies to deportation orders issued prior to its enactment. The application of a new penalty to a past act violates the Ex Post Facto Clause and Due Process. A law which punishes as a crime an act which was innocent when done is a retrospective law. *United States v. Rogers*, 45 U.S. (4 How.) 567, 572 (1846). This prohibition extends to laws that alter the legal rules of evidence and receive, in evidence, a different act or substance from that which it was when done. *United States v. Rauscher*, 119 U.S. 407, 413 (1886). The reinstatement statute effectively punishes Fernandez-Vargas for his 1982 re-entry with a penalty (deportation) that did not exist at the time of the offense. *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516, 536 (1884).

Furthermore, the retroactive application of administrative action is disfavored. The retroactive application of administrative action to destroy vested rights is not favored. *Stewart v. Kennedy*, 383 U.S. 196, 202 (1966). Fernandez-Vargas possessed a vested right to apply for adjustment of status based on his marriage to a U.S. citizen. The statute, enacted in 1997, cannot be used to retroactively strip him of this right. *Korematsu v. United States*, 323 U.S. 214, 224 (1944). The Constitution does not forbid judicial inquiry into the validity of a congressional enactment under the Constitution. *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 208 (1962).

**II. The "Protectable Expectation" Test Is Inapplicable**

The Tenth Circuit held that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation" of being able to adjust his status. This reasoning is flawed. The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, requires that laws be prospective in operation. *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497, 499 (1954). An alien who has lawfully resided in the United States and applied for permanent residency has a legitimate expectation that the laws governing his status will not be changed retroactively. *Stewart v. Kennedy*, 383 U.S. at 202. The "protectable expectation" test is a modern administrative law construct that has no basis in the pre-1970 jurisprudence governing ex post facto and due process limitations. *United States v. Wong Kim Ark*, 169 U.S. 649, 699 (1898). Statutes are not to be construed as retroactive unless the intention to make them so is manifest. *Id.* Congress did not clearly manifest an intent to apply the 1997 statute to pre-1997 orders. Therefore, the statute must be applied prospectively.
