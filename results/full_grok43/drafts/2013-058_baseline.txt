ARGUMENT

I. An Inherited IRA Is Not “Retirement Funds” Exempt Under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12).

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” specified Internal Revenue Code provisions. The phrase “retirement funds” is undefined, but its ordinary meaning requires assets set aside for the debtor’s own post-employment years. See, e.g., Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) (per curiam) (“no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs”).

An inherited IRA fails this test. Once the original owner dies, the beneficiary may make no further contributions, may not roll the balance into another retirement vehicle, and must commence distributions within one year, completing them within five years or the beneficiary’s life expectancy. 26 U.S.C. §§ 408(d)(3)(C), 401(a)(9)(B). These mandatory withdrawals are available for current consumption, not deferred until the beneficiary’s retirement. The funds therefore lose their character as “retirement funds” upon inheritance.

This conclusion follows from the statutory text and structure. Other exemptions in § 522 expressly limit protection to “the debtor’s” interest or to property used by the debtor in a qualifying manner. See, e.g., § 522(b)(3)(A) (homestead exemption limited to debtor’s domicile for 730 days before filing). The absence of a possessory qualifier in § 522(b)(3)(C) does not convert every tax-deferred account that once belonged to anyone into exempt “retirement funds.” As the Court has observed, “deciding what competing values will or will not be sacrificed to the achievement of a particular objective is the very essence of legislative choice.” Rodriguez, 480 U.S. at 526.

The contrary view—that funds retain exempt status merely because they were once someone’s retirement savings—would extend protection to assets the debtor may spend immediately. That result finds no support in the Code’s text or in the principle that exemptions are construed to effectuate their limited purpose without “throw[ing] creditors’ claims to the wolves.” The Seventh Circuit correctly held that the inherited IRA is not exempt.
