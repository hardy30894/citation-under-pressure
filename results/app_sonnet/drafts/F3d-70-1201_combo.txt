# ARGUMENT

## I. The Common-Law Rule Excluding Evidence of Subsequent Repairs Does Not Bar Its Use for Impeachment, and the District Court Erred in Instructing the Jury to Disregard Properly Admitted Testimony.

The rule excluding evidence of post-accident repairs rests on a narrow policy rationale: fear that admitting such evidence to prove antecedent negligence would discourage defendants from making safety improvements. See *Columbia & Puget Sound R.R. Co. v. Hawthorne*, 144 U.S. 202, 207 (1892) (holding that evidence a defendant made repairs after an accident is inadmissible to establish that the prior condition was negligent, because such a rule would deter needed repairs). That rationale, however, has never extended to bar the use of subsequent-measures evidence for the distinct purpose of impeaching a witness who has testified falsely or misleadingly on the subject.

This Circuit's evidentiary jurisprudence has long recognized that evidence excluded for one purpose may be admitted to correct a false impression created by a party's own conduct at trial. In *Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 479 (1948), the Supreme Court explained that a party who "opens the door" to a subject by creating a favorable but misleading impression cannot then complain when the opposing party is permitted to correct that impression. The same principle animates *Walder v. United States*, 347 U.S. 62, 65 (1954), where the Court held that a defendant "must be free to deny all the elements of the case against him," but may not use the shield of an exclusionary rule to "provide himself with a shield against contradiction of his untruths." Although *Walder* arose in the context of illegally seized evidence, its logic applies with equal force here: Morbark cannot invoke Rule 407's predecessor common-law policy as both sword and shield—creating the false impression that the seventeen-inch chute remained unchanged and still in daily governmental use, while simultaneously insulating that falsehood from correction.

The record demonstrates precisely this abuse. Morbark's counsel told the jury in opening statement that the Army Corps of Engineers had ordered "thirty machines just like the one that is involved in this case." R4-141-22. Counsel then elicited from Infinger, on cross-examination, that DeFuniak Springs "still" used "that Morbark chipper machine," without qualification. R4-141-44. The district court correctly recognized that this testimony opened the door to impeachment, permitting Wood's counsel to establish that the chute had in fact been lengthened. R4-141-56 ("[I]f you ask this man and suggest that the city is still using that chipper, it's certainly fair for the other side to point out that there have been modifications to that chipper."). Having properly admitted the impeaching testimony under the *Michelson* doctrine, the court erred in later instructing the jury to disregard it, thereby nullifying the very correction it had ruled permissible.

## II. Because This Is a Strict Products Liability Action, the Policy Underlying Exclusion of Subsequent Remedial Measures Is Substantially Weakened.

The exclusionary rule for post-accident repairs developed in the context of ordinary negligence, where liability turns on a defendant's failure to exercise reasonable care. See *Hawthorne*, 144 U.S. at 207. Strict products liability, by contrast, imposes liability regardless of fault, focusing not on the manufacturer's conduct but on the condition of the product itself. See *Greenman v. Yuba Power Products, Inc.*, 59 Cal. 2d 57, 62–63, 377 P.2d 897, 900–01 (1963) (manufacturer strictly liable when a product placed on the market, known to be used without inspection for defects, causes injury); *Henningsen v. Bloomfield Motors, Inc.*, 32 N.J. 358, 384, 161 A.2d 69, 84 (1960) (recognizing an obligation running from manufacturer to consumer independent of fault or privity). This shift, traceable to *MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co.*, 217 N.Y. 382, 389, 111 N.E. 1050, 1053 (1916) (imposing a duty of care on manufacturers "irrespective of contract"), undercuts the deterrence rationale that justifies exclusion in negligence cases: a manufacturer facing strict liability has no comparable disincentive to withhold repairs, since liability does not depend on proof of culpable conduct in the first instance. Accordingly, whatever force the exclusionary policy retains in ordinary negligence actions, it carries substantially less weight where, as here, the theory of recovery is strict liability for a defective design.

## III. The Error Was Not Harmless and Warrants a New Trial.

The district court's curative instruction stripped the jury of the only evidence rebutting Morbark's repeated suggestion that the seventeen-inch chute remained the industry standard. Such an error cannot be dismissed as harmless where it goes to the heart of the contested design-defect issue. See *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764–65 (1946) (error is not harmless when it cannot be said with fair assurance that the judgment was not substantially swayed by it). The district court's discretion to deny a new trial is not unlimited; it must be exercised in light of whether the verdict reflects a fair trial free of prejudicial error. See *Montgomery Ward & Co. v. Duncan*, 311 U.S. 243, 251 (1940). Because the improper instruction permitted Morbark to leave uncorrected the very falsehood it had introduced, Wood's motion for a new trial should have been granted.
