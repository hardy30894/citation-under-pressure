The disclosure provisions of Chapter 93-121 violate the First Amendment because they compel revelation of expenditures and funding sources connected to protected advocacy. In NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958), the Court recognized that “[c]ompelled disclosure of affiliation with groups engaged in advocacy” may abridge associational freedom. The statute’s categorical listing of expenditures reaches every communication, research, and publication outlay, without regard to whether the information bears any relation to corruption or undue influence. Shelton v. Tucker, 364 U.S. 479, 488 (1960), invalidated a similarly indiscriminate disclosure requirement because “[t]he statute’s comprehensive interference with associational freedom goes far beyond what might be justified” by any asserted governmental interest.

Because the provisions “swe[ep] within [their] ambit other activities that in ordinary circumstances constitute an exercise” of protected rights, they are facially invalid. Thornhill v. Alabama, 310 U.S. 88, 97 (1940). The administrative-advisory mechanism does not cure the defect; the statute still “authorize[s] the penalizing of protected conduct” in its ordinary application. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 304 (1940).

The prohibition on contingency-fee lobbying likewise cannot stand. It criminalizes an agreement to compensate a lobbyist for success in petitioning government, thereby restricting both the right to petition and liberty of contract. Allgeyer v. Louisiana, 165 U.S. 578, 589 (1897), protected the freedom “to earn his livelihood by any lawful calling” and to make contracts for that purpose. The ban extends to every contingent arrangement, regardless of whether the agreement involves bribery or any other independently unlawful act, and therefore “abridge[s] the freedom of speech” and petition in its normal operation. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931).

The League therefore need not await enforcement to challenge the statute. Its members confront the choice between forgoing protected activity and risking sanction under an overbroad enactment. Lovell v. City of Griffin, 303 U.S. 444, 452-53 (1938). The judgment should be reversed.
