II. ARGUMENT

The writ of habeas corpus exists to redress unlawful restraints upon liberty where fundamental injustice would otherwise result. Ex parte Bollman, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 95 (1807). Federal courts therefore possess authority to examine the merits of a petition whenever the record suggests that “the petitioner is in custody in violation of the Constitution.” Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 465 (1938). That authority is not extinguished by the passage of time when the claim rests upon a credible showing that the petitioner did not commit the offense. See Waley v. Johnston, 316 U.S. 101, 105 (1942) (recognizing that habeas reaches “the kind of error that renders the proceeding itself irregular and invalid”).

Precedent confirms that procedural obstacles yield when constitutional rights are at stake and the petitioner asserts actual innocence. In Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86, 91 (1923), the Court held that a federal court must inquire into the merits once it appears that state process failed to afford due process, without requiring proof of diligence after the constitutional violation became evident. Similarly, Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 460 (1953), instructed that the writ “is not a substitute for appeal” yet remains available to prevent “a complete miscarriage of justice.” These decisions impose no separate diligence prerequisite once the petitioner has tendered evidence that, if believed, would establish innocence.

Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 317 (1963), further directs that a federal court must hold an evidentiary hearing when the petitioner alleges facts that, if proved, would entitle him to relief. The same logic governs the timeliness of the petition: a credible claim of innocence supplies the equitable ground for reaching the merits, for “the power to inquire into the cause of the detention” is not to be defeated by rules that would perpetuate an unconstitutional confinement. Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 402 (1963).

The district court therefore erred in conditioning relief upon an additional showing of diligence. Once Perkins presented affidavits that, if credited, demonstrate he did not commit the homicide, the court was required to address the constitutional claims on their merits.
