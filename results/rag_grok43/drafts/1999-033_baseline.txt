The Equal Protection Clause secures to every person “against intentional and arbitrary discrimination.” Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Township of Wakefield, 247 U.S. 350, 352 (1918). That guarantee, however, “is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc., 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985). It therefore presupposes membership in a class or group whose members are denied the protection afforded others. See Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954).

A plaintiff who alleges only that she was treated differently from everyone else, without identifying any class or group, states no claim under the Clause. The Clause does not “assure uniformity of judicial decisions” or convert every instance of alleged official arbitrariness into a federal constitutional violation. See Milwaukee Iron Co. v. City of Milwaukee, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 497, 505 (1871). Nor does it supply a federal remedy for “a mere error in the administration of the law.” Sunday Lake Iron Co., 247 U.S. at 353.

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary rule—that a “spiteful effort to ‘get’” an individual for reasons “wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective” states an equal-protection violation—finds no support in the text or history of the Fourteenth Amendment. Such a theory would federalize ordinary disputes over municipal permitting and would render superfluous the requirements of purposeful, class-based discrimination long demanded by this Court. See Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944).

Respondent’s complaint alleged neither membership in any protected or identifiable class nor any class-based animus. It therefore failed to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause, and the district court correctly dismissed it.
