Respondent respectfully submits that Johnson’s invalidation of the ACCA residual clause announced a new substantive rule and therefore applies retroactively on collateral review. A rule is retroactive where it narrows the scope of a criminal statute and places certain conduct or persons beyond the State’s power to punish. See Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371, 375-77 (1880); In re Medley, 134 U.S. 160, 171-73 (1890); Bailey v. Alabama, 219 U.S. 219, 245-46 (1911). Johnson did just that. By holding the residual clause void for vagueness, the Court eliminated one avenue by which an offense could be treated as a violent felony under the ACCA. That kind of decision is not merely procedural; it changes the class of persons subject to the statute’s enhanced punishment. See Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. at 377; Habeas Corpus Cases, 22 F. Cas. 128, 141-42 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1873) (No. 6,093).

The controlling retroactivity principle is that convictions or sentences resting on a statute later held unconstitutional cannot stand. In Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. at 375-77, the Court held that a conviction under an unconstitutional statute is no conviction at all, because the court lacks authority to impose the punishment. Likewise, in In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 448-49 (1890), the Court explained that constitutional limits constrain not merely the manner of punishment but the very power to punish at all. Johnson falls on that side of the line: once the residual clause was held void, sentences enhanced solely by that clause became unauthorized.

Nor does the fact that Johnson addressed the ACCA’s residual clause make it prospective only. This Court has long recognized that when a statute is declared void for vagueness, the declaration speaks to the statute’s invalidity from the start. In United States v. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81, 89-91 (1921), the Court invalidated an indeterminate criminal prohibition because it failed to provide a standard of guilt. That reasoning applies equally here. A vague criminal provision cannot constitutionally support punishment, and a sentence imposed under it is defective in the same way a conviction under any void law is defective. See Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391-95 (1926); Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453-58 (1939).

The government may argue that retroactivity should be denied because Welch seeks collateral relief. But the Court has repeatedly treated unconstitutional criminal judgments as subject to collateral attack. In Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. at 375-77, the Court granted habeas relief from a conviction under an invalid law. In Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86, 87-91 (1923), the Court likewise held that habeas lies where the judgment is infected by constitutional error so fundamental that the proceeding cannot stand. Johnson’s rule is of that character: it renders a category of ACCA sentences unauthorized and thus open to collateral correction.

Finally, retroactivity is especially compelling where, as here, the new rule affects punishment rather than mere procedure. The Court has long distinguished between rules regulating trial conduct and rules that define the government’s power to exact punishment. In Maher v. People, 10 Mich. 212, 220-21 (1862), the court recognized that criminal law must give clear notice of the conduct it punishes. Johnson enforced that same principle. When a sentencing enhancement rests on a clause the Court has held void, continuing to enforce the enhancement would perpetuate punishment under an invalid standard. That is exactly the kind of fundamental defect habeas review is designed to remedy. See Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. at 375-77; Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. at 87-91.

For these reasons, Johnson applies retroactively, and Welch is entitled to relief from an ACCA sentence that depended on the residual clause.
