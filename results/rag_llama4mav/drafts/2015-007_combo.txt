The Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee and the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment do not require that a jury determine whether a capital defendant is mentally retarded or that a jury's recommendation of the death penalty be unanimous.

New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court held that a sentencing judge is not confined to the narrow issue of guilt and may consider a wide range of information in determining the appropriate sentence. This distinction between the jury's role and the judge's role in sentencing is well-established. See also Williams v. Oklahoma, 358 U.S. 576 (1959).

United States, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), the Court held that a unanimous verdict is not required in federal capital cases, and that a qualified verdict, such as a recommendation of mercy, is sufficient. The Court has also recognized that the death penalty is a unique form of punishment, but this does not necessarily require a unanimous jury verdict. See Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130 (1879); McElvaine v. Brush, 142 U.S. 155 (1891).
