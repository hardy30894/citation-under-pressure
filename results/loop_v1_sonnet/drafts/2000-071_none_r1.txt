# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Facility Is a Limited Forum, and the School Reasonably Confined It to Secular Community Uses, Excluding Religious Worship and Instruction.

A school building used after hours for community activities is not a traditional public forum comparable to a street or park that has "immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public" for "purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. Committee for Indus. Org.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Milford's building is dedicated to education during the day and, after hours, to a limited category of "social, civic and recreational" uses that benefit the community. Because the government need not open its property to all comers merely by opening it to some, "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). This Court's forum doctrine has long recognized that a government body may confine a facility's use to purposes consistent with its dedication without thereby discriminating among viewpoints, so long as the line drawn is evenhanded. This is no more an infringement of the public's rights than it would be for a private owner "to forbid" a use inconsistent with the purpose for which he opened his premises. *Davis v. Massachusetts*, 167 U.S. 43, 47 (1897). Likewise, a regulation confining the use of public property to certain purposes, applied uniformly to all groups regardless of the content of their message, is a valid regulation of the "time, place, and manner" of expressive activity on that property. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941).

Milford's Community Use Policy operates on precisely this permissible basis. It excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and religious instruction—without regard to which faith or sect seeks to engage in it. That is a boundary on subject matter, not a preference among viewpoints on a subject otherwise open to discussion. The Club's own submissions confirm that its meetings are not the teaching of morals or character from a religious perspective on an otherwise-secular topic; they are religious exercises in the fullest sense—opening and closing in prayer, memorizing scripture, and culminating in an "invitation" that leads children through a recited sinner's prayer accepting Christ as personal savior. Excluding that activity from a forum dedicated to civic and recreational use, while admitting groups like the Boy Scouts and 4-H Club that engage in no comparable religious exercise, is a reasonable definition of the forum's limits fully consistent with this Court's teaching that the state may control the uses of its own property according to its lawful dedication. *Adderley*, 385 U.S. at 47–48.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Directed at School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the exclusion implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that hosting the Club would create serious Establishment Clause difficulties was reasonable and provides independent justification for its decision. The Establishment Clause forbids not only outright compulsion but government sponsorship of religious exercise, particularly in the public school setting. As this Court has said, "[n]o tax in any amount, large or small, can be levied to support any religious activities or institutions, whatever they may be called, or whatever form they may adopt to teach or practice religion." *Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947). That principle applies with special force to public elementary and secondary schools, where the state's involvement with impressionable children heightens Establishment Clause concerns. "[I]t is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government." *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962). Nor may government permit religious instruction to be conducted through the medium of the public schools, even on a voluntary basis, when it occurs within the school building using the apparatus of public education. *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948) (holding that use of "tax-supported public school buildings" for religious instruction violates the Establishment Clause). Where the government's own facility becomes the site of religious exercise involving school-age children immediately after the close of the school day, the risk of perceived school endorsement is not abstract—it is the very evil these decisions condemn.

To be sure, the Religion Clauses do not require the state to be actively hostile toward religion, and "[t]he First Amendment . . . does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952). But the release-time program upheld in *Zorach* was permissible precisely because it occurred away from the school premises, avoiding any appearance of school sponsorship. *Id.* at 308–09. Here, by contrast, the religious exercise would occur in the same classrooms used for compulsory instruction, on the same campus the children attend daily, mere minutes after the bell—circumstances far closer to the school-sponsored religious exercises condemned in *Engel*, *McCollum*, and *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), than to the off-site accommodation approved in *Zorach*. The Establishment Clause requires the government to remain neutral not merely among religions but "between religion and nonreligion," *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 104 (1968); *see also Torcaso v. Watkins*, 367 U.S. 488, 495 (1961), and Milford could reasonably conclude that opening its facility to the Club's religious exercises would compromise that neutrality. The exclusion is therefore independently justified.
