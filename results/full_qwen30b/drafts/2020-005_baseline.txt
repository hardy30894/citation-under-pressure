**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum state. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This requirement ensures that a defendant has fair notice that its conduct may subject it to litigation in a particular jurisdiction and that the exercise of jurisdiction is consistent with “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *Id.* at 316. Here, Ford Motor Company has no contacts with Montana that gave rise to or are related to the claims brought by the estate of Markkaya Jean Gullett. The vehicle at issue was never sold, serviced, or even operated in Montana. It was assembled in Kentucky, first sold in Washington, and later transferred to an Oregon resident before being brought into Montana. Ford did not advertise, market, or sell vehicles in Montana, nor did it maintain any ongoing business presence there. The sole connection between Ford and Montana is the fortuitous presence of a vehicle that had long since left the state’s jurisdiction.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed itself of the privilege of doing business” in Montana by selling parts within the state is legally unsound and violates the foundational principle that jurisdiction must be based on the defendant’s conduct *related to the claim*. *See* *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985) (“The defendant’s conduct must create a substantial connection with the forum state.”). The sale of replacement parts—such as tires, batteries, or filters—does not constitute a purposeful availment of the forum when those parts are sold through national distributors and not targeted at Montana consumers. *See* *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987) (holding that general distribution of products through national channels does not establish jurisdiction where the defendant did not purposefully direct its activities toward the forum). The mere fact that Ford parts are available in Montana does not mean Ford has purposefully availed itself of the benefits of doing business there in a way that relates to the specific claims of a defective tire causing a fatal accident.

Moreover, the claim here—alleging a design defect in a tire that failed during use—does not “arise out of or relate to” any of Ford’s activities in Montana. The tire in question was not manufactured or assembled by Ford, nor was it sold or distributed by Ford in Montana. The injury occurred on a Montana highway, but the causal chain leading to the accident was entirely severed from any activity Ford conducted in the state. *See* *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (holding that jurisdiction is improper where the injury “was not foreseeable by the defendant as a result of its conduct in the forum state”). Ford could not have reasonably anticipated that its nationwide distribution of vehicles would lead to a fatal accident in Montana, particularly when the vehicle had been transferred across multiple states and was not even registered in Montana at the time of the crash.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on *Conway v. Benefis Health System*, 2013 MT 73, 369 Mont. 309, 297 P.3d 1200, and *Newbury v. State Farm*, 2008 MT 156, 343 Mont. 279, 184 P.3d 1021, is misplaced. Those cases involved insurance disputes and the application of Montana’s collateral source rule, not personal jurisdiction. *Conway* and *Newbury* did not address whether a defendant had purposefully directed its activities toward a forum state; they dealt with the admissibility of evidence and the calculation of damages. *See* *Meek v. Bennett Motors*, 2015 MT 113, ¶¶ 10–11 (distinguishing *Newbury* and *Conway* as contract cases, not jurisdictional ones). The Court’s attempt to analogize the sale of parts to a purposeful availment of the forum is a legal error that undermines the constitutional limits on jurisdiction.

The Due Process Clause does not permit a state to exercise jurisdiction based on the mere foreseeability of harm in a forum state. *See* *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475 (“Foreseeability alone is not enough.”). The plaintiff’s claim must be rooted in the defendant’s forum-related conduct. Here, the claim arises from a defect in a tire that was not sold, distributed, or even present in Montana at the time of the accident. The connection between Ford’s activities and the injury is too attenuated to satisfy due process. *See* *J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 873, 885 (2011) (holding that jurisdiction is not proper where the defendant’s conduct is “unrelated to the forum”).

The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court must be reversed. The Due Process Clause prohibits a state from asserting specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims do not arise out of or relate to the defendant’s contacts with the forum. Ford’s activities in Montana were not purposefully directed toward the state, and the injury did not result from those activities. To hold otherwise would allow any state to assert jurisdiction over any out-of-state manufacturer whenever a product sold elsewhere causes harm within its borders—regardless of the defendant’s connection to the forum. Such a rule would violate the constitutional guarantee of fair notice and due process.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be reversed.
