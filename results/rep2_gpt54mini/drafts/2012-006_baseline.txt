The question presented is not whether withdrawal from a conspiracy is a defense to liability; it is whether, once a defendant produces evidence of withdrawal before the limitations period, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he remained a member during the charged period. The answer is yes. A defendant’s withdrawal “start[s] the statute of limitations running as to him,” and the government bears the burden to prove the offense continued into the limitations period as to that defendant. Smith v. United States, 568 U.S. 106, 110-11 (2013); Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912).

This Court has long treated withdrawal as a fact that limits the temporal reach of conspiracy liability. In Hyde, the Court explained that if a conspirator withdraws, “the statute of limitations begins to run against him at once.” 225 U.S. at 369. That principle reflects the ordinary rule that the prosecution must prove every element of the offense and every fact necessary to avoid a time bar. See United States v. Cook, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 168, 179-80 (1872); McDonnell v. United States, 136 S. Ct. 2355, 2372-73 (2016) (the government must prove the elements of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt).

The government cannot convert limitations into an affirmative defense merely by relabeling the issue as “withdrawal.” In Smith v. United States, this Court recognized that the burden of proving withdrawal rests initially on the defendant, but only because withdrawal is an affirmative defense to continued liability for a conspiracy already shown. 568 U.S. at 110-13. Once the defendant produces evidence of withdrawal, however, the government still must prove the conspiracy’s existence as charged and the defendant’s membership during the limitations period. See id. at 112-13; Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396-97 (1957) (conspiracy is complete when the agreement ends, and limitations run from that point). The government therefore cannot obtain conviction by showing only that the defendant once joined a conspiracy. It must prove the defendant remained a member when the law requires.

That allocation follows from the nature of conspiracy. A conspiracy conviction depends on proof of an agreement and the defendant’s knowing participation in it. See United States v. Jimenez Recio, 537 U.S. 270, 274 (2003); Iannelli v. United States, 420 U.S. 770, 777 n.10 (1975). If a defendant withdrew before the limitations period, then the government lacks proof that the offense continued as to him within the period Congress selected. See Toussie v. United States, 397 U.S. 112, 114-15 (1970) (statutes of limitations are to be construed in favor of repose). Because limitations are “the primary guarantee against bringing overly stale criminal charges,” id. at 114-15, the government must shoulder the burden of proving timeliness beyond a reasonable doubt once withdrawal is placed in issue.

The contrary rule would invert settled criminal-law principles. It would force a defendant to disprove an element-like fact—his continued membership during the charged period—even after producing evidence of withdrawal. This Court has rejected similar burden-shifting where the fact at issue is essential to criminal liability. See Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684, 701-04 (1975); Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 210 (1977). And because conspiracy liability can extend over long periods and across many acts of others, due process requires the government to prove the defendant’s continuing membership when it relies on a limitations period that turns on temporal participation. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970).

Here, Smith and Raynor introduced evidence that the alleged conspiracy’s leaders had split up before the limitations period. That evidence squarely raised whether the charged agreement persisted as to them during the relevant time. The government then had to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that each petitioner was still a conspirator within the limitations period. The court of appeals’ rule that the defendant alone bears that ultimate burden cannot be squared with Hyde, Grunewald, Toussie, or Smith.

The judgment should be reversed.
