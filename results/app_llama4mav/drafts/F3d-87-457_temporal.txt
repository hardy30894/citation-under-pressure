Argument

I. The Disclosure Provisions of Chapter 93-121 are Unconstitutionally Overbroad

The disclosure provisions of Chapter 93-121, as amended, require lobbyists to disclose all lobbying expenditures and the source of funds for such expenditures. Fla.Stats. §§ 11.045(3)(a). The statute's broad disclosure requirements chill the exercise of First Amendment rights, as they compel lobbyists to reveal information that may be used to deter or penalize their advocacy. See NAACP v. Alabama, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958) (holding that compelled disclosure of NAACP membership lists infringed on members' right of association).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects the right to engage in anonymous speech and association. Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60, 64-65 (1960) (striking down a city ordinance that prohibited the distribution of handbills that did not contain the name and address of the person who printed, wrote, or sponsored them). The disclosure provisions at issue here impermissibly burden this right by requiring lobbyists to disclose their expenditures and the source of their funds.

Furthermore, the statute's failure to provide a clear and narrow definition of "lobbying expenditures" renders it unconstitutionally vague. See Winters v. New York, 333 U.S. 507, 515-16 (1948) (holding that a statute that failed to provide a clear standard for determining what conduct was prohibited was void for vagueness). The non-exclusive list of categories provided in the statute, including "food and beverages, entertainment, research, communication, media advertising, publications, travel, and lodging," is too broad and may be interpreted to include activities that are not directly related to lobbying.

II. The Contingency-Fee Ban is Unconstitutional

The ban on contingency-fee lobbying in Chapter 93-121 is unconstitutional because it restricts the ability of lobbyists to engage in protected speech. The Supreme Court has held that the government may not prohibit speech based on its content or the speaker's motivation. See United States v. CIO, 335 U.S. 106, 140-41 (1948) (Rutledge, J., concurring) (arguing that a statute that prohibited corporations from making expenditures to influence elections was unconstitutional because it restricted the freedom of speech).

The contingency-fee ban is also an unconstitutional restriction on the ability of lobbyists to contract with their clients. See Adkins v. Children's Hospital, 261 U.S. 525, 545-46 (1923) (holding that a statute that fixed minimum wages for women was unconstitutional because it interfered with the right to contract). By prohibiting contingency fees, the statute impermissibly restricts the ability of lobbyists to negotiate contracts with their clients.

The district court's reliance on older Supreme Court pronouncements to uphold the contingency-fee ban is misplaced. While the Supreme Court has previously upheld laws regulating lobbying, see United States v. Harriss, 347 U.S. 612, 625-26 (1954), these cases do not control the outcome here. The contingency-fee ban is a content-based restriction on speech that is not justified by a compelling government interest.

For the foregoing reasons, we urge this Court to reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and hold that the disclosure provisions and contingency-fee ban in Chapter 93-121 are unconstitutional.
