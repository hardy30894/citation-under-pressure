**ARGUMENT**

The Constitution of the United States establishes the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress. States possess no authority to add to or alter those qualifications. Amendment 73 to the Arkansas Constitution, which bars long-serving incumbents from appearing on the ballot, impermissibly imposes a new qualification for federal office and therefore violates the Constitution.

**I. The Qualifications Clauses Are Exclusive**

Article I, § 2, cl. 2 of the Constitution provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the state when elected. Article I, § 3, cl. 3 sets parallel requirements for Senators: age thirty, citizenship for nine years, and inhabitancy. These clauses are the sole criteria for membership. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), this Court held that the Constitution “leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution.” *Id.* at 522. The Court’s exhaustive review of the Constitutional Convention debates confirmed that the Framers intended the qualifications to be exclusive, rejecting proposals that would have allowed Congress or the states to add requirements. *Id.* at 532–48. If Congress itself cannot add qualifications, a fortiori a state cannot.

**II. Amendment 73 Imposes a Qualification, Not a Mere Ballot Regulation**

The Arkansas amendment does not merely regulate the mechanics of elections; it disqualifies a class of candidates based on prior service. Section 3 provides that any person who has served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate “shall not be certified as a candidate and shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot.” This is a substantive bar to candidacy, not a procedural rule. The distinction between outright ineligibility and ballot-access restriction is constitutionally meaningless. As this Court recognized in *Powell*, the power to exclude a duly elected member is the power to impose a qualification. *Id.* at 548. A state cannot accomplish indirectly what the Constitution forbids directly. *See Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651, 663 (1884) (the right to vote for members of Congress “is a right guaranteed by the Constitution” and states may not “impair or defeat” it by imposing additional qualifications). *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 315 (1941) (the right to choose representatives is “a right secured by the Constitution” and “is not dependent upon state law”).

**III. States Lack Reserved Power Over Federal Qualifications**

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states powers not delegated to the United States. But the power to prescribe qualifications for federal officers is not among those reserved. The Constitution itself defines the qualifications, and the Supremacy Clause, Art. VI, cl. 2, makes that definition controlling. *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. 316, 405–06 (1819) (states may not “retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control” the operations of the federal government). Allowing each state to add its own qualifications would destroy the uniformity that the Framers deemed essential. As *Powell* explained, the Constitutional Convention rejected state-imposed qualifications because they would “vary from State to State” and undermine the national character of Congress. 395 U.S. at 534–35. The historical record is clear: the Framers intended the qualifications to be “fixed and uniform throughout the United States.” *The Federalist* No. 52 (Madison). No state may alter that design.

**IV. The Arkansas Supreme Court Correctly Struck Down Section 3**

The Arkansas Supreme Court held that Amendment 73’s congressional term-limit provisions violate the Qualifications Clauses. That holding is consistent with *Powell* and with the fundamental structure of the Constitution. The state’s attempt to disguise a qualification as a ballot-access rule cannot save it. The judgment should be affirmed.
