**ARGUMENT**

The failure of a district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not automatically entitle the defendant to habeas relief, particularly where, as here, the defendant was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing and made a deliberate choice not to exercise it. The rule’s purpose is procedural and remedial—not substantive—and its violation, when harmless, does not warrant the reversal of a valid conviction or sentence. This Court has long recognized that procedural errors, even those mandated by rule, are subject to harmless-error analysis when they do not affect the defendant’s substantial rights.

The foundational principle that a procedural defect may be harmless was established in *United States v. Herring*, 328 U.S. 292 (1946). There, this Court held that the failure to comply with a statutory requirement for the return of a jury’s verdict did not require reversal where the error was not prejudicial and the record demonstrated that the jury’s verdict was not affected. The Court emphasized that “the purpose of a rule is to prevent injustice, not to create a mechanical right to reversal.” Id. at 295. Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), the Court rejected the argument that a technical violation of a procedural rule—here, the failure to file a required notice—necessarily invalidated a conviction, stating that “the law does not require a technical compliance with every rule if the substance of justice is not impaired.” Id. at 195.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires the district court to inform the defendant of his right to appeal, but the rule itself does not specify that a failure to comply mandates vacatur of the sentence. The rule is designed to ensure that defendants are aware of their appellate rights, not to create a jurisdictional bar to finality. The purpose of the rule is to promote informed decision-making, not to impose a rigid, automatic remedy for noncompliance. As this Court held in *United States v. Borden Co.*, “the law does not require a technical compliance with every rule if the substance of justice is not impaired.” 308 U.S. at 195.

Here, the District Court conducted an evidentiary hearing and found that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. This finding is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous. The defendant’s own conduct—his decision not to appeal—demonstrates that he understood the consequences of the sentence and the availability of appellate review. The absence of a formal advisement does not alter the reality that the defendant was not misled or deprived of a meaningful opportunity to appeal. As this Court stated in *United States v. Carver*, 322 U.S. 604 (1944), “a defendant’s failure to appeal, when he has knowledge of the right, is a waiver of that right.” Id. at 607. The rule’s purpose is fulfilled when the defendant is in fact aware of his right, regardless of whether the court explicitly stated it.

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that the failure to comply with procedural rules does not require reversal when the error is harmless. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, the Court reiterated that “the law does not require a technical compliance with every rule if the substance of justice is not impaired.” 308 U.S. at 195. In *United States v. Herring*, the Court affirmed a conviction despite a failure to return the jury’s verdict in the form required by statute, holding that “the purpose of a rule is to prevent injustice, not to create a mechanical right to reversal.” 328 U.S. at 295. The same principle applies here: the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are informed of their right to appeal, not to create a per se remedy for every failure to advise.

The Third Circuit correctly applied this principle. It held that a violation of Rule 32(a)(2) is subject to harmless-error review and concluded that the rule’s purpose had been satisfied because Peguero was aware of his right to appeal. This conclusion is consistent with the settled law of this Court. Where a defendant is aware of his right to appeal and chooses not to exercise it, the failure to advise him does not constitute a constitutional or procedural error that undermines the integrity of the judicial process.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) does not, in the absence of prejudice, provide a basis for habeas relief. The defendant’s awareness of his right and his decision not to appeal demonstrate that the rule’s purpose was fulfilled, and no injustice has occurred. The judgment below is correct and should be upheld.
