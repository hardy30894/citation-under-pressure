**Argument**

The Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power. For decades, the Supreme Court has upheld congressional delegations so long as Congress states a policy and an intelligible standard to guide the executive. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 425-27 (1944); *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-79 (1948). That standard is plainly satisfied here.

Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. It authorized scheduling only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” and it limited that judgment by specifying the factors to be considered: the drug’s abuse potential, scientific evidence, current scientific knowledge, history and pattern of abuse, public health risk, dependence liability, and whether it is a precursor. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h); cf. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426-27 (1944) (upholding delegation where Congress supplied general policy and standards). The statute further requires notice to the Secretary of Health and Human Services and consideration of the Secretary’s views. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). That is far more guidance than the Court required in cases sustaining broad wartime and economic delegations. *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 785-86 (1948); *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947).

The Act’s subject matter also strongly supports the delegation. The Court has recognized that where Congress legislates in a complex, technical field, broader discretion is permissible because Congress cannot “do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives.” *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 425-26 (1944); *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). Drug scheduling is precisely such a field: Congress must respond to rapidly changing chemical formulations and emerging public-health threats. A temporary-scheduling mechanism is therefore a measured administrative tool, not an abdication of lawmaking.

Nor does the fact that scheduling carries criminal consequences make the delegation invalid. The Court upheld a statute with criminal penalties in *Yakus*, explaining that Congress may authorize executive implementation so long as it establishes the governing standard. 321 U.S. at 424-27. Likewise, in *Lichter*, the Court sustained a wartime recapture scheme that affected criminal liability because Congress had set forth the policy and left only execution to the executive. 334 U.S. at 783-86. The Constitution does not require Congress itself to micromanage every application of a statute that it has enacted with adequate standards. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928).

The respondents’ subdelegation argument fails for the same reason. Congress often authorizes the head of an executive department to act through subordinates, because “[t]he head of a department may act through subordinate officers.” *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 512-13 (1974) (describing the established principle); see also *The Confiscation Cases*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 454, 457 (1869) (recognizing that executive duties may be performed by authorized agents). The Attorney General is the head of the Department of Justice, and Congress expressly vested the scheduling power in that office. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h). Nothing in the statute requires that the Attorney General personally sign every scheduling determination. To the contrary, where Congress places authority in an executive department, execution through responsible subordinates is the ordinary mode of administration. *United States v. Germaine*, 99 U.S. 508, 510-11 (1879); *United States v. Norton*, 97 U.S. 164, 166-67 (1877).

The Third Circuit correctly rejected the claim that the delegation to the Attorney General is invalid because the statute operates in the criminal law. The Court has never adopted a special nondelegation rule for criminal statutes; rather, it has consistently asked whether Congress set the policy and standard. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 425-27 (1944). Congress did so here. The temporary-scheduling provision is therefore a constitutional delegation of administrative authority, and the Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA was an authorized exercise of that administrative structure.
