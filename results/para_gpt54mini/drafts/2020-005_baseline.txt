Ford’s petition asks this Court to discard a settled due-process principle: specific jurisdiction is proper when the defendant’s forum conduct and the plaintiff’s claims bear the constitutionally required relationship. The Due Process Clause does not require a strict causal chain between in-state contacts and each element of the claim. It requires only that the defendant have “minimum contacts” with the forum such that the exercise of jurisdiction “does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945).

This Court has repeatedly recognized that specific jurisdiction turns on the relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation. In World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980), the Court held that jurisdiction is appropriate where the defendant “purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State.” Later cases explain that the inquiry is not limited to whether the forum contact itself caused the injury. In Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462, 472-76 (1985), the Court upheld jurisdiction where the defendant deliberately created “continuing obligations” with the forum, even though the suit arose from a contract relationship rather than a single in-state tortious act. Likewise, Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court, 480 U.S. 102, 112-13 (1987), emphasized that due process turns on “purposeful availment” and the “relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation,” not on a mechanical causation test.

That understanding is confirmed by Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court, 582 U.S. 255, 262-63 (2017), which framed the governing question as whether the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to the defendant’s contacts with the forum.” The Court used both phrases disjunctively, showing that “relate to” means something distinct from strict causation. If Ford’s reading were accepted, the “relate to” language would be stripped of independent force. But courts do not construe due-process doctrine to render words redundant. See, e.g., Daimler AG v. Bauman, 571 U.S. 117, 139 n.19 (2014) (describing specific jurisdiction as hinging on the “affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy”); Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S. A. v. Hall, 466 U.S. 408, 414 n.8 (1984) (contrasting the “arise out of” standard for specific jurisdiction with general jurisdiction).

Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction here fits squarely within those principles. Ford extensively marketed, sold, and serviced the same model vehicles in Montana. The alleged defects were not unique to a single chassis; they concerned Ford’s nationwide design, manufacturing, and warnings for the Explorer. Gullett was killed in Montana when the Explorer rolled on a Montana highway. That is precisely the kind of forum-related product-liability controversy that “relates to” Ford’s Montana activities under Bristol-Myers Squibb, 582 U.S. at 262-63, and that satisfies due process under Burger King, 471 U.S. at 475-76.

Ford’s contrary rule would improperly resurrect a causation-only test rejected by modern jurisdiction doctrine. The Court has long recognized that a corporation may be haled into a forum where it has systematically cultivated the market for the very product at issue. See Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc., 465 U.S. 770, 774-75 (1984). In Keeton, the plaintiff’s claim need not have been caused by every forum contact; it was enough that the defendant’s in-state activities supported the suit. The same is true here. Ford “purposefully availed” itself of the Montana market by serving it for years with the same Explorer model and its parts, and Montana may adjudicate claims concerning injuries from that product in that market.

Nor does due process allow Ford to escape jurisdiction merely because the precise Explorer involved first entered the stream of commerce elsewhere. In World-Wide Volkswagen, 444 U.S. at 297-98, the Court explained that a defendant is subject to jurisdiction when it can reasonably anticipate being sued in the forum because of its own forum-directed conduct. Ford did exactly that. It did not passively place a product into the stream of commerce; it deliberately targeted Montana consumers with sales, advertising, and service networks for the Explorer. That is enough. See Burger King, 471 U.S. at 475; Keeton, 465 U.S. at 774-75.

The judgment should be affirmed. On Ford’s view, a multinational corporation could extensively cultivate a state market for a product, profit from that market, and yet avoid local jurisdiction whenever an injured resident bought the product through an intermediary. That cramped approach conflicts with International Shoe, Burger King, Asahi, Bristol-Myers Squibb, Keeton, and World-Wide Volkswagen. Due process requires a “fair warning” that a defendant may be sued where it has deliberately served the forum market and the litigation relates to that conduct. Burger King, 471 U.S. at 472. Ford had that warning here.
