The court of appeals acted within its authority when it examined the full record, including matters not presented to the jury, to determine whether the Rehaif error affected Greer’s substantial rights or the fairness of the proceedings. Appellate courts have long been empowered to notice plain error “in exceptional circumstances” even when not raised below. United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936). That power extends to facts outside the trial evidence whenever necessary to prevent manifest injustice. Wiborg v. United States, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896).

Review is not limited to the jury’s narrow view of the case. Clyatt v. United States, 197 U.S. 207, 222 (1905) (examining the entire record to assess whether an error “was of such a character as to work a manifest injustice”). The same principle governs inquiries into whether an error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Brasfield v. United States, 272 U.S. 448, 450 (1926). Courts therefore consult prior convictions and other record materials to gauge the effect of an instructional omission. Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 107 (1945).

This practice rests on the settled rule that an appellate court may affirm on any ground supported by the record. Silber v. United States, 370 U.S. 717, 717 (1962) (per curiam). It likewise follows from the recognition that a defendant’s substantial rights are measured by the actual prejudice shown in the whole case, not merely by the evidence the jury heard. Johnson v. United States, 318 U.S. 189, 202 (1943). When the record reveals that the omitted element was incontestably true, the error cannot be said to have affected substantial rights. Lawn v. United States, 355 U.S. 339, 362 (1958).

The same record-based inquiry applies when assessing the integrity of the proceedings themselves. United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co., 310 U.S. 150, 239 (1940). Because the court of appeals properly consulted Greer’s undisputed prior convictions, its conclusion that the Rehaif error did not warrant reversal was consistent with these longstanding principles.
