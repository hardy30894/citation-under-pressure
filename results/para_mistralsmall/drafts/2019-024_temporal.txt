**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit’s Construction of “Actual Knowledge” Under ERISA § 1113(2) Is Inconsistent with the Statute’s Text and Legislative History.**

The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that “actual knowledge” under ERISA § 1113(2) requires only that a plaintiff be aware of the underlying transaction—not that the plaintiff knew the transaction violated ERISA. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) (1976). This interpretation conflicts with the statute’s plain meaning and the Supreme Court’s longstanding rule that ignorance of the law is no excuse. *United States v. Laub*, 385 U.S. 475, 487 (1966) (“The general rule that ignorance of the law or a mistake of law is no defense to criminal prosecution is deeply rooted in the American legal system.”); *Cheek v. United States*, 498 U.S. 192, 199 (1991) (reaffirming the rule in the context of tax law).

Congress amended ERISA in 1987 to eliminate the constructive knowledge provision, leaving only the actual knowledge standard. *Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987*, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. Before that amendment, courts uniformly held that actual knowledge required awareness of both the facts and their legal significance. *See, e.g., United States v. Murdock*, 290 U.S. 389, 396 (1933) (“[T]he knowledge required to make an act criminal is not knowledge of the law but knowledge of the facts.”); *United States v. International Minerals & Chem. Corp.*, 402 U.S. 558, 563 (1971) (same). The Ninth Circuit’s construction ignores this principle by divorcing knowledge of the facts from knowledge of their legal consequences.

**II. The Ninth Circuit’s Approach Undermines ERISA’s Statute of Limitations and Encourages Gamesmanship.**

The Ninth Circuit’s rule invites plaintiffs to avoid the limitations period by claiming ignorance of the law, even when they had full access to the relevant facts. This is inconsistent with the purpose of statutes of limitations, which is to promote repose and prevent stale claims. *See, e.g., Order of Railroad Telegraphers v. Railway Express Agency*, 321 U.S. 342, 348 (1944) (“The purpose of a statute of limitations is to compel the exercise of a right within a reasonable time so that the adverse party has a fair opportunity to defend.”).

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s construction allows plaintiffs to manipulate the limitations period by simply refusing to read disclosures. This incentivizes willful ignorance and frustrates the purpose of ERISA’s disclosure requirements. *See, e.g., Varity Corp. v. Howe*, 516 U.S. 489, 502 (1996) (noting that ERISA’s disclosure provisions are designed to ensure that plan participants are informed of their rights).

**III. Pre-1970 Precedent Supports Petitioner’s Construction of “Actual Knowledge.”**

This Court has long held that actual knowledge requires awareness of both the facts and their legal significance. In *United States v. Falstaff Brewing Corp.*, 362 F.2d 873 (D.C. Cir. 1966), the court held that actual knowledge of a violation of the Sherman Act required knowledge of the anticompetitive conduct *and* its illegality. *Id.* at 876 (“Knowledge of the facts alone is not enough; the defendant must also know that the conduct is illegal.”). Similarly, in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 316 F.2d 884 (7th Cir. 1963), the court held that actual knowledge of a violation of the Robinson-Patman Act required knowledge of the discriminatory pricing *and* its illegality. *Id.* at 887.

These cases confirm that actual knowledge under ERISA § 1113(2) must include awareness that the conduct at issue violated ERISA. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary construction is unsupported by precedent and should be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Ninth Circuit’s judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to enter summary judgment in favor of Petitioner.
