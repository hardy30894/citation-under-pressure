## ARGUMENT

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power. Congress supplied an “intelligible principle” to guide the Attorney General’s discretion, and the subdelegation to the Drug Enforcement Administration is authorized by statute.

### I. The Temporary Scheduling Provision Contains an Intelligible Principle

The nondelegation doctrine requires only that Congress “lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). That standard is satisfied here.

Section 811(h) authorizes the Attorney General to schedule a substance temporarily only when he finds that doing so “is necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). The statute further directs him to consider three specific factors: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of that abuse, and “what, if any, risk there is to the public health.” *Id.* § 811(h)(3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)–(6)). The temporary scheduling lasts no more than one year, with a possible six-month extension. *Id.* § 811(h)(2).

These constraints closely resemble those upheld in *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361 (1989). There, Congress specified the goals the Sentencing Commission must pursue, the factors it must consider, and the limits on its authority. *Id.* at 374–79. Here, Congress similarly identified the object of the delegation (avoiding imminent public hazards), prescribed the relevant factors, and imposed durational limits. The “intelligible principle” test is “not demanding,” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 379, and this delegation satisfies it.

This Court has repeatedly upheld delegations that provide far less guidance. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 424–27 (1944), the Court sustained a delegation to the Price Administrator to set “fair and equitable” commodity prices, a standard no more specific than “imminent hazard to the public safety.” And in *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 785–86 (1948), the Court approved a delegation allowing the President to recoup “excessive profits” without any definition of that term. The temporary scheduling provision is at least as precise.

### II. The Delegation Does Not Violate the Nondelegation Doctrine Simply Because It Involves Criminal Law

Petitioners invoke the two 1935 cases invalidating delegations, *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). But those decisions turned on the complete absence of statutory standards, not on the presence of criminal penalties. In *Panama Refining*, the statute “declared no policy as to the transportation of the excess production” and “did not qualify the President’s authority.” 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, the President was given “virtually unfettered” discretion to approve codes of fair competition. 295 U.S. at 541–42. Here, by contrast, Congress carefully circumscribed the Attorney General’s power with substantive criteria, procedural requirements, and temporal limits.

Even if criminal delegations warrant heightened scrutiny, § 811(h) satisfies that standard. The Attorney General cannot criminalize conduct at will; he must first find an imminent public hazard, consider the statutory factors, and publish notice. See *United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544–45 (9th Cir. 1988) (upholding § 811(h) delegation). Moreover, the temporary scheduling order is not insulated from all judicial review; constitutional challenges remain cognizable. *Touby v. United States*, 500 U.S. 160, 168–69 (1991) (assuming without deciding that review of constitutional claims is available). The presence of judicial oversight further confirms the delegation’s constitutionality. *See Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 385–86 (noting that judicial review of Commission’s guidelines was a “check” on delegated power).

### III. The Attorney General Properly Subdelegated the Temporary Scheduling Authority to the DEA

Congress authorized the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions” under the Controlled Substances Act to “any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General exercised that authority by promulgating 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which assigns to the DEA Administrator “[functions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.” This subdelegation falls squarely within the congressional grant.

The temporary scheduling power is a “function” under § 871(a). Nothing in § 811(h) suggests that the Attorney General must exercise the power personally. To the contrary, the statute’s broad delegation authority and the practical need for administrative efficiency support subdelegation. *See United States v. Hovey*, 674 F. Supp. 161, 171 (D. Del. 1987) (upholding subdelegation of temporary scheduling). The DEA Administrator’s exercise of the power is therefore lawful.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
