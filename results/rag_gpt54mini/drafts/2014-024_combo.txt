**Argument**

Section 875(c) should not be construed to impose criminal liability absent proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication as a threat. “[W]e must avoid a construction of the statute that raises constitutional doubts” where a reasonable alternative exists. *Jones v. United States*, 529 U.S. 848, 857 (2000). Here, the better reading of § 875(c) is that Congress punished only true threats—communications knowingly or purposefully made as threats—not speech that merely sounds threatening to a reasonable listener.

This Court has long recognized that criminal statutes are ordinarily read to require some culpable mental state. In *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250-63 (1952), the Court explained that mere omission of an express mens rea term does not eliminate the common-law presumption of guilty intent. Section 875(c) contains no express mental-state term, but that silence does not authorize strict liability for speech. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly construed federal statutes to include a mental element where the text is silent or ambiguous. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251-53 (1922); *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605-06 (1994); *Liparota v. United States*, 471 U.S. 419, 425-27 (1985).

That interpretive principle is especially important here because § 875(c) regulates speech. The First Amendment permits punishment only for “true threats,” a category the Court identified to protect persons from “the fear of violence” and the disruption that fear causes. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969). But *Watts* did not approve objective liability untethered to the speaker’s state of mind; instead, it treated the context, conditional language, and reaction of the listeners as evidence bearing on whether the defendant actually meant a threat. *Id.* at 708. The same is true of *Black v. United States*, 538 U.S. 343, 359-60 (2003), where the Court held that a “true threat” is a serious expression of an intent to commit unlawful violence. Although *Black* involved cross burning, its reasoning confirms that the gravamen of a threat is the speaker’s intent to threaten, not merely the effect on a hypothetical reasonable recipient. See *id.* at 359.

The Third Circuit’s objective test also conflicts with due process. This Court has held that criminal guilt ordinarily requires notice and blameworthiness, not mere happenstance audience reaction. *Lambert v. California*, 355 U.S. 225, 228-30 (1957). And when a statute is susceptible to two constructions, one of which would raise serious constitutional problems, the Court adopts the narrowing construction. *Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council*, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988). Reading § 875(c) to require subjective intent avoids criminalizing protected speech that is crude, hyperbolic, artistic, or self-conscious, yet not intended as a threat.

The Court’s precedents in analogous speech cases underscore this point. In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447-49 (1969), the Court held that advocacy may be punished only when it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it. In *NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886, 927-29 (1982), the Court rejected liability for speech unless it was intended to produce unlawful results. Those cases reflect a consistent constitutional rule: when speech may be punished because of its dangerous character, the speaker’s own purpose matters.

Finally, this Court has never treated threat statutes as dispensing with mens rea. In *Sexton v. California*, 189 U.S. 319 (1903), the Court recognized that the threat itself must be tied to the criminal extortionate purpose the statute targets. Likewise, in *Cole v. Arkansas*, 333 U.S. 196, 201-02 (1948), the Court emphasized that due process forbids conviction on a theory the jury never actually passed upon. An objective-only instruction here likewise allowed conviction without the jury finding that Elonis intended his Facebook posts as threats.

The government may argue that an objective standard better protects recipients from fear. But *Watts* and *Black* show that the Constitution already balances that concern through the “true threat” doctrine; it does not license punishment of speech absent proof of the speaker’s subjective culpability. Because § 875(c) can and should be read to require proof that the defendant intended his communication as a threat, Elonis’s conviction must be reversed.
