**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise such a challenge, and even if she did, the statute at issue is validly enacted under Congress’s authority to implement treaty obligations. The Court has long held that private parties cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to attack federal legislation absent a showing of direct injury to their own rights or interests. Moreover, the Constitution empowers Congress to enact laws necessary and proper to carry out treaties duly ratified by the United States. This principle was firmly established in *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), and reaffirmed in subsequent decisions interpreting the Treaty Power and its relationship to state sovereignty.

First, Bond lacks standing to assert a Tenth Amendment challenge. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the Tenth Amendment does not confer individual rights upon private citizens. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939), the Court rejected a claim by private utility companies that the federal government’s operation of a public power project violated the Tenth Amendment. The Court explained: “The sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment. As we have seen[,] there is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states, and, if this were not so, the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment].” Id. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366. This holding remains binding precedent: private parties cannot litigate constitutional claims arising from federal encroachment on state powers unless they are directly injured in their own legal rights. Here, Bond was prosecuted for her own conduct—attempted poisoning using toxic chemicals—and not as a representative of a state or its interests. She has no personal stake in the balance between federal and state authority beyond her own conviction. Thus, her claim is an “undifferentiated, generalized grievance,” which falls short of the concrete injury required for standing. See *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284 (10th Cir. 2004) (citing *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992), but noting that the principle of standing applies equally to constitutional challenges brought by individuals); cf. *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (holding that a private party lacks standing to challenge a federal law on Tenth Amendment grounds when the state has not objected).

Second, even if standing were not an obstacle, the statute implementing the Chemical Weapons Convention is validly enacted under Congress’s authority to execute its treaty obligations. The Treaty Power is a plenary grant of authority to the national government. As the Court stated in *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432, “It is obvious that there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could.” The Constitution vests the President with the power to make treaties with the advice and consent of two-thirds of the Senate, see U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2, and authorizes Congress to pass laws necessary and proper to carry them into effect, see id. art. I, § 8, cl. 18. This framework contemplates that treaty implementation may extend into areas traditionally reserved to the states, so long as the treaty itself is validly ratified. The Court has repeatedly upheld this principle. In *United States v. Belmont*, 301 U.S. 374, 379 (1937), the Court declared: “The treaty-making power is one of the most important attributes of sovereignty. It is a power which, like other powers of the Government, is supreme within its sphere.” Similarly, in *United States v. Pink*, 318 U.S. 234, 243 (1943), the Court affirmed that “the treaty power is not limited by the ordinary rules of jurisdiction or by the limitations imposed by the Constitution upon the legislative power of Congress.” These cases establish that the validity of a treaty-implementing statute turns not on whether it intrudes on state regulatory domains, but on whether the underlying treaty is valid and properly ratified.

The Chemical Weapons Convention was duly ratified by the United States Senate and signed by the President. The Implementation Act of 1998, which includes 18 U.S.C. § 229, was enacted pursuant to Article I, Section 8, Clause 18—the Necessary and Proper Clause—to fulfill the nation’s international obligations. The Court has never held that a treaty must be implemented through a specific enumerated power; rather, it has recognized that the Treaty Power itself provides independent constitutional authority. As the Court observed in *United States v. Curtiss-Wright Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304, 316 (1936): “The treaty power is not merely a power to make treaties, but also a power to enforce them.” This power extends to criminal enforcement where necessary to prevent violations of international commitments. Indeed, in *United States v. Schooner *Peggy*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 103, 114 (1815), the Court held that “a treaty made under the authority of the United States is the supreme law of the land,” and thus binding on all courts and officials.

Bond’s argument that the statute is vague or overbroad fails under settled precedent. A statute is not unconstitutionally vague simply because it covers a broad range of conduct, so long as it provides fair notice of prohibited behavior. In *United States v.
