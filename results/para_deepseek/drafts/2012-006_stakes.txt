ARGUMENT

I. The Government Bears the Burden of Proving Every Element of a Conspiracy Beyond a Reasonable Doubt, Including That the Conspiracy Continued Into the Statute of Limitations Period.

The Fifth and Sixth Amendments require that the government prove each element of a charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). For a drug conspiracy under 21 U.S.C. § 846, the government must establish (1) an agreement to violate drug laws, (2) each defendant’s knowing and voluntary participation, and (3) that the conspiracy existed within the relevant statute of limitations period. *Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391, 396 (1957) (government must prove that conspiracy “continued to exist” within the limitations period). The statute of limitations is an essential element of the offense, not a mere affirmative defense; the government therefore bears the ultimate burden of proof on this issue. *United States v. Ben Zvi*, 242 F.3d 89, 96 (2d Cir. 2001) (“the government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period”).

II. Withdrawal Does Not Shift the Burden of Proof to the Defendant; Rather, It Triggers the Government’s Obligation to Prove Continued Membership Beyond a Reasonable Doubt.

A defendant who withdraws from a conspiracy ceases to be liable for acts committed after the withdrawal. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 464 (1978). Withdrawal is an affirmative defense for which the defendant must produce some evidence. *Id.* at 464–65. But once the defendant raises a colorable claim of withdrawal, the burden of persuasion remains on the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1234 (7th Cir. 1981) (“the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant did not withdraw before the limitations period began”); *accord United States v. Lash*, 937 F.2d 1077, 1082 (6th Cir. 1991) (same). The defendant’s withdrawal does not transform the statute-of-limitations element into an affirmative defense; it merely requires the government to disprove the withdrawal to satisfy its burden of showing continued membership. *United States v. Smith*, 209 F.3d 847, 853 (6th Cir. 2000) (“the government bears the burden of proving that the conspiracy existed within the limitations period, and the defendant’s withdrawal is relevant only as a rebuttal to that proof”).

Here, the petitioners presented evidence that the alleged leaders of the conspiracy, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, ended their association before the limitations period began. This evidence—which the government did not dispute—established that the conspiracy, as a unified enterprise, had dissolved. The government therefore bore the burden of proving that the petitioners remained members of a single, ongoing conspiracy after the leaders’ split. The district court incorrectly denied the motion for new trial, and the court of appeals erred in affirming, by effectively requiring the petitioners to prove their withdrawal or to disprove their continued membership. That approach inverts the constitutional allocation of proof.

III. The Court of Appeals’ Decision Conflicts with Settled Precedent and Undermines the Protections of the Statute of Limitations.

The statute of limitations serves to protect defendants from stale charges and to ensure that the government acts diligently. *United States v. Marion*, 404 U.S. 307, 322 (1971). By placing the burden on the defendant to prove that the conspiracy did not continue into the limitations period, the D.C. Circuit’s holding dilutes that protection. The government must prove the timeliness of its prosecution, just as it must prove all other elements. *See, e.g., United States v. A-A-A Elec. Co.*, 788 F.2d 242, 247 (4th Cir. 1986) (“the government bears the burden of proving that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period”). The petitioners’ evidence of withdrawal placed the government’s proof squarely at issue; the government’s failure to rebut that evidence should have resulted in acquittal or a new trial. *See United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d at 1234–35 (reversing conviction where government failed to prove defendant’s continued membership after withdrawal).

The decision below cannot be squared with *Grunewald* or the long line of cases requiring the government to prove the conspiracy’s existence within the limitations period. This Court should reverse and hold that once a defendant produces evidence of withdrawal, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the statutory period.
