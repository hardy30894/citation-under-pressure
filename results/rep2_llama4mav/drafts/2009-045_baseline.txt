The Supreme Court has established that a district court may, in its discretion, enhance a lodestar attorney's fee award in "rare" and "exceptional" cases where there is specific evidence to support an exceptional result and superior performance. Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984); Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air ("Delaware Valley I"), 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986). The Eleventh Circuit has correctly interpreted this precedent to allow for enhancements based on the quality of representation and results obtained. Kenny A. v. Perdue, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008).

In Blum, the Court held that an upward adjustment may be justified in the "rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was 'exceptional.'" 465 U.S. at 899. The Court in Delaware Valley I reaffirmed this principle, stating that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." 478 U.S. at 565.

The district court in this case made detailed findings, based on its own observations and experience, that the plaintiffs' counsel provided exceptional representation and achieved outstanding results. Kenny A. v. Perdue, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286, 1290 (N.D.Ga. 2006). The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the district court did not abuse its discretion in enhancing the lodestar fee award. Kenny A., 532 F.3d at 1242.

The petitioners argue that the district court's enhancement was improper because it was based on the court's own "testimony" rather than evidence in the record. However, the district court's observations and findings are not "testimony" in the classical sense, but rather explanations for the enhancement, as required by Blum and Delaware Valley I. The district court's unique position to evaluate the attorneys' performance and results justifies its discretion to grant an enhancement.

Furthermore, there is no circuit split on this issue. Multiple circuits have held that a district court may enhance a lodestar fee award based on exceptional performance and results. See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co., 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co., 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999).

The Supreme Court's precedent and the decisions of the lower courts demonstrate that a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained in "rare" and "exceptional" cases. The district court's enhancement in this case was proper, and the Eleventh Circuit's decision should be affirmed.
