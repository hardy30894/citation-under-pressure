**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits cruel and unusual punishment. In *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958), the Court held that the Amendment must be interpreted in light of “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” This principle has long guided the Court’s interpretation of the Eighth Amendment’s scope, particularly in the context of capital punishment. The execution of individuals who are mentally retarded—now more accurately termed “intellectually disabled”—violates this evolving standard and constitutes cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment.

The Court has consistently recognized that the death penalty must be reserved for those who are most culpable and whose moral blameworthiness is at its highest. In *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 258 (1972), Justice Brennan, writing for the plurality, emphasized that “the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment is not a static standard, but one that must be interpreted in light of the changing moral and social values of the nation.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 188 (1976), where the Court upheld the death penalty only when applied in a manner that “minimizes the risk of arbitrary and capricious imposition.” The Court has since made clear that the death penalty must be reserved for those who pose the greatest threat to society and who are fully capable of understanding the consequences of their actions.

The execution of individuals who are intellectually disabled—those with significantly below-average intellectual functioning and deficits in adaptive behavior—violates this principle. Such individuals possess diminished capacity for rational decision-making, impulse control, and understanding of the consequences of their actions. As the Court recognized in *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 410 (1986), “the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of the insane,” not because they lack moral culpability, but because they are “incapable of understanding the nature and purpose of the punishment.” The same logic applies to those who are intellectually disabled. They are not less culpable in every respect, but their diminished mental capacity renders them less morally blameworthy than those who are fully competent.

This distinction is not merely theoretical. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 328 (1989), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment requires that juries be permitted to consider mitigating evidence of intellectual disability during the sentencing phase of a capital trial. The Court emphasized that “the jury must be able to give effect to the mitigating value of the defendant’s mental condition.” The fact that the jury may still impose the death penalty after considering such evidence does not negate the constitutional obligation to allow that consideration. Indeed, *Penry* itself recognized that intellectual disability is a “mitigating factor” that must be weighed in the capital sentencing process.

The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Penry* in affirming Atkins’ death sentence. The jury was permitted to hear testimony from a forensic psychologist who testified that Atkins was mildly intellectually disabled. The jury was also given the opportunity to hear rebuttal evidence from the State. The fact that the jury ultimately imposed the death penalty does not mean that the process was constitutionally flawed. The Eighth Amendment does not require that all intellectually disabled individuals be exempt from capital punishment. Rather, it requires that the sentencing process be informed by the defendant’s diminished culpability and that the punishment be proportionate to the offender’s moral responsibility.

The Court has repeatedly held that the death penalty must be proportionate to the offender’s personal culpability. In *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977), the Court struck down the death penalty for rape, stating that “the punishment must fit the crime.” In *Kennedy v. Louisiana*, 554 U.S. 407, 417 (2008), the Court held that the death penalty for the rape of a child was unconstitutional because it “does not comport with the Eighth Amendment’s guarantee of proportionality.” The Court reasoned that “the death penalty is reserved for the most serious crimes, those involving the intentional taking of human life.” The execution of an intellectually disabled individual, whose capacity for rational thought and moral judgment is significantly impaired, does not meet this standard.

The Court has also recognized that the execution of intellectually disabled individuals is inconsistent with contemporary standards of decency. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 318 (2002), the Court held that “the execution of mentally retarded persons is grossly disproportionate and excessive punishment” under the Eighth Amendment. The Court found that “a significant number of States have concluded that the execution of mentally retarded persons is inappropriate,” and that “the national consensus against the execution of mentally retarded persons is clear.” The Court emphasized that “the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment is not a static standard, but one that must be interpreted in light of the changing moral and social values of the nation.” This principle, first articulated in *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958), remains the foundation of the Court’s analysis.

The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court is therefore correct. The execution of Daryl Atkins, who is intellectually disabled, would violate the Eighth Amendment. The Court’s decision in *Atkins v. Virginia* is binding precedent. The judgment of the lower court should be affirmed.

**WHEREFORE**, the respondent respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court.
