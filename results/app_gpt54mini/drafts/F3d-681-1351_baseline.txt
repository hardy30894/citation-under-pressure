**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board Misapplied Harari I and Erred in Holding that the ’880 Application Is Entitled to the ’566 Filing Date.

The question on remand was narrow: whether the intervening ’838 and ’768 applications satisfied 35 U.S.C. § 120 so that the ’880 application could claim the April 13, 1989 filing date of the ’566 application. Section 120 permits an application to obtain an earlier filing date only if the later application contains “a specific reference to the earlier filed application” and the applications are otherwise in continuity. 35 U.S.C. § 120; see In re NTP, Inc., 654 F.3d 1279, 1296 (Fed. Cir. 2011). The Board erred by reading Harari I to decide far more than it did, and by collapsing the distinct inquiries of incorporation by reference, written description, and § 120 priority.

Harari I did not hold that the ’579 application was incorporated into every application in the chain as a matter of law. Rather, it held only that, “at this stage of the proceedings,” the identity of the incorporated reference was clear to a reasonable examiner “in light of the documents presented.” Harari v. Hollmer, 602 F.3d 1312, 1352-53 (Fed. Cir. 2010). That holding addressed whether the preliminary amendment added new matter under 35 U.S.C. § 132. Id. at 1353-54. It did not decide whether the later applications independently complied with § 120’s continuity requirements or whether the ’838 and ’768 applications themselves contained the necessary disclosure and reference structure to carry the ’566 filing date forward. See Lockwood v. American Airlines, Inc., 107 F.3d 1565, 1571-72 (Fed. Cir. 1997) (priority requires compliance with the statutory requirements for each link in the chain).

This Court has long held that priority under § 120 is not automatic and must be established application by application. In re Wertheim, 646 F.2d 527, 536-37 (C.C.P.A. 1981). A later application may claim the benefit of an earlier filing only if each intermediate application in the chain satisfies the statute. Encyclopaedia Britannica, Inc. v. Alpine Electronics of America, Inc., 609 F.3d 1345, 1352-53 (Fed. Cir. 2010). The Board’s remand decision ignored that principle and treated the copied incorporation language as if Harari I had conclusively established continuity back to 1989. It had not.

II. The Copied Language Does Not Establish That the ’838 and ’768 Applications Properly Incorporated the ’579 Application for § 120 Purposes.

The Board’s analysis improperly presumed that the incorporation language, once deemed sufficient to defeat a new-matter attack in the ’880 application, was necessarily sufficient to satisfy § 120 in the intermediate applications. But incorporation by reference depends on whether the reference is actually clear and complete in the document at issue. Advanced Display Systems, Inc. v. Kent State University, 212 F.3d 1272, 1283-84 (Fed. Cir. 2000). And the statutory priority inquiry asks whether the earlier application itself provides the requisite disclosure and reference support. In re Daniels, 144 F.3d 1452, 1456-57 (Fed. Cir. 1998).

The ’566 application’s language was, on its face, internally imprecise: it referred to an application “filed on the same day as the present application,” when the present application was the ’566 application itself, not the later descendants. J.A. 1204. Harari I allowed use of surrounding documents in the initial filing context to identify that reference as the ’579 application. 602 F.3d at 1353-54. But that pragmatic construction cannot substitute for the statutory requirement that each intermediate application provide the chain of priority. See Rasmussen v. SmithKline Beecham Corp., 413 F.3d 1318, 1322-23 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (priority depends on what the application as filed conveys). The Board failed to identify any independent basis in the ’838 or ’768 applications showing that they met § 120’s specific-reference requirement or that they preserved the same disclosure with the necessary continuity.

III. The Board’s Decision Collapses the Distinction Between New Matter and Priority, Contrary to Federal Circuit Precedent.

The Board’s ruling is especially flawed because it treated a finding that the preliminary amendment was not new matter as if it resolved the separate question of whether the ’566 filing date could be claimed through a multi-generational continuation chain. Those are different inquiries. Written description and new matter ask whether the applicant is impermissibly adding disclosure. Purdue Pharma L.P. v. Faulding Inc., 230 F.3d 1320, 1323-24 (Fed. Cir. 2000). Priority under § 120 asks whether the chain of applications legally preserves entitlement to the earlier date. PowerOasis, Inc. v. T-Mobile USA, Inc., 522 F.3d 1299, 1305-06 (Fed. Cir. 2008).

The Board’s approach would allow an applicant to bootstrap priority from a single ambiguous incorporation statement, even where the statutory prerequisites for each intervening application are not shown. That is inconsistent with § 120 and with the principle that priority claims must be strictly proven. See Transmission Systems & Engineering, Inc. v. International Union, United Automobile, Aerospace & Agricultural Implement Workers of America, 757 F.2d 198, 203-04 (Fed. Cir. 1985). The judgment should therefore be reversed, and the Board directed to hold that the ’880 application is not entitled to the April 13, 1989 filing date of the ’566 application.
