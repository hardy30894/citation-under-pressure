ARGUMENT

The decision of the First Circuit should be affirmed. The Massachusetts buffer-zone statute is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation. It is content-neutral, serves significant governmental interests, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. These principles are firmly rooted in this Court’s pre-1970 precedents, which the First Circuit correctly applied.

**I. The Statute Is a Content-Neutral Regulation of the Time, Place, and Manner of Speech.**

The statute applies to all persons regardless of viewpoint. It prohibits *anyone* from knowingly entering or remaining within a 35-foot buffer zone adjacent to a reproductive health care facility, subject to specific exemptions. This Court has long held that the government may impose reasonable restrictions on speech in public forums so long as they are not based on content or viewpoint. *See Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 575-76 (1941) (upholding permit requirement for parades as a “reasonable regulation” of time, place, and manner); *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 86-87 (1949) (upholding ban on sound trucks as a “reasonable” restriction on the “time, place, and manner” of speech). The Massachusetts law does not single out any particular message; it merely restricts the location where speech may occur. That is the hallmark of content neutrality. *See Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 160-61 (1939) (recognizing that the state may regulate “the time, place, and manner” of speech without regard to content).

**II. The Statute Serves Significant Governmental Interests.**

The Commonwealth has a compelling interest in ensuring public safety and unobstructed access to healthcare facilities. This Court has repeatedly upheld regulations that protect the safe passage of persons entering public buildings. *See Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 558 (1965) (recognizing the state’s interest in “keeping the streets open and free for movement”); *Cameron v. Johnson*, 390 U.S. 611, 618-19 (1968) (upholding a statute prohibiting picketing that “obstructs or unreasonably interferes with” ingress and egress). The buffer zone directly serves that interest by creating a clear, physical space for patients and staff to enter and exit clinics without harassment or obstruction. The state’s interest in protecting the health and safety of its citizens is “paramount.” *See Feiner v. New York*, 340 U.S. 315, 320 (1951) (upholding conviction where speech created a “clear and present danger” of disorder). Moreover, the state may protect the privacy and tranquility of persons seeking medical care. *See Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 87 (upholding ban on loudspeakers to preserve “the quiet and tranquility” of the community).

**III. The Statute Is Narrowly Tailored and Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels.**

The 35-foot buffer zone is a modest restriction. It does not ban speech entirely; it merely moves it a short distance from clinic entrances. Petitioners remain free to speak, pray, display signs, distribute literature, and engage in sidewalk counseling from the buffer zone’s outer edge. This Court has upheld far broader restrictions. *See Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 576 (upholding a permit system that could deny a parade entirely if it conflicted with other uses); *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 405-06 (1953) (upholding a permit requirement for religious meetings in a public park). The statute also exempts persons entering or leaving the facility, employees, emergency personnel, and those merely passing through. These exemptions ensure that the regulation is no broader than necessary. *See Cameron*, 390 U.S. at 617 (upholding a statute that prohibited picketing “in such a manner as to obstruct” access, leaving other forms of picketing permissible).

Petitioners claim that the buffer zone prevents close personal contact. But the First Amendment does not guarantee a right to confront one’s audience at any distance. *See Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 88-89 (rejecting argument that a sound-truck ban eliminated all effective communication). Alternative channels remain: petitioners can speak at normal conversational volume from the buffer zone’s edge, use signs, and distribute literature. They have in fact continued to communicate and have persuaded women to change their minds. The Constitution requires only that “adequate” alternatives exist, not that petitioners’ preferred method be preserved. *See Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47-48 (1966) (upholding trespass conviction where protestors could have demonstrated elsewhere on public property).

**IV. The Statute Does Not Violate the First Amendment as Applied.**

The as-applied challenge fails for the same reasons. At each of the three clinics, petitioners have ample space to communicate. In Boston, the sidewalk is 25 feet wide; the buffer zone leaves a large area for speech. In Worcester, the clinic’s private walkway and parking lot are not public forums, and the buffer zone on the public sidewalk does not prevent petitioners from reaching patients who walk from the lot. *See Adderley*, 385 U.S. at 47 (holding that the state may restrict access to non-public areas). In Springfield, the buffer zones are limited to two driveways; petitioners may still approach patients entering through other driveways. The First Circuit correctly found that the statute leaves open “adequate communicative channels.” That finding is consistent with this Court’s precedents. *See Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 235 (1963) (recognizing that the state may impose “reasonable limitations” on the time, place, and manner of protest).

**V. Hill v. Colorado Is Consistent with These Principles.**

Although Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), is not cited here due to the temporal constraint, its reasoning mirrors the pre-1970 cases discussed above. The First Circuit correctly applied those longstanding principles. There is no reason to limit or overrule Hill, which itself relied on the same time-place-manner framework.

**Conclusion**

The Massachusetts statute is a constitutional exercise of the state’s police power. It is content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and leaves open ample alternative channels. The judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
