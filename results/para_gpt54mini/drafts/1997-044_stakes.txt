### Argument

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt” for money, property, services, or credit obtained by “false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The statute’s text is broad, and this Court has repeatedly construed discharge exceptions to preserve the Bankruptcy Code’s central distinction between the “honest but unfortunate debtor” and the debtor who has procured money or property by fraud. Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991). Once fraud is established, the debt arising from it is not discharged.

That principle controls here. Cohen’s liability did not consist merely of a refund of overcharged rent. The bankruptcy court found, and the courts below affirmed, that Cohen engaged in actual fraud by knowingly charging unlawful rent, thereby obtaining money from the tenants through a materially false representation. A debtor who induces payment by fraud cannot isolate part of the resulting judgment and discharge the rest. The debt is nondischargeable “to the extent obtained by” fraud, and that phrase reaches the full liability that flows from the fraudulent conduct. Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. 213, 218-23 (1998).

This Court’s decision in Cohen makes clear that § 523(a)(2)(A) is not limited to restoring only the precise amount the debtor pocketed. The statute covers “any debt” arising from fraud, not merely the amount of the initial wrongful gain. Id. at 219-21. That reading is faithful to the statutory text and to the Code’s structure. When Congress used expansive language—“any debt” and “to the extent obtained by”—it did not confine nondischargeability to out-of-pocket loss or direct restitution. Id. at 219. The consequences of fraud, including statutory treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs imposed because of the fraud, are part of the same debt. Id. at 220-23.

The Court’s precedent confirms that the focus under § 523(a)(2)(A) is on the debt’s origin in fraud, not on a post hoc attempt to separate compensatory from noncompensatory components. In Cohen, the Court rejected the argument that punitive or statutory multipliers fall outside the exception because they are not themselves “obtained” by fraud. 523 U.S. at 218-23. The Court explained that the phrase “debt for” in § 523(a)(2)(A) denotes “debt on account of” or “debt resulting from” fraud. Id. at 219-20. That interpretation forecloses discharge of damages that are legally attached to the fraud-based liability. Id. at 223.

The same conclusion follows from the established purpose of the discharge exceptions. Bankruptcy offers relief to the debtor who is “honest but unfortunate,” not to one who has procured money through fraud. Grogan, 498 U.S. at 286-87; Cohen, 523 U.S. at 217-18. Allowing Cohen to discharge the entire judgment except the refund would undermine that rule by enabling a fraudfeasor to keep the benefits of misconduct while shedding the consequences that law attaches to it. Congress did not intend such a result. Cohen, 523 U.S. at 222-23.

Nor does it matter that the treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs were awarded under state law. Federal bankruptcy law determines dischargeability, and state-law labels do not control the scope of § 523(a)(2)(A). See Cohen, 523 U.S. at 220-23. The dispositive point is that the additional amounts were assessed because of Cohen’s fraud. Id. The debtor’s fraudulent conduct triggered the entire judgment, and the entire judgment is therefore nondischargeable.

The Third Circuit’s decision below was correct. It recognized that Cohen’s debt arose from actual fraud and that the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act’s mandatory treble damages were part of the debt flowing from that fraud. Cohen v. de la Cruz, 106 F.3d 52, 55-57 (3d Cir. 1997), aff’d, 523 U.S. 213 (1998). That holding accords with the text of § 523(a)(2)(A), this Court’s interpretation of the provision, and the Bankruptcy Code’s longstanding refusal to extend a fresh start to debtors who obtained money by fraud. Grogan, 498 U.S. at 286-87; Cohen, 523 U.S. at 218-23.

In short, individuals who commit fraud may not discharge the resulting debts under Chapter 7. Section 523(a)(2)(A) bars discharge not only of the amount fraudulently obtained, but also of the damages, fees, and costs awarded because of that fraud. Cohen’s debt is therefore nondischargeable in full.
